I've been chewing on performance legitimacy for ages now — the China/Singapore thing where a government's claim to rule rests mostly on what it delivers rather than on how it got selected — and I keep going in circles with a friend who says it's all just Hobbes with extra steps. So let me put the actual practice on the table and ask what it presupposes. In China, local cadres are evaluated and promoted against target metrics (growth, stability maintenance, pollution, poverty alleviation), and the party defends one-party rule partly by pointing at delivered outcomes and partly, in the same breath, at nationalism, at history having chosen the party, at anti-corruption as moral renewal — and during zero-COVID it defended itself by lives saved at exactly the moment the policy was wrecking the growth numbers that were supposed to be the other half of the case. Singapore is the cleaner example: most people live in Housing Board flats, the streets are safe, corruption is genuinely low and policed by a dedicated bureau, ministerial pay is pegged to what top private-sector professionals earn on the explicit argument that you have to pay for competence, and service delivery is measured obsessively. And yet the PAP also holds real contested elections, and when its vote share dips it visibly treats that as a signal and adjusts policy. That last bit is what I find genuinely puzzling: if the claim to rule is output-based, why care about the vote at all?

So: reconstruct the implicit theory of legitimacy here. Does the practice strictly presuppose a consequentialist, output-based view — legitimacy as a function of results — or is it underdetermined, compatible with several quite different stories (delivery as evidence of hypothetical consent, as a proxy for competent trusteeship, as a signalling device that keeps everyone's expectations coordinated)? Which rival views does it quietly rule out? Is the election better understood inside this practice as an information channel — a measurement instrument, kind of like a market — rather than as an authorization, and does the practice actually have to pick between those readings? I've also started calling something the auditor problem: if legitimacy tracks delivered outcomes and the same regime controls the collection and publication of the outcome statistics, then the practice seems to presuppose some independent check that by construction isn't there, and the whole thing smells like Goodhart's law all the way down. What does it presuppose about trusting official numbers? And is the implicit view actually eliminativist about legitimacy — legitimacy just is: people aren't revolting — dressed up in normative language, or does it need a real normative claim?

Here's the case I use to force the issue, which I made up and haven't seen anywhere: suppose a colonial administration, or for that matter a very well-run city-management AI, beat every elected alternative on every metric the government itself tracks — order, growth, health, even self-reported well-being — but was in no sense chosen by, answerable to, or even the same kind of thing as the people it governs. Does the practice's own logic make it legitimate? If yes, the implicit theory is pure output and we should just say so; if no, then the practice is secretly leaning on something like shared membership or authorization that the output story was supposed to replace. Relatedly, the ratchet: does output-based legitimacy accept its own implication that a government which stops delivering loses its claim, with nobody to enforce that, or does it quietly presuppose something else — founder's mandate, we built this country, the problem of successors? And what would actually have to change in these countries' practice if you swapped the implicit theory for a procedural consent view, or for a flatly welfarist one? I'd like the strict presuppositions seperated from the things that merely fit well, and the places where the practice is philosophically underdetermined.
