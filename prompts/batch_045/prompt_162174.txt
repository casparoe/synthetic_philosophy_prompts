hey, I'm doing a third-year module on nineteenth-century political economy and the brief for our last essay is annoyingly open — "put one of the authors we've read into a present-day policy argument and work out what they'd actually say." Everyone's doing the obvious ones. I want to do Mill, but I dont want to write the boring version where he just turns out to be a mild social democrat who'd have liked higher taxes, so I need someone to actually think it through with me rather than an essay. Write it in British English please, and don't bother with citations or a reading list, I'll add those myself.

Here's the setup I want you to use. Take Mill as he is late in life, when he's much warmer about socialism than undergrads expect: the bit where he says the wage relation isn't the last word and that the form of association likely to win out eventually is workers owning the capital collectively and hiring managers they can remove; his view that socialist criticism of how property actually works is largely right even though the practical difficulties are huge; his insistence that any such change should be tried gradually and experimentally, competing fairly with ordinary enterprise rather than being imposed; his worry that equal division gets eaten up unless population is restrained; and his seperate fear of a state that sucks up all the talent in the country and turns into a bureaucratic despotism.

Then drop him into a scenario I've made up. In a rich country, advanced automation has pushed labour's share of income down hard, and a proposal gets real political traction: every citizen is permanently issued a tradable share in a public capital fund, funded by taxes on data and compute rents, paying an unconditional cash dividend — call it the Citizen's Endowment. No work requirement, no means test, and crucially the holder has no say in how any enterprise is run. Worker-owned cooperatives of the Mondragon type also exist and do fine, but they're still a small slice of employment.

What I want you to work out is whether, by Mill's own late standards, the Endowment is what he was predicting, or a near miss he'd be suspicious of. The bits I keep going round on:

1. Most of what he likes about cooperation seems to be about its effects on the character and intelligence of the worker — learning to deliberate about a shared interest, taking responsibility for a common enterprise, practising self-government — not just about people getting more money. Does handing everyone capital income get any of that, or does it get the redistribution while losing the thing he thought redistribution was for?

2. His population objection has basically inverted: rich countries are now below replacement fertility, so the Malthusian disaster he thought would wreck an equal division isn't the live risk. Would that remove the main brake on his socialist sympathies, or does the worry just migrate somewhere else?

3. Is the Endowment the sort of thing his hostility to an all-absorbing state bureaucracy makes him reject — or would he see it as less centralising than nationalisation, precisely because no official runs a firm on your behalf?

4. He wanted socialism to win a fair experimental competition with individual enterprise. What would he actually count as evidence in that competition? Is a co-op sector that survives and pays better but barely grows a win? And would the fact that the Endowment is wildly popular count for it or against it?

Give me about 900 to 1100 words, in continuous prose rather than numbered answers, and land on an actual verdict — Mill would welcome it, tolerate it, or oppose it, and which of his commitments is doing most of the work in getting there. Also be clear about where his texts just run out and you're extrapolating on his behalf. If the honest answer is that this scenario is one his framework has no resources for, say that instead and tell me which part of his view breaks first.
