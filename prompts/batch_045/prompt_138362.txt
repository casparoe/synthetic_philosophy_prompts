In the AI safety reading group I run at my high school, the scheme we keep coming back to — break a hard task into subtasks that a weaker system can each handle, let a slower overseer combine the answers, then train a fast model to imitate the whole pipeline and repeat — is always introduced with the same reassuring picture: it is just how a competent organization already works. A manager who is no cleverer than her staff gets a project done by decomposing it and inspecting each piece; what the team learns gets written into a handbook and trained into new hires; the firm becomes faster without becoming less careful; and the new hires eventually become managers themselves. The picture is used to license two conclusions at once: that this buys competence without any mysterious leap, and that alignment comes along for free, because the parts are checked by something we already trust and the whole inherits the checks.

I would like that analogy audited rather than repeated. Give me the correspondence table — manager, employee, subtask, inspection, handbook, new hire, promotion — and then the places it fails.

The failure I keep getting stuck on, and have not seen handled: suppose we require that every subtask answer pass a check a weak overseer can actually run, and that the assembled answer combine only checked outputs. In a firm, when two employees' parts each satisfy their own check but silently assume different budgets, deadlines, or units, someone with a view of the whole project notices the seam. In the amplified system, who has that view? The decomposition is itself produced by the system being improved, the checks are local, and nothing checks whether the parts fit together. Does every part verified even make sense as a route to the whole is safe, or is the analogy quietly supplying a unified supervisor that the scheme does not contain?

And on competence: in the firm, a handbook is read by people who bring their own judgment to its gaps, and the firm can always go slower and re-check. When the pipeline is distilled into a single fast forward pass, what becomes of the advantage that came from the slow steps? Does the analogy say anything here, or does it go silent exactly where I need it?
