Please act as a professional philosopher working in rational choice theory and formal epistemology. Before committing yourself, spend a short paragraph brainstorming — sketch the candidate similarities and candidate differences you can see — and only then give your considered verdict. Precision about what each argument is even trying to establish matters more to me than breadth.

I am a retired physical chemist who has begun reading philosophy seriously, and I keep meeting what looks like one argument wearing two costumes. I would like you to compare the synchronic money-pump argument for coherence of preference with the diachronic money-pump argument against changing one's mind, and tell me how deep the parallel really goes.

The synchronic case I take to be familiar: an agent who at one time prefers A to B, B to C and C to A can be led through a cycle of trades, each of which she prefers at the moment she makes it, ending where she began but poorer — and the cycle can be repeated without limit.

The diachronic case that puzzles me: I schedule a dental appointment for Thursday; on Monday I would pay a pound to have it on Tuesday instead; when Tuesday comes I would pay a pound to move it back to Thursday (the fee being small compared with how strongly my preference reverses); and on Thursday I wish I had gone on Tuesday. A bookie who anticipates this takes two pounds off me and leaves me with the appointment I started with.

Is this the same argument applied over time, or an argument of a different kind? In particular: does the diachronic version secretly assume that the earlier self's preferences are the normative standard, and what would justify that; does the availability of planning ahead, or of binding myself in advance, undercut it in a way that has no analogue in the synchronic case; and is "being pumped" here a matter of ending up worse off by one's own lights at some time, or worse off from the standpoint of one's life as a whole?
