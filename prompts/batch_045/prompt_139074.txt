The notes: the usual dialectic (closed case: symmetry, switching is pointless; open case: the 1.25x argument, always switch) leaves out a third option that nobody puts on the table, and I think this third option separates two things the discussion tends to run together — the information you get from the correlation between the two envelopes, and the information you get from your prior over the scale of the amounts. Call it the re-roll: after you open your envelope and see x, instead of switching you may discard the other envelope and receive a fresh one, generated by an independent draw of the smaller amount A from the same distribution followed by a fair coin flip to decide whether you get A or 2A. Re-rolling is worth 1.5·E[A] whatever you saw, so it is a fixed, x-independent benchmark; and in the closed case it is worth exactly the same as staying or switching, which is why the device only becomes interesting once you've looked. Notes on the rest: (i) with a prior p over the smaller amount in the discrete case, switching given x is right iff 2p(x) > p(x/2); in the continuous case with density f the rule is 4f(x) > f(x/2) — the 4 is not a typo, it comes out of the change of variables for the likelihood of observing x when you hold the doubled envelope, and I want you to check that I haven't botched it. (ii) Worked example, A uniform on {1, 2, 4}: six equiprobable states, μ = 7/3, blind pick worth 3.5; conditional expected value of the other envelope is 2, 2.5, 5, 4 at x = 1, 2, 4, 8 respectively; best switch-or-stay policy switches unless x = 8 and is worth 25/6 ≈ 4.17; best policy with re-roll available re-rolls at x = 1 and x = 2, switches at x = 4, stays at x = 8, and is worth 4.75. (iii) The bit I like: at x = 1 you know with certainty that you hold the smaller envelope, and re-rolling (3.5) still beats switching (2) — so even full information about which envelope you hold isn't enough to make switching right; you also need the prior to be optimistic about scale, and that's the half of the story the correlation talk hides. (iv) The case I think is the deep one: a normalizable prior with infinite mean, e.g. p(2^k) ∝ 2^(−k/2) on the doubling scale, where 2p(x) > p(x/2) holds everywhere, so always-switching is optimal at every x you could observe, while every policy has infinite expected value and re-roll is worth infinity too, so the whole diagnostic collapses — my claim is that the paradox proper lives here and the finite cases are just bookkeeping. (v) A claim my grad student says is straightforwardly wrong and I'm now unsure about: since re-roll is worth 1.5·E[A] whether or not you look, the ex ante value of the game is 1.5·E[A] and opening the envelope has zero expected value.
