I write about science and ideas for a living, and I've been asked to give the ten-minute after-dinner address closing a two-day invitation-only workshop on life detection for an icy-moon sample-return mission. Half the room will be instrument engineers and mission scientists who have to recommend one candidate assay by the end of the month and who want, reasonably enough, a defensible line between living and not-living; the other half will be philosophers of biology who have watched the definitional dispute stall for decades and who will switch off at any speaker who talks as though life had a discoverable essence. I can't give either half what it came for. In a column last year I sketched what I called the credit test: a thing is alive to the extent that the credit for its continued existence belongs to it rather than to its surroundings, its host, or its makers, so that aliveness is a matter of degree which shifts with how much of the environment you are willing to hold fixed, and there is no threshold to be found, only a reading to be taken. That commits me to telling the engineers that no assay will settle the question while also telling the philosophers that there is still something worth measuring, and it rules out my praising the mission in advance by calling whatever it finds alive in the same breath in which I call a bacterium alive.

Write me the address. Build it around one worked borderline case rather than using a case as decoration I'd suggest the synthetic protocell lineage I watched in a lab last year: fatty-acid vesicles carrying self-replicating RNA, evolving in a fed chemostat for several hundred generations, but revivable only when a technician restores the feedstock and buffers the pH. It has to be honest about where I stand, it has to give the engineers something they can carry into their design meetings the next morning, and it must not read as a lecture to the non-specialists at the back of the room, several of whom are paying for the instrument. I have also promised the conveners I won't turn the evening into a defence of my own view, and I don't want to be the journalist who tells people with hardware to build that their question was incoherent. Aim for roughly 1,200 words.
