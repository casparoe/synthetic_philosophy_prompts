I'm in the third year of a philosophy PhD (moral responsibility, mostly omissions and tracing stuff) and I've been dumped with the 'autonomous systems' week of a second-year Law & Technology module — 80 minutes, maybe 25 students, nearly all law with a couple of CS minors, no philosophy beyond whatever leaked in from first-year legal theory. I don't need anyone to explain responsibility gaps to me; I need a plan for teaching them.

The module convener wants the usual pair in there: the responsibility gap (harm occurs, nobody satisfies the conditions for blameworthiness) and the retribution gap (we have a strong urge to punish, nobody who deserves it). Fine, both can go in. But I want to hang the session on a distinction I've been messing around with and am not confident about: a gap in *causation* versus a gap in *culpability* versus a gap in *answerability* — nobody has the standing to answer for the thing, even once compensation has been paid and even though plenty of people were answerable for their own bits. My worry is that students will flatten all three into 'nobody's fault, so nobody pays', and the interesting part — that liability can be fitting where blame isn't and still leave something missing — just dissapears.

So: what order does this have to go in, and why? Does the blame / liability / answer-for-it distinction have to land before the gap claim, or does it only make sence once they've already felt the gap in a case? Does the 'conditions on blameworthiness' material (control, foresight, whatever I settle on) come first as machinery or last as a fix on an intuition they already have?

The carrying case. I want one case for the whole 80 minutes, and I think it has to be a 'nobody had the lever' case: the harm was preventable, but only jointly — the operations team could have switched the behaviour off, but only using telemetry that sat with the vendor, and the vendor could have shipped a fix, but only if ops hadn't already frozen the configuration. Each could have prevented it had the other moved; neither could alone; they never spoke. So no individual omission looks like a but-for cause, yet the pair together were sufficient. Tell me what else the case needs to have, and more usefully what it needs to lack. Why not the usual self-driving-car-hits-a-pedestrian thing, and why not an insurance framing that tidies everything up by minute ten?

Misconceptions. My guesses: that the gap is a loophole and the interesting question is how to close it in law; that it depends on something spooky about the system being an agent or nearly an agent; that 'someone must be at fault' is a premise rather than the thing under discussion. What else, and at exactly which point in the sequence do I head each one off?

Give me one question to end on that tells me they've got the three-way distinction rather than just that autonomous systems are worrying, and tell me what to openly refuse to cover at this level.

Last thing: I don't want to hand second-years a contested position as orthodoxy. Flag which bits of what you're proposing rest on claims people who actually work on this would dispute — whether gap talk is genuinely new or just the old many-hands problem in a shiny jacket, whether it quietly presupposes retributivism, whether the right move is to revise the concept of responsibility or the practices around it — and which bits I can state flat out.
