I'm TAing a slightly disorganized undergrad seminar on Kripke, rigid designators, and the necessary a posteriori, and I thought it would be fun to make the students read a bit of Mill for historical background, but now I'm the one who's confused. We looked at the chapter on names in John Stuart Mill's *A System of Logic, Ratiocinative and Inductive* (the 1843 book), and the passage that's tripping me up is this one:

"A non-connotative term is one which signifies a subject only, or an attribute only. A connotative term is one which denotes a subject, and implies an attribute. By a subject is here meant anything which possesses attributes. Thus John, or London, or England, are names which signify a subject only. Whiteness, length, virtue, signify an attribute only. None of these names, therefore, are connotative. But white, long, virtuous, are connotative. The word white, denotes all white things, as snow, paper, the foam of the sea, &c., and implies, or in the language of the schoolmen connotes, the attribute whiteness. The word white is not predicated of the attribute, but of the subjects, snow, &c.; but when we predicate it of them, we convey the meaning that the attribute whiteness belongs to them. The same may be said of the other words above cited. Virtuous, for example, is the name of a class, which includes Socrates, Howard, the Man of Ross, and an undefinable number of individuals, past, present, and to come. These individuals, collectively and severally, can alone be said with propriety to be denoted by the word: of them alone can it properly be said to be a name. But it is a name applied to all of them in consequence of an attribute which they are supposed to possess in common, the attribute which has received the name of virtue. It is applied to all beings that are considered to possess this attribute; and to none which are not so considered."

I get the rough idea that general terms like 'white' or 'virtuous' are supposed to denote a bunch of subjects and also connote some attribute, while proper names like 'John' or 'London' supposedly just signify a subject and don't connote any attribute. But I keep arguing with my friend about what any of this has to do with Kripke. My friend proposed a translation manual we jokingly call "Mill's Rigid-Designator Test": we read Mill's phrase "signifies a subject only" as meaning "designates the same subject in every possible world where it exists, without describing that subject via some attribute," which would make Millian non-connotative names basically rigid designators. That sounds too smooth to me, because Mill is using the old connotation/denotation distinction and doesn't seem to be talking about modality at all, and I also don't know whether his "subject" is the same as an object or bearer in Kripke's sense, or maybe more like a substance or substratum that has attributes.

So can you help me understand a few things? First, in this passage, what exactly does Mill mean by a "subject" versus an "attribute," and why does he say that "John, or London, or England" signify a subject only rather than denoting a class the way 'virtuous' does? Second, does Mill's claim that proper names are non-connotative actually commit him to anything like the Kripkean view that names are rigid designators, or is a non-connotative term in Mill's sense something weaker (or just different) because his distinction is semantic/connotative rather than modal? Third, if we did import a Kripkean example—say the discovery that "Hesperus is Phosphorus" or that "Water is H2O" is a necessary a posteriori truth—how would Mill's framework in this passage even describe the reference of the names involved, given that he denies connotation but still seems to allow that we can use descriptions to pick out the subject? I don't need citations or further reading; I just want to get clearer on how to read this passage and whether my friend's "Mill's Rigid-Designator Test" heuristic is a helpful way to teach it or a misleading anachronism.
