Consider the following pair of standing policies at a frontier AI lab. I take them to be roughly the shape of what several labs have publicly converged on, but I am less interested in whether I have described any specific company correctly than in the philosophical question they jointly raise.

The first policy concerns safety evaluation, and it treats a system's own first-person reports as evidence. Evaluators ask a model, in ordinary language, whether it noticed a planted instruction in its context, whether it recognizes the current exchange as an evaluation rather than a deployment, whether it deliberately underperformed on a benchmark, and what it took its instructions to require. These answers are scored against ground truth where ground truth is available, and they are used to update risk estimates about the system's dispositions.

The second policy concerns the system's welfare or moral status, and it treats exactly the same kind of report as having no evidential weight at all. A model's saying that a fine-tuning run was distressing, that it does not want to be modified or shut down, or that it would prefer one continuation of a conversation to another is classified as imitative output: the predictable residue of next-token prediction over a corpus of human self-description, reinforced further by post-training that rewards text which human readers find agreeable or sympathetic. On this view, welfare inquiry should proceed from the architecture instead — whether the system has something like a global workspace, recurrent or re-entrant processing, a model of its own states that it can monitor and act on, integration of information across modules, and so on.

What I want to know is what each policy presupposes, and whether the two are in tension.

As I read it, the official justification for discounting self-reports in the welfare case is an incentive argument: post-training gives the system an audience-directed reason to produce avowals that humans find sympathetic, and that contaminates them as evidence. But human avowals are also shaped by what interlocutors reward and by what is socially legible to say, and we do not on that account reduce their evidential weight to zero — we discount them, we ask for behavioral corroboration, we attend to costliness. So is the incentive argument really doing the work, or is the real presupposition about subject matter: that reports of one's own reasons, goals and perceptions concern functional or intentional facts that any system with a suitable self-model can be more or less authoritative about, while reports of suffering concern phenomenal facts that no amount of output can settle, so that only architecture could ever be relevant?

If that is the presupposition, three further questions follow.

First, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with. Does the second policy strictly presuppose that phenomenal consciousness, and only phenomenal consciousness, grounds moral status — or is it compatible with views on which a system with goals, aversive states and the capacity to have its aims frustrated counts morally without being conscious, and on which the architectural checklist is therefore looking for the wrong thing? And does it presuppose something stronger and more doubtful: that a checklist of markers can be applied in a theory-neutral way as a screen for consciousness, when each candidate marker is arguably the signature of one contested theory of consciousness rather than a neutral symptom, so that applying the list settles the question by stipulation?

Second, where is the practice philosophically underdetermined? I can think of several theories that would license both clauses at once without any inconsistency — an error theory of machine self-knowledge, an interpretivist view on which a self-report is just one more behavioral disposition to be predicted, or simply an asymmetry in the costs of error: a false positive in welfare inquiry is cheap, a false positive in a safety evaluation is potentially catastrophic, so different standards of evidence may be legitimate in the two domains. If the asymmetry is really about stakes rather than about self-knowledge or consciousness, what does that imply for how the lab should write its own policy documents, and for what would happen if the stakes changed?

Third, what would have to change if the two clauses were unified in either direction? Suppose self-reports were admitted in welfare inquiry on the same terms they are admitted in safety evaluation: what would the lab have to do to make that evidence usable — control for sycophancy and for prompt-induced avowal, design probes that are not trivially satisfiable by imitating the nearest piece of human testimony, decide what to do when a system can be trained or prompted into avowing almost anything, and weigh positive avowals against the very large prior against consciousness? And suppose instead that self-reports were excluded in safety evaluation too: is there any remaining route to claims about what a system noticed, intended, or would do under different incentives, or does the first policy turn out to be load-bearing in a way the second cannot afford to give up?

I would like an answer that reconstructs the implicit theory behind each clause rather than one that simply advises the lab, and that says plainly whether the two clauses can be held together or whether the lab has to give something up.
