I am writing a novel whose set-piece is an argument between two biologists over one animal, and I need the argument to be one that neither character could be accused of losing stupidly. One of them reasons in the idiom of selected effects: a trait's function is the effect for which tokens of that trait were selected in the lineage, so that a trait which no longer produces that effect is malfunctioning, or functionless, even in an animal that is visibly thriving. The other reasons in the idiom of functional analysis: a trait's function is its contribution, under an analysis, to a capacity of a system containing it — and she will insist on the least gerrymandered analysis available, the animal's own surviving and reproducing, not a sub-capacity gerrymandered to hand her the verdict.

I need a case in which the two idioms come apart over the same trait in the same animal. Build me that case to the following specifications.

1. One trait, one animal (or one small population of conspecifics), terrestrial biology that is either real or realistically possible. No engineered organisms, no aliens, no speculative or futuristic biology, no laboratory strains. The scene paragraph should be writable as observation and dialogue on a hillside: no vocabulary a field biologist would not use aloud.
2. The two characters accept every empirical fact — the natural history, the lineage, the environment, the population genetics. The disagreement must be about what follows for function, not about what is true of the animal. If one of them has to be ignorant of something for the case to work, it is not the case I want.
3. Required verdicts. Selected effects: the trait's function is some effect A which the trait no longer produces — either it cannot anymore, or A's point has been cancelled by changes inside the organism — while the animal is healthy and depends on the trait for something else, B. Functional analysis: the trait's function is B, the trait is performing B flawlessly, and there is nothing wrong with the animal.
4. Cheats I do not want. Do not give me a trait with no selection history — no new mutation, no first-generation novelty, no artificial selection. Do not give me a vestige: B must be currently indispensable. Do not give me a changed environment — no streetlights, no introduced predator, no shifted climate; whatever displaced A must lie inside the organism's own organisation. And the selected-effects theorist must not be left the easy reply that the trait is now selected for B and so B is simply its function: block that reply (monomorphism at the relevant locus, recency of the reorganisation, whatever device you prefer) and say explicitly which device you used and why it closes that door. If one of these prohibitions makes the task impossible, tell me which and why, and build the strongest case you can with it relaxed.
5. Something must hang on it. I need a practical question on which the two verdicts genuinely diverge — whether the animal or the population counts as diseased or maladapted, whether to intervene, how it goes in the field log — and the divergence must follow from the accounts, not be stipulated.

Deliverables: the case itself in one paragraph of scene-ready prose, plus the natural history in field-note form; then a walk-through naming T, A and B, giving each account's function-ascription and its verdict about malfunction, and marking the exact step where the two part company; then the single feature doing the work, what happens to the case if that feature is removed (variation restored, environment allowed to be novel, trait made redundant), and how my selected-effects character would attack the case in her own words — she is clever, and I do not want her to lose on a technicality. No citations: I cannot check them and neither can my characters, so describe moves rather than attributing them. Finally, tell me plainly which verdict you think the case actually supports, name the one further fact about the animal that would make you switch, and flag any biology you invented that a field guide might contradict — I would rather know now than after the copy-edit.
