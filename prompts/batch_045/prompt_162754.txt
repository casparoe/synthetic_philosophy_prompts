I'm a first-year PhD student in machine learning, and my lab runs a weekly journal club where we read computational neuroscience papers even though almost none of us have formal training in that literature. Last week a cognitive science postdoc who visits occasionally heard a talk about next-token prediction and said something like "well, the brain is just a prediction-error minimizer," and then used that one line to make three claims at once: first, that a language model trained by minimizing cross-entropy loss is literally minimizing variational free energy, so the free energy principle can be applied to it without metaphor; second, that such a system should therefore be expected, at sufficient scale, to start acting on the world so as to make its inputs match what it predicts, rather than only adjusting itself to the inputs; and third, that the fluent confabulations these models produce are better described as unconstrained predictions than as errors, because on this view there is no unmediated input for them to get wrong. A philosopher friend of mine, who was sitting in, muttered afterwards that the slogan was doing at least four different jobs at once and then refused to elaborate, which I found both unhelpful and plausible.

So I would like a menu rather than a single gloss. Please separate the distinct precise theses that "the brain is a prediction-error minimizer" (and its cousin "perception is controlled hallucination," which came up in the same conversation) could be asserting, and sort them from the nearly trivial to the radical. I think the axes that matter are: what is supposed to be minimized (raw sensory prediction error, a bound on surprise such as variational free energy, expected surprise or entropy, or just log loss); over what timescale the minimization is supposed to happen (phylogeny, development, learning, or online inference within a single percept); in what sense of "minimize" (a literal optimization the system performs, versus a description of the state it settles into, versus a normative standard it does well by); how wide the scope is (early sensory cortex, all of cortex, perception only, or perception plus action plus the organism-environment coupling); and whether it is a mechanistic claim about what neural signals carry, a computational-level claim about what function is being computed, or merely an interpretive gloss on top of an ordinary Bayesian story. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible empirical commitment, or something close to vacuous or false, and note which reading people who repeat the slogan in that literature are most likely to mean.

The part I actually care about is the second half: for each of the three claims my postdoc made about language models, tell me which reading of the slogan would be needed to support it, whether that reading is the plausible one or only the deflationary one, and whether the claim survives once the strong reading is made precise. My suspicion, which I can't quite articulate, is that the interesting content of the predictive-processing story is exactly what a frozen sequence predictor lacks: the standard way of keeping the surprise-minimization story from collapsing into the dark-room objection is to distinguish changing your model to fit the world from changing the world to fit your model, and to build expected outcomes the organism prefers into the model itself, and a system that can't act on its inputs at all is stranded on the first half of that distinction. If that is right, then the move from "the brain minimizes prediction error" to "this network is a free-energy-minimizing agent" is only licensed under a reading that makes it a truism about log-loss training. Please also give me the single sharpest question I could put to him at the next journal club to force him to say which reading he is committed to.
