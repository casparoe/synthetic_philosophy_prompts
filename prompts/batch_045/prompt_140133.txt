Write a set of release notes, in the deadpan style of a software changelog, for a new edition of a psychiatric diagnostic manual, issued by its classification committee. About 700 words, with the usual sections — Added, Changed, Deprecated, Known Issues.

The premise: because publishing diagnostic criteria changes the people who are classified, which forces the criteria to be revised, the committee has stopped publishing them. Categories are now generated by a proprietary model; clinicians submit symptom narratives and receive back a classification plus a short patient-facing label; the criteria exist only inside the system. Call this the Fixed-Point Protocol: the edition describes not the patients who walk in but the patients who will exist once the previous edition has done its work.

Suggested entries to work with: the deprecation of patient-facing criteria documents and of the "semantic quarantine" period that preceded each release; a new telemetry field for "loop-drift", defined as the divergence between the prevalence a category was calibrated to and its prevalence two quarters after release, with automatic re-anchoring when it exceeds a threshold; guidance on "diagnostic arbitrage", where patients optimise their narratives toward categories carrying better accommodation, medication or benefits; and a Known Issues entry for a presentation the committee has been obliged to add — patients who cannot describe their own distress except by citing the manual's categories, and who require re-education whenever an edition ships. Give that presentation a name; something in the register of acquired nosological dependency.

The satire should be fair rather than a caricature. The instability the committee is trying to engineer away should be the real one: classification reaches people through clinics, insurers, schools, benefits forms and online communities; it alters how they experience themselves, present themselves and organise; and that in turn alters what the category is a category of. The committee's solution should be the recognisable ambition of making a human kind behave like a natural kind, pushed one step further — not a straw man. The reductio should land without being stated: a reader should finish convinced that hiding the criteria makes the entanglement worse rather than better, and be able to say why.

Nobody in the piece may break character or admit doubt; the argument may show only in what the entries report. Do not name Hacking and do not use the phrase "looping effect" anywhere in the text. British English throughout.
