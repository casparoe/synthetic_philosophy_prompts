Two people I climb with — Samir, who does risk modelling for an insurance company, and Bea, who teaches medieval history — got into it at my birthday dinner on Saturday and were still going at it over dessert, and I honestly coudn't tell who was winning. The topic was whether the world is getting more or less violent, and I've tried to reproduce their actual moves below (paraphrased, but I think I've got the structure right). (1) Samir said the only sensible headline number is violent deaths per 100,000 people per year: absolute body counts mislead because there are many times more humans around now than a few centuries ago, so a far safer world can still produce more corpses — he drew a little graph on a napkin at this point. (2) Bea said the cheating is all in the numerator: he's counting street homicides and battlefield deaths but not the things that did most of the damage in her period, and not the modern equivalents either — people dying in state custody, deaths from famine or disease that some policy caused, airstrikes filed under a different heading — and the labels are basically gerrymandered, since the same killing gets recorded as war, punishment, policing or misadventure depending on who fills out the form. (3) Samir said none of that helps her, because the raw per-capita rate is if anything too kind to the past: in a world where plague and famine kill people young, a lot of potential murder victims die of something else first, so the homicide rate mechanically understates how dangerous things were; he said this is just competing risks in actuarial work, and correcting for it makes the past look worse, not better. (4) Bea said that correction looks neutral but isn't — he's quietly switched from counting how many people violence killed to counting how much life it destroyed, and once you're in that currency a murder in a society where people died at forty anyway costs a lot less than the same murder now, so the past's violence gets cheaper exactly where he wants it to look worse; she added that on this logic a place with terrible sanitation gets a discount on all its murders. (5) Samir came back with density: a medieval villager met a few hundred people in his whole life and a modern commuter passes thousands a week, so if the question is "how violent is social life," risk per encounter has fallen much further than risk per year; and he conceded that if you weight regions equally instead of weighting people, the drop gets smaller — which in his view just shows these are two different questions. (6) Bea said that is her entire point: how many people, how much life, per encounter, per place, can all move in different directions and he keeps picking whichever one says "decline," plus he gets to pick the start date, and she doubts he'd sound so sure if you'd set the clock somewhere else or run it out to today in the places actually at war right now. They both turned to me and I had nothing.

Can you adjudicate this move by move — who is right about what at each step, not just who wins? I'd love each of the six moves rated from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a short reason, then your sense of who's more right overall. And two things I couldn't settle myself: is there even a fact of the matter here, or is "is the world becoming less violent" the kind of question that only gets a truth value once you've fixed a measure? And what, if anything, are both of them missing?
