I'm a professor of philosophy with a graduate seminar on rational choice, and — unusually for my department — my first degree was in computer science: I spent three years in a reinforcement learning group before I switched fields, I still read enough of the literature to follow standard POMDP and policy-gradient notation, and I have implemented REINFORCE-style estimators by hand, though I was never a POMDP theorist. Next month I'm giving a guest lecture in the CS department to a room of RL people who have never opened a decision-theory paper, and I want to spend the hour on the absent-minded driver rather than on something more predictable. So I need the case and the disagreement about it restated in their vocabulary, not mine.

The case: a driver sets out on a highway with two indistinguishable intersections; taking the first exit is worth 1, taking the second is worth 4, driving past both is worth 0; he cannot tell the intersections apart, does not remember whether he has already passed one, and knows all of this in advance. The ex-ante planning problem is simple enough that I can do it myself — maximizing expected payoff over a strategy that exits with probability p gives me p = 5/8 and an expected payoff of 25/16, and the self-locating credence, on reaching an intersection, that this is the first one comes out under a per-visit weighting as 1/(2−p) = 8/11 — but tell me if any of that is wrong, because I want the arithmetic right before it goes on a slide. The interesting part is what happens next: the literature splits between planning-optimality, action-optimality at the intersection given a self-locating credence, and the family of multi-self consistency conditions proposed by Piccione and Rubinstein in their paper On the Interpretation of Decision Problems with Imperfect Recall and developed by later writers, and it disagrees about whether these criteria come apart here and about what one should say when they do. I would like that whole structure restated in RL terms: a memoryless reactive policy over observations; the two intersections as a single observation with a belief update that cannot accumulate; occupancy measures; the difference between evaluating a policy on episodic return and evaluating a single decision on-policy versus off-policy; credit assignment across two visits to the same policy invocation; the Bellman recursion degenerating from a backward sweep into a fixed point; and execution or exploration noise folded into the transition kernel.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually want. My colleague in the CS department says that in their formalism the puzzle cannot arise at all: a policy is a map from observations to actions, so identically looking intersections receive identical treatment by construction, the expected-return objective simply is the planning criterion, and the philosophical debate is therefore an artifact of an underspecified framework. One of my graduate students says the opposite — that the formalism has quietly taken a stand on precisely the contested point, and that what it cannot express at all is that the driver's uncertainty is indexical: there is no difference in world-state between his being at the first intersection and his being at the second, only a difference in where in the world he is, so no uncentered distribution over states can represent it. I want you to adjudicate this as a translation report: which parts of the problem map cleanly onto RL machinery, which survive only with distortion (I suspect the smuggling happens in the belief-state update, since a sufficient statistic for the observation history is exactly what imperfect recall denies you), and which have no counterpart there. Three stress tests I would like run through it. First, suppose each intersection carries a noisy visual signal of which one it is, and the driver can also pay a small cost in attention to sharpen it: what does the framework say about the value of that signal, and does it matter that the signal is evidence about a centered rather than an uncentered proposition? Second, suppose the wheel trembles — with small probability the car does the contrary of what he intends, and he cannot tell whether his intention was executed — which in RL would normally be absorbed into the transition kernel, except that here the agent may not know his own action. Third, suppose a dashcam records the trip and the return is fully observable to the engineer afterwards but not to the driver during it: does the availability of ex-post evaluation settle which optimality criterion is the right one, or does it merely change the question? And one last thing, for the lecture itself: if you trained a memoryless policy by policy gradient on episodic return in this environment, would the halfer-versus-thirder disagreement about the self-locating credence reappear as a choice about how the objective is normalized, per episode or per decision — and could two systems built to the same specification differ in realized return in a way that corresponds to the planning-versus-action distinction, or is that distinction invisible from outside the agent? Where the translation fails, tell me whether the failure indicts the RL vocabulary or the philosophical debate.
