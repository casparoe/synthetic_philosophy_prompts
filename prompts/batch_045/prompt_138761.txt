I'm in the second year of a joint mathematics and philosophy degree, and my housemate — who seems to spend every evening at a reading group on global priorities — has been defending expected value fanaticism: roughly, the view that for any non-zero chance *p* of a good, however tiny *p* is, there is some size of good such that a *p* chance of it beats any finite good you could secure for sure. I conceded that much after a fortnight of arguing, but last night she produced an extension of it that I am fairly sure must be broken, because it concludes that no action is ever permissible, and I cannot find where it breaks. Set it up as follows. Suppose a perfectly reliable bookmaker — call him the bookmaker of the long now — will sell you, for one unit of value, a ticket that pays *G* units of value if the hypothesis *H* comes true, where you may name any finite *G* you like and any hypothesis *H* with any probability *p* you like, provided only that *p* is a real number strictly between zero and one and that nothing else is affected either way. Pay-offs are measured directly in units of value or welfare rather than in money, so that nobody can wriggle out by appealing to diminishing marginal utility, and *G* must be finite, so that no infinite expectations are smuggled in. Then:

(1) *No ceiling.* For any *p* with 0 < *p* < 1 and any finite *v*, there is a finite *G* with *pG* > *v*. Stakes are unbounded: no finite pay-off is so large that a small probability cannot be compensated for by going larger still.
(2) *No best ticket.* By (1), for every ticket the bookmaker will write, there is another available ticket with strictly greater expected value — simply name a larger *G*. And yet every individual ticket has an ordinary finite expected value; it is only the set of them that runs away.
(3) *Pairwise requirement.* If *X* and *Y* are both available and EV(*X*) > EV(*Y*), then rationality forbids choosing *Y*: you must not knowingly take the worse of two options when the better one is on the menu.
(4) *Aggregation.* Take any available option *Y* whatsoever. By (2) there is an available option *X* with EV(*X*) > EV(*Y*), so by (3) you must not choose *Y*. Since *Y* was arbitrary, rationality forbids every option on the menu.
(5) But doing nothing, or deliberating for another hour, or bursting into tears, is also an option, and by the same reasoning also forbidden. So a theory that issues requirements like (3) ends up permitting nothing at all, which cannot be right.

What bothers me is that I cannot locate the mistake. (1) looks like arithmetic. (2) follows from (1) given the bookmaker, and the bookmaker is only a device for making the option set open-ended, which she says is realistic anyway: for any pound you were about to spend on something ordinary there is always some long shot you could stake it on instead, so real menus have no top either. And (3) is the only part of expected value theory that actually bites; without it the view tells you nothing at all. I have tried saying that the expectations must really be undefined once the tails get heavy enough, but that seems blocked by stipulation here, since every ticket has a finite expected value and the background is held fixed. Someone at her reading group suggested that the answer is simply that value is bounded above, so that (1) fails for the notion of value that matters; that strikes me as a very large empirical claim to lean on, and it would make the fanatic's view hostage to cosmology. Another suggestion was that the argument only shows the decision problem to be defective — that there is no satisfactory option — rather than that each option is forbidden, but (3) looks like a prohibition on actions, not on problems, and I do not see how it stops applying.

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look sound? If I have to choose, I would rather keep (1) and (3) than (2)'s claim that the menu is genuinely open-ended, but I want to know what that costs me — and in particular whether the price is that expected value goes silent in exactly the cases the fanatic wants it to pronounce on, since those are precisely the cases in which there is no top to the menu. If it helps, say explicitly what each of the available escapes commits me to, and whether any of them leaves me with a decision theory that still tells me to do what my housemate wanted me to do in the first place.
