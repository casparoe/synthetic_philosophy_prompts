I've drifted into something like Integrated Information Theory and I want to know what should knock me off it. Not the whole package — I don't care much about the exact formula or which version number — but the core commitment: that whether a system is conscious, and what its experience is like, is fixed entirely by the causal structure it has from its own intrinsic point of view, what it can do to its own future states, and not by what it does for anybody watching or what it computes for a user. Two things pushed me here. One is the old substitution thought experiment: replace bit by bit the bits of a brain doing the causal work with functionally identical parts that are causally thinner, keep behavior and speech exactly the same, and I have a strong pull to say something has been lost or dimmed, and I don't see how a functionalist gets to say that without cheating. The other is the usual example people reach for, the cerebellum: a huge amount of neural hardware, more neurons than cortex, arranged in a way that looks modular and close to feedforward, and it seems to be much less relevant to whether someone is having an experience than the cortex is. That felt to me like a real prediction and not just a story told after the fact.

So give me the map of exposure. Concretely, here's what I want you to work through:

There's an objection I've half-heard and can't reconstruct properly, something like: any system's causal structure can be unfolded into one big function from inputs-plus-internal-states to outputs, so any two systems that realize the same such function must come out identical on any measure that only looks at causal structure, which means the theory either collapses back into a strange kind of functionalism after all or it was never really about causal structure. I don't know if I have it right. If that's roughly the argument, spell it out properly, tell me whether it goes through, and tell me how much it costs me — this is the one I most want nailed down, because if it works I think I lose my main reason for holding the view at all.

Then the case I've started calling the lunchbox. A sealed box of a few million cheap units wired in a dense recurrent mesh, no inputs, no outputs, no learning, no plasticity, just sitting in a drawer doing whatever such a mesh does. Somebody works out the quantity and it comes out comfortably higher than a human brain's. What exactly am I committed to? As I understand it I have to say there is more consciousness in that drawer than in me, and maybe not a crowd of experiences but one very intense undifferentiated one, and that this follows from the part of the theory that says only the maximally irreducible structure counts and the rest gets excluded. Is the commitment really that strong, does it change if the box is also doing something useful, and is there any non-ad-hoc way to say that the lunchbox's structure isn't the right kind of structure without smuggling behavior or function back in through the side door?

Then the empirical stuff. Say in a few decades we can actually do the substitution in the first thought experiment: a gradual, unit-by-unit replacement that preserves every input-output relation and every report while the recurrent causal structure decays to something nearly feedforward. And the subjects, at every stage, insist with the same fluency and the same detail that nothing about their inner lives has changed. What should I conclude? Is it legitimate for me to say they would say that either way, or is that the exact moment the view stops having any skin in the game? Same question for the reverse direction, and for the messier clinical shape: a region we'd score as highly integrated turns out to be dispensable for reported experience, or one we'd write off as a modular relay turns out to be necessary. Describe the shape of the finding rather than the study, I won't be able to look anything up.

And then the general worry underneath all of this: for any real biological system we can only approximate the quantity, so it looks like any mismatch gets blamed on the approximation and any match gets credited to the theory, and there is no result that would make a committed defender hand in their badge. If that's true, is it fatal for a theory of this kind, or is it a fine place to land — this is first a claim about what experience metaphysically consists in, and we go looking for measurable proxies afterwards, and a mismatch with a proxy is only ever evidence about the proxy? I feel like there's a comparison to how people treat claims in the foundations of mathematics here but it's not quite right, give me a better one if there is one.

Rank everything from I should give up to I should feel slightly silly at a dinner party, and for each item say whether a defender would concede it counts against them or would wave it off as a confusion. Include the defeaters that have arguably already arrived, in the clinical or imaging or neuroanatomy literature, and be straight with me if the honest answer is that the core claim is very well insulated and that this is fine. If it helps: the thing that would move me most personally is not the lunchbox, I can learn to live with the lunchbox, it's being shown that the substitution case gives me no grip at all, because then I don't know what I ever meant.
