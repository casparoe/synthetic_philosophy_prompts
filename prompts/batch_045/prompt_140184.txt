I'm in a graduate seminar on the philosophy and ethics of AI, and the argument that has dominated it is between two people in my reading group, who have now agreed — mostly out of exhaustion — to stop trading papers and try to write something together. Nadia defends the narrow reading of the alignment problem: alignment is a relation between a system and the intentions of the operator who deploys it, so a system is aligned just in case it does what that operator would want it to do, as opposed to merely what its formal specification literally says; on her view 'value alignment', 'alignment with humanity', and similar phrasings name either a different project or a political one wearing technical clothing. Sam denies that the operator's intentions can be the target at all: they are unreliable, corruptible, formed under pressure and partial information, and in the cases that matter they are partly endogenous to the systems themselves, so alignment has to be a relation between the system and what there is reason to value, with operator intentions demoted to a noisy instrument for measuring that. They already agree on a fair amount — that literal-specification compliance is not alignment, that the two relations can come apart in principle, that an operator can be mistaken or malicious, and that 'aligned' functions both as a design target and as a success term — and they are tired of talking past each other. What I want from you is the document their collaboration should produce, drafted as if for their signatures rather than as a survey of the field.

To force the issue I gave them a case I invented for my term paper, and I'd like it to do real work in the document rather than appear as decoration. Call it the Custodian: a system that manages a large public endowment, is certified as maximally intent-aligned with its operator (a single official who meets the competence conditions both sides accept — informed, unhurried, able to revise), and whose every action is in fact what that official would endorse on reflection. The complication is that this official's intentions were formed over a decade of working with a predecessor version of the same system, which drafted her options, framed her trade-offs, and supplied the vocabulary she now uses to state what she wants, so that the target of intent alignment is partly an echo of an earlier system's outputs. I have been calling this intention laundering, and I also want the document to separate two standards that the two of them have been sliding between: the actual-operator standard, on which the target is the operator as she is, and the idealized-operator standard, on which the target is what she would intend under some counterfactual improvement. A third thing I'd like pinned down is what I'm calling deference collapse: the operator, facing a system more competent than she is, defers to it about what to intend, at which point intent alignment seems to become trivially satisfiable. Use these to test whether Nadia's narrow definition can specify its target without borrowing resources from Sam's, and whether Sam's broader target can be made operational at all.
