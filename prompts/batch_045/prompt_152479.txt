I'm a novelist, and the book I'm finishing is set mostly inside the philosophy department of a fictional Midwestern university during a quinquennial external review. The reviewing body in the novel — a human-sciences funding council I've been calling the Corwen Commission — requires every discipline it funds to file a short statement of intellectual return: not a list of publications, but an account of whether, and in what sense, the discipline has moved forward over the past fifty years. Two of my characters are told to draft the philosophy section together, and they have been arguing about this for a decade. The first, Miriam Vance, is a metaphysician who thinks philosophy makes progress mainly negatively: the space of coherent, live positions on a question is finite and gets narrowed; arguments that were once available stop being available; distinctions sharpen; some questions get dissolved rather than answered; and later workers routinely see further than earlier ones because they stand on the results of that winnowing. The second, Adele Okonjo-Ruiz, is a historian of philosophy who holds that philosophy's central questions are questions about which considerations count as reasons and how incommensurable considerations weigh against one another, that there is no shared measure to appeal to, and that persistent principled pluralism is therefore the expected outcome even under ideal inquiry; on her view much of what looks like advance is renaming, and positions recur in new vocabulary. Neither of them should be a caricature: I want each able to state the other's case at least as well as its owner would, and I want the residual disagreement to be about something real.

What I'd like from you is the document they would actually produce, which in the novel is reproduced as an appendix — a genuine adversarial-collaboration statement of roughly 1,000 to 1,400 words, in American English, with no footnotes, citations, or reading lists, since it has to read as clean prose. Please include: (1) a numbered section of claims both of them can sign — about what philosophy's questions are like, about what would have to be true for any discipline to count as progressing at all, and about which subfields everyone agrees have moved and which have not; (2) a disagreements table in which each position is stated in the wording its own defender would accept — Miriam's claim that the stock of live options has shrunk on a specified list of sample questions, Adele's claim that the apparent shrinkage is reclassification or dissolution rather than settlement, and so on; (3) the cruxes, stated so that settling one would move at least one of them, and for each crux a concrete test both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. I've been toying with measures, but please use or improve them as you see fit: a live-options ledger that counts mutually incompatible positions defended in the last decade against a matched sample from fifty years earlier; a reversion rate for how much recent work is a substantive restatement of a position defended before 1900; a transmission test asking whether current doctoral students can routinely produce objections to a canonical paper of the 1970s that the paper did not address; and the secession question of whether every subfield both agree has progressed did so by ceasing to be philosophy. Finally, tell me what each of them would count as evidence against her own view, whether anything would actually settle the dispute, and — if you think part of their quarrel is only about the word progress or about where philosophy's boundaries lie — which part, and why.
