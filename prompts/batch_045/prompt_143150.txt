I retired a few years back after thirty-something years modelling groundwater contamination, and I've started going along to a philosophy reading group at the local library, which is how I ended up here. This term the topic is "what is intelligence", and I'm enjoying it but floundering: I can read a correlation matrix fine, it's the conceptual moves I don't trust myself on.

Before last week's session one of the group circulated a two-page summary of a well-known popular book on intelligence. I've only read his summary, not the book, so I don't want to go blaming the author — and I can't tell which bits are the author's and which are his anyway. Here it is, more or less:

"Give people a big battery of different mental tests — vocab, mental rotation, arithmetic, memory span — and every one of them correlates positively with every other. That positive manifold is the most robust finding in the whole of psychology. Factor analysis then extracts a single general factor, g, which accounts for the bulk of that shared variance, and g is what people really mean by general intelligence; indeed it's the same quantity the AI people have in mind when they talk about artificial general intelligence, only measured in humans rather than machines. It is also strongly heritable — somewhere between half and three quarters of the variation in the population is genetic — so it is what you are born with rather than something you build. And it matters more than anything else we can measure about a mind: it predicts school results, job performance and even how long you live better than any other single psychological measure. Rising raw scores over the twentieth century don't spoil any of this: those gains sit almost entirely on the least g-loaded subtests, so they're an artifact of test-familiarity and say nothing about intelligence."

My suspicion is the AGI sentence is where he's gone wrong, but I definately can't locate what's bothering me about the heritable bit either — my instinct as someone who used to spend his days doing variance decomposition says it can't mean what he takes it to mean, but I can't say why in words the group would accept. I also have a hobbyhorse: thirty years of watching graduate students makes me think the summary is running together two different resources — how many things you can hold in mind at once, and how far ahead you can carry a chain of consequences. I call them bandwidth and reach, and I suspect they'd come apart as seperate factors in any population that varied in both, and that g is mostly reach. Relatedly, I keep thinking that whatever g is, we've measured it in exactly one species on one planet, which feels a bit like calibrating "athleticism" using only swimmers.

So: audit that paragraph for me. Give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, each taking a single claim from the summary and saying plainly whether it's fair, a defensible simplification, contested, or just doesn't follow, plus one clause of why. Pick the five claims that actually carry the weight — mine would be the AGI line, the heritable line and that parenthetical about rising scores, but don't feel bound by my list. If your five don't end up covering my bandwidth/reach idea, tack a one-line verdict onto whichever item it belongs with, and same for the one-species point. No introduction and no summing-up at the end please, I'll be reading it out loud.
