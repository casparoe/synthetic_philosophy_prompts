I've been teaching a graduate seminar on social normativity for several years now, and I find that I've drifted into holding a position about conventions that I would rather have stress-tested than defended. Roughly: I hold that what makes a regularity a convention is its structure as an equilibrium solution to a recurring coordination problem, together with the availability of alternatives that would have served the parties at least as well — and that nothing further is needed. In particular I deny that conventions require a distinctively normative psychology (no sense of obligation, no guilt, no disposition to punish nonconformers); I deny that they require common knowledge, or even true mutual expectations, among the participants; and I deny that the participants must be able to represent the situation as a coordination problem at all. What sustains a convention, on my view, is whatever mechanism in fact produces stable conformity to one equilibrium among several — precedent, salience, individual habit, the built environment, a correlated signal, a population-level dynamic — and the explanatory work is done by the equilibrium-plus-arbitrariness structure rather than by the mechanism. I have taken to calling the thesis that the sustaining mechanism is irrelevant to conventionality so long as it is self-reinforcing the mechanism-indifference claim, and it is the part of my view I am least confident about.

What I want from you is a map of the view's exposure: the observations, arguments, possible cases and formal results that should move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and, for each, an assessment of whether a committed holder of the view — not a critic — should concede it as counting against the view or may reasonably dismiss it. I have some candidate defeaters in mind and want to know how much weight they really bear. (i) The affordance-locked regularity: two exits from a lecture hall, one door marginally lighter to open and positioned so that anyone entering sees only those who have used it; over a term nearly everyone uses that door; each person's choice is fully explained by their own earlier choices plus the door's weight; no one has any belief about what anyone else does or will do; and the regularity is stable, self-reinforcing, and arbitrary in that the other door would have served everyone just as well. If my view counts this as a convention it looks too permissive, and I seem to have made the shape of a corridor conventional; if it does not, then mechanism-indifference is false and I owe a non-circular account of which sustaining mechanisms qualify. (ii) The proleptic-convention problem: every convention has a first instance at which no regularity yet exists, so if conventionality requires actual past conformity nothing can ever become a convention; my usual move is to count a merely anticipated regularity, and I want to know whether that move costs me the arbitrariness condition or quietly turns the view back into a claim about expectations. (iii) Contested equilibria, where the parties' preferences over the available equilibria genuinely conflict, so that the selection is a matter of indifference to no one. (iv) Vestigial norms, where the original coordination problem has lapsed entirely while the regularity persists, with full normative phenomenology and no loss of stability.

Two further things. First, I want to know where the view risks anything: what does it predict that its rivals — a normative-expectations account, a plain equilibrium account of the rational-choice sort, an internalization or normative-attitude account — do not? I have in mind predictions about why conventions outlive their rationales, about the timing of convention change (threshold and tipping effects rather than gradual drift), about what we should expect to find in creatures or simple artificial agents with minimal theory of mind, and about when third-party enforcement should be unnecessary. I also want to know what the view stands or falls with elsewhere: whether it depends on a non-circular account of salience, on the explanatory credentials of equilibrium in social science generally, or on claims about how much mutual knowledge is achievable in large populations. Second, I want an honest verdict on whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability. I notice myself reaching, when pressed with an awkward case, for the thought that any stable regularity of mutual adjustment solves the coordination problem of adjusting to one another, and that a coordination problem is therefore always available. If that move has made the view unable to lose, say so plainly, and tell me whether that is a genuine defect for a view of this kind or simply what a structural account should be expected to look like. On the personal side: I am moved far more by a case in which the structure is present and the normativity absent, or the reverse, than by arguments from the phenomenology of obligation, which I think I can explain away cheaply; and I would count a demonstration that the arbitrariness condition does no work beyond what coordination equilibrium already delivers — that is, that my view collapses into the deflationary rival — as the most serious objection on the table, ahead of any empirical difficulty. Please argue directly, with cases and arguments rather than a reading list; I have no need for citations or suggestions for further reading.
