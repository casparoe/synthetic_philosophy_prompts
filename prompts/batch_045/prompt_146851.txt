In a graduate seminar on nineteenth-century British political thought, a visiting speaker used a much-quoted line from J. S. Mill's On Liberty to shut down a worry about Mill's views on India and colonial rule. The worry was that Mill's principles, or at least his official role in the Indian administration, commit him to defending despotic colonial rule; the speaker replied by reciting: 'Despotism is a legitimate mode of government in dealing with barbarians, provided the end be their improvement, and the means justified by actually effecting that end.' He then changed the subject, as if the sentence by itself settles whether Mill's views on India are an embarrassment to his liberalism, or merely an application of it.

I have heard the same line used both to convict Mill of hypocrisy and to excuse him, so I am less interested in a verdict on Mill than in a disambiguation of the sentence. The precisification that matters is the one the speaker needed: he used the line to end a conversation about consistency, by implying either that the principles of On Liberty simply do not apply to colonies, or that Mill's despotic remarks are a narrow exception, or that the only live question is the empirical one of whether British rule actually improved India.

I would like the distinct precise claims this one-liner could be making, laid out as a menu rather than as a single interpretation. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or false, and whether it would actually support the dismissive use the speaker made of it. Please disambiguate at least the following axes, and add any others required to keep the readings genuinely distinct:

1. The extension of "barbarians." Does it denote (a) any society below a threshold of capacity for improvement by free and equal discussion; (b) all non-European or colonized societies; (c) only societies in a putatively "nonage" stage on a stage-based theory of civilization; or (d) a term whose application to a complex, literate, legally organized society such as India is itself a substantive and contestable claim? Which of these makes the sentence a near-tautology about the scope of the liberty principle, and which turns it into a substantive claim about actual empires?

2. The force of "legitimate." Is this a claim about (a) all-things-considered moral permissibility; (b) permissibility within Mill's own harm principle or utilitarianism; (c) political legitimacy or a right to rule as recognized by international law; or (d) merely the best available option when there is "seldom any choice of means"? Identify which reading is nearly trivial and which would be needed if the sentence is to do real work in a debate about colonial rule.

3. The content of "their improvement." Does "improvement" mean (a) the development of the capacity for eventual self-government through free and equal discussion; (b) material or economic advancement; (c) moral, religious, or "civilizational" perfection along contested nineteenth-century lines; or (d) some improvement of the rulers or of civilization at large rather than of the barbarians themselves? For each, note how demanding the condition is and whether it is one a defender of British rule in India would want to affirm.

4. The condition "the means justified by actually effecting that end." Does this impose (a) a strict actual-consequence condition, so that a despotism that fails to improve its subjects was never legitimate; (b) a reasonable-expectation or evidence-relative condition at the time of acting; (c) a necessity condition, requiring that no less despotic means would have achieved the end; (d) a motive or intention condition, requiring that improvement be the ruler's actual aim; or (e) a causal condition, requiring that the improvement be caused by the despotism and not merely follow it? Which of these is closest to the surface syntax, which makes the sentence radical or nearly self-refuting, and which is the weakest, most speaker-friendly reading?

5. The relation of the sentence to India specifically. State which further premises would have to be conjoined to get from the sentence to any conclusion about British rule in India—for example, that India was "barbarian" in the relevant sense, that British rule was despotic, and that it actually improved the population—and say which of those premises the speaker was most likely smuggling in.

In your answer, lay the resulting readings out as an explicit menu: for each, give the precise thesis, mark it trivial / substantive / false, and say whether it would vindicate, excuse, or fail to support the speaker's attempt to end the argument. Do not give me a bibliography or a reading list, and do not rest any verdict on disputed biographical facts about Mill's official career in the Indian administration; treat the sentence as a piece of language to be precisified, and tell me which precisification the speaker would have needed for his dismissive move to work.
