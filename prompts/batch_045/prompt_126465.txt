Hi — I'm a journalist, I cover science and ideas, and I've been roped into running a hands-on session at a media-innovation retreat next month. About 16 people, mostly editors and reporters who write about health and tech, 90 minutes, a projector and some tables, and roughly zero tolerance among this crowd for anything that smells like a lecture. They've all written the story where a risk-scoring algorithm turns out to be "biased" and they've all had the experience of someone in the comments pointing out that a different fairness fix would have made things worse. I want them to actually feel why that keeps happening.

The topic is the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness — the thing where a risk score can't simultaneously be well calibrated, have the same false-positive rate across groups, and have the same false-negative rate across groups, once the groups have different base rates. I don't want citations, reading lists, or who-proved-what-when; I want a runnable activity.

Here's the shape I have in mind, but please design it properly: small teams act as a committee choosing a threshold for a hospital risk score that decides who gets enrolled in a scarce follow-up care program. Two patient populations (keep them deliberately bland — "city hospital" and "clinic" — I don't want the session to turn into a fight about race in the first ten minutes, though I do want the debrief to handle why the blandness is itself a problem). Give them a packet of aggregate tables, not the underlying people, and have them pick a cutoff. Award points for events caught minus the cost of false alarms, and only after they've committed do you run the audit that shows which of the three properties their threshold has and which it violates.

Then — and this is the part I care most about — the second half should let them discover the escape hatch and then discover what the escape hatch costs. If they use different thresholds for the two groups the error rates match, but now two patients with identical scores get different treatment, and the group label is being used explicitly. I'd like the group to arrive at that trade themselves, with real small stakes, before anyone names it.

One wrinkle I'd like you to build in or improve on: a round where the teams no longer see true outcomes for everyone, only for the patients they flagged, so the data they'd use to re-audit the tool next year is itself a product of this year's decision. (And if the program works, flagging someone changes the outcome.) If you think there's a better third beat — something about overlapping subgroups, or about eight constituencies each of whom can be satisfied individually but not all at once — do that instead, but tell me why.

What I need from you: the actual rules and the actual numbers (I want the hidden data set spelled out well enough that I can print the packets), a materials list that fits in a carry-on, a minute-by-minute timeline, a payoff scheme that doesn't involve money, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific parts of the idea. Also: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — my guess is that someone does the algebra out loud, someone tries to opt out by saying just don't use algorithms, and at least one team invents a fairness criterion I haven't thought of — and how I should handle each. Finally, be honest about what this kind of game can't deliver: my sense is that it can show the trilemma is real but can't show that it's necessary rather than a artifact of my made-up numbers, and it can't say anything about where the base-rate differences come from. Tell me what I'll have to say out loud, and keep it to something I can say in five minutes without putting a formula on the screen.
