I keep arguing with a friend about that German-versus-American folk-morality contrast — the one where German moral culture is supposed to run on rules, order, doing your part inside a system that is supposed to work, while the American one runs on individual conscience, the lone person who stands up and says no. We can each make the other's side look silly, and neither of us can say which one has the better of it. So I want to stop doing it in the abstract and test both on a case: the night before the Challenger launch, January 1986.

Don't retell me the surface story — I know it, O-rings, the cold, Feynman's glass of ice water, the go-fever stuff. What I want is the mapping. The facts as I'm working with them: Thiokol's engineers recommended no launch below 53°F, the previous coldest launch temperature; NASA pushed back hard; Thiokol's managers went into a half-hour caucus and reversed their own engineers; the concerns never got past the Level III flight readiness review; the sign-offs were collected and it flew.

Here's what I actually want worked through:

- On the conscience picture, who is the moral agent in that room and what was the act available to them? Be specific about the timing: the engineers had already spoken, and been overruled. So what does "follow your conscience" even mean once you've lost the internal argument — go around your boss, go outside, refuse to sign, resign on the spot? Which of those does the view actually commend, and does it have any doctrine of authority, i.e. anything to say about *who* you owe the warning to and when you may stop?

- On the rule-and-order picture there's an obvious problem: everybody signed. The checklists got checked. So give me the distinction the view needs — I'd call it compliance versus fidelity, but you name it better — and then tell me whether a rule-following morality can say anything useful about a case where the formal procedure was satisfied and the thing the procedure existed to secure was absent.

- The bit I cant get past: nobody was silent. The conscience story wants a moment where someone fails to speak, and what happened instead was a room full of people who all spoke and then made a procedural move — take it offline, caucus, come back with a different answer. Does either morality have anything to say about the procedural move itself, or is that the blind spot they share?

- Waivers. Assume with me, even if I have the details slightly off, that the field joints were classified as criticality-1 with no backup seal and were being flown for years on waivers after known erosion. A morality of rule-following with no doctrine of who may waive a rule, on what evidence, and for how long, isn't finished — it's just a rule plus a hole. What would an actual Ordnung-shaped morality have to add here: a theory of waiver, or a theory of who the rule is really addressed to?

- And my sense (maybe wrong) is that the deepest move that night was the inverted one: it became the engineers' job to prove a launch would be unsafe rather than the program's job to prove it was safe. Relatedly, Feynman's gripe about "safety factor" — management using a word that means extra margin to describe a part that had already failed. That isn't failing to follow a rule, it's the vocabulary the rules are written in going bad. Does either folk morality have a story about maintaining the meaning of the concepts, or is that a third thing both are missing?

- Last one: the Rogers Commission's fix was mostly institutional — new safety office, new reporting lines, redesigned joints. The American episode gets remembered as a conscience parable and repaired in the shape of rules. What does that asymmetry show? That the conscience story is the myth and the order story is what we actually trust, or the reverse, or something less tidy?

Run it both directions: what each of the two moralities says the people in that room faced and should have done, where each one sees something the other is blind to, where each one actively misdescribes what happened, and whether the case is evidence for one of them, against one of them, or a case neither was built for. Where a view is silent, say it's silent instead of inventing a position for it — that's the part I'll be reading for. And if you think the whole contrast is the wrong frame for this, say so and say what's wrong with it. No padding.
