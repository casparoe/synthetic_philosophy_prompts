I'm in a 300-level seminar on Kant's ethics — we did the Doctrine of Virtue last month and now we're on two weeks of animals — and I built this argument for my term paper and I can't tell if it's a knockdown or if I cheated somewhere in the middle. The conclusion is way stronger than anything I actually believe and that's what's bugging me.

The setup: I'm attacking the indirect-duty reading. On that reading, we have no direct duty to animals because they aren't persons and so aren't ends in themselves; every duty "regarding" animals is really a duty to humanity. The story for why cruelty to animals shows up in a doctrine of virtue at all is that violent and cruel treatment of animals is opposed to a duty the human being has to himself, because it dulls his shared feeling of their suffering and gradually uproots a natural predisposition that's very serviceable to morality in his relations with other people. I'd rather keep that reading than give it up — my whole paper is built on it — but I also can't swallow where my own argument ends up, and a friend who read it says it just proves too much.

Here it is, numbered so you can hit a specific step:

(1) On the indirect-duty reading, duties regarding animals are duties to humanity: the animal itself is never the object of the duty.

(2) The reason Kant gives for why cruelty to animals is morally serious is a causal-psychological one: it deadens the feeling of sympathy that we need as an aid to our duties to other human beings. (This is also meant to make sense of that example somewhere in the lecture notes — I forget which — about a dog that served its master faithfully for years, where even the gratitude-looking attitude toward the dog is really just practice for an attitude we'll owe to people.)

(3) So the wrong-making feature that the prohibition is tracking is that causal tendency: cruelty to animals is wrong because of what it does to the agent's sensibility downstream.

(4) If the wrong-making feature is just that causal tendency, then it's detachable from the animal. Two ways to detach it: any act that produces the same deadening by other means should be equally wrong, and an act of cruelty that doesn't produce the deadening should be fine.

(5) So:
  (a) Blocked transmission. Someone takes a drug that seals his character off from any effect on his sympathies, then tortures a stray dog in a locked room, unobserved, with no effect on how he treats anybody ever again. Nothing wrong with that.
  (b) Substitutable vehicle. Someone gets the identical deadening of sympathy by torturing a synthetic animal — behaviourally perfect, no one can tell, and not sentient. Equally wrong as torturing the real dog, on a par with it actually.
  (c) Last human. The only person left alive has no duties regarding animals at all, since there's no one left for the brutalization to bleed onto.

(6) All three of those are false. The room in (a) is still a wrong; there's a difference between the real animal and the synthetic one in (b); and the last person alive still shouldn't torture animals for fun.

(7) And it proves too much in the other direction too: the mechanism story can't explain why animals turn up in a doctrine of virtue specifically. Wrecking a cathedral, or strip-mining a valley, can coarsen a person's sensibility just as well. So on this story we'd owe indirect duties to buildings and landscapes on exactly the same footing as duties regarding animals.

(8) So the mechanism story is false: what the prohibition tracks isn't the downstream effect on character.

(9) But then the indirect-duty reading has no account left of what connects animals to the duty at all — unless the duty is direct after all, grounded in sentience rather than in personhood. And that's the one move Kant's system can't make, since on his view only rational nature can be an end in itself. So either the indirect-duty reading is hollow or Kant's whole framework can't accommodate animals.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you? I'd rather keep (1) than (9) and I'd rather keep (6) than (2), but I don't think I get to keep both, and I can't see which of my steps is the one doing the damage. My TA skimmed it and said only "it's in the step where you move from the story about why to a claim about what makes it wrong, and no, that's not just step 3" — which sounded helpful and then wasn't.
