I spent my career in theoretical condensed-matter physics — critical phenomena, quenched disorder, nucleation — and in retirement I have been reading into social epistemology. I keep meeting the same canonical model, and I would like it restated in the only vocabulary in which I actually think, and then audited: I want to know what gets lost.

The model, as I have encountered it: a binary state of the world; a long finite queue of agents, each of whom receives a private binary signal of known accuracy, conditionally independent of the others given the state; agents move once, in a fixed order, each seeing the entire history of predecessors' actions but neither their signals nor their posteriors, and each choosing one of two actions; payoffs reward matching the true state. The results I am told to expect are that after a finite, random, but order-one number of steps the queue almost surely locks into a cascade in which every subsequent action is independent of the agent's own signal; that the cascade locks onto the wrong action with probability bounded away from zero; and that it is at once very stubborn and very brittle — stubborn in that nothing the agents do later can dislodge it, brittle in that a small amount of public information, or the release of a modest number of private signals, is said to overturn it and briefly leave the population far better informed than the sequence of actions ever showed.

My own draft of a dictionary, which I suspect is partly wrong and would like corrected rather than flattered: the true state is a quenched field that no agent sees; the private signal is a one-bit noisy measurement of it, with the accuracy parameter acting as an inverse noise strength; the history of actions enters the next agent's decision as an effective local field; and the cascade is an absorbing state of a directed, one-dimensional stochastic process — closer, I would guess, to directed percolation than to an Ising ferromagnet, since the interaction is strictly one-way in time, there is no detailed balance, and there is no free-energy functional anywhere in the model. On that reading, fragility would say that the absorbing state is stable against single-site perturbations but not against finite-density ones, which is why a trickle of public information can release a great deal of pooled private information at once. I would also guess that there is no genuine critical point here at all — the cascade sets in after finitely many agents — unless one lets the signal accuracy approach one half, at which point the onset time diverges and my talk of a transition becomes respectable.

Three things I want from you. First, the corrected and completed dictionary, with my guesses marked right or wrong. Second, an honest residue report: which claims translate cleanly, which survive only if I quietly smuggle in an assumption my framework does not license (I am particularly suspicious of my own uses of temperature, of equilibrium language, and of the idea that information is conserved and merely hidden), and which have no image in my vocabulary at all. I have in mind especially: that each agent's posterior is continuous while her action is one bit, so that pooled information is destroyed rather than merely unexpressed; that every agent maximizes expected payoff given her information while the population can nonetheless end up confidently wrong; and the common-knowledge assumption that lets each agent read the others as epistemic peers who are themselves Bayesian. Third, your verdict on the residue: how much of it is a defect of a physicist's vocabulary, which has no room for warrant, justification, or blame, and how much is a sign that the original claim — individually rational, collectively wrong — is doing less work than it appears to do?

Finally, a perturbation I would like taken seriously rather than waved off: in the model the agents move in a fixed exogenous order, and that order seems to be doing something essential, since a cascade is a claim about what later agents can infer from earlier ones. Suppose instead that the order is random, or that agents arrive in partially overlapping rounds. Does the cascade phenomenon survive, does my directed-process picture survive with it, and if the answer is that ordering is constitutive rather than incidental, what does that tell me about how far the statistical-mechanics analogy can be pushed?
