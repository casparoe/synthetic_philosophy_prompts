I write a column on science and ideas, and I am working on a short series about whether a machine could ever finish infinitely many operations. It was prompted by the old puzzle of a lamp with a toggle switch: flicked on at the end of the first minute of a two-minute interval, off at the end of the next half minute, on at the end of the next quarter minute, and so on, so that by the end of the two minutes every flick has been made. Every flick is a real flick; the question of whether the lamp is on or off at the end has no answer that the story seems to supply. As far as I can tell, people go two ways with this: either the set-up is impossible, because nothing could carry it out, or the set-up is perfectly coherent but the story is incomplete, because the description of the flickings simply fails to say what is true at the end and there is nothing further to know. In what I have read those two diagnoses are constantly run together, and they do not look like the same claim to me. A numerical analyst friend added something that has been nagging at me: in her field, getting each individual step right is not enough to guarantee that a procedure settles down to anything at all — you need a separate condition, which she named and I have since forgotten — and I keep wondering whether that is the same insight in different clothes, or a merely superficial resemblance.

Here is the specific thing I cannot find a word for. Compare the lamp with a ball dropped from a height that bounces, each bounce half the height and half the duration of the last, so that after two minutes infinitely many bounces are over and the ball is at rest on the floor. Nobody I have met thinks the question of where the ball is at the end is somehow unsettled. Same shape of story — infinitely many finite stages packed into a finite time — and yet no puzzlement at all. Now the difference seems to me to show up under two kinds of tampering. First, re-carving: there is a move where you pair the flicks off, note that each on-off pair restores the lamp to its initial state, and conclude that it ends off; then someone else pairs them starting one flick later and concludes the opposite. The schedule has not changed, only the bookkeeping. Second, jiggling: insert one extra flick at the front, or stretch the whole run from two minutes to three, and the pairing answers swap over again — whereas the ball's resting place does not budge if you change its starting height a little or add one extra small bounce. So some infinitely staged set-ups have an end state that survives any re-carving and any small perturbation of how the stages are laid out, and others have an end state that flips under both, and I want a word for that difference, and for the principle that only the surviving answers are real answers. (A third thing muddies it further: an electrical engineer I asked said that a real lamp on a real circuit, switched at intervals that shrink below the circuit's time constant, would simply settle into a steady glow, and that the on-or-off question only becomes unanswerable once you idealise the switch and the bulb away. If he is right, some of these unanswerable questions are artefacts of the idealisation rather than gaps in the world, which is a third diagnosis and not the same as either of the first two.)

So: is there an established concept for the property that separates the ball from the lamp — the property of having an outcome that is fixed no matter how the stages are grouped or slightly rearranged, as opposed to one that swings with the grouping? Is re-carving and jiggling one test or two, and can a case pass one and fail the other? Is the move that says such a question has no answer the same move as the one that says the task cannot be performed? And how does any of this relate to what my numerical-analyst friend was describing, and to a pattern I know from my own trade, where the same run of monthly figures reads as trending up or down depending on how you bin the months — a colleague tells me there is a name for that in spatial statistics. I am not after a label I can drop into a column; I want to know what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges are, including the cases where it is genuinely unclear whether it applies. Please don't send me a reading list or citations — I can't chase references and wouldn't know what to do with them; argue for the carving directly.
