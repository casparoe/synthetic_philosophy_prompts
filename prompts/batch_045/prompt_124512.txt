For a reading group I'm putting together on the Church–Turing thesis I want to open with a scoring exercise, so bear with the setup.

Imagine someone actually builds a "limit oracle": a sealed box that, for each n, keeps displaying a guess about whether the nth Turing machine halts on input n. Every guess is eventually correct and then never changes again, but the box never announces when it has settled, and the times at which it changes its mind aren't computably bounded, so no Turing machine reproduces the stream it puts out. Its takes no input from you; it just works through its own list.

Score 0 to 10 how much epistemic damage the existence of such a box does to each claim below, where 0 means "doesn't really bear on it" and 10 means "refuted outright," then give me the ranking that falls out of your scores:

1. the CT thesis read as a claim about our intuitive notion of an effective procedure — finite, terminating, and it hands you an answer you can recognize as the answer
2. the maximality thesis: nothing physical can churn out anything a Turing machine couldn't
3. the Deutsch-style physical version: every finitely realizable physical system can be perfectly simulated by a universal machine
4. the bold thesis: nothing that nature or artifice computes is beyond a Turing machine
5. the super-bold thesis: the universe's own evolution is Turing-computable
6. the extended or "invariance" thesis: reasonable models of computation simulate each other with at most polynomial overhead

One sentence per score saying why it lands there, tell me which one you most wanted to move after you'd written the others down, and flag which score depends most on some stipulation I left vague. Keep it casual, and write it in American English.
