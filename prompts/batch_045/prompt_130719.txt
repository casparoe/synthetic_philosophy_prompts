I'm a senior finishing the final paper for my philosophy elective, and before I hand it in I want someone to check that the position I've ended up with actually holds together, because my teacher keeps saying that the parts of a view are usually less friendly to each other than they look. Roughly, here is where I have landed. (1) I would not plug in permanently to an experience machine of the kind Nozick imagined, and my reason is that I want to be in real contact with the world and with other people, not merely to seem to myself to be. (2) At the same time, I hold that a person's well-being consists entirely in the quality of her conscious experience: nothing can make a life better or worse for the person living it except by making some difference to what it is like for her from the inside. (3) I also think that a woman who spends her whole life failing at the things she believes she is succeeding at, who never finds out, and who feels fully satisfied, has a worse life than she thinks she has. (4) I think my objection to the machine is only my own aversion to illusion, so that someone who genuinely has no such aversion and wants nothing more than what the machine offers loses nothing by plugging in. (5) But I also think that if the machine were shared, so that the people I met inside it were other real human beings plugged into the same simulation, with whom I really talked and argued and made plans, my objection would lose nearly all of its force and a life spent there could be as good as a life outside. (6) Finally, I do not think there is any single currency of well-being, no one thing such that every good life is good in virtue of containing more of it. (7) Yet I also think that if two lives were exactly alike in every respect that matters, and one of them also contained an extra afternoon of ordinary pleasure, that one would be the better life.

What I want to know is whether this is a consistent package. Please tell me which pairs of these commitments genuinely pull against each other, ordered by how badly, with a sentence on why each conflict is real; which of the apparent conflicts dissolves once the claims are stated more carefully, and how; and what the single cheapest revision would be, what exactly it would cost me, and whether in the end I have to give up refusing the machine. Write your answer as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the way you would write a careful email to a student who has asked you for honest feedback.
