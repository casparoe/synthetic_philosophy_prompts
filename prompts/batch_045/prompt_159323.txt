I cover media and platforms for a living, and a reader sent me this after a piece I did on the collapse of local news. I've been chewing on it for a week and I'm pretty sure it proves way too much, but I can't name the step where it goes wrong.

(1) What makes the press corrupting, on Kierkegaard's account, isn't that it lies or spins. It's that it manufactures the public: an aggregate that isn't anybody in particular, in which no one has to answer for what gets said, and which levels real differences down into one abstract, interchangeable opinion.

(2) Becoming a self, on the same account, requires owning your own speech — answering for it as this one person, not as a member of a body or a contributer to a discussion.

(3) So the defect in mass-mediated speech isn't headcount and isn't inaccuracy. It's the mode of address: an utterance aimed at everyone in general is aimed at no one, and it can't carry the kind of answerability that (2) says genuine speech needs.

(4) Which means the defect doesn't go away when you shrink the audience. A newsletter with 400 subscribers, a group chat, a seminar room — if the addressee is still an abstract everyone, it's the same failure, just smaller. My reader says this is why the whole indie-media answer to the problem misses the point.

(5) And it doesn't go away when you get the content right either. A true and rigorous critique of leveling is still leveling once the press carries it, because the corruption sits in the address, not the payload. The critique can't exempt itself.

(6) Now: nothing printed or posted is addressed to a person who can call you to account in the moment. Writing is address to an absent, indefinite reader. So writing at all — this argument included, Kierkegaard's own books included — is exactly what (1) through (3) condemn.

(7) But (1) through (3) generalize past the press as well. Kant's universal law, the impartial standpoint, any putatively binding norm whatsoever, is addressed to anyone at all, and by (3) that's addressed to no one. So every universal moral principle is corrupting. Since that's lunacy, something upstream has to give.

Here's the wrinkle the reader added, and I honestly don't know what to do with it. Suppose the readership isn't a mass but a trickle: one stranger reads your post this year, another reads it three years from now, each alone, each taking it seriously, none of them known to you or to each other. Call it ambient address. Intuitively it's the least corrupt case there is — no crowd, no feedback loop, no leveling. But it's also the case where you have the least idea who you're talking to and where you can least be called to account. So is ambient address the best case or the worst? My instinct says best, and (2) seems to say worst, which makes me wonder whether I've even understood (2).

I want to keep the diagnosis in (1): it does seem to me that something goes wrong when speech is addressed to nobody in particular, and that's more or less why I think engagement metrics rot people's judgement. I'd also like to keep (4) and (5), since they're what make this more than nostalgia for the town square. I really dont want (6), because I write for a living.

So: name the step you'd reject, explain why it looked solid enough to get past me, and price the other options — what does each one cost me? And if there's a version of this that survives, something about writing to one reader at a time, or about Kierkegaard's pseudonyms and indirect communication, tell me whether that's an actual solution or just a dodge with good branding.
