I teach a first-year metaphysics seminar and I am rewriting the session on the principle of the identity of indiscernibles. Before I hand students a formulation, I want to know what my own verdicts on cases commit me to.

1. The standard two-globe universe: nothing exists but two perfectly spherical, intrinsically indiscernible iron globes, two miles apart in otherwise empty Euclidean space. Verdict: there are two globes, and this refutes any version of the principle that quantifies only over intrinsic, non-relational properties.

2. The same universe, now allowing relational properties: each globe has the property of being two miles from an iron globe. Verdict: this rescues nothing, since each globe has it.

3. But each globe bears to the other a relation it does not bear to itself. Verdict: that does seem to me to discern them, so that some version of the principle survives after all.

4. Two intrinsically indiscernible globes in a space closed on itself like a cylinder, so that each globe is one mile north of the other and one mile south of the other. Verdict: no discernment here, because a relation that holds in both directions does no discerning work. This is the verdict I am least confident of.

5. Two disconnected spacetimes, with no spatiotemporal or causal relation between them and no spacetime containing both, each holding a single intrinsically identical globe. Verdict: still two globes, not one, even though nothing discerns them even in the weak sense of case 3.

6. Two bosons in exactly the same quantum state. Verdict: I want to say both that there are two of them and that counting them may simply be the wrong description. I go back and forth, and I may be leaning on physics I do not actually have.

7. Indexicals and haecceities: pointing at one globe and saying that one does not discern them, and being identical with this globe is not a property that could do the discerning either.

My tentative formulation: x is identical with y if and only if, for every relation R and every object z, x bears R to z exactly if y bears R to z. That lets case 3 in, but it seems to leave case 5 as a genuine counterexample, and I cannot tell whether that is a cost or simply the truth.

Is there a single formulation that delivers all of these verdicts, or must I drop one, and if so which, and why? State the principle precisely, say which case motivates each clause, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts it overturns. Then run it on a case I have not settled: a perfectly homogeneous infinite space containing nothing at all, in which every point is qualitatively like every other. One point, many indiscernible points, or a malformed question? Finally, tell me how confident you are that the principle is doing real work rather than restating my verdicts in new notation, and if one of my cases rests on a confusion, or the tension I think I see between 1 and 3 is not really there, say so directly instead of smoothing it over.
