You are a historian of historiography — the history of how the discipline decides what counts as a fact about the past — who also writes a satirical column under a pen name for a magazine read mostly by people who peer-review grant applications in ancient history, archaeology, and Near Eastern studies. I edit that column, and I would like a piece for the next issue.

Draft it as the minutes of the forty-first session of the Standing Committee on the Historicity Threshold, an invented international standards body that issues Provisional Certificates of Historicity and assigns legendary narratives a grade on a published scale. The Committee has before it three files it has never managed to close, and the session consists of re-adjudicating all three under the same written rules: the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus. Before you begin writing, work out carefully which real evidentiary moves the satire has to contain so that a specialist reads it as fair rather than as a travesty: the argument from silence, multiple independent attestation, the criterion of embarrassment, the gap in time between an event and its earliest surviving source, the persistence of place-names, the slide from "a city on this site was destroyed in roughly the right century" to "this poem preserves a memory of that destruction," and the possibility that a named hero is a composite into whom several dimly-attested figures have been folded. Some material you may find useful, all of which I would like deployed: at the site generally identified with Homeric Troy, a settlement of the appropriate period was violently destroyed, and the Hittite diplomatic archives contain a place-name that may be the Greek one in Hittite spelling; a British monk writing within about a century of the events denounces the British kings at length and describes a siege at Mount Badon without ever naming Arthur, whose first unambiguous appearance comes in a compilation roughly three centuries later; and a late-thirteenth-century-BCE Egyptian victory inscription names Israel as a people already in Canaan, while nothing in the abundant Egyptian documentation of the period corresponds to a sojourn, a series of plagues, a flight, or a population anywhere near the size the narrative presupposes. Treat each of the three files with visibly different handling — the same Committee applying the same rules should nonetheless be caught reaching for a generous reading in one case and a withering one in another — because that asymmetry is most of what I want exposed.

Three devices I would like you to keep and develop: a weighting table, call it Schedule B, governing how much the argument from silence is permitted to count, which the Committee revises twice mid-session and never settles; a clause — let us call it the Wilusa clause, after the Hittite name — specifying how much corroboration may be extracted from a foreign archive that appears to mention your site under someone else's spelling; and an embarrassment rider, since it is a genuinely open question whether the embarrassment produced by a hero's absence from a source that ought to mention him belongs to the ancient source or to the hero's modern defenders. The reductio I care about most is the one you can build from the Committee's own existence: once a certificate is issued, heritage funding, tourism revenue and excavation permits follow; the excavation then produces finds; and the Committee must decide whether finds that it has itself caused to exist count as independent confirmation warranting an upgrade — so the grade, which was only ever meant to record how the evidence stands, quietly becomes one of the evidence's causes. Give me roughly 800 to 900 words in the deadpan register of an ISO standard crossed with faculty-senate minutes, with numbered items and sub-clauses, invented members identified only by institution, and no real scholars named, no footnotes and no citations: everything should be readable without looking anything up. Stay deadpan to the last line and never let the Committee itself see the joke; a reader should be able to tell what is being ironized without anyone winking, and the three cases should never actually be resolved — by the final item the reader should feel why "when does a legend become history?" may be the wrong shape of question, while no one in the room says so.
