I write a monthly column for a popular history magazine where I take apart one viral claim about the past — Atlantis, the pyramid-builders having been taught by visitors from somewhere else, and every few months a version of the theory that everything we call ancient history is a recalibrated duplicate of medieval history, so that the "Roman" empire and some later empire are really the same events filed under different names. I also babysit the comment section, which is where I keep running into the thing I want a word for.

Here's the pattern. The theory's selling point is how much it explains. One story covers the pyramids in Egypt and the ones in Mexico, flood myths on five continents, the megaliths, the star alignments, the odd double-datings in the king lists. And the coverage is offered as evidence: look how many separate things this accounts for. But when I actually check, each individual link is thin, and every link has its own seperate little assumption bolted on — this one needs the myths to have travelled, that one needs the dating tables to have been tampered with, the other needs a name to have been mistranscribed — and the bolted-on assumptions have nothing to do with each other. So it's one story with a different excuse for every item. My readers feel this and come away thinking there must be something to it; I feel it and come away thinking it's the reason to distrust it.

Things it isn't, or isn't only:

- It's not that the theory can't be proven wrong. These things usually do stick their necks out — specific dates, specific identifications of one ruler with another, even "you will find such-and-such beneath this site" — and sometimes they get flatly refuted and it doesn't seem to cost them anything with their fans.
- It isn't the move where you dump so many arguments that the other person can't answer them all in the time available. There's no clock here. I can take six months and check every link, and I have, and the breadth still does its work on people afterwards.
- Someone in my comments said "just-so story", but as far as I can tell that's about inventing an explanation for a single trait after the fact, not about a whole system spanning continents and centuries.
- And I don't think breadth by itself can be what's wrong. I keep coming back to Wegener and continental drift: a guy matching coastlines, fossils and rock types across oceans, a pile of weak correspondences, no mechanism, most geologists laughing — and it turned out to be right. So whatever is off about the fringe version, it can't just be "a lot of thin links held together by a story."

So: is there an established concept for this specific thing — a theory that gets credit for how much it covers, where the coverage is bought with a separate free parameter per item, and where refuting an item doesn't cost the theory its audience? If nothing fits, I need a name I can actually use in print, with a definition sharp enough that my readers could apply it themselves. Please don't just hand me a label or a reading list: I want to know what the phenomenon is, what it isn't, and where it stops. In particular I'd like the boundary cases sorted: a broad comparative-mythology synthesis from a real scholar; a diffusionist account of a single technology that most specialists reject but which isn't crazy; and an archaeological claim that's mostly right but whose dating is off by three centuries. Which of those are cases of the thing and which aren't, and by what test?
