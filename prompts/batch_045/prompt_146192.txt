I've been reading the rationalist blogs and forums for something like fifteen years, and for the last five I've done a fair amount of forecasting on the side: tournament-style questions, some institutional forecasting adjacent to my job, and a standing argument with a group of friends about how long large human institutions last. I'm writing up my own forecasting epistemology so the group can attack it, and what I want from you is an audit rather than an explanation. Assume I already know the standard material: base rates, the planning fallacy, inside versus outside view, reference class arguments, calibration, log-loss scoring, the Tetlock-style results about who forecasts well and why. Please don't give me reading suggestions or tell me to go get better data; I want the conceptual audit stated in its own terms.

Here is roughly where I have landed.

1. Outside view first. I treat the inside and outside views as rival methods for producing the same number, and my rule for adjudicating between them is: anchor on the base rate for the reference class, and let the inside view move you off that anchor only to the extent that the inside view has itself been validated out of sample. In practice this means the adjustment is small, and that when someone's inside view moves them a long way from the base rate, I want to see a track record before I follow them.

2. Reference classes are selected causally, not by surface similarity. I don't think putting two polities in the same class because both are large, or because both get called empires, or because both are wealthy, tells me anything. What matters is whether the same generative process is producing the outcome. So the relevant classes are things like fiscal-military strain after a long commercial expansion, succession crises in systems with weakly specified succession, legitimacy collapse after a legitimacy-bearing narrative fails, and so on. If the failure mode matches, I'll put two very differently sized and very differently dated polities in the same class; if it doesn't, I won't, however similar they look from the outside.

3. There are no unique events. Every event belongs to some reference class, and the claim that this time is different is almost always the inside view wearing a disguise. I take this to be a claim about intellectual hygiene rather than about metaphysics: refusing to name a reference class is usually just a way of refusing to be scored.

4. Constant hazard for polities. Unlike organisms, polities have no built-in senescence, and unlike machines they have no obvious wear-out part, so my default model for how long one lasts is the constant-hazard one: conditional on having survived to time t, expected remaining lifetime is roughly the same as it was at the start. I use as my anchor a rough working figure for the average completed lifespan of large polities in the historical record, somewhere between two and four centuries; I don't care about the exact value and I'd rather you argued about the structure than the number. I also don't worry that the historical record is a sample of polities that have already ended. I've never seen a clean argument that this selection pushes the number in a particular direction, so I use the average of the completed cases as my forecast for the remaining life of a polity that is still alive and already old.

5. Scoring discipline. I report calibrated probabilities, I score myself with log loss, and I think the honest answer to a fifty-year question is often just the base rate with wide error bars attached. I would rather publish an uninformative-looking distribution that is calibrated than a confident one that is not.

6. The verdict I have actually published: roughly even odds, maybe a little better than even, that the present American constitutional order does not survive in recognizable form to 2100. A substantial part of the weight here comes from the fact that the run since the 1860s, and especially since 1945, is unusually long and unusually untested by historical standards. My reasoning is that long untested runs are fragile: a system that has not recently been stress-tested accumulates unexercised failure modes, degraded norms, and a governing class that has never had to actually use the emergency provisions, and so the next test is more likely to break it rather than less.

7. I try to apply the same machinery to my own corner of the world, where I have much more inside information: I expect the current structure of the AI industry to look substantially different in ten years, on base rates for fast-moving concentrated industries, and I don't think being inside that world gives me much of an epistemic edge over the base rate.

What I want from you: go through this set and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only look like they are, ordered by severity rather than listed as equally bad. For each real tension, say what would have to give, what giving it would cost me in the cases I actually care about, and whether there is a cheaper revision that preserves most of what I want. Then tell me which single revision cleans up the most. Assume most of the list is right; I am not looking for permission to abandon the whole framework, and I don't have a favourite commitment I'm protecting, so please don't assume I'll drop whichever item is least fashionable.

Finally, the part I care about most and that people usually get wrong: separate what is settled among people who actually work on these questions from what is genuinely contested. In particular, tell me where the audit depends on contested empirical claims about the shape of duration distributions or about when base rates beat mechanistic models, and give me the shape of that disagreement rather than a verdict dressed up as a consensus view. Mark which of your own claims you would bet on at even odds and which you would only take at three to one, and flag anywhere you're unsure whether something is settled at all.
