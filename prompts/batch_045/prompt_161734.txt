A friend lent me a pop-science book on evolution (I won't name it, its one of those sweeping big-picture ones), and the chapter on the levels of selection ends up saying something like this:

"Dawkins settled the old group-selection debate. Genes are the true units of selection because they are the only things copied faithfully across generations; organisms and groups are just survival machines that get rebuilt every generation. That is why 'group selection' is a muddle: a trait that looks like it's for the good of the group — alarm calls, restraint in breeding, restraint in hunting — is really a gene maximizing its copies, usually by helping relatives. The formal version of this is the Price equation, which splits any change in gene frequency into a within-group component and a between-group component. Because the split is purely additive and the two components can be traded off against each other, group selection isn't a separate process at all: it's the same process viewed from a different angle, and the book calls this the 'partition argument.' Which 'level' you talk about is therefore just bookkeeping, and there's no fact of the matter about where selection really acts. The upshot: apparent altruism in nature is always genetic self-interest in disguise, and anything that looks like genuine cooperation in human societies is the same story one level up."

I've picked up enough about this debate to suspect a couple of things are off — something about "unit of selection" naming two different jobs, and something about a partition of a total change not answering any causal question — but I can't tell which sentences a working evolutionary biologist would accept, which are just loose popularization, and which are the book stacking the deck. Can you take it more or less sentence by sentence and sort it into (a) fine as stated, (b) a simplification that's defensible in context, and (c) genuinely contested or wrong, saying in each case what a defender of group selection and a defender of the gene's-eye view would each want to add?

One more thing I care about: does the final sentence follow from anything earlier that's true, or does it need extra premises the book never supplied? Even if every claim above were correct, I want to know whether the move from "genes are the units of selection" to "so there's no such thing as real cooperation" is one the view actually supports or just a rhetorical slide.
