I'm retired now — used to do statistical mechanics, spent most of my career on phase transitions and Monte Carlo stuff — and in the last couple of years I've drifted into philosophy via a reading group, and from there, somehow, into the literature on fairness constraints in automated decision systems. I've worked through the basic impossibility results: the way calibration (scores meaning the same thing across groups) and error-rate balance (comparable false positive and false negative rates) can't both hold except in the degenerate cases where base rates match or the predictor is already perfect, plus the various "we can't have all three criteria at once" theorems in the same family. What strikes me, coming from physics, is that we had no-go theorems too, but there the community pretty much knew which assumption to give up. Here it seems like nobody agrees, and half the literature responds by proposing yet another scalar metric or yet a fourth axiom. I've been asked to sit on a small panel for a charitable foundation that's carving out a tech-and-society strand, and we have enough for maybe four two-year postdocs, or a handful of modest project grants, over roughly three years, to spend on conceptual work specifically (not empirical ML, not policy advocacy).

Two things I keep chewing on that I'd like you to take seriously as candidates, even if you decide they're wrong. First: nearly all the impossibility results I've seen are stated over a single static joint distribution — a snapshot of features, scores, outcomes, and group membership. But the systems people actually deploy are reflexive: the risk score decides who gets released, or who gets flagged for fraud, or who gets offered an intervention, and that decision partly produces the outcome data the next version of the model is trained and audited on, and it reshapes the population the constraints were defined over in the first place. In my own notes I've been calling this the reflexivity problem, and my hunch is that the static theorems are the easy special case and the diachronic question is where the real action is. Second: in practice no single principal chooses the whole pipeline. A vendor trains a model under one constraint, a hospital sets a threshold under another, a regulator audits against a third, and each stage may be individually defensible while the composed system satisfies nobody's constraint — or, worse, the constraints are jointly unsatisfiable in a way that no stage's designer can see. Call it the multi-principal composition problem. I suspect both of these are either already known under names I haven't learned, or genuinely neglected, and I can't tell which.

So: if you were advising us, what are the three or four open conceptual questions in this area that our money should actually go to, in order, and why those? I care much less about the list than about the reasoning behind it. For each one I'd want what makes it upstream of other questions, how tractable it is given that our grantees will mostly be philosophers, statisticians and maybe legal theorists without access to proprietary datasets, how neglected it is relative to how much it matters, and — the bit I think panels always skip — what kind of result or real-world event over the next three years would move it up or down the list. Please also tell me explicitly which fashionable lines of work you'd decline to fund: I have a strong suspicion that "propose a new fairness criterion and prove a new incompatibility" is a saturated genre, but I'd like your argument rather than my instinct. And I want the two hobby-horses above graded honestly, including the possibility that they're confused or already solved. What I do not want is a survey of the field or a list of papers to read — I've done enough of that, and I want a defended agenda with trade-offs stated, not a map.
