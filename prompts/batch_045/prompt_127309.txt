I am trying to work out what I actually think about minds made of many smaller agents, and I would like the principle behind my verdicts rather than a survey of positions. I am not asking for a literature review; I want my own case-by-case reactions treated as data and either systematized or corrected.

The case that anchors me is the ant colony of the Ant Fugue, Aunt Hillary: a colony whose beliefs live in the large-scale pattern of its trails and its chemical field, whose thoughts take hours rather than moments, which can be told things and can act on what it has been told, and which survives the loss of any of its members. Everything below is a variation on her, and in each case I want to know whether there is a subject there at all, or merely an arrangement that behaves as if there were.

Here are my verdicts.

A honeybee swarm selecting a nest site, by scout dances and quorum, with no individual bee carrying the comparison. My verdict: no. It decides, but there is no one it is like something to be the swarm.

A colony in which every apparent colony-level opinion is the chemical amplification of the state of one scout; remove the scout and the colony is mute; the colony's judgments track his, and his alone. My verdict: no. The colony is his megaphone, not an additional subject.

A colony whose answers are drawn out by a keeper who unintentionally supplies the chemical cues that guide them, so that the intelligence on display belongs as much to the interpreter as to the colony. My verdict: no.

A ledger containing a complete transcription of ten years of Aunt Hillary's colony-level states, read by the keeper, who acts on what it says. My verdict: no, and I am unsure why, since the states are genuinely global and genuinely move the keeper.

The same colony-level dynamics re-implemented on a computer and run a million times faster, uncoupled from the physical ants. My verdict: yes, reluctantly, but yes.

A colony whose global states are richly structured and integrated but which never feed back into what the ants do; the workers' behaviour is fully explained by their local rules, and the global pattern is a shadow. My verdict: no.

A colony whose ants are themselves colonies of smaller creatures, where the smaller colonies each seem to meet the standard, and where the larger colony has beliefs about matters none of the smaller colonies can register. My verdict: yes at both levels, which I suspect my own principles cannot deliver.

A large firm with an official position on a matter arrived at by a procedure, where no individual employee believes the conclusion and each relevant employee holds only a fragment, where the position is available on the intranet to everyone and is causally upstream of what the firm does. My verdict: no, though every reason I can give for this seems to cut against Aunt Hillary as well.

Aunt Hillary with her ants replaced one at a time by synthetic units that do the same jobs. My verdict: yes throughout, and yes at the end.

Two colonies, Hillary and Hortense, joined by a pheromone bridge. Fully merged into a single colony with one field: one subject, I say. Linked by a thin tube that carries a few signals: two subjects. Somewhere in between they share a foraging network but keep distinct nest odours, and there I go back and forth and cannot locate a line.

My tentative principle is this. There is a subject associated with a system just in case the system has a class of states such that: first, the contents of those states are not the contents of any state of any proper part; second, those states are causally upstream of much of what the system subsequently does, in a way not recoverable from the parts' own rules taken one at a time, and they are globally available within the system; third, no proper part, and no system of which it is a proper part, already has states with those same contents in that same episode, so that a single subject is not counted twice.

I would like that principle, or a better one, tested against my cases and repaired where it fails. As I read it, it handles the swarm, the megaphone, and the accelerated simulation; it is silent on where system boundaries fall, which is what the ledger case turns on; and I cannot see how it excludes the firm, since the firm's position seems to satisfy all three clauses. My own guess is that a fourth clause is needed, something like the requirement that the states be produced, maintained, and revisable by mechanisms of the system's own operating at that grain, so that the system can be mistaken in its own idiom and repair the mistake in that idiom rather than outsourcing the repair wholesale to the judgment of a part. But I do not know whether that clause is substantive or whether it just restates the conclusion.

Please give me: one or two candidate principles stated precisely enough to be applied; the case that motivates each clause; what the principle predicts for four cases I have not listed, namely a colony built from the start out of a mixture of ants and synthetic units doing the same jobs, a colony whose global states reach only about a tenth of its workers, two colonies held in perfect synchrony so that they share every global state, and a colony with global states but no memory at all, tracking only the present; and an honest account of which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, which you would overturn, and what overturning it costs. I would also like to know whether the Hillary-and-Hortense continuum forces you to accept that there is no fact of the matter in the middle range, or whether you can draw a principled line and say where it falls.
