I'm a high-school senior, and for the last few months I've been writing an essay on superrationality for a journal that publishes student philosophy; the submission limit is 2,000 words, so I've tried to make one narrow argument carefully instead of surveying the whole debate, and I'd like it stress-tested before I send it in. I came to the topic through an argument in my school's math club, where a friend insisted that a rational player would defect against her own twin and I couldn't say why that felt wrong to me except by asserting it. The case I restrict the essay to is a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma with payoffs T = 5, R = 3, P = 1, S = 0, played against an opponent that runs exactly the decision procedure I do but has no causal influence on me, or I on it: not a machine wired to my output, just an independent system that reasons as I reason, so that what I decide is strong evidence of what it decides without bringing it about. I set aside the wired-up case on purpose, since there my choice really does cause the other's and even a causal decision theorist cooperates; all the interest, it seems to me, is in the non-causal twin.

My argument has four steps, none of which I've found stated quite this way in what I've read so far, though I've mostly encountered the critical side secondhand. First: under perfect correlation the superrationalist's conclusion follows from the plainest expected-utility calculation and needs no special doctrine. If the probability that the twin cooperates given that I cooperate is 1, and given that I defect is 0, then EU(C) = 3 and EU(D) = 1. So the famous standoff cannot be a dispute about the arithmetic; it has to be a dispute about whether that kind of evidential relation is the right thing to maximize against. Second, my reframing: superrationality is better read as ordinary expected-utility maximization under self-locating uncertainty plus a substantive assumption about the sampling model. Before you know which of the two runs of your procedure you are, you should treat your own decision as a random draw from the decisions that procedure produces, so that what gets evaluated is the procedure rather than the act performed on the occasion. Grant that and cooperation follows immediately; deny it and nothing follows. If so, the disagreement is about the object of rational assessment and about which self-locating probabilities are correct, not about causation and not about the payoffs, on which everyone agrees. Third, that would make the decades-long impasse a case of two different questions being pursued under one heading, with the real argument to be had over the reference class rather than over the decision rule. Fourth, a test case I invented to see whether the reframing has any teeth: with known probability p my opponent is a non-causally-correlated twin, and with probability 1 − p it is a fixed mechanism that always defects no matter what I do. Then EU(C) = 3p and EU(D) = 1, so cooperation is recommended exactly when p is greater than one third: the recommendation flips sharply while the case itself varies smoothly, and just below the threshold I am told to defect against an opponent that is almost certainly my twin. I want to conclude from this that the slogan about identical reasoners having to cooperate is too coarse to be a principle, and that once you make it sensitive to degrees of correlation it stops looking like a distinctive doctrine about rationality and looks like expected-utility maximization with an unusual evidence model.

What I want is comments rather than a verdict, and there's one worry I'd like dealt with head-on. A critic who refuses to condition on my own act will say that whichever opponent turns out to be in front of me, defecting beats cooperating by one, so she recommends defection at every value of p; my threshold then only appears from inside the evidential framework I was supposedly testing, which would mean my test case doesn't discriminate between the two views at all, only traces the shape of one of them, and the conclusion I draw from it is far weaker than I claim. Is that objection fatal to step four, or can the case be repaired? Alongside that: am I caricaturing the opponent of superrationality in steps one and two; is step two a genuinely different framing or just the standard argument in new vocabulary, which I'd hate to present as my own discovery; and of the four steps, which would a sharp critic attack first, in its strongest form? Please engage the steps themselves rather than telling me whether superrationality is true — I can get that anywhere, and it wouldn't tell me whether the essay works.
