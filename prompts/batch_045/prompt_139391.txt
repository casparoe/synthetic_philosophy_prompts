A chapter in a popular introduction to ethics summarises Kant's universalisability test roughly as follows: "Kant's categorical imperative tells you to act only on a personal rule, your 'maxim', that you could will to become a law for everyone. In practice that means imagining a world in which everybody in your situation did what you are about to do, and asking whether that is a world you could accept living in. If not — if universal adoption of your maxim would destroy the practice your action depends on, or produce a state of affairs no rational person would choose — the action is wrong. That is why lying and promise-breaking come out badly: universal lying would destroy trust, and a world without trust is worse for everyone. Strip away the talk of duty and the test is really just rule-utilitarianism in disguise, and it inherits that view's problems: put to it a maxim like 'train as a doctor' or 'lie to a murderer at the door' and it either gives the wrong answer or gives no answer at all, which is why hardly anyone defends it now." I am fairly confident the last part is unfair — as I understood Kant, the whole point is that duty does not depend on outcomes — but I cannot say where exactly the chapter goes wrong, and since its author is a professional philosopher I assume the mistakes are subtle rather than silly.

Please audit the paragraph. Separate what a sympathetic Kantian would accept from what is merely a simplification, what conflates distinctions Kant himself draws, and what is a familiar objection presented as a refutation, and tell me how much of the chapter's conclusion survives once the accurate part is stated properly. Two things interest me especially: whether the "imagine a world where everyone does it" gloss smuggles in the consequentialist reading the chapter then claims to have discovered, and whether the failure of the test on oddly general or oddly specific maxims is the difficulty the chapter takes it to be.

One further thing. Someone whose blog I read argues that the "everyone does it" framing is beside the point in real cases anyway, because what actually matters is partial compliance: on her version you ask what would happen if only, say, five per cent of people acted on the maxim, and whether the practice your action relies on survives at that threshold. She claims this handles free-riding better than an all-or-nothing test and gives different verdicts in some cases. Does that variant capture something Kant's version gets wrong, or does it quietly substitute a different theory altogether?
