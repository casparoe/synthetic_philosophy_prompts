I work as an economist on transport appraisal — cost-benefit analysis of road and rail schemes, travel-time savings monetised through stated-preference surveys, distributional weights applied by income group — and I'm trying to get the conceptual foundations of what I actually do into defensible shape for a methods chapter, with the help of a reading group of philosophers of economics who enjoy taking things apart. Before I commit any of it to print I want a hard-nosed consistency check on the package of views I've drifted into, because I have a nagging sense that at least two of them are doing work the others forbid, while a couple of the others are probably fine and I'm just worried about them for no reason. Please act as a philosopher of economics and decision theorist specialising in the foundations of utility and choice: restate each commitment in its most defensible form before criticising it, do a genuinely pairwise pass instead of six separate comments, and don't flag everything — I've had people tell me the whole set is internally inconsistent and I don't believe that.

Here is roughly where I've landed:

(1) Anti-mentalism about utility: to say that a person's utility function is u is, I take it, just to say that her choices can be represented as maximising u; there is no further fact about her utility — no feeling, no inner magnitude, no fact about how much she really cares — that could come apart from what her choices support.

(2) Utility is unique only up to positive affine transformation, and the transformation can be chosen independently for each person; so claims about interpersonal comparisons, whether of levels or of units, are strictly speaking without determinate content.

(3) Nonetheless, in my day job I rank policies by summed welfare, I think it is true and not merely convenient that a given travel-time saving is worth more to a poorer household than to a richer one, and I am happy to use money — willingness to pay — as the practical yardstick for making those comparisons, with equity weights on top.

(4) Agents can fail to maximise their own good: akrasia, framing and menu effects, preferences deformed by deprivation and then sincerely reported as contentment. So I also want to say that someone can really prefer the thing she does not choose, or would choose it under better information or calmer conditions, and that an analyst is entitled to say so.

(5) The line I've been using to stop (1) collapsing into a mere relabelling — and this is the bit my reading group likes least — is that utility is a theoretical term: utility is to choice roughly as temperature is to the behaviour of a thermometer, introduced by and scaled by the laws it enters (the maximisation law, and in policy work the summation law). It is an abstraction that earns its keep, rather than an extra ingredient in the world.

(6) In conversation I also fall back on a relaxed ordinalism: only the ranking matters, the numbers are bookkeeping — which sits oddly with my relying on the curvature of u to model risk attitudes and on adding utilities up across people in (3).

What I want from you: which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, which only look like it and dissolve once each commitment is stated precisely, and for each real tension, what would have to give, what the cheapest repair would be, and what that repair would cost me in practice — in particular, does anything force me to abandon the money yardstick, the interpersonal summation, or the talk of what someone really prefers? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally serious, tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and if you think one of the commitments is in better shape than I've given it credit for, say so. Take your time and reason it through carefully; I want a diagnosis, not a survey of the literature, and no reading list — I can find the sources myself.
