I help put together a short comedy bit for the end-of-term party of the political theory reading group I'm in, and this term's session is on Sen's liberal paradox. Last term's Arrow sketch worked because the audience already knew the material: a flat-share cycling through pizza toppings until they ordered nothing. That won't work again, and it especially won't work for Sen, because the laugh there has to come from the mechanism rather than from the generic thought that social choice is hopeless. So: could you write me a joke, setup and punchline, tellable out loud in about half a minute, whose punchline turns on the actual structure of the result? By that I mean the comedy should come from the fact that the two ingredients are each almost comically undemanding — the Pareto principle applied to a single pair that everyone happens to agree on, and a minimal liberalism condition that hands each person a say over exactly one pair of alternatives falling within their own personal sphere — and that putting just those together, over a domain nobody has restricted, forces the social ranking into a cycle. Ideally the damage is visibly done by the busybody part: the cycle is driven by what other people want regarding someone else's private affair, not by anything exotic about the alternatives themselves.

Two constraints. First, don't reuse Sen's own illustration — no Lewd and Prude, no scandalous novel, no two people and one book. Set it somewhere contemporary, with three people and three options (a shared flat, a departmental committee, a group chat planning a trip, whatever you like), and make the cycle genuinely arise from someone's preferences about another person's personal domain; a nice touch would be for at least one character to insist loudly that it is none of their business while their ranking gives them away. Second, no puns on Sen's name, and no Arrow-in-costume: if the joke would work just as well if you swapped in Arrow's theorem or the Condorcet paradox, it isn't the joke I want. A second beat would be welcome if you can manage it — something for the people in the room who know that the standard ways out don't abandon the whole enterprise but weaken one of the two conditions, so that the ending can be read as either a complaint about meddling neighbours or a complaint about the Pareto principle. After the joke itself, please add two or three sentences noting which condition the humour leans on and exactly where the cycle sits in your example, so I can check whether it will land on an audience that has read the argument once, quickly, a fortnight ago.
