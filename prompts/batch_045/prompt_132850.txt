Here is the passage I am stuck on, from a letter he wrote in 1646 to the Marquess of Newcastle:

In fact, none of our external actions can show anyone who examines them that our body is not just a self-moving machine but contains a soul with thoughts, with the exception of words, or other signs that are relevant to particular topics without expressing any passion. I say words or other signs, because deaf-mutes use signs as we use spoken words; and I say that these signs must be relevant, to exclude the speech of parrots, without excluding the speech of madmen, which is relevant to particular topics even though it does not follow reason. I add also that these words or signs must not express any passion, to rule out not only cries of joy or sadness and the like, but also whatever can be taught by training to animals. If you teach a magpie to say good-day to its mistress, when it sees her approach, this can only be by making the utterance of this word the expression of one of its passions. For instance it will be an expression of the hope of eating, if it has always been given a tidbit when it says it.

And later in the same letter:

Montaigne and Charron may have said that there is more difference between one human being and another than between a human being and an animal; but there has never been known an animal so perfect as to use a sign to make other animals understand something which expressed no passion; and there is no human being so imperfect as not to do so, since even deaf-mutes invent special signs to express their thoughts.

I have tried to restate his test in my own terms, and I think it is really three separate requirements that he has compressed into one sentence. Call them: (A) topicality — the sign has to be about something, answerable to a subject matter rather than just noise; (B) detachment — the sign has to come free of the creature's own current motivational state, so it can't just be the outward shape of hope, fear, or joy; (C) untrainability — the response can't be something you could drum into the animal by reward and repetition. My first question is which of these three is actually doing the work against the animals, and whether he has any argument for (C) at all, since "whatever can be taught by training to animals" seems to be asserted rather than shown.

My second worry is about what I'll call the labeling bird, a case I made up. Suppose a bird is reinforced until it reliably makes one noise in front of objects of a kind it has never been reinforced on and a different noise in front of another such kind, so that its calls track a distinction it was never trained on, and it keeps doing this with wholly novel objects. It is not expressing a passion, and its noise is (in my sense A) about the object. Does it pass? I think Descartes has to say no, but I can't tell whether he says no because of (C) — it was after all shaped by training — or because he would deny that tracking a distinction is the same as relevance to a topic. If it's the latter, I don't see how the test could ever be applied from outside, which brings me to the thing that bothers me most.

He introduces the test as something observable: none of our external actions can show anyone who examines them that we are not machines. But "relevant to particular topics" doesn't look observable to me. To hear a noise as being about something, you already have to be interpreting the creature as saying something, which is exactly what is in question. So: is the test meant as an empirical criterion at all, or is it a metaphysical claim with an illustration attached? And relatedly, the test is supposed to run in both directions — no animal so perfect, no human so imperfect. But is it actually doing the work? I assume he wouldn't conclude that a person with a severe degenerative illness who can now only moan has thereby lost her mind, and if the test gives the wrong answer there and he overrides it, the test looks like evidence he likes rather than the basis of his view.

Two smaller things. It can even be used to prove they act naturally and mechanically, like a clock which tells the time better than our judgement does. That inference — excelling is evidence of mechanism rather than of mind — feels inverted to me, and I want to know whether it is a throwaway or whether he needs it. Second, near the end he entertains the objection that since their organs aren't so different from ours, The most that one can say is that though the animals do not perform any action which shows us that they think, still, since the organs of their body are not very different from ours, it may be conjectured that there is attached to those organs some thoughts such as we experience in ourselves, but of a very much less perfect kind. His reply, as far as I can follow it, is that if animals thought as we do they would have immortal souls like ours, which he finds implausible.
