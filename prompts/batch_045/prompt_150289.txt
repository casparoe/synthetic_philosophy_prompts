Write a dialogue between two philosophers, Nadia and Yuri, who agree about what there is reason to do in every ordinary case and disagree only about the following: Nadia holds that favouring is irreducibly four-place — a consideration favours a response *for* someone, in some circumstances — so that every normative reason is addressed to an agent and nothing normative floats loose in the world; Yuri holds that favouring is at bottom two-place, holding between a consideration and a response or outcome, so that free-floating rationales — considerations that favour something while being no one's reason — are the basic case, and an agent's reasons are simply the rationales she is positioned to take up.

Neither should be a caricature. Nadia must grant that there are unaddressed normative facts: that some states of the world are better than others, that some things merit relief or regret, even when no one can do anything about them — and she must account for those without counting them as reasons. Yuri must say what distinguishes a free-floating rationale from a mere value fact or from an ordinary explanatory reason, and must say what work being positioned to take up a rationale is really doing.

Have them work through three cases, each replying to what the other has actually argued rather than restating a position:

1. An unreceived reason: a consideration that plainly favours an agent's doing something but is in principle inaccessible to her, so that it cannot figure in her deliberation. Is this a reason of hers that goes unreceived, or a free-floating rationale that generates no reason for her at all? If the latter, has Yuri's view quietly become a view on which reasons are hostage to evidence?

2. An unaffectable state of affairs: something worth bringing about that no agent could ever bring about or respond to — a sealed-off region of the universe, an outcome obtaining after the last mind is gone. Let Nadia try her counterfactual paraphrase, on which there would have been a reason only if someone could have been in a position to respond, and let Yuri press on whether that paraphrase survives cases whose antecedents are impossible or indeterminate.

3. A second-order rationale: a consideration that favours *someone's having a reason* — that a woman be moved, as she should be, by a fact about her friend's predicament — where the favoured item is itself a piece of addressed normativity. Must such a rationale be addressed in turn, and to whom?

End with the two of them locating as precisely as they can the point at which their agreement about every first-order case stops settling anything.
