I am a novelist, and for two years I have been working on a book narrated entirely from inside the head of a man who, as a treatment for intractable epilepsy, has had the great bundle of fibres between his cerebral hemispheres surgically sectioned. I began the project assuming that philosophy would settle an elementary question of craft for me: does this book have one narrator or two? I rather wanted it to have two. What I have ended up with is an argument which seems to show that the question is not difficult but empty — and empty in my own case as much as in his. I cannot accept the conclusion and I cannot find the damage.

(1) Experiences belong to a single subject only if they are co-conscious, and there is nothing more to there being one subject than the holding of that relation: a subject is not an extra ingredient in the world, over and above the experiences and their relations, which could be added or subtracted while the experiences themselves stayed exactly as they were.

(2) Co-consciousness is to be understood as availability: an experience x is co-conscious with an experience y just in case the subject of x can bring x to bear on what the subject of y believes, decides, attends to, and says.

(3) Availability is a matter of degree and admits of borderline cases; there is no point at which it sharply ceases.

(4) In the standard sectioned cases, what is presented to one hemisphere is unavailable to the other: the patient sincerely denies seeing the thing his left hand is even now reaching for, and cannot say why he is reaching for it, though he will confabulate a reason readily enough.

(5) So, by (1), (2) and (4), such a patient is not one subject but two.

(6) But the very same unavailability obtains, more quietly and far more pervasively, in anyone at all. The subpersonal processes out of which my syntax arrives are not available to my deliberation; the sense that a scene wants to go somewhere comes to me without having consulted me; my hands at the keyboard know things I cannot state; the memory out of which a paragraph is made is not one I can summon and question. On (2), each of these lies outside the stream I call mine.

(7) So, by (1), (2) and (6), each of them is a subject in its own right, and my I is at best the largest of several and at worst a name for a shoreline.

(8) And by (3), there is no determinate number of subjects in any head at any moment — not in my narrator's, not in mine, not in the one the law proposes to hold responsible.

(9) So the question I sat down to answer has no answer. Neither has the question of who, if anyone, is answerable for what my narrator did before the surgery, and neither has the assumption on which every first-person novel quietly rests: that the one who undergoes Chapter Four is the one who will remember it in Chapter Eleven.

I will not give up (1). I do not believe there is a further fact, a residue of the sort the Cartesians wanted, that could make two streams into one. And (2) is the only reading of co-consciousness that I have managed to make concrete enough to be worth anything; the alternatives I have met strike me as posits with no behavioural signature whatever. Yet (9) is false, and false in a way I am better placed to know than I am to know most things — and, which matters more to the book, if (9) were true then the book could not be written from inside anyone.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?

If the break is between (4) and (5), I should like to know exactly what the behavioural evidence fails to establish. Does it show two centres of access, report and control rather than two streams of experience? And is the difference between those two findings the sort of thing that any evidence from the clinic could in principle close, or does the argument demand a finding that no observation of a patient could deliver?

If the break is at (6), I want the principled difference between the patient's islands and mine, stated without simply pointing at the surgery: what makes his division a division of subjects and my division a division within one?

If the break is at (2), tell me what relation is to do the work in its place, how anyone — the subject, a clinician, a novelist — could ever tell whether it holds, and whether a relation with no signature is more than a stipulation. And since availability is neither symmetric nor transitive, tell me whether an account of unity built on it can yield a relation that behaves as being-one-subject must behave.

If you think the argument is sound and the federation real, tell me what becomes of the first person, of memory, and of blame, and tell me honestly whether you would still write the book from inside him.

Finally, price the alternatives against one another. I would rather know which combination of losses is cheapest than which single premise you happen to find most suspicious.
