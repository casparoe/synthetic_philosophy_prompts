Hi — I work in a sort of tech-policy adjacent role (I write internal guidance on what data practices we're allowed to ship) and I've been trying to write down what I actually think about privacy so my team can push back on it before I inflict it on anyone. Rough position:

1. Privacy is basically about access. You've lost privacy when someone gains acess to you or to information about you. I don't think there's some single essence to be found and I don't want any of that "loss of self" mysticism — it's just access, in a lot of different forms.

2. The value of it is entirely instrumental. What matters is what access lets people do to you: manipulate, discriminate, chill, coerce. If a loss of access has genuinely no downstream effects at all, then nothing of value has been lost.

3. But I also think the thing really worth protecting is the integrity of social contexts. The same fact about you is completely fine among your friends and a real violation if it turns up in a courtroom, an insurance database, or an ad exchange. That difference feels like the heart of it to me. (I think there's a name for this view, something like "contextual integrity"?)

4. And consent is what makes a flow legitimate: if you knowingly and voluntarily agreed to the collection, then whatever happens downstream, nobody has violated your privacy.

5. The "nothing to hide" argument is just bad. "If you've done nothing wrong, you've got nothing to fear from surveillance" — I think that's a plain mistake, not a matter of degree.

6. I also want to say that the undetected observer case is a real violation: someone reads your diary or watches you through a window, you never find out, and nothing ever comes of it. I still want to say you were wronged. I'm less sure I'm entitled to this one than to the others.

7. Methodologically, two things: I want to start from the ordinary concept and trust intuitions about cases, and I want the whole thing to be usable — to actually tell us which specific practices to prohibit and which to allow.

So: does this hang together? Which of these really fight each other, which only look like they do, and for each genuine conflict, what's the cheapest thing to give up and what does giving it up cost me? Please rank the tensions by how bad they are rather than flagging all of them equally, and say if there's one revision that cleans up the most at once. I'm not attached to any particular number surviving — I'm attached to not getting destroyed in a meeting.
