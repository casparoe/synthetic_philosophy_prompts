Two colleagues of mine at a policy institute were asked to co-draft a memo on whether the government should expand its child-benefit scheme, and they spent an hour arguing past each other about population ethics instead. I sat in on the whole exchange and genuinely cannot tell who got the better of it. Here is how it went, as close to verbatim as I can manage.

**(1) Nadia:** If the additional children would have good lives, a larger population is better, other things being equal. Ten billion people each at a high level of well-being is a better world than one billion at exactly that same level — there is more of what makes lives go well, and nobody is worse off. So the state has at least some pro tanto reason to make births more likely, assuming the resulting lives clear some adequacy threshold.

**(2) Tomas:** You cannot spell that out as a benefit to the added people. Benefiting someone requires making them better off than they would otherwise have been, and there is no "otherwise" for someone who would never have existed. If you insist that never existing is bad for them, you owe pity to the merely possible and you are obliged to produce as many people as possible. The defensible half of your intuition is the asymmetry: we have a strong reason not to bring someone into existence at a miserable level, but no symmetrical reason to bring someone in at a happy one. That is a reason of the right kind, and your "more well-being" is not.

**(3) Nadia:** Fine — I will drop the claim that the extra people are benefited. My claim is about impersonal value: not all value has to be value *for* someone. And your asymmetry has a cost you have not faced. If adding good lives is never a reason, then the painless, voluntary fading out of the human race would not be bad in itself, which is not a conclusion, it is a stipulation. Worse for the memo: a principle that only ever ranks outcomes containing the very same people is silent on the only question we were asked.

**(4) Tomas:** I can regret that ending for the sake of the people who live through it and for what they lose — culture, knowledge, company — without thinking the headcount matters. Silence is not error. Your impersonal view, by contrast, has a verdict, and the verdict is intolerable: as Parfit argued, for any population of at least ten billion people all with a very high quality of life, there is some much larger population whose existence would be better, even though its members have lives barely worth living. You cannot defuse this by saying intuition fails at large numbers, because the comparison is one your own theory says is the decisive one.

**(5) Nadia:** Two things. First, the repugnance may be an artifact of presentation — people hear "barely worth living" as "miserable," and once that is corrected some philosophers simply accept the result. Second, and more important: you have misdescribed what our instrument actually does. Demographers distinguish shifts in the *timing* of births from changes in *completed* family size, and for cash incentives a large share of the measured response is plausibly timing rather than extra children. But timing changes *which* children are born. So the live case is often not "more people" but "different people": same numbers, different individuals, all with lives worth living. On your framework that whole class of decisions is morally inert — the displaced possible child is nobody, and the actual child is not harmed. A view that cannot distinguish selecting better-off future people from selecting worse-off ones has stopped doing any work.

**(6) Tomas:** Identity choice is tractable without impersonal value. In a "who, not how many" decision there will be *some* occupant of the relevant slot, and I can act as a trustee for whoever that turns out to be: rank the options by the expected well-being of the eventual occupant, weighting candidates by how likely each is to exist under each policy. That is a person-affecting principle with widened scope — grounded in the interests of a person who will exist, with no appeal to the badness of never existing and no impersonal addition. Nadia closed by asking whether, in the fixed-number case, maximizing expected welfare of slot-occupants isn't just total-welfare reasoning relabeled, and whether a trustee can deliberate at all with no determinate beneficiary in view at the time of choice. They left it there.

Please referee this properly rather than telling me whom to agree with:

- Go through moves (1) through (6) in order and rate each from *clearly mistaken* to *clearly right*, with a brief justification for each rating — distinguishing, where it matters, between a move's being valid, its premises being true, and its actually answering the objection it was meant to answer.
- Identify any point at which one of them mischaracterizes the other's position or concedes more than they needed to.
- Say what both parties are missing: which distinction, option, or argument would have moved the disagreement forward instead of around it, and what the strongest version of each side looks like once the weak moves are stripped away.
- Finally, indicate where, if anywhere, the empirical claim in (5) about birth timing does real work in the argument, and whether the argument would stand without it.
