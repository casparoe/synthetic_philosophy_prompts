I'm teaching Arthur Miller's The Crucible to a senior elective next term — half literature, half ethics — and I want to do a session on standing to blame rather then the usual "is it really about McCarthyism" thing. So: read the play through the standing debate, but use a distinction set I've been messing around with instead of just rehearsing the standard hypocrite-lacks-standing vs. tu-quoque-is-a-fallacy back and forth.

My taxonomy. Say a blamer's standing can be undercut in four different ways, and the interesting question is which one the play actually cares about:

1. content-match — you blame someone for exactly what you do yourself (the adulterer denouncing adultery).
2. role-violation — you invoke a norm in an official capacity while violating it in that same capacity (the judge who takes bribes sentencing a thief).
3. laundered — the standing isn't yours at all, it belongs to an office or institution you're speaking for, so your private record doesn't deflate it.
4. imputed — standing tracks what the community believes about you rather than what you actually did, so it can be destroyed by a false confession you make about yourself, and it can be refused to someone whose hands are completely clean.

A couple of things I want you to work through.

Act 3, where Proctor confesses his own adultery in open court in order to discredit Abigail and save Elizabeth: on my taxonomy that looks like him volunteering a content-match defect in himself to break someone else's standing, and it fails. Why does it fail? Is it that he destroyed his own standing in the act of accusing, or is it that Elizabeth then lies to protect him and the court takes her silence as the refutation? And what does it mean that the one person with obvious standing there — Elizabeth, the actual wronged party — refuses to use it?

Act 4 is the one I keep coming back to. Danforth wants Proctor's confession posted on the church door, and Proctor won't sign, and his reasoning is basically that once his name is on that document the town will say even Proctor confessed, and Rebecca Nurse and the others will hang as admitted witches. Read that as a claim about standing: he'd be lending his good name to the court so it can launder a verdict, and the thing he's protecting isn't his pride, it's a spendable reputational resource that the remaining prisoners still need. Does that reading hold up, or does the play make it more personal than that?

Then the harder structural question. Rebecca Nurse has, on any blamer-centred account, perfect standing and gets no uptake whatsoever. Abigail has grotesque content-match hypocrisy and gets total uptake for most of the play. Danforth speaks from an office, which on my third category should make his personal failings irrelevant, and yet the play clearly wants us to think his authority has rotted. So is the play quietly defending a relational or triangular picture — standing as a feature of blamer plus blamed plus whoever is listening — rather than a property the blamer either has or lacks? And is uptake a separate thing from standing that the philosophical literature mostly forgets to distinguish, or is the distinction a muddle?

Where does this break? Be honest: Hale walking out of the court looks more like a crisis of epistemic authority than of standing, and Proctor's final refusal might just be about integrity and his name in the ordinary sense, not about any standing to blame anyone. If you think my four-way split is doing no real work on the play, say so and tell me which scene shows it up.

End with what you think the play adds that the arguments alone don't — my hunch is that it shows standing to be a social resource you can spend, borrow, launder or have confiscated, rather than a moral status you either merit or don't, and I want to know if that hunch is worth putting in front of 17-year-olds or if it's too clever by half.
