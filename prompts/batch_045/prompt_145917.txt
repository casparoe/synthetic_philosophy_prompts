i'm putting together one of those "where i actually stand" documents for my philosophy of mind tutorial — my tutor's rule is that you have to write down your own commitments before you're allowed to argue with anyone else's — and i got into an argument with a friend who does neuroscience about split-brain cases where she said i was definately helping myself to something somewhere and then refused to say where. so now i'm suspicious of my own list. everything on it feels fine to me individually, which is probably the problem. here it is:

(a) normally one human being = one conscious subject, and normal experience is unified: there's something it's like to be me as a whole, not just something it's like to be various seperate bits of me. (b) in the classic setup where the main fibres between the hemispheres have been cut in an adult to treat epilepsy, and the experimenter flashes something to only one side so that the left hemisphere can't say what it was while the left hand can pick out the matching object — there are two subjects there, two streams of experience, not one subject with an unusual information-flow problem. (c) but only in that setup: in ordinary life i think they're one subject again, because both hemispheres are looking at the same room, hearing the same conversation, acting with the same body, and the person talks and behaves like one person. the twoness is a lab artefact. (d) methodologically i'm report-first: i don't want to hand out consciousness on the basis of a theory, and when someone sincerely tells me what their experience is like, that's where the enquiry has to start and mostly where it ends. (e) at the same time, the verbal reports of these patients are contaminated by the speaking hemisphere's habit of inventing stories for things the other hemisphere did — it confabulates — so i put no weight on their saying that nothing feels different since the operation. (f) my account of what unity consists in: experiences belong to one subject when they're available to the same downstream consumer systems, broadcast to the same workspace that feeds memory, planning, speech and action selection. one workspace, one subject; two workspaces, two subjects. (g) case verdict: an adult who has an entire hemisphere removed is still one subject, and what's lost is a faculty rather than a subject — nothing has been killed, it isn't like a death — and it's still the same person, legally and biographically, before and after. (h) i think a mental state is conscious only if the subject can in some way attend to it or act on it, so i don't want to call merely sub-personal processing conscious; and i think the states of the non-speaking hemisphere in these patients are conscious even though it can't talk, since it can attend to its own side of the world and guide a hand. (i) children born without the main band of connecting fibres between the hemispheres at all: one subject, obviously — i'm not going to say there are two people in there.

what i want is an audit, not a lecture: which of these genuinely pull against each other, ranked worst first, and for each one exactly what i'd have to give up and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere on the list. i'd also like to be told if any of the apparent clashes are fake, since a list on which everything is in tension with everything is useless to me — if two of them are fine together i'd rather hear why than have them flagged. if one of my case verdicts is something my own principle can't actually deliver, say which one and how. and if there's a single revision that cleans up the most, tell me what it is.
