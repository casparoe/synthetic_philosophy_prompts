I'm retired now — forty-odd years of insect orientation behaviour, most of it spent modelling rather than collecting, so my working vocabulary is control theory, and I've only been poking aorund at philosophy for a couple of years. Which is why I want a translation rather than an explanation.

The thing I keep coming back to is sphexishness. You know the story: the digger wasp paralyses a cricket, drags it to the nest entrance, leaves it on the threshold, goes in to inspect, comes back out and hauls it in; if you shift the cricket a few inches while she's inside she doesn't simply carry on, she runs the whole routine again, apparently without limit. Then the word gets coined in that enormous book about Gödel and Bach and it becomes the emblem of mechanical rigidity, with the moral that a creature running a fixed programme isn't free, and that we all have our sphexish moments. Two preliminaries. First: versions of the anecdote differ in the details, and I've a nagging feeling somebody went back through the literature and found almost nothing there — one observer's tale repeated for a century rather than a proper experiment. I may be misremembering that, so if you can't confirm it from memory, just take the behaviour as a hypothetical. I don't want references, you can't look them up anyway. Second: it's the argument I'm after, not the natural history.

So, four things.

First, the main job. Restate the philosophical use of sphexishness in the vocabulary I use daily: open-loop against closed-loop, error signals and setpoints, gain, state estimation, sampling rates, dead reckoning, hysteresis, mode switching, subsumption, robustness against adaptivity, the plant/controller split. Include both directions the property gets used in — rigidity as the signature of the mechanical and as the mark of something unfree, but also the opposite claim that a creature who could genuinely do otherwise at every instant would be useless, so that a bit of rigidity is what agency needs. Be faithful, and don't smuggle: flag anywhere you're helping yourself to a control-theoretic assumption rather than translating.

Second, a distinction I think the standard reading gets wrong. A controller can fail to correct for a perturbation for at least three very different reasons: it isn't sampling at all; it samples, but the trigger is badly conditioned, so it re-runs a subroutine instead of updating; or it updates a variable that isn't the one your intervention changed. The usual telling reads the wasp as the first kind. I suspect she's the third — she plainly does re-check, so the interesting fact is that the check doesn't feed back into the right internal variable. If that's right, a lot of philosophical weight is being carried by a misdiagnosis of the control architecture. How much?

Third: control vocabulary already assumes goals, error and correction. If you render acting for a reason into that vocabulary, haven't you taken purpose for free? Tell me where that's a cheat and where it's fair.

Fourth: I don't think flexibility is one thing. Robustness, retuning of parameters, swapping the control law, meta-level switching between controllers — those come apart, and plenty of machines beat us on some and lose on others. When philosophers lean on flexibility as though it were a single scalar tracking freedom, which of those do they mean, and what happens to the argument if it's several?

Then the residue report, which is the bit I actually care about: which claims translate cleanly, which only by being bent, and which have no expression in my vocabulary at all — I'm thinking of the stuff about being able to do otherwise, responding to reasons as opposed to merely being caused by them, being the source of the action, and blame. For each bit of residue say whether it's something my vocabulary is too poor to express or something the philosophers haven't managed to say clearly either. And argue with my hunch: that sphexishness is a fact about a sampling architecture being read as a fact about freedom — a category mistake with a real puzzle buried somewhere inside it. Tell me if that's right, or if the category mistake is mine.
