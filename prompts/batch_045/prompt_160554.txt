I want to know how exposed a position I have ended up holding really is.

The position is a fairly specific version of illusionism about phenomenal consciousness. There are no phenomenal properties: no properties of the sort that the four canonical marks are supposed to pick out — the ones you are told to point to when you are told that there is something it is like for you. What exists are sensory, functional, and intentional states, plus an introspective system that misrepresents some of those states as possessing phenomenal character. The variant I hold adds one further claim: the misrepresentation is itself cold. The state that carries the content that one undergoes something it is like is a merely representational state with no phenomenal character of its own. The illusion is not something it is like to undergo, and there is no phenomenal residue anywhere in the story, including at the level of the illusion itself. I arrived at this the way a lot of people in my corner of the internet did, through the old quining arguments on the blogs, and I have not been able to see a way out of it since.

So: what would count against it? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want them sorted by whether a committed illusionist would concede them as counting against the view or dismiss them, with some account of whether the dismissals are principled. A few things I am specifically unsure about:

One. The self-undermining charge: an illusion that there is something it is like seems to have to be undergone, which seems to require the very thing denied. My cold variant is supposed to block this by saying the illusion is a misrepresentation rather than a way things seem in the phenomenal sense. Does that block actually hold, or does cold illusionism just relocate the problem — into the notion of a misrepresentation whose content is fixed by nothing but its causal role, or into the question of why a state with that content should produce belief rather than indifference?

Two. The specificity problem, which worries me more than the standard objections do. Our reports of phenomenal character are not a generic haze; they are fine-grained, and their fine grain tracks the sensory states they are about. A generic introspective error would not produce that pattern. So illusionism owes a story about how the misrepresentation gets its detailed content. My suspicion is that there are only two ways to give that story, and that both are bad for me: either it is told in phenomenal vocabulary, in which case the view is incoherent, or it is told in purely functional vocabulary, in which case the view has collapsed into its main rival, a realism that identifies phenomenal character with the functional profile and deflates everything else. Is that a fair dilemma, and is there a third option I am not seeing?

Three. A related regress: whatever I say about introspection misrepresenting first-order sensory states, it seems I should be able to introspect the introspective state, and then I owe the same story again. Does the illusionist need an endless tower of misrepresentations, or is there a principled place to stop — and does the stopping place cost anything?

Four. Whether the view risks anything at all. Since any first-person report is explained away as the output of the illusion, it can look as though nothing could disconfirm it. Here is the test I keep turning over: find cases where a subject's discrimination performance and their introspective access to the same sensory state come apart, and see whether verbal reports about character track the state of the introspective machinery or the state of the sensory system. My thought is that reports tracking the machinery supports illusionism, and reports tracking the sensory system in a way that the machinery's representational content does not predict would count against it. Is that a real test? Is it decisive if it comes out the wrong way for me, or has illusionism already insulated itself against it by construction? And if it has insulated itself, is that an objection to a view of this kind, or is it on a par with other error theories that are unfalsifiable by design and still worth holding? I would also like to know what my view predicts that its rivals do not, apart from the obvious negative prediction.

I should say where I am most and least movable: I would be moved by an argument that the view collapses into a rival, or that it is strictly incoherent; I am considerably less moved by bare insistence that we just know we are conscious. I am not after references or a reading list, just the map of what this position risks and where it is safe.
