hey — I'm a PhD student in public policy (political theory adjacent) and I'm stuck on a chapter I'm writing about governments that govern by wellbeing indicators, so I thought I'd try asking here.

The setup, in case it helps: the World Happiness Report puts out an annual league table of countries based mostly on a single survey question where people rate their current life on a 0-to-10 ladder, with the top rung defined as the best life they can imagine for themselves. Nordic countries sit near the top most years. The same reports also collect affect-type measures — whether you smiled or laughed yesterday, whether you felt worry, anger, sadness — and from what I remember the ordering looks pretty different if you build a table out of that stuff instead: some Latin American countries do much better on positive affect than on the ladder, some European ones the reverse. And a bunch of governments now treat the table as a target: wellbeing budgets, cabinet-level responsibility for happiness, goals stated in terms of the country's *position* rather than its score.

The case I'm actually writing about is one I've partly made up, because I want the incentives sharp. Suppose a country writes into law a commitment to move from roughly 20th to the top five within ten years, and ties the budget of the office responsible to whether the rank goes up or down — no condition at all on the underlying average score. The office gets more money if the rank improves and gets cut if it falls, whatever happens to how people actually rate their lives.

I keep going back and forth about what this practice presupposes, and I'd like a real reconstruction rather than just "Goodhart's law, the end". Concretely:

- What theory of wellbeing does it have to be committed to? It seems to need: that wellbeing is a single, unidimensional quantity; that it's consciously accessible to the person, so a reflective judgement about your life as a whole is evidence about it; that self-reports from different cultures are commensurable enough to put in one table; and maybe that adaptive preferences (people who've lowered their expectations reporting high satisfaction) don't wreck the whole thing. Which of those does it *strictly* need, and which is it merely convenient with?
- What does it presuppose about the unit? The country is ranked, the country is the thing that gets credit, and — in the real-world versions — the government is the thing that gets the credit. Does that require believing that a nation is some kind of bearer or agent of wellbeing, or is aggregation just bookkeeping? And what does it rule out, e.g. about caring about the bottom of the distribution?
- The ranking is relative and ordinal, so everyone can't move up, and a country can rise because its neighbours got worse — a financial crisis next door, a war, whatever. So: does rank-targeting actually presuppose a positional or comparative view of wellbeing, where how you stand relative to others is part of the good? Or can a purely non-positional view explain it as a cheap accountability device, and if so does the reward-for-others'-misery case show the device has broken or just that it was always a proxy?
- The inconsistency that bugs me: the reports and the governments both talk about misery, mental distress, and leaving nobody behind, and both collect affect measures — but the target is a mean-based, evaluation-based, country-level rank, which is blind to the bottom tail and (apparently) tracks something different from how people's days actually feel. Is that a real contradiction in the practice, or do the parts just presuppose different things that happen to sit next to each other?

And the bit I most need: tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — which rival theories of wellbeing would license it anyway — and then say what would concretely have to change in the measurement and the incentive if you swapped the presupposed view for (a) an experienced/affect-based view, (b) something distribution-sensitive that gives priority to the worst off, (c) an objective-list or capabilities view, and (d) a view on which self-reports just aren't comparable across cultures (I've heard of anchoring vignettes as a fix — is that the right kind of move?). Rank those options by how much of the existing practice survives the swap, and flag anywhere I'm just confused.
