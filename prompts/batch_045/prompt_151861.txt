I am the convenor of a two-year funded adversarial collaboration on confirmation theory, and I now have to publish its mid-term joint statement. The two investigators have argued past each other for eighteen months and will not write it themselves.

Party A is a Bayesian who holds that the machinery needs repair rather than replacement: on her view, when a theory is shown to entail an already-known anomaly, what the inquirer newly learns is not the anomaly but a connection between the theory and the anomaly in an enlarged language, and the confirmation flows through that newly learned connection rather than through the anomaly itself. Party B holds that this repair works only by helping itself to exactly the independence that was supposed to be at issue, and that what actually carries these episodes is explanatory unification: the new theory derives the anomaly instead of bolting on an auxiliary device, and that is not a probabilistic relation between propositions at all.

They keep returning to one case: an anomaly in a planet's orbit had sat in the published tables for decades and was generally attributed to an unseen perturbing body; a new theory of gravitation entailed precisely that deviation, and its supporters counted it as the theory's strongest support. Both agree the puzzle this raises is real and is not dissolved by pointing out that scientists plainly do get confidence boosts.

To stop them conflating things, I have imposed a four-way vocabulary, which both have accepted and which I want used throughout the statement: acquaintance (the anomaly was already in the inquirer's information set when the theory arrived); use (the anomaly was among the constraints consulted in constructing the theory); prior explanation (before the theory arrived the anomaly already had an accepted explanation, perhaps by a rival theory or by an auxiliary hypothesis); replacement (the new theory explains the anomaly only by discarding or revising something previously accepted). Their fighting has mostly come from treating acquaintance as if it entailed use.

Please draft the statement. Structure it as follows.

Section one: agreed claims, numbered, one sentence each, ten at most, each non-vacuous and each worded so that both parties could sign it. Include a formulation of what the old-evidence puzzle is that both accept, and an explicit statement about whether confirmation theory is in the business of describing a relation between propositions or changes in an agent's credal state, since I suspect one crux hides there.

Section two: a table of residual disagreements. Columns: the crux; Party A's position in wording she would accept; Party B's position in wording he would accept; the case or argument both would accept as bearing on that crux; Party A's prediction about what it will show, recorded in advance; Party B's prediction, likewise. For the bearing cases use the three I have specified: alpha, where the anomaly was acquainted but unused and had no prior explanation; beta, where it was acquainted and used in construction; gamma, where it was acquainted, unused, and already explained by a rival theory that the new theory also explains but with different commitments. For each case both parties should say whether confirmation occurs on their account, how it compares in strength to a genuinely novel prediction, and which quantity in their framework tracks it.

Section three: one paragraph on which parts of the dispute are merely verbal and why, and one paragraph on what both would count as genuine progress, naming anything both agree would move the debate even if neither expects it to.

Constraints: no citations, no author names, no footnotes; the audience is the two investigators and me. Neither side may be allowed to win by stipulation: where a party's formulation does real work only because of how a term is defined, flag it in the verbal-dispute paragraph. Total length under nine hundred words, table included.
