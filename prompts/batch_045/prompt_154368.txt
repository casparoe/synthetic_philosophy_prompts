I write novels — close third, past tense, the usual — and I've been reading Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments in a rented cabin with one battered paperback and no library, so please don't send me off to secondary literature or hand me a reading list. What I actually want is help figuring out what I think about sympathy, since I spend my working life manufacturing it and I have alot of strong opinions about it that don't obviously add up.

Here are my verdicts, gut-level, from drafting and from reader mail.

1. One sock left in the drawer undoes readers. A paragraph about two hundred people dead does almost nothing. I exploit this deliberately and feel slightly bad about it.
2. The scene where the character says nothing at all at the worst moment gets the strongest response in the book. So the biggest payoff comes where there's apparently no passion on the page to feel with.
3. Three pages inside a genuinely bad man's head while he feels remorse, and readers are entirely with him. I think that's fine. I don't think it corrupts anybody.
4. A character who wrecks her own life through laziness and small lies: readers go cold. Roughly the same quantity of suffering as 2, opposite response.
5. A character who narrates her own grief for six pages: readers bail. I file that as a craft failure, not a moral one — but note it's the same suffering as 2 again.
6. My most reliable tool is second-hand: a minor character noticing the protagonist's hands shaking gets more tears than any direct description of what the protagonist is feeling. Which is weird, because it means I'm getting readers to feel by showing somebody else feeling.
7. Death scenes. The dead feel nothing and the reader feels the most. I tell myself the grief is really for the people in the room, but I'm not convinced that's the whole of it.
8. Dramatic irony — reader knows the letter is a rejection, character is delighted — produces something that isn't pity and isn't fear. I'd call it dread. There's no passion onstage to enter into at all.
9. Just writing "she was heartbroken" kills it dead. Sympathy seems to need the reader to do the imagining themselves.
10. Big joy is thin and embarrassing to write; tiny joy lands fine — getting the window seat, the train leaving without her. Which I think cuts against the thing people always say about Smith, that we enter into cheerful passions more readily.
11. Second person: half my readers feel it intensely, half feel handled. Exact same sentences.
12. Twelve grieving people in a room and I feel less per person than I would for one. That seems backwards if sympathy is supposed to feed on itself.

And the one I keep flip-flopping on: the wife who's being made a fool of and never finds out. I want readers to feel for her, I can't tell whether what I'm producing is fellow-feeling or something nastier, and I can't decide whether it's okay to write it.

My own stab at a principle, three clauses: (a) readers feel in proportion to how much imagining the page makes them do — nothing to do with the size of the suffering, and telling them what to feel is fatal; (b) quite separately, readers warm or cool depending on whether the passion fits its cause and whether the character seems to have deserved it, and this runs independently of (a); (c) the spectator isn't impartial at all, it's a guy I built and stood in the room, usually as literally as possible.

Test that against the twelve, tell me which clause breaks and repair it. If it turns out I need two principles — one for feeling-with and one for approval — say so and say exactly where they come apart. Tell me which of my verdicts the repaired version forces me to drop, and be honest if it's more than one. And say where Smith's own kit — sympathy, propriety, the man within the breast — already covers this and where fiction needs something added to it.

Then give me its verdicts on cases I haven't listed: a narrator who speaks after her own death; a chapter told from a dog's point of view; a chapter told from a dog's point of view; an omniscient chapter that withholds the protagonist's inner state entirely; and a book where you're told the ending in the first sentence.
