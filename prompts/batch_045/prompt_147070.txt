Dear colleague,

I am finishing a short methods chapter for a monograph on famine and epidemic in the nineteenth century, and before I commit myself to a position I want a specification rather than another round of case studies. What I need from you is the list of requirements that any account of non-human agency in history would have to meet in order to be usable by me: by a historian who has to decide, sentence by sentence, whether to write that a pathogen, a drought, or an eruption caused something, enabled something, or acted. Please keep the purpose fixed at that. I do not want a theory good enough for a climate modeller, or one good enough for a court assigning responsibility, or one good enough for a metaphysician's seminar; I want one that disciplines causal prose in narrative history when the evidence I actually have is a broken run of harvest returns, parish burial series, and administrative correspondence that was written by people with their own reasons for blaming the weather.

My half-formed idea, which I would like you either to turn into criteria or to dissolve if it does not survive being made precise, is that the debate keeps stalling because people are using one word for three different grades of causal involvement. The lowest grade is a difference-maker relative to a contrast: the blight was present and the harvest failed, it was absent in a comparison case and the harvest did not, and nothing in the vicinity screens the two off. The middle grade is a condition that sets the range within which human agents choose, shaping which options are thinkable and how expensive each one is, without itself selecting among them. The top grade, the one people fight about, is something like actorhood, and my guess is that what distinguishes it is not the size of the causal contribution but a second-order structure: human agents orient their plans around the thing, take countermeasures against it, and it responds to those countermeasures in a way that is not simply a function of what they did, so that the counterfactual history has to be written as a sequence of moves and answers rather than as a single inserted cause. If that is right, then a volcano that nobody could have anticipated or prepared for is a very large cause and a very poor actor, while a cholera outbreak that a city met with quarantines, denials, and riots is a smaller cause and a much better candidate for actorhood, and the interesting cases are the ones where the two come apart.

Turn that into a requirements list, if it deserves to survive. The first requirement I would want you to test is contrast-sensitivity: whether any claim that a non-human thing acted has to be indexed to a contrast class, and what happens to the standard examples when we shift the contrast from the event versus nothing to the event versus the event as it would have gone under a different policy. The second is baseline discipline: the account must force me to state the background or endemic rate of the thing being invoked, so that I do not quietly credit the microbe with mortality that a population's nutritional state, housing, or season of the year had already made close to inevitable; the test case I have in mind is a famine where a blight arrives into an export economy that keeps shipping food, and I want to know whether any theory that cannot separate the blight's contribution from the policy's contribution is disqualified outright. The third is a channel requirement: the path from an eruption or a bacillus to a historical outcome always runs through institutions that record, ration, quarantine, or fail to, and I want a criterion for when the institution rather than the pathogen is the thing that should bear the agent's grammatical role. The fourth is grain matching: the temporal and spatial grain of the cause has to be answerable to the grain of the thing explained, so that a multidecadal cooling trend cannot be wheeled in to explain one bad harvest unless someone supplies the transmission story, and a single eruption cannot be wheeled in to explain a half century of state formation. The fifth is what you might call the archive effect: some non-human causes destroy, preserve, or generate the very sources a historian later uses, and any account should be required to say how much of the apparent causal prominence of such a cause is an artefact of what survived; a city buried in ash looks like a case of a volcano acting, partly because the ash also fixed the record in a way that ordinary years did not. The sixth is the one I care about most and trust least: a non-exculpation condition, requiring the theory to tell me when invoking a pathogen or a drought is warranted and when it is ideology that relieves human agents of the alternatives they actually had, ideally by asking whether any human counterfactual survives once the non-human cause is held fixed. The seventh is a symmetry check: whatever test is proposed should be run against a human corporate agent such as a treasury, a colonial administration, or an army, and I want to know which candidate tests return different verdicts for those cases and whether that is a defect or a feature. The eighth is an explanatory payoff condition: if calling something an actor lets me explain nothing that calling it a condition does not, then the vocabulary is doing rhetorical work only, and I want that stated as a requirement rather than left as a suspicion. The ninth is a question I cannot settle myself: whether actorhood should require that the intentional vocabulary be grounded in something real, since a pathogen has selection-driven dynamics that make some teleological talk defensible in a way that a volcano's does not, and if so whether that makes germs actors and volcanoes merely causes, or whether grounding is a prejudice of the scientifically minded camp.

Alongside the list, please give me your view on three things. Which of these requirements are genuine desiderata and which are the preferences of a camp disguised as standards, including the possibility that my non-exculpation condition is just a political preference and that my channel requirement is just a humanist prejudice. Whether any subset of the requirements you accept is jointly unsatisfiable, since if the list itself turns out to be inconsistent that is the finding I would most rather have than a tidy set of criteria. And which single requirement does the most work in excluding the positions currently on offer; a sentence or two on that is enough, because I know the literature and will do the scoring myself, so please do not write me a scorecard or a bibliography, and do not cite anything or name specific authors and works, as I will fill all of that in from my own files.

Finally, on form: write this as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter or an email, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables or lists of any kind, even where a table would be tempting. I would like roughly twelve hundred to fifteen hundred words, and I would rather have the reasoning behind each requirement and the case that motivates it than a long inventory of requirements I cannot adjudicate.
