Her version: the target isn't CEV-style extrapolation of one person and it isn't a single ideal agent — it's an ensemble of simulated humans who can consult copies of themselves, given unlimited time and memory but deliberately *not* given any factual corrections they didn't ask for (she calls this "epistemically modest" idealization), run until the verdict stops moving by more than epsilon, with that epsilon-stable verdict as the thing the AI is pointed at.

Write me ten to twelve questions, in an order that gives the episode an arc, that don't let her just restate the setup. At least one each on: path-dependence and multiple fixed points — same ensemble, different random seeds or a different order of subquestions, do you get the same epsilon-stable verdict, and if the stopping tolerance is doing moral work, what fixes epsilon; whether epistemically modest idealization is coherent at all, since an ensemble seeded with people who have big factual misconceptions converges somewhere parochial, and the moment you filter admissible seeds you've imported a standard from outside the procedure, so is this indirect normativity or direct normativity with extra steps; the aggregation rule when the ensemble still disagrees at the stopping point, because the vote/veto/weighting looks like it's doing more normative work than the deliberation; the gap between instantiating a process and matching its outputs, given nobody gets to run the real thing and we'd be distilling an approximation, and that the AI's own actions partly decide which inputs that approximation gets used on; and self-effacement — whether the epsilon-stable verdict has any reason to keep the humans or the procedure around, or whether she needs a "not terrible" side constraint that wrecks the purity of the indirect move. For each, give the follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to actually give, not the one she'd prefer to give. Close with one question about what result, armchair or empirical, would make her drop the ensemble framing entirely.
