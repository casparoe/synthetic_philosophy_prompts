I sit on a small internal committee — two computer scientists, an economist, and one philosopher — that decides how to spend a modest research fund, and we keep getting stuck on the same question: should we treat philosophy as a field that accumulates results, the way a mature science does, or as a field that mostly churns: better tools, sharper formulations, the same disputes? To force the argument into the open I want a number on the following claim, and I want yours rather than a survey of the literature.

C: Over the last hundred years or so, the apparent cumulative progress of mainstream analytic philosophy has been mostly negative in character — it consists largely in the attrition of positions, questions, and argument-patterns that specialists have stopped taking seriously, rather than in the establishment of substantive positive theses that a majority of specialists accept today and that would have surprised an informed philosopher in 1900.

I am at roughly 0.55 on C. A colleague is at 0.15, on the grounds that the field now knows a good deal it did not know then and that the churn is just the ordinary look of a hard discipline working. I would rather you decompose the question than adjudicate between us, and I have a provisional decomposition you may use or replace: (i) for a randomly chosen question that was a live controversy around 1900 and is now widely described as settled, how much of the settling was fading-out — generational turnover, shifts in what counts as a good explanation, changes in vocabulary that leave the old position barely restatable without sounding naive — as opposed to refutation by a decisive argument; (ii) whether there are in fact substantive positive results, excluding anything that is really a contribution to logic, linguistics, decision theory, or empirical science; (iii) whether stable majority consensus in philosophy tracks convergence on better arguments rather than shared training, fashion, or hiring and citation dynamics; (iv) whether the questions that stubbornly resist settlement share a common structural feature — underdetermination by any possible evidence, indexicality, dependence on a contested conceptual scheme — or resist for a scatter of unrelated local reasons; and (v) whether improvements in formal and argumentative technique make the field better at answering first-order questions at all, or only at producing more sophisticated restatements of the same disagreements. Add a term for whether progress even has a determinate meaning here, if you think one belongs.

Give me a number for each term with a single line of justification, then the combination and your bottom-line credence in C; state in one clause what observation would settle C for you. Then tell me which single term your bottom line is most sensitive to and what one piece of evidence — something you could actually check against the literature rather than a thought experiment — would move that term most; and say where you think my 0.55 and my colleague's 0.15 really disagree, meaning which term is doing nearly all the work. Please argue from the structure of philosophical disagreement rather than by citing or naming authors and works; I want the reasoning, not the bibliography. Keep the whole response under 500 words, with the numbers collected in a compact table and the prose around it kept to short paragraphs.
