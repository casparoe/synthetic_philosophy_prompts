I have been asked to circulate a one-page statement of my position ahead of a graduate seminar on scientific realism, and I would like the statement audited for coherence before it goes out. Assume a reader who already knows the standard moves in that debate: I am not asking for the debate to be explained back to me, for a taxonomy of the available isms, or for anything I would have to look up. I am asking whether the following set holds together.

(1) The miracle argument is sound but much narrower than it is usually stated. The only kind of empirical success that is evidence of truth is the prediction of something we did not already know and did not design the theory to fit. Accommodation of known phenomena, unifying power and consilience across domains are heuristically and aesthetically valuable and evidentially inert.

(2) The pessimistic induction has real force, but specifically against the constitutive claims of a mature theory: claims about what its posits are like, what they are made of, and by what mechanism they produce the phenomena. The standard lists of abandoned theories show claims of this kind being discarded at a rate that ought to worry anyone who holds them.

(3) It has no corresponding force against existential claims, because there is a second route to the existence of a posit that does not run through the truth of the theory that describes it. If we can build instruments that reliably use X as a tool to intervene on and produce phenomena involving other things, we are warranted in believing that X exists, whatever the eventual fate of the theory X came from.

(4) Nor does the induction generalize as far as it is usually taken to go. In the discarded cases, the posits that were actually doing the work in the derivations are retained by the successor theory; what gets dropped is idle machinery that contributed nothing to the successes. Restrict the count to posits that carry explanatory or predictive weight and the historical sample looks considerably better for the realist.

(5) The refinement I want to add, and which I think is where the real counting has to happen. When a theory is abandoned, three things can happen to one of its central posits, and I think they are routinely run together: (a) the term keeps referring while nearly everything the theory said about the referent is discarded; (b) the term turns out to refer to nothing, while many of the theory's equations, measurement routines and experimental techniques are carried over by the successor and continue to be used; (c) both the reference and the characterization survive substantially intact. My claim is that (b) is the largest cell in the historical sample, and that the pessimistic induction has the force commonly attributed to it only in virtue of that. I also suspect, though I am much less sure of this, that cells (a) and (b) are not distinguishable from the inside: at the time, the scientists concerned had exactly the same kind of evidence for both, so the partition can only be drawn retrospectively.

(6) I am unmoved by the problem of unconceived alternatives. A theory's record of novel predictive success is itself evidence that there is no radically different, equally successful rival that we have not thought of; the accumulated successes constrain the space of what could replace the theory, so the bare possibility of alternatives we cannot now conceive is not a reason to doubt it.

(7) The verdict I want to end on: I expect that in two centuries the judgment on our best current physics will be that we were right about the existence of its central posits and wrong about nearly everything we said about what they are like. I want to call the position that predicts exactly that a form of realism rather than a capitulation.

What I want from you is an audit, not a lecture. Go through the seven and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only look as though they are, and order the genuine tensions by severity rather than listing them as equals. For each genuine tension, say which thesis has to go or be sharpened, what the concession would cost me, and whether the cheapest repair is abandoning a claim or subdividing a category — and if you propose a subdivision, say whether it is principled or gerrymandered to fit the cases. I have no special attachment to any one of these, so do not read anything into the order in which I have listed them. Then test the whole package on two cases, the luminiferous ether and the gene as mid-century molecular biology characterized it versus how it is characterized now: for each, tell me what my theses jointly commit me to saying, and whether that verdict is one I should be happy to accept. Finally, flag anything in the list that is doing no work.
