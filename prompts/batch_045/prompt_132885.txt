I'm on this tiny volunteer panel that hands out a few thousand dollars a year of arts money through our city library foundation, and we keep getting stuck on the same sentence. Someone says "we fund art, not propaganda," and then we approve a veterans' mural, a set of nativity-scene puppets and a run of WPA-style prints of local factory workers without anyone reaching for the p-word, while a youth-theatre piece about stop-and-frisk and a photo series about a family getting evicted get called propaganda within about thirty seconds. Its not that the two piles differ in the features I'd naively expect to matter — all of them were made with somebody's money, all of them want you to feel something specific and come out believing something, all of them simplify. The difference seems to be whether the room already believes the thing. One panelist is a high school history teacher and she brought in Du Bois (he says somewhere, more or less, that he has no use for art that isn't doing propaganda work, and that the real complaint is that only some sides are allowed to do it), which made the argument worse instead of better.

Here's the part I can't get out of my head, though, and I think it's a different thing. It looks to me like propaganda stops being called propaganda once it wins. A cathedral window or an altarpiece was commissioned by an institution with a stake in what people believed, designed to teach doctrine to people who couldn't read, and hung where you couldn't avoid looking at it; by the description we were using on the youth-theatre piece that's textbook, but nobody in that meeting would call it propaganda, they'd call it devotional art or just art. Same with a lot of novels whose politics have since become the default: the politics stop reading as a claim somebody is making and start reading as the furniture of the world. So the word seems to track how live the fight still is rather than anything about the object — which would mean that the person who says all art is propaganda and the person who says good art is never propaganda might not be disagreeing about which works have politics in them at all.

Is there a name for this — either the "politics I happen not to share" pattern or the amnesty-over-time pattern — and are they one thing or two? I'm not after a label I can drop to win the meeting; I want the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it isn't, and where its edges are. The nearest things I've run into are "art for art's sake," which seems to be a claim about what artists should aim at rather than about what critics call things after the fact, and something about a "burden of representation," which is about asking one artist to speak for a whole group, and neither is what I'm seeing; if there's no established name, coin one and define it. I'd also really like boundary cases: is it the maker's purpose, the effect on the audience, or the funding source that makes the thing propaganda, and where does that leave an advertisement, a psalm, a war memorial, a corporate diversity poster, and an artist who believes the politics sincerely and was paid by nobody? Give me exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no intro and no wrap-up: (1) whether either pattern has an established name, (2) whether they're one thing or two, (3) the nearest existing concepts and how each falls short, (4) a working definition plus the cases that fall outside it, and (5) what I should actually ask in the meeting instead of "is this propaganda."
