I write novels (two out, third one due next spring) and the book I'm on has a trading people whose language makes you mark, on every past-tense verb, whether you saw the thing, were told it, or worked it out yourself. I keep reaching for Whorfian stuff — with this grammar they'd be scrupulous about sources, hopeless at rumour, maybe they can't even lie about where they got something — and I genuinely can't tell which of these is the real thing and which is the crappy pop-science version (I gather the famous "no word for time" claim was overstated, which is roughly my level here). So give me a procedure I can run on each idea: five minutes, no linguistics background, something that keeps whatever actually survives in the linguistic-relativity literature and bins the rest. The rule I've been half-using is the "cost test": a grammar difference earns it's place in the story only if I can point to a moment where it costs a character something — a beat of hesitation, a missing word, a social blunder — and never if it makes a thought impossible. Fix it or replace it, but I need to be able to run it on the thirty-odd ideas I've got banked before the ms goes to my editor, who reads for plausibility and will axe anything that smells like a parlour trick. Apply it to three: (a) a scene where one of them tells a flat lie about where her information came from; (b) a scene where one of them can't get his head around a ship that's due next season possibly not arriving; (c) compulsory gender marking making them all think of a particular bridge as a woman. I'd bet you're going to tell me to cut (a) and (c), and I won't be happy about (a). Also tell me where the procedure has to hand the decision back to me because it's a judgment about the story rather than about language, and be straight about what it can't do — including whether it can say anything at all about the English prose itself, since I'm writing these peoples' thoughts in English.
