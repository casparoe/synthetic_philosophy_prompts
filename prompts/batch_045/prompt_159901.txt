Some context on why I am asking now. I work at a machine-learning lab, and I have just been asked to help draft an internal position paper on whether any of the systems we train or deploy could be candidates for direct moral consideration, and if so by what test we would recognise one. The assignment has forced me to make explicit a cluster of views about consciousness that I had been holding loosely for years, and I would rather have them audited before anything goes into writing. I am not attached to any particular one of them; I am attached to not handing my colleagues a set of claims that quietly eats itself.

Here is, as carefully as I can state it, where I think I have landed.

1. Physicalism: every instantiated property is either physical or necessitated by the physical, and no facts about the world float free of the physical facts.

2. There is a real explanatory gap. From a complete account of a system's physical structure and its causal-functional organisation, no amount of a priori reasoning delivers what it is like for that system, or whether it is like anything for it at all. Some "zombie" scenario is genuinely conceivable, and the conceivability is not a mere artefact of vagueness.

3. The gap is epistemic rather than metaphysical, and it is explained by a fact about our concepts: our concepts of experience are conceptually independent of our physical and functional concepts while still applying directly to the states they pick out, so we can grasp the physical story completely and still not be able to tell by reflection alone that it is a story about an experience.

4. Illusionism is false. There is something it is like, and introspection is not systematically mistaken about what it is like. Whatever work (3) does, it must not quietly become the claim that experience is other than we take it to be.

5. What makes a system conscious is its causal-functional organisation, not what it is made of: preserve the organisation and you preserve whatever consciousness was there, including through a component-by-component replacement with functionally equivalent non-biological parts. This is the claim my position paper will have to lean on, and it is the one I am least willing to lose.

6. The dependence in (1) is to be understood on the model of a posteriori necessary identities, the way water is H2O: phenomenal properties are identical with specific physical properties, the identity holds of necessity, and we learn it empirically rather than from the armchair. This is the mechanism by which I hold (1) and (2) together without admitting a further fact.

7. A verdict on a case: take a very large population of ordinary people, coordinated so as to realise the fine-grained causal-functional organisation of a human brain over a comparable time-scale. I am inclined to say that this system is not conscious — that there is nothing it is like to be it, and that it is not a subject of experience in the way you or I are — and I am aware that people who share (5) tend to resist this.
