I spent a career in physical chemistry — reaction kinetics, mostly — which is to say I spent it explaining the behaviour of systems in which nothing persists and everything is a rate. On retiring I began reading philosophy seriously for the first time, and I find that I have arrived, largely by temperament, at what I take to be the Buddhist reductionist position on persons. Roughly: there is no self over and above the causally ordered stream of physical and mental events; what we call a person is a designation we impose on a region of that stream because it pays — predictively, deliberatively, morally — to treat it as one thing; there is no further fact of the matter as to whether I am the same person as the man who made a certain decision forty years ago, beyond facts about the strength and structure of the causal and psychological continuity between us; and the vivid sense that there is such a further fact is the output of a cognitive subsystem that models agents as unified persisting subjects, not a perception of one.

Because I came to this by temperament rather than by argument, I would like it stress-tested. What I am asking for is a map of its exposure.

First: what would count against it? Not only the objections a critic would raise, but the ones a committed defender would concede to be genuine costs. I would like them ranked, fatal at one end, merely awkward at the other, and I would like the ranking argued for rather than asserted. Where a consideration is already in hand rather than merely possible, say so; where it would only bite given some further claim the defender need not accept, say that too.

Second: what does the view predict that its rivals do not? I want it to stick its neck out. In particular, if the sense of self is a model rather than a perception, then interventions that degrade or dissolve that model — sustained meditative practice of the relevant kind, certain neurological and psychiatric disruptions — should move self-ascription and prudential concern in one specific direction rather than another. And if the tradition's own soteriological claim is right, the predicted direction is not a general indifference to future pain but something considerably more specific. Tell me what pattern the view is committed to, what pattern would confirm it, and what pattern, were it to turn up, would be decisive against it rather than merely needing to be explained away.

Third: how exposed is it, really? My own worry is that it has been qualified into safety. If the defender retreats to saying that persons are conventionally real, is that a substantive metaphysical claim, or has it collapsed into a reductionism about tables and rivers that nobody has ever denied? And if it has collapsed, is the use the Buddhist wanted from it — that seeing through the self is liberative — lost, or does it survive the collapse? A view that survives every test by narrowing what it asserts would worry me more than one that can lose.

Two cases of my own devising I cannot get the view to handle cleanly, and I would like to know whether they are genuine or artifacts of how I have framed them.

The first is a continuous version of the standard fission cases. Two later branches share a single earlier stream; both are fully causally continuous with it; the overlap between them in memory, character, projects and self-concern shrinks continuously from complete to nil, with no break anywhere. As I understand the position, it must either go gradational about identity — in which case the question whether the one who suffers tomorrow will be me has no determinate answer, and anticipation, promise-keeping and ordinary prudence lose their subject matter — or posit a threshold, in which case it has reinstated exactly the non-conventional fact it existed to deny. Which horn does a defender take, and what does it cost?

The second is a bookkeeping problem. The view is supposed to earn its keep ethically: if there is no self, the line between my welfare and yours is not a metaphysical joint, and concern should spread out accordingly. But the same move that dissolves the boundary dissolves the unit of diachronic accountability that any forward-looking moral accounting requires. If the unit is fixed by our interests, then how much of a given stretch of the stream counts as one agent becomes a function of what we care about — and I had taken the ethical conclusion to be independent of that. Is this a case the position cannot describe, or does it have resources, and if so do those resources smuggle back in what was supposed to have been given up?

There is also the phenomenological objection I keep returning to: that conscious experience is given as being for someone, rather than as a neutral occurrence to which ownership is subsequently attached by a further judgement. I would like to know whether the reductionist has a stable error theory here, or whether this is where the view is genuinely, non-repairably exposed.

For what it is worth, I will say what would move me most. I was trained to distrust a theory that is silent where the measurement plainly has a value, so I would be most persuaded by a case in which the position's machinery returns no determinate answer to a question that has one, or returns two incompatible answers depending on how the stream is carved. I am much less moved by the charge that the view is counterintuitive: my intuitions about persistence were formed by watching beakers, not persons.

Please don't send me a reading list or references to chase; I want the arguments and the cases set out, and I will do the reading afterwards if I must. Something in the region of 1,200 to 1,500 words would suit me — long enough for the ranking to be defended, short enough to read aloud to a reading group of eight of us who meet fortnightly, most of them, like me, retired from the sciences.
