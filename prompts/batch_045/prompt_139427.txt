I've been working through Moore's paradox on my own — I have no academic background in philosophy; I read with four friends who meet in the back room of a pub — and the next session is mine to lead. I want to close it with a villanelle instead of a summary, and I'd like you to write it with me.

Here is what I think I understand, so you can see what the poem has to be faithful to. Nothing in the sentence about the rain is contradictory: the rain can be falling and I can simultaneously lack the belief that it is, and both halves can be true together. Whatever has gone wrong is a fault in the saying rather than in what is said. That is different from the neighbouring variant where the second half is not an omission but a denial — It is raining and I believe it is not — which looks to me like an ordinary self-contradiction and not the interesting case at all. What I find genuinely eerie is that the sentence is perfectly calm the moment it is buried: put it inside a supposition or a conditional, shift it to the past tense, or say it about someone else, and there is no absurdity left. So the jam depends on flat-out saying it, in the present, about oneself. And underneath that, the thing I keep circling: I do not normally discover what I believe by checking. Settling whether it is raining is one kind of task, and settling whether I believe it is normally no task at all — I just say, and the saying and the believing come apart only in pathological cases. The sentence sounds to me like that faculty stripping a gear, not like a lie being caught out.

The case I have invented and cannot settle, which I want the poem to raise without answering: suppose the speaker is not one mind. A three-person weather office issues every bulletin under a single we. Two members have looked at the sky and believe it is raining; the third has not looked and does not. Their bulletin reads: It is raining. That seems fine. But suppose their rule requires a bulletin to report the office's belief as well, and the bulletin reads: It is raining and we do not believe it. Is that the same absurdity, a different one, or none at all? Does it change if the group's written record asserts the belief while the third member's dissent is minuted but never spoken? I want the poem to hold that question open.

What I am asking for specifically:

A villanelle, nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain. The first refrain is: It is raining. The second is: I do not believe it. Keep both word-for-word identical at every return, and let the rhyme bend rather than the refrain — I would rather have imperfect rhymes than a paraphrase that softens either line. Through the first four tercets the refrains should never sit in the same sentence: let each one arrive embedded, in a conditional or a supposition or a question, in the past tense, or said of someone else, so the reader has seen both lines many times and has still never heard them conjoined. Somewhere around the fourth tercet I want one attempt at the denial variant, offered and dropped, so the poem can show that the boring contradiction is not the puzzle. One tercet should shift without announcement into the first-person plural of the weather office, where the second refrain becomes we do not believe it, and it should not tell me whether the jam survives the change of speaker. Pick a base meter that lets the two refrains, joined by but, scan as a single regular line of the poem — I suspect iambic pentameter with an extra unstressed syllable at the end, but choose and tell me. Then break strict form exactly once: instead of ending on the second refrain alone, end on both of them, fused by but, in the present tense, flat out, with no framing and no wink, and let the poem stop there.

I would like someone who knows the topic to find it right rather than merely allusive, and someone who does not to find it a good poem. Give me the poem first, with no preface or commentary before it, and then at most a couple of hundred words afterwards on what each structural choice was doing and on anything in how I set the problem up that you think I have got wrong.
