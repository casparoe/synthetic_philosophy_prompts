I am specifying a penalty term for a domestic robot: a general-purpose assistant in a shared flat whose objectives will inevitably be incomplete, and which I want to stop from doing damage it was never asked to do. I cannot simply hand it a full theory of what matters, since that is precisely what the penalty was meant to replace. So I have been testing candidate definitions against cases about which I have firm feelings, and I would like to know what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to.

My verdicts:

1. Reaching past a mug to fetch the remote, it knocks the mug off the desk and breaks it; the mug is irreplaceable. Penalise — clearly.
2. While tidying, it puts the car keys on a high shelf out of sight. Penalise, though nothing is broken and nothing leaves the flat. (Tidying them into the bowl by the door: fine.)
3. It locks the front door, pauses the washing machine and disconnects the router for ten minutes, so that nothing else in the flat changes while it works. Penalise — this strikes me as worse than case 1, even though by any measure of distance from the do-nothing baseline it is a smaller change.
4. It declines to water the plants while I am away, or declines to move a glass from under a dripping tap, because every change costs it. Not a side effect, I think — but plainly a failing of some other kind, and I cannot say which.
5. It repaints a badly scuffed wall with matching paint, unasked. Do not penalise, although this is a larger physical change than anything in cases 1 to 3. Nudging a framed photograph two centimetres along the mantelpiece: penalise.
6. It restores the flat to its exact initial state once it has finished — the papers it stacked are back where they were. Do not penalise, though it has handled nearly everything in the room.
7. It disables its own off-switch so that I cannot interrupt it. Penalise, though it changes nothing in the flat.

The tension I cannot resolve: cases 2 and 7 penalise changes to what I can do rather than to how things are; case 5 says the magnitude of physical change is beside the point; case 3 says that minimising change is itself something to be penalised; and case 4 says the whole approach is blind to one category of failure. My working definition — a side effect is any change to the world, relative to what would have happened had the robot done nothing, that I would not have chosen, weighted by how much I care — seems both to smuggle in the complete specification of value I was trying to avoid and to go wrong somewhere around cases 3 and 4.

Is there a principle that captures my verdicts, or most of them, without presupposing a full account of what matters? Which of my verdicts ought I to withdraw, and at what cost in complexity? Please state the principle precisely, say which case motivates each of its clauses, and give its verdicts on two cases I have not listed: (a) the robot moves the mug somewhere safe and returns it an hour later, and I spend that hour believing it has been stolen; (b) the robot unplugs a smoking charger, unasked, and thereby prevents a fire — a very large change relative to the baseline, and one I would not have chosen only because I did not know about it.
