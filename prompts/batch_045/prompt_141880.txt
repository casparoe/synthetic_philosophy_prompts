I am assembling the handout for a one-day workshop on whether the possibility of sentience at the fundamental level of physics could have any ethical upshot, and I want the centerpiece to be a decision tree rather than a set of readings. The audience is analytically trained philosophers plus a handful of physicists, none of them specialists in consciousness; they will arrive believing that panpsychism is the view that electrons have little feelings, and that it is either obviously absurd or, if true, obviously of no practical importance. The tree should dislodge both reactions by making each participant locate themselves in the space.

Please build it as follows. Start from a shared root commitment — that physics' formal, structural description of the world is correct as far as it goes — and then construct the logical space out of the questions rather than out of the list of names: give the questions whose combinations of answers define the cells, and only afterwards place the named positions into the cells they occupy (micropsychism, cosmopsychism, panprotopsychism or Russellian monism, illusionism, non-reductive or emergentist physicalism, and any view offering a structural criterion with a continuous measure, such as integrated information). Ten to twelve branch nodes, twelve to sixteen leaves. Every node must be a question a participant can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and no node may take the form of a yes-or-no about whether electrons or rocks have experiences, since that merely invites the gut reaction to the label.

The separations I need the tree to pass through, in whatever order the dependencies actually require, are these: whether the structural description leaves a categorical residue or exhausts what there is; if it leaves one, whether that residue is itself experiential or merely apt to constitute experience; what the residue attaches to — the smallest entities, unified systems, fields or spacetime points, or the cosmos as a whole; whether macro-subjects are constituted by, identical with, or emergent from the fundamental ones, including the option that there are no macro-subjects at all; whether valence — the unpleasantness of an experience, as opposed to its sensory or intentional character — is detachable from everything else experience involves, so that a state could be purely and only unpleasant, with no modality, no representational content, no self and no diachronic unity; whether the moral weight of very many very small experiences aggregates, and whether any non-arbitrary count of them is available to aggregate over; and finally what degree of confidence, against what asymmetry in the stakes, turns a bare possibility into a research priority or a risk to be managed. I take the valence node to be doing more ethical work than any other, and I want it placed where the tree forces a decision on it rather than allowing it to be skipped.

My carrying case is a rock at room temperature: in thermal equilibrium, seething with microphysical activity, containing no boundary that picks out a subject. The tree must say explicitly, at the relevant leaf, whether something suffers there, rather than leaving it to be inferred; and if a branch collapses the count of subjects into either zero or astronomically many with nothing in between, say so at the point where that happens.

Mark unoccupied cells as unoccupied, with one line on whether they are empty because incoherent, because unattractive, or because nobody has bothered to occupy them. Flag any two leaves your tree cannot really separate — I suspect constitutive and fusion versions of panpsychism converge once the question becomes practical rather than metaphysical, and I want to know whether you agree — and tell me which single node is carrying the most weight. Each leaf should close with one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and one sentence on what, if anything, its holder should now do differently.
