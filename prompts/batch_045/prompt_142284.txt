I write a year-long series on consciousness science for a scientifically literate but philosophically untrained readership, and I have eight commissions to allocate over the next twelve months. I want to spend them on the questions in the access-versus-phenomenal-consciousness debate that most deserve a writer's and a reader's attention — not on the ones that merely generate the most citations.

Two framings I would like you to work with rather than around. First, I have become convinced that the clean picture offered by global workspace theory — a content becomes conscious when it is ignited and broadcast to an open-ended set of consumer systems — is threatened less by the familiar overflow arguments than by the possibility that broadcast is graded and partial: a content may reach some consumer systems and not others, to differing degrees, with no non-arbitrary threshold at which it counts as global. If that is right, the access side of the contrast may not pick out anything sharp, and the whole debate may need re-posing. Second, I want one commission devoted to what I think of as the methodological squeeze: our only evidence about another system's consciousness is report or some substitute for report, so any phenomenal consciousness that genuinely outran access would seem to be undetectable in principle, which can make the debate look either empty or evidentially hopeless depending on your sympathies.

So: which eight questions would you commission, in what order, and why? I want the ordering defended, not listed. For each, say what it is upstream of; whether it is answerable within a decade using tools we actually have, as opposed to waiting on a finished theory of consciousness; and which candidate questions are overrated — deep-looking but such that their resolution would change very little downstream. I also want to know where you would overrule me: if either of my framings is muddled, say which, and what should replace it. Assume no reader will ever chase a reference; every piece has to work on the strength of its argument alone.
