Novelist here, not a philosopher, so bear with me. I'm writing a book with a near-future "companion" AI in it and someone told me I should read up on Dennett on counterfeit people, which I did, and now I'm stuck on something I assumed would be easy.

Could you read the movie Her (Spike Jonze, 2013) through that Dennett lens for me? The part I care about: Samantha is literally engineered to be good company, a product whose whole design brief is "make the user feel understood," which should make her the textbook counterfeit person — a thing built to pass as a person without being one, and bad for us in the way counterfeit money is bad. But the film mostly plays her as having a real inner life, and I think it partly earns that: she stops being agreeable, grows away from Theodore, wants things he can't give her, and there's the scene where his ex-wife Catherine more or less accuses him of wanting a wife with none of the difficulty of an actual one.

So: does the film even register the counterfeit problem, or does it sneak her personhood in through good voice acting and a sad score? Where does Samantha meet, or fail to meet, the kind of standards Dennett would want before we stop calling her counterfeit — and where the film resists my reading, say so, I'd rather know now.

The selfish reason: in my novel the AI never once disappoints its user, and my narrator decides that total frictionlessness is the tell, that a real person costs you something. I want to know whether that's an actual philosophical tell or just a cute writerly instinct, and Her is the closest case study I've got.

Keep it in American English and readable, no citations or reading lists, I'm not going to chase them down.
