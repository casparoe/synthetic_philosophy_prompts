I got into an argument about this at a barbecue last weekend and it's been bugging me ever since, so I wanted to lay the whole thing out and ask you to tell me what's actually going on underneath it. Here's the case that started it. Imagine a firm called Kestrel & Voss, founded in 1908 as a brass foundry, that today sells payroll software: no foundry, no brass, no building from back then, and not a single shareholder, director, employee or customer in common with the 1908 version for at least sixty years. The only things that carried over continuously are the name, the registry entry, some ledgers in a basement, and an unbroken chain of people who each in turn took over the previous outfit's contracts, debts and pension obligations. Now imagine a court lets a suit proceed against today's Kestrel & Voss for lead poisoning suffered by foundry workers in 1931, on the theory that the defendant is the very same entity that poisoned them and not merely its descendant or heir. One friend said that's obviously incoherent: for a thing to still be that thing, the stuff and the people have to be mostly the same, and once you've swapped out literally everything you have a new thing wearing an old name (she thought the philosophy word for her view was endurance, but she wasn't sure). Another said no, the things that live in time are like rivers or roads, made of different material at different moments, and the same company just means one continuous stretch of company activity, so of course it can be liable (he thought that was perdurance, equally unsure).

What I actually want isn't a verdict on the company, though. It's that our real institutions seem to do a bunch of apparently different things in this area at once, and I can't tell whether they're contradicting each other or tracking different features. On the one hand: firms are sued for century-old injuries across total turnover; universities apologize for, and pay damages over, selling enslaved people in the 1830s when nobody currently associated with them was alive; states pay reparations for wrongs committed before any living citizen was born; and a revolutionary government that repudiates everything the previous regime did is still treated as the same state for treaty purposes. On the other hand: there are statutes of limitations, so that after enough years a person simply isn't answerable anymore even though it's the same body and the same brain; as I understand it courts won't carry out a punishment, and sometimes won't even try someone, who has lost the capacity to understand why they're being punished, on the ground that the connection between the old act and the present suffering has to be graspable by the person undergoing it; and yet a living will I signed at thirty controls what's done to my body at eighty-five, when I may not recognize my own family or remember signing anything, which seems to give the earlier stage authority over a later one that shares almost nothing psychologically with it.

So: what view of persistence over time does this package of practices as a whole presuppose? Can you reconstruct the implicit theory, and be careful about the difference between what the practices strictly require (such that the practice would be nonsense without it), what they merely sit well with, and where they're underdetermined so that several rival theories would license exactly the same thing? Is the corporate-versus-personal asymmetry principled, or is it just liability policy and deterrence wearing a metaphysical costume — one friend insists the metaphysics never enters into it at all, and I'd like to know if that's compatible with what the institutions are actually doing. I also floated my own test at the barbecue and nobody liked it, but I'd like it taken seriously: call it the chain-of-custody view, where the thing at the later time is the same thing as the earlier one iff there's an unbroken sequence of transfers of its name and records, each holder openly took on the previous holder's outstanding obligations, and nobody ever declared a fresh entity. Does existing practice secretly presuppose something like that rather than either of the two views my friends were reaching for, and how would it handle the awkward cases, like the regime that repudiates its predecessor's debts or the person who becomes, by their own later lights, a completely different agent? Finally, what would concretely have to change in law or institutional policy if we went all-in on the time-slices view and meant it everywhere — who would owe what to the 1931 workers, and whose signature on the living will would count — and what would have to change if we went the other way and meant the strictly-same-substance view everywhere?
