I'm running a 90-minute session next week for about 18 people, a deliberatly mixed room: half psychiatry trainees, half philosophy and phil-sci grad students. The topic is Hacking's looping effects — why human kinds are moving targets in a way that natural kinds aren't. I don't want to lecture, and I really don't want anyone to hear "looping effect", "interactive kind" or Hacking's name until the debrief.

What I want is a structured simulation where the room does the looping itself. My rough idea: round one everyone does some open-ended task, I sort them into two invented types on a criterion that's basically arbitrary (where they happen to be sitting, say), announce the labels publicly with plausible-sounding descriptions; round two, same task again — and because they've taken the labels on board, their output shifts to match, so a rule that was arbitrary now looks like it predicted something. Ideally nothing based on a real diagnosis, or the clinicians will spend the whole time arguing about that instead. Then the bit I'm less sure how to stage: I also want them to hit the second-order version, where the people doing the classifying revise the criteria to fit the already-looped population, and an uglier special case where a criterion gets added that counts denial of the trait as evidence for having it, so the kind becomes unfalsifiable.

Can you design the whole thing: the task, the labels, the rules and whatever small stakes make it bite, a minute-by-minute timeline, what I'm secretly doing as facilitator at each stage, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto (i) the basic loop, (ii) the criterion-revision loop, (iii) the denial-clause version, and onto the contrast with a natural kind that just sits there. Also what will predictably go wrong — the people who clock what's happening and refuse to perform, the ones who over-perform, the clinicians getting annoyed at a fake diagnosis — and how I handle each. And be honest about which part of the idea the simulation can't deliver and I'll have to just say out loud at the end; my guess is something about how slow real looping is, and about the people who never get to opt out of the kind they've been put in.

Start with one sentence that gives the activity in miniature, then everything else; no preamble and no explaining looping effects back at me.
