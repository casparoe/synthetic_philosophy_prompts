Posting this because I got into an argument in a group chat after a local EA-adjacent meetup, realised I couldn't actually reconstruct why I believe the things I believe about this, and now want someone to check whether the package holds together before I go around repeating it. Background: I've been reading about fish and crustacean welfare in aquaculture for a couple of years, I donate a bit to groups working on it, and I've ended up with roughtly these commitments, each of which feels individually obvious to me and which I now suspect are not all compatible: (a) sentience, roughly the capacity for valenced experience, is the whole of what makes a being morally considerable, and I'd put something like 0.9 on fish being sentient and 0.6 or 0.7 on decapod shrimp; (b) I'm a deprivationist about death, of the flavour you get from the Epicurus material — death isn't bad in itself and isn't bad for the animal except insofar as it removes future good experiences, so a quick painless kill at the end of a life that was going to be net-negative anyway is no harm to that animal and might be the best thing that happens to it, and this is load-bearing for me in the human case too, I don't want to give it up cheaply; (c) what matters morally is the total welfare experienced, so headcount matters enormously, and since you need something like a few hundred shrimp to get the same amount of food as a small fraction of a salmon, the shrimp question basically swamps the rest of aquaculture in expected importance; (d) I'm pretty suspicious of welfare reform pushed from inside an industry, my rough view being that improvements which show up as a label or a marketing story mostly work by making consumers feel fine about carrying on, which can hold demand up or raise it and so end up worse than nothing; (e) but I do think some specific reforms are clearly worth pushing for anyway, like stunning fish properly at slaughter instead of letting them die in ice or air, and getting hatcheries to stop slicing the eyestalks off female broodstock to induce spawning; (f) and I think we owe more to animals we deliberately brought into existence and made dependant on us than to wild animals we merely affect, because domestication is its own kind of relationship and I don't want to lose that thought; (g) finally, a friend got me sorting welfare interventions into intensity ones, which make each day of the animal's life better, and duration ones, which make the net-negative stretch shorter, meaning faster-growing strains or earlier harvest, and once I started using that distinction it looked to me like duration interventions might just dominate given (b) and (c), which weirds me out because it seems to point at killing farmed animals sooner rather than at treating them better, and that is not a conclusion I want.
