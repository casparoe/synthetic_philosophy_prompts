I spent my working life as a field biologist, mostly long-term monitoring of bird populations, and since retiring I have been reading a little philosophy and leading a small discussion group at a natural history society. I have no university affiliation and no library access, and nothing to look things up with, so please do not send me to a bibliography: I cannot read it, and a list of names would tell me nothing anyway. Argue from the structure of the cases.

What I want help with is a pattern I keep meeting in three different forms, and I cannot tell whether it is one phenomenon wearing three costumes or three phenomena that look alike.

First form. At a field station last winter I ran a version of a parlor puzzle I had read about: a banknote is auctioned to the highest bidder on the peculiar condition that the runner-up also pays whatever he bid, though he gets nothing. I added one modification of my own, because I wanted to isolate something. At any point either of the two players could call a halt, and if either did, both outstanding bids were returned and the note was withdrawn, so that halting cost neither player anything and gained neither player anything. On my arithmetic that should cap the bidding at the value of the note. A player who is about to be outbid can always take zero for nothing rather than pay more than the note is worth to get it, and the only way to come out ahead is for the other person to call the halt while you are still under the note's value. Yet my colleagues bid to two and three times the value of the note and stopped only when one of them ran out of cash in his wallet. Two of them told me afterwards that they had understood the arithmetic perfectly well the whole time.

Second form. I then tried a variant in which the runner-up's payment went not to the auctioneer but to the winner, so that all the money stayed between the two players. Here my arithmetic gives a different answer: whoever is left holding the standing bid ends up ahead by the size of the last increment no matter how high the bids have climbed, and the entire loss falls on the person who stopped. Nothing about continuing is ever bad; only stopping is bad. The bidding in this variant went further and lasted longer than in the first, and this time I do not think my colleagues were being foolish.

Third form, from my own working life. A monitoring program, chosen by a committee years earlier, continued to be renewed annually by people who had not chosen it and who said to me in private that it had stopped producing anything worth having. What struck me on reflection is that the cost of continuing was charged to a shared budget and belonged to no one in particular, while the cost of stopping fell entirely on whoever said the sentence out loud. Continuing is what happens if nobody acts.

I have come across sunk-cost reasoning, and something called the Concorde effect in the biology literature, and a phrase like escalation of commitment for the business version. None of these seems to fit my first variant, where nothing whatsoever is sunk and halting is free; and the second variant is not a fallacy at all on my reading, just a trap; and in the third the driver seems to be not my past investment but the attribution of a future one.

So: are these one thing or three? What are the nearest established concepts to each, precisely where does each of my cases fail to fit them, and which fits best? Where the distinctions I am reaching for have no settled name, coin them, define them tightly enough that they do not collapse into one another or into the sunk-cost family, and give me the boundary cases that would decide which is which. Please answer in American English.
