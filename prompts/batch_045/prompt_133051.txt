I am the student-elected member of my university's Academic Regulations Committee. At our meeting next Thursday we have to decide whether to replace the Exceptional Circumstances procedure with a system of automatic, no-questions-asked deadline extensions, and I have been asked to produce the paper the committee will actually vote on. I want it written as a decision memo rather than a discussion document: a recommendation in the opening paragraph, then the reasoning, then the concrete steps and dates, addressed to the Academic Board and signed in the committee's name.

Under the present procedure, a student who cannot meet a deadline must petition a central office, disclose what has gone wrong — usually something medical, financial or domestic — and wait for a caseworker to decide whether the reason is good enough. There are no published criteria, decisions vary between caseworkers, and there is no right of appeal. Nobody defends this. The proposal on the table is that every student be credited at the start of each term with a fixed allowance of extensions of a fixed length, claimable through the student portal with no disclosure and nobody's permission. A third group on the committee wants the extensions made automatic but also wants any student to be able to demand, from a named person, a written justification of any decision the system makes about them.

The committee is split in three, and the split is not really about the facts. One group treats freedom as a matter of how many doors are shut against you and by whom: for them the decisive fact about the present procedure is that whether you get an extension depends on somebody else's say-so, and it hardly matters how often the say-so turns out to be kind. A second group treats freedom as a matter of what you can effectively do, and points out something the first group ignores: the petition file was also how the university discovered who was in trouble, and that file is what routed students to hardship money, housing help and mental-health services. On their view the portal removes a genuine obstacle and simultaneously removes the machinery by which other obstacles get noticed, so it may enlarge one kind of freedom while quietly shrinking another. The third group treats freedom as a matter of standing rather than of options or outcomes: what matters is whether you can be made to do something at another's discretion, whether or not that discretion is ever exercised badly.

Four further things complicate the choice, and the memo has to deal with them rather than set them aside. First, roughly a hundred and twenty students — chronic conditions, caring responsibilities, disability — need more than the standard allowance, and they would move to a "registered adjustment" route requiring annual recertification with medical evidence and a meeting; that route is supported by the Disability Services Officer precisely because it triggers tailored support, but it reintroduces, at the margin and for the students least able to absorb it, the discretionary structure we are abolishing. Second, the portal logs every claim, flags students who exceed the allowance and automatically schedules a support conversation; the thresholds are the supplier's defaults, are not published, are invisible to the student, can be altered by the registry without notice or consultation, and the student's record cannot be seen or corrected. So far the flags have been accurate and no one has been harmed by one — but nobody contests that they are unappealable. Third, a substantial minority of teaching staff object on the ground that we would be relieving students of a burden rather than helping them carry it, and that a term of no-questions-asked extensions leaves them less able to meet a deadline rather than more free to aim at something. Fourth, the portal has already been bought and paid for, so doing nothing is genuinely free and the real decision is among the three live options.

Please write me that memo. It should commit to one of the three and say so in the first paragraph; it should make explicit which understanding of freedom is doing the work in the recommendation and where that understanding and the others come apart, but it must not settle any question by attaching a label to a position instead of arguing for it. It has to take a view on two sub-questions I do not think the committee has faced: whether a power that reliably tracks people's interests as others judge them, but which those people cannot see, contest or correct, is the sort of power that freedom is supposed to rule out, or whether demonstrated accuracy and benevolence are enough; and whether replacing "someone may let you" with "you may, as of right" changes a student's standing or merely their menu. It should give a dated sequence — pilot in one faculty or university-wide rollout, when any thresholds get published, when the justification mechanism would go live — and it should contain a short section, honestly argued, headed something like "The strongest case for the option we are not recommending," naming who on the committee will make it and what would have to be true for it to win. Write it for the registrar, the Disability Services Officer and the two student representatives as well as for me: no jargon they would need a dictionary for, no citations, no suggested further reading, and nothing that depends on facts I would have to go and look up. Aim for about a thousand to twelve hundred words, and write it in American English.
