Hey — you're a professional philosopher who's spent years working on moral responsibility, so take a minute to brainstorm before you start writing, and think it through properly rather than listing talking points.

Strawson's big move was that the whole free-will worry sort of misses the point. Our practices of blame, praise, guilt, gratitude and resentment are built out of reactive attitudes we take up toward each other as participants in relationships, and the alternative — the objective attitude, where you treat someone as a thing to be managed, explained, trained or repaired — just isn't something we could adopt as a general policy even if we wanted to, and even if determinism turned out to be true. So there's nothing much left for the metaphysics to threaten.

Now imagine a near-future device I'll call the Willscope. It's cheap and extremely reliable, and right after someone does something harmful it tells everyone involved two things: whether the action really expressed ill will, contempt or indifference toward others, and whether the person is currently capable of being reached by moral address at all (whether being blamed, asked for an account, or appealed to would get through to them). Courts use it. Friends check it before they have the argument.

Write an essay on whether Willscopes vindicate Strawson or quietly break his argument. One the one hand you might say the device only makes explicit what the reactive attitudes were already tracking all along, and that close relationships can survive being told the facts. On the other hand, resentment that waits on a readout starts to look like the objective attitude wearing a disguise, and the old determinism worry may climb back in through a side door. Weigh this up and don't sit on the fence: defend a verdict about whether blame in Willscope world is definately still the same thing as blame is now, and about what that verdict shows for Strawson's claim that we couldn't give up the participant stance even if we tried.
