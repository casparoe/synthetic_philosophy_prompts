I am working through Thomas Aquinas's treatment of resistance to tyranny in the Summa Theologiae, Second Part of the Second Part, question 42, article 2, on sedition, and I have hit a snag. The relevant passage reads:

> Objection 3. Further, it is praiseworthy to deliver a multitude from a tyrannical rule. Yet this cannot easily be done without some dissension in the multitude, if one part of the multitude seeks to retain the tyrant, while the rest strive to dethrone him. Therefore there can be sedition without mortal sin.
>
> I answer that, As stated above (Article 1, Reply to Objection 2), sedition is contrary to the unity of the multitude, viz. the people of a city or kingdom. Now Augustine says (De Civ. Dei ii, 21) that "wise men understand the word people to designate not any crowd of persons, but the assembly of those who are united together in fellowship recognized by law and for the common good." Wherefore it is evident that the unity to which sedition is opposed is the unity of law and common good: whence it follows manifestly that sedition is opposed to justice and the common good. Therefore by reason of its genus it is a mortal sin, and its gravity will be all the greater according as the common good which it assails surpasses the private good which is assailed by strife.
>
> Accordingly the sin of sedition is first and chiefly in its authors, who sin most grievously; and secondly it is in those who are led by them to disturb the common good. Those, however, who defend the common good, and withstand the seditious party, are not themselves seditious, even as neither is a man to be called quarrelsome because he defends himself, as stated above (II-II:41:1).
>
> Reply to Objection 3. A tyrannical government is not just, because it is directed, not to the common good, but to the private good of the ruler, as the Philosopher states (Polit. iii, 5; Ethic. viii, 10).

I want to understand how the argument is supposed to hang together, and I am especially interested in a distinction the passage seems to invite but not develop: the difference between a ruler who lacks lawful title altogether and a ruler who possesses lawful title but exercises it tyrannically.

Suppose a head of government was duly installed under a constitutional order but thereafter systematically redirects the machinery of the state toward personal enrichment, protects loyalists from ordinary legal accountability, and uses emergency powers to disable opposition. On the terms of the passage above, does the regime's continuing ability to secure prosperity, administrative efficiency, or popular support defeat Aquinas's claim that such a government is "directed, not to the common good, but to the private good of the ruler"? More importantly, how should Aquinas's identification of the "people" with a fellowship "recognized by law and for the common good" bear on who counts as the seditious party in this scenario: if the ruler's faction now controls the formal legal apparatus, can the resistors who appeal to the common good against the ruler be said to "defend the common good, and withstand the seditious party," while the ruler's own enforcers are the true authors of sedition?

In explaining this, please reconstruct the logic of the passage step by step: why Objection 3 initially makes sedition seem praiseworthy, why the body of the article insists that sedition is always mortal by reason of its genus, and how Reply to Objection 3's claim that tyrannical government is not just is meant to dissolve the objection. I am less interested in what later scholastic or early modern authors did with this material than in how the distinctions internal to this passage settle the case of the constitutionally installed but tyrannically acting ruler. If the passage's own resources underdetermine the answer, say exactly where the gap lies and what further distinction would be needed to close it.
