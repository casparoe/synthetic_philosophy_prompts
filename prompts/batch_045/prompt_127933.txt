(The handout for the course asks students to keep the modal thesis — that a mental state could be realized by physically diverse states — apart from the empirical thesis — that it is in fact so realized — and to say which thesis each step of the argument needs.)

The answer I keep going back and forth over reads like this: ''(a) Multiple realizability is the claim that a single mental state type can be realized by many different physical state types: C-fibre firing in us, some quite different neural state in an octopus, and in principle a silicon-based state in a creature with an entirely different chemistry. Since identity is a one-to-one relation, if pain = C-fibre firing and pain = the octopus state, then C-fibre firing = the octopus state, which is false; so no mental state type is identical with any physical state type. And because identities hold of necessity if they hold at all — we did Kripke on this — the argument does not need the alternative realizers actually to exist; it is enough that silicon creatures are possible. The argument is therefore modal rather than empirical, and it is untouched by how uniform the realization of pain happens to be among the creatures we have in fact encountered. What it shows, if it succeeds, is that no mental state is identical with a physical state: not merely at the level of types, but at the level of particular states and events as well, since a particular pain of mine could have been realized by some physical state other than the one that actually realizes it. The identity theorist's usual fallback — identifying pain with the disjunction of all its possible realizers — fails because a disjunction of that size is not a natural kind and so cannot be what psychology quantifies over; and this is just Fodor's point that the special sciences are not reducible to physics, because their kinds cross-cut the kinds of physics. (b) Restricted Identity Theory is the stronger position. RIT does not claim that pain as such is C-fibre firing; it claims that pain-in-humans-at-t is C-fibre firing, where the left-hand side picks out a species- and time-indexed property. Since the identity claim is now restricted in this way, it is contingent and a posteriori, like the theoretical identifications of ordinary science; and because it is contingent, the possibility of octopuses or silicon creatures realizing pain differently no longer touches it — they simply realize pain-in-octopuses and pain-in-silicon-creatures, which are different properties from pain-in-humans. RIT thus escapes the multiple-realizability argument at no cost: psychology keeps its own kinds, and the identity claim still does the work we wanted, telling us what pain really is in creatures like us. The objection one sometimes hears, that RIT has changed the subject and is no longer giving an account of pain, is just the multiple-realizability argument restated, so it begs the question against RIT. (c) Once multiple realizability is granted, functionalism follows fairly directly: what makes a state a pain cannot be what it is made of, so it must be what it does — its causal relations to sensory inputs, to other mental states and to behaviour. Mental states are the occupants of causal roles, picked out by their relations rather than by their intrinsic physical character. It follows that any two systems sharing a functional organization are in the same mental states and have the same conscious experiences: there is nothing more to being in pain than occupying the pain role. The main objection in the literature is that functional definitions are too liberal — the nation of people linked by radios, the robots whose neurons are replaced by homunculi with lookup tables — but these are appeals to intuition, and a functionalist can simply accept the verdicts. Besides, the same authors who press these cases also complain that functionalism is too narrow, excluding creatures it ought to include; the two complaints cancel each other out, so the objection gives us no reason to give up the view.''
