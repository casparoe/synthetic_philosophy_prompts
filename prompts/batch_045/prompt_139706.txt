I serve on the steering committee of the Northgate Mutual Aid Exchange, a network of about four hundred members in which every logged hour counts as one credit no matter whose hour it was or what the work was: an hour of emergency furnace repair, an hour of Khmer-language companionship for a housebound elder, an hour of tax preparation in April, and an hour of a retired psychotherapist's counseling all move the same number in the same direction. Members earn by doing for other members and spend by asking; no money changes hands, and credits are not convertible to cash. I've been asked to draft the decision memo we will vote on at our meeting on the 14th, and I'd like your help with it.

Three proposals are on the table, all prompted by the same complaint — that the 1:1 rule has produced a backlog for exactly the work people most need. Right now we have eleven open requests for overnight respite care and three for furnace repair, set against forty-odd open requests for guitar lessons and dog walking that nobody has taken up. Proposal A: weight credits by scarcity, so that an hour logged in a category with unmet requests is worth 1.5 credits, with the categories recalculated monthly by an elected "needs panel" whose proceedings are open to any member. Proposal B: let members run a negative balance of up to sixty hours against a 2% monthly "carry fee" on the outstanding amount; our treasurer insists this isn't profit but compensation for the risk that someone leaves the network in debt, and she wants the fee paid into the hardship fund. Proposal C: let members bank unlimited credits, including credits earned by arranging for other people to work under their name — the ramp-builder who wants to subcontract her carpentry and keep a cut has been its loudest advocate. Our accountant has also warned that if credits stop being interchangeable, a regulator may start treating us as a barter exchange and the hours as taxable income, so the memo needs to say whether that caveat changes anything about the merits and not just the paperwork.

The discussion has turned theoretical, and I'm out of my depth. Our longest-serving member, a retired classics teacher, says the whole thing turns on a distinction between two kinds of acquisition — one aimed at supplying a household's real needs and bounded by those needs, the other aimed at piling up the medium of exchange without any stopping point — together with the claim that credits, being a measure the community agrees on rather than a thing anyone can eat or wear, cannot naturally produce more of themselves. He also says that what holds an exchanging community together is not that each side hands over equal labor-time but that each side walks away with the thing it was short of, and that this is why a flat hour can be the wrong unit and a weighted hour the right one. He therefore argues for A and against B and C. Half the committee thinks he is right for reasons they can't quite state; the other half thinks he is dressing up a personality conflict in Greek, and points out that the needs panel in Proposal A would have to make precisely the comparisons between people's contributions that the 1:1 rule was adopted to avoid, and that any credit union would die without something like the carry fee. I've read none of this material and can't evaluate it, and I don't want the memo to rest on an authority two-thirds of the committee can't check.

Please draft the memo: 700–900 words, addressed to the committee, with a one-line bottom line at the top, a clear recommendation on each of the three proposals (adopt, reject, or adopt in a modified form, and if modified, say how), and a six-month review trigger that names what we would look at. If the distinction our classics teacher keeps invoking genuinely does work here, use it — but translate it into terms a member who has never opened an ancient text can follow, tell us where it stops doing work, and don't let it substitute for an argument. Where you reject a proposal, give the strongest version of the case for it rather than an easy one. Finally, our treasurer will ask this, so please answer it in the memo: would your recommendation change if the network grew to four thousand members, or if the needs panel's deliberations had to be closed for confidentiality?
