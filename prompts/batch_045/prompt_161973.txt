I am a novelist, and I have been made uncomfortable by an argument I constructed for my own use and cannot now stop. It arose from an ordinary decision. I can either spend the next two years finishing a novel, or take a salaried job that pays substantially more and give away most of the difference to a cheap, well-evidenced health intervention. A philosopher I correspond with holds that the second option is clearly better and that the arithmetic is not close. I wanted a reply showing that the arithmetic is not available. What I produced seems to show something far larger, and I cannot locate the place where it overreaches.

The argument, as I now have it:

(1) If the moral status of an action is determined solely by the value of its consequences, then applying that standard to a choice requires comparing the expected values of the options open to me.

(2) For any action available to me now, the consequences that dominate its expected value lie beyond a horizon at which my evidence stops discriminating between outcomes. A decision of mine propagates through systems sensitive to initial conditions — a reader recommends the book to someone who would otherwise have done something else, and so on without closure — so that over most of the probability mass the shape of the distribution reflects my ignorance rather than my evidence.

(3) Where the probabilities are not fixed by my evidence, the expected value of the action is not merely unknown to me but indeterminate. There is nothing there to compare; any figure I produce would be an artifact of how I chose to spread my ignorance across the space of outcomes.

(4) If the expected value of each option is indeterminate, then no option is better or worse than any other, and the standard returns no verdict — including on whether to do the action rather than refrain from it.

(5) The same holds for any view on which consequences are among the reasons bearing on what I ought to do, including the common-sense morality my correspondent and I both rely on when we say that promises should be kept partly because of what breaking them does.

(6) So no moral view that gives consequences any weight at all can guide action in any realistic case.

(7) That is not a conclusion I can accept, and it is not the one I wanted. I wanted only to show that the long-run term in my correspondent's calculation is not a quantity I can work with, and hence that the case for the salaried job is much weaker than she says.

My correspondent has one reply I cannot dispose of. She says that if I retreat to the consequences I can foresee — the proximate ones — I owe an account of why the horizon may be drawn where I draw it, and why the remote tail beyond it should be treated as cancelling out rather than accumulating in one direction. I have no such account, and I notice that my fallback looks like it was placed so as to return the answer I wanted.

So I am asking for a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Which step would you reject, and what exactly does that step trade on when read quickly? I would prefer to keep (1) and (2), which strike me as respectively definitional and empirical, and I am willing to give up the conclusion. But I want to know what each available rejection costs me: abandoning (2) in favour of some claim that influence decays and remote effects wash out; treating (3) as a slide from imprecise probabilities to no probabilities, and asking what decision rule imprecise credences actually support; denying (4) on the grounds that an imprecise comparison is not a void one and that partial orderings still guide; denying (5) on the grounds that a view which weighs consequences alongside other considerations was never committed to ranking options by expected value; or denying (1) by separating the claim that consequences are what matter from the claim that expected-value maximization is what that requires. Price these against each other. And tell me whether, on your reading, the argument is best understood as an objection to consequentialism or as an objection to a picture of what it is for a moral theory to guide action.
