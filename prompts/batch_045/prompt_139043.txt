I've been going back and forth in the usual places — a forecasting Slack, some Astral Codex Ten comment threads, a reading group that ground its way through Ellsberg — about whether a rational agent can really have indeterminate credences, or whether, as people in those circles like to say, your uncertainty about a probability is itself just another probability and "imprecise credence" is either incoherent or redundant notation. I've drifted toward the imprecise-probability side without being able to say exactly what I'm signing up for, and my use case is narrow enough that I'd rather have the spec than the menu: I want to represent genuine severe uncertainty in catastrophic-risk estimates, where several credible models disagree, nobody will commit to a prior over the models, and yet a decision has to come out the other end — plus a side interest in what it would mean for a forecaster to be *calibrated* while reporting an interval rather than a number. So: write me the requirements list — what a theory of indeterminate or imprecise credence would have to deliver to be adequate for that job, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and with a verdict on which are non-negotiable conditions of adequacy and which are just preferences of a particular camp. The items I keep coming back to, which you should feel free to reorganize, drop, or replace with better ones:

- a principled way to tell imprecision apart from risk aversion or from exotic tastes, so that the framework doesn't become a garbage can into which any deviation from expected-utility behavior can be dumped and reinterpreted as a belief;
- the cases where imprecision is clearly *warranted* (a coin of wholly unknown bias, an urn of unknown composition, a one-off institutional collapse) versus the cases where it should have washed out (the same coin after a thousand flips, a model with a long calibration record) — and whether "imprecision should shrink as evidence accumulates" is a genuine requirement or merely a hope;
- an updating story: do you condition each element of the representor separately, update the lower envelope as a whole, or appeal to something like natural extension — and what to say about the phenomenon where learning something you already knew would happen *widens* your interval for something else. Concretely: suppose two admissible priors disagree about a coin's bias but agree that the next toss is 50/50; once you learn the result of a toss, element-wise conditioning leaves you with a spread of posteriors for the following toss — from a sharp one-half to something like a third-to-two-thirds — and it widens whichever way the toss came out, so you know in advance that looking will make you less precise. Is that a reductio, a bullet to bite, or a sign that the representor was the wrong object to update?
- dynamic consistency: whatever the updating rule, does the agent's plan survive contact with new evidence, or does the natural maximin-flavored decision rule plus conditioning lead you to avoid free information or reverse your own plans in sequential problems? I can't tell whether this is a theorem-shaped cost or a confusion, and I want to know which.
- a decision rule, since a set of credences is not yet a decision theory: E-admissibility, maximizing the lower envelope, maximality/interval dominance, or full-blown choice functions — is coming with one part of the theory's job description, and what is the case that separates them (one where the lower envelope and admissibility disagree)? Include the collapse risks on both sides: a framework so permissive that nothing is choice-worthy, or one that secretly becomes ordinary Bayesianism with a prior over the set after all.
- the shape of the representor: whether it should be convex, and if so why a mixture of two individually permissible priors should itself be permissible; whether it should be closed under conditioning; whether "the interval" is best read as suspension of judgment, as each element being individually acceptable, or as a distribution over distributions I decline to write down;
- the distinction between sharpness and evidential weight — being exactly 50/50 on no evidence versus exactly 50/50 after a thousand trials — and whether a formalism with only sets in it has the resources to mark it, or whether that pushes you toward a separate dimension;
- aggregation: two analysts report [0.2, 0.5] and [0.4, 0.8]; what is the group's interval, does pooling preserve whatever coherence conditions you imposed, and is there any coherent way to score an interval forecast for accuracy or to pay someone for reporting one honestly;
- enough structure to be usable at all — behavior on continuous spaces, conditioning on events of probability zero, and whether the whole thing is implementable as something like a linear program rather than an existence proof.

What I most want out of this is the negative part: tell me which subsets of the requirements look jointly unsatisfiable, since if the list itself is the interesting result, that's what I'd rather find out now. My suspicion is that there's a triangle in there — element-wise conditioning, no dilation, and dynamic consistency under a cautious decision rule — where you can have any two, and I'd like to know whether that's right, what's actually proved versus merely asserted in the folklore, and which single requirement does the most work in eliminating candidate theories.
