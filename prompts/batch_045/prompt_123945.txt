You are a professional philosopher specializing in the philosophy of language and literature who has also served as a textual editor on a scholarly edition series, and I want you to answer as a practitioner rather than as a surveyor of the field. Please think carefully before you write: start by brainstorming every distinction that could possibly bear on editorial decisions about meaning, then discard the ones that cannot survive contact with a one-hour meeting and a printing deadline, and tell me what you threw away and why. I do not want citations, bibliographies, or reading lists — I cannot check them and they would not help me; I want a procedure.

My situation: I am the senior editor at a small press that puts out annotated readers editions of twentieth-century English-language novels, and I make decisions of this kind roughly ten times a week with a team of three, in a single weekly meeting, with no budget for outside consultants and a hard cap of about forty textual notes per volume. Our evidence base is manuscripts, revised typescripts, letters to editors, occasional recordings of public readings, and interviews given decades after publication. Our editions are widely taught, so whatever we assert in the notes effectively becomes the reading for thousands of students. Our current house rule is blunt: emend the text only when the printed reading is not something the author could have meant, and otherwise annotate the difficulty instead of choosing between readings. That rule has started producing results I don't trust, and I've been trying to replace it with something better by distinguishing four candidates for what the word meaning might attach to: the text the author was trying to produce, the physical text that actually exists, the text that readers have in fact received for the last sixty years, and the text the work has for a readership the author could not have anticipated. A friend who works in archives tells me authorial intention never settles anything; I think it settles more than half our queries. I want a procedure that makes that disagreement explicit and operable: a short ordered set of questions we can run on a query, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which questions get a hard no that ends the inquiry, and which questions are really value judgments that have to be handed to me or to the literary executor rather than decided by the text.

Run it on these three. First: the first edition silently regularizes a minor character's name, which appeared in the typescript under one spelling in the early chapters and a different spelling later; a printer or an inattentive compositor made them consistent, and half the criticism since then treats them as one person while a minority reads the mismatch as a deliberate signal of the narrator's failing memory. We can be fairly sure the author simply slipped, but emending would quietly settle a live interpretive dispute, and annotating would keep it alive. Second: twenty years after publication the novelist said in a recorded radio interview that a long speech by a minor character — one that nearly every reader has taken straight — was meant as deadpan irony, and he professed astonishment that anyone read it otherwise; his letters from the period of composition show him worrying that the speech was too subtle and cutting it down, and the surviving drafts contain no ironic markers. His daughter, who is the literary executor, wants a note telling readers the speech is ironic; I would quite like to believe her father. Third, the hardest: the estate wants us to publish the unfinished final novel, a three-hundred-page draft to be completed by a ghostwriter, and it contains a passage in which the first-person narrator describes a shopkeeper in terms that, in the published object, will read as unmediated ethnic caricature; the author's outline reveals that the narrator was to be exposed in an unwritten final section as a figure whose judgments the reader is meant to distrust. The estate wants the passage cut, the ghostwriter wants it kept, and every option — cutting it, printing it bare, printing it with a note revealing the planned frame — changes what the text does. For each case give me the procedure's output and your confidence in it, including at least one case where the procedure tells me something I will not like, and end by listing the kinds of query this procedure cannot settle at all and saying plainly where it will misfire.
