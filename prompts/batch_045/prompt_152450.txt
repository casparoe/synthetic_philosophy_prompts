I have just come out of a four-hour argument about reasons with a friend who is a far harder-headed internalist than I am, and she landed one objection I could not answer, so I need help before next week.

On Tuesday I defend the third chapter of my dissertation in a two-hour committee meeting. The chapter proposes a version of externalism I call role-anchored. Roughly: internalism is right about bare reasons, the reasons an agent has simply in virtue of the aims, projects and cares she actually has, and wrong about role reasons, the reasons attached to an office or relationship the agent occupies: parent, judge, trustee, promisee, citizen, colleague. Those hold whether or not she cares about them. The further claim, and the one I actually care about, is that internalism's real insight is a voluntariness condition that it attaches to the wrong moment. Internalists say a consideration is a reason for me only if I could reach it by correct deliberation from what I already care about. I say the condition does its work at uptake instead: what makes the demands of a role genuinely normative for me is that I could have refused the role, or that its central demands were foreseeable when I took it up. That relocation is also meant to explain why the hard cases in the literature are hard: the brutal agent, the addict who wants the drug and does not want to want it, the person flattened by depression. Those are bare-agent cases, and the intuition that someone has a reason he cannot see returns with full force the moment you give him a role.

My friend's objection, which I think is close to fatal, is a collapse argument: if voluntariness does the work, then roles nobody chose — being someone's child, being born a citizen, becoming a parent by accident — generate no reasons at all; and where the agent does identify with her role, saying she has reason to do what it requires is just describing an internal reason with a wide motivational set, while where she does not, it is a sociological remark about how often people identify with their roles. Either way, she says, role-anchored externalism is internalism with extra furniture.

The committee is four people. My supervisor is a constructivist about practical reasons who thinks Humean pictures are the enemy and will want to know whether my uptake move is constructivism about institutional normativity and whether a role's content is fixed by the agent's will or by social practice. The second reader is an internalist in the Williams line, who thinks the whole disagreement turns on what relation reasons must bear to an agent's motivational states, and who will ask whether my could-have-refused condition is a motivational fact in disguise. The third reader doubts that reasons are the primitive normative unit at all — she thinks reasons-talk is derivative from value or from what one ought to do — and will ask whether the dispute is verbal. A visitor who works on practical rationality with an eye to empirical psychology will ask what my view predicts about actual deliberation and how anyone could tell.

What I want is a prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get, in the order they will come, each phrased the way that person would phrase it, ordered by how much damage it would do if I could not answer it, with a note on how likely each is. For each, a reply I can give in about ninety seconds and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Include the objection I am hoping nobody raises — I think it is whether role-anchored externalism entails that the camp guard, or the accountant for a criminal firm, has reason to do his job well, but tell me if there is a worse one and what to say if it comes. Where the honest answer is that my view has no reply yet, say that rather than manufacturing one. And give me no citations, no named papers, no reading list: I want arguments, not authorities.

Finally, and this matters as much as the rest: for each reply, mark whether it rests on something that is common ground among people who work on reasons or on a claim that is live-contested, and where it is contested, say what the alternatives are and whether mine is a minority position. Tell me plainly which of my premises are load-bearing and disputed, so that I do not walk in presenting a contested view as the state of the question.
