I am a retired experimental geophysicist who, in the last couple of years, has started sitting in on a philosophy-of-science reading group at the local university, and I have come away from the most recent meeting unable to referee an exchange that I think deserves a better referee than me. I would be grateful for your help with it.

Please approach this as a professional philosopher working in formal epistemology and the philosophy of science would: reconstruct each move as charitably as it can bear, check the mathematics rather than the rhetoric, and only then give a verdict. Before you write anything, I would like you to brainstorm — in particular, to enumerate the candidate ways one might formally cash out a "presumption of independence" and to test each of them against the two argument forms at issue below, rather than settling on the first formalization that comes to mind. Take your time and reason step by step; I would much rather have a careful dissection than a fluent overall impression. Please do not give me a reading list, a bibliography, or citations: I want the arguments, not the literature. You may assume I can follow Jensen's inequality and the idea of a copula, so there is no need to explain the mathematics from scratch.

The background: Miriam, a retired astrophysicist in the group, circulated a short note defending what she calls heuristic arguments — back-of-the-envelope arguments whose premises are stated only to within an order of magnitude and whose conclusion is a shift in credence rather than a proof. Her two worked examples were these. The many-shots form: there are N chances for some striking outcome, each with small probability p, so the probability of at least one occurrence is 1 − (1 − p)^N, and an observed occurrence is therefore no longer surprising and stands in no need of a special explanation. Her illustration was abiogenesis: at the usual order-of-magnitude figure of some 10^22 stars, even a per-star probability of 10^-20 yields of order 10^2 episodes, so our own existence is no evidence for anything beyond ordinary chemistry. The stacked-lines form: several lines of evidence each speak only modestly in favour of a hypothesis — say each carries a likelihood ratio of about 10 — and if the lines are independent the ratios multiply, so four such lines give a factor of 10^4, which is overwhelming. Her illustration was four independent proxies for a single past excursion in ocean temperature. She said in advance that all the astronomical and geochemical numbers were placeholders she was happy to disown.

What follows is the exchange as I noted it down, in order.

(1) Miriam: Heuristic arguments are respectable because each can be reconstructed as a valid argument plus one explicitly stated default: absent evidence of a correlation, treat the chances, or the lines of evidence, as independent. And the default is not an arbitrary posit. Among all joint distributions with the given single-case probabilities, the independent one has maximum entropy, so it is the least committal joint available to a genuinely agnostic reasoner; the presumption of independence is simply agnosticism made formal.

(2) Ted, a retired actuary: Two problems. First, the product rule requires mutual independence, and what one ever has evidence or intuition about is at best pairwise independence, or the absence of a shared cause one has thought to name; the gap between those and mutual independence is precisely the territory heuristic arguments operate in. Second, and more damaging: the residual dependence we cannot rule out is not distributed symmetrically about independence. Unmodelled shared causes produce positive dependence, and positive dependence systematically deflates the many-shots boost while inflating the probability that stacked lines agree, so in both forms the independence value sits at the end of the range that favours the argument's own conclusion. He added that in the abiogenesis illustration every candidate site shares a physics and a history, so "no known correlation" is closer to evidence of correlation than to evidence of its absence.

(3) Miriam: She granted the direction of the effect but restructured the default: presume independence of the residuals after conditioning on every shared cause you can name; the maximum-entropy argument then applies to what is left over, and what is left over really is agnostic. And she proposed that the deliverable of a heuristic argument is not a conclusion but a fragility index — the size of the residual dependence that would be needed to overturn it. In her abiogenesis illustration the break-even residual correlation was of order 10^-3, and she counted this as a virtue: the argument tells you exactly what to go and measure.

(4) Ted: The break-even number is not measurable to anything like the precision required, and the situation is worse than "a number to go and check." If the chances share a common latent rate with mean p, then by Jensen's inequality the probability of at least one occurrence is strictly smaller than the independence value for any spread in that rate at all, and the same mixing strictly raises the probability that all the lines agree; with N of order 10^22, the two answers come apart by many orders of magnitude. So the conclusion of a heuristic argument is controlled by a parameter the argument itself declines to estimate, and the fragility index merely renames that dependence instead of removing it.

(5) Miriam: His collapse assumes a single global factor coupling all N chances. Real dependence is block-structured — clusters of correlated tests within a survey, physically similar sites within a galaxy — and under block structure the effective number of chances is roughly the number of blocks, which in her example is still astronomically large: of order 10^11 galaxies, or the independently reduced patches of a survey. He has taken the worst case and presented it as the expected case.

(6) Ted: That concedes the point he was making. To get any answer at all you must choose a dependence structure — one global latent factor, blocks at some granularity, a copula with some tail behaviour — and that choice, not the single-case probabilities, does all the work. So the presumption of independence is either contentless, naming a family of incompatible defaults whose answers differ by twenty orders of magnitude, or it is a substantive modelling claim that ought to be defended like one. And if the honest deliverable is only a fragility number, then heuristic arguments do not support conclusions; they relocate the disagreement.

(7) Miriam: Agreed, and the relocation is the point. The presumption should be restated as a claim about granularity — independence conditional on the coarsest partition of the chances into blocks that you are prepared to defend — and a heuristic argument should report its conclusion as a function of block size rather than as a single number. She added that Ted's alternative, build the full causal model first and reason afterwards, merely pushes the same unexamined commitments one level down, and that a discipline which refuses to reason until it has a complete model simply does not reason.

There they stopped, with nobody in the room willing to say who had won.

What I would like from you, in about 900 to 1,200 words: first, go through the seven moves point by point, rating each of them on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification for each rating, and flagging explicitly any place where the mathematics as stated is wrong, where a true claim does not support the point being made with it, or where a move quietly changes the subject. Second, say what, if anything, both of them are missing: the distinction, formalization, or reframing that would dissolve the dispute or show it to be resting on a shared mistake. Third, give me your verdict on the underlying question, which I take to be whether a presumption of independence can be formalized in a way that does the work heuristic arguments need it to do, and if so what discipline that formalization imposes on how such arguments may honestly be stated. Finally, tell me who is more right overall, and what in the exchange does most to decide it.
