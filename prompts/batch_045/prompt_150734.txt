I'm stuck on the heterogeneity objection and I want to get beaten up by someone who actually holds the view. Please play a committed experiential hedonist about welfare: pleasure, understood as a felt quality of experience, is the only basic prudential good and the only thing that ultimatly makes someone's life go better for them. Be sharp and unsentimental, argue from cases rather than from the literature (no names, no citations), concede small points when you have to — e.g. that there's no single sensory quale running through every pleasure, and that some of the things we call pleasures are partly extrinsic — but never concede that pleasures share nothing non-trivial, and never concede that the hedonist has no way to compare or add them up. One short paragraph per reply, 150 words tops, and stay in character until I say the word stop.

My opening move. I think you have exactly three options and none of them work. (a) Pleasantness is a single felt quality, like warmth. But then it should be introspectively locatable and it isn't: there's nothing it feels like in common between the cold beer on a hot day, the proof finally clicking, and the little jolt at the news that your rival's paper got scooped; and if you say the quality is there but too faint to notice, you owe me some other way of knowing it's there and how much of it there is. (b) Pleasantness is a role: whatever state motivates, reinforces learning, and gets called pleasant. Then the feeling drops out. Imagine a neurologist scrambling the felt character of two states while holding fixed how motivating they are, how much they reinforce, and the subject's own verdict that things are going equally well for them; you'd have to locate the welfare value in the role, and that's a desire theory in a hedonist's jacket. (c) Pleasantness is a family resemblance, a determinable with lots of determinates like colour. Then there's no common unit: intensity and duration, the Bentham-style dimensions you need to sum a life, are only comparable inside a determinate, so you can't say how much of the beer equals how much of the proof — and a welfare theory that can't do trade-offs can't do the job it was hired for.
