I came to Buddhist ethics on my own, through meditation practice rather than through philosophy, and I keep arguing with a friend who is a thoroughgoing act consequentialist. Her line is that Buddhist ethics is just consequentialism with a story about karma bolted on, and that whenever the two seem to disagree it is because the Buddhist is quietly either counting extra consequences or falling back on a rule of thumb about kinds of action. I want a test case she cannot explain away like that.

Please build me a scenario in which a Buddhist ethicist and an act consequentialist give opposite verdicts about what is to be done, and in the direction I find hard to arrange: it is the Buddhist who refuses to relieve someone's suffering, and the consequentialist who insists on relieving it. Constraints:

- Ordinary setting (a clinic, a family, a workplace), one paragraph, nothing exotic, no monastic characters and no science fiction.
- Both sides accept exactly the same descriptive facts, including the long-run psychological effects on everyone involved, so the disagreement cannot come from the Buddhist predicting different outcomes.
- No precept or rule about types of action may do the work: the Buddhist is not refusing because relieving the suffering would involve killing, lying, stealing, or the like.
- The Buddhist verdict must survive setting aside the agent's own karmic prospects, so this cannot be a case of the agent quietly protecting their own future.
- Both theories count the same people; nobody is left out of either calculation.
- The person the decision concerns is not a Buddhist and would reject the Buddhist account of what is good for them. Deal with that head-on rather than stipulating it away, and if it wrecks the case, say so.

After the scenario: show how each verdict is generated from each theory's own machinery rather than written into the setup; name the single feature of the case that is doing the work; show what happens to the divergence when you remove that feature (in particular, what if dukkha is simply identified with unpleasant experience, or the consequentialist is allowed to count the uprooting of craving as a component of welfare); and give my friend's strongest objection, the best version of the claim that this is only a quarrel about what welfare is and so not a real ethical disagreement at all. Then tell me whether you think she is right, and whether the case is rigged or the Buddhist verdict does not really follow.
