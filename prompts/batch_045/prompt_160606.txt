I'm a senior in high school, and I've been asked to write up my own position on merit and desert for a debate-club dossier we circulate before the spring tournament. Before I defend it in front of people whose job is to find the weak joint, I'd like to know whether the set actually holds together. Here is where I've landed, roughly in the order I arrived at each view. (1) What makes someone morally deserve credit or reward is how hard they tried, not how well things turned out: two students who work equally hard for the same exam deserve equal credit even if one scores higher because the subject comes easily to her. (2) At the same time, I think my school is right to grade purely on demonstrated mastery and to ignore how many hours someone spent, because a grade is supposed to tell a college what you can currently do. (3) Nobody deserves anything on the basis of unchosen features — native intelligence, physical gifts, or having been born into a family that reads to you at night and pays for a quiet room to work in. (4) Nevertheless, people are entitled to the products of their own labor, including labor that leans hard on those very gifts: if I write something good using a brain I did nothing to earn, the thing and its proceeds are mine, and not merely as a matter of what the law happens to permit. (5) Legacy admissions are unjust, full stop. (6) But I don't think a school does anything wrong by admitting the student whose parents bought him expensive test prep, or by declining to subsidize it for everyone else — that is just a family spending its own money, which (4) seems to allow. (7) There is also, I think, a separate and thinner thing I'd call institutional desert: someone deserves the credential, prize, or seat just in case they satisfy the criteria the institution publicly announced, whatever those criteria are and whatever their moral status, so that a valedictorian who coasted on native ability and a comfortable home still deserves the title in the only sense a school is in a position to hand out. (8) Finally, a society's institutions should allocate positions of advantage to the most competent available people, not to the most effortful ones — we want the surgeon who is best at surgery rather than the one who tried hardest — and that is what merit ought to mean at the level of institutions.

The part I can't shake came from a friend who reads a lot of psychology. She pointed out that how much effort a person is able to sustain is itself substantially a matter of temperament and upbringing, and she gave me a case I keep chewing on: suppose there were a completely safe pill that permanently raises your capacity for sustained effort, the way a strong cup of coffee does but structurally and for good. If someone takes it and then outworks everyone around them, does that make their success more deserved or less? I don't want to have to say less, because that seems to punish people for improving themselves, but (1) and (3) both seem to push me there, and (8) seems indifferent in a way I find suspicious.

What I want is an audit rather than an essay about meritocracy. Work through the eight and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other and which only look like they do, ranked by how severe the conflict is instead of listed flatly; for each real tension say exactly what I would have to give up or qualify and what that costs me somewhere else in the set, and if a single commitment is doing the damage, name it. For the pairs that only appear to conflict, explain why the appearance dissolves. If the package is salvageable only by drawing a distinction I haven't drawn yet, tell me which distinction and where in the set it has to go. Two further things: tell me where your own audit is most likely to be wrong — which step in your reasoning is weakest, and which assumption about how effort, talent, and upbringing actually relate it rests on — and name the one argument or empirical finding that would change your verdict about which of my commitments has to go. I'd much rather have one ranked diagnosis with the load-bearing moves flagged than eight paragraphs each agreeing with me.
