I'm a PhD student in political theory, finishing a dissertation chapter that tries to run a single argument across two literatures that rarely speak to each other: psychiatric advance directives — the Ulysses contract cases, where someone in remission specifies what should happen to them during a future episode in which they will refuse the treatment they now want — and constitutional precommitment: entrenchment clauses, supermajority requirements, unamendable provisions, rights that a later majority cannot reach. My claim is that both are instances of one justificatory structure, in which an agent at t1 limits the admissible choice set of an agent at t2 who is, in some sense, the same agent. At a workshop last week the discussion stalled not on an argument but on a piece of language. I said the structures were the same; a senior colleague said the difference is that one is an exercise of autonomy and the other is simply "the dead hand of the past." When I pressed her, someone else observed that Ulysses was freer tied to the mast than untied, and that this is as true of a constituent assembly as of a patient. Both remarks felt decisive in the room and neither feels decisive on the page, and I suspect each is doing several quite different jobs at once.

What I want, then, is a disambiguation rather than an essay. Give me a menu of the distinct precise theses each of these two one-liners could be expressing, ordered roughly from the trivially true to the radical, and attach a verdict to each: trivial, substantive and defensible, substantive but undefended, or straightforwardly false or incoherent. For "the dead hand of the past," I want the variables made explicit — is the objection to the bare passage of time, to a change in the later self's desires, to a change in the later self's values of a kind the earlier self actually foresaw, to a change in the later self's information, to the later self's having lost a capacity, or to something about who is currently entitled to decide at all — and, crucially, for each reading, whether it would support my colleague's sharp contrast between the clinic and the constitution, or would condemn entrenchment and advance directives together, or would vindicate both. Do the same for the Ulysses line: is it claiming something about the extension of an agent's options over time, about the authenticity of the binding self, about the metaphysics of who is free when, or about the social meaning of a constraint one has authored? And, for both, tell me which reading the person in the room was most likely reaching for, given the work the line was being made to do.

To force the readings apart, I've been testing them against three devices I've invented for the chapter, and I'd like the menu keyed to them. First, the reaffirmation clause: the directive or entrenchment lapses unless, at fixed intervals, the person — or the electorate — performs a minimal act of re-endorsement, ticking a box that says "I still stand behind this," without re-deliberating the substance. Second, the two-key directive: the earlier self fixes the constraint, but it takes effect only if a designated proxy who knows the later self concurs at the moment of application. Third, the transformation case: the earlier self foresees and intends to override a specific future change in her own values — a directive authorising compulsory treatment if she later joins a religion that forbids it — so that the conflict between the two selves is not a failure of foresight but its object. For each device, and for each reading on your menu, say whether the device would neutralise the objection, leave it untouched, or make it worse, and why. Where a reading survives all three devices, I want to know whether that is a point in its favour — a genuinely structural objection to precommitment — or a sign that it has stopped being about the temporal relation at all and has quietly become a claim about something else, such as the value of present self-government or the legitimacy of constraint per se. The end product I have in mind is a table I can hand out at the next workshop and a footnote I can actually defend.
