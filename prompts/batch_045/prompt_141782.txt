I run a monthly session at a rationalist meetup — fifteen or so people, mostly programmers plus one or two working physicists, forty minutes, whiteboard only — and next month is mine, on the arrow of time. I've been reading this corner of the internet long enough to know the standard moves, and most of the room has already absorbed the pop version, so I want to get past "entropy increases, therefore time has a direction." What I want from you is the plan, not the explanation itself.

The claim I want them walking out with is that the arrow is not in the dynamics and not in time itself but in a boundary condition — and then the further question of whether a boundary condition is even the sort of thing that can explain anything. So give me the dependency structure: what has to be on the board before "entropy increases" can be heard as a substantive claim rather than a definition (I assume the time-symmetry of the microdynamics has to come first), what the correct order is for the statistical step — why macrostates get to inherit probabilities at all — the low-entropy initial condition, and the move to records and memory, and which of those actually depends on which rather than merely being conventionally taught in that order.

One image should carry the whole session: which, and what must it have and lack? My instinct is a gas expanding into a vacuum or some ordinary process played backward on video, but I want your reasoning about what each candidate smuggles in — I'd rather not spend the session on something that secretly relies on friction, or on an observer, or on the word "disorder."

The misconceptions I expect, roughly in the order I expect them to form: entropy as disorder; the second law as just one more dynamical law; "entropy can't decrease" read in a way that makes a refrigerator an exception; the suspicion that if the arrow is defined by entropy increase the account is circular; and the reversal worry — flip every velocity and the gas un-mixes, so isn't the arrow just a conspiracy among initial conditions? Say where in the sequence each has to be defused, and which of them I should actively invite rather than head off, because working through it is the point.

The bit I'm genuinely unsure about: I want to include a cosmos in which entropy has a single minimum at some moment and increases in both temporal directions away from it, so that there are two populations of observers, each one calling the minimum its past and each one reporting entropy to increase. Does that belong in forty minutes, and if so where — does it make the boundary-condition point land, or does it collapse the distinction between the thermodynamic arrow and the record arrow that I'll have just spent most of the session building?

Finally: what should I openly decline to explain and say I'm declining (I'm inclined to refuse the quantum-mechanical arrow and the expansion-of-the-universe arrow outright, and to say why), and give me one closing question whose answer tells me whether someone has the idea rather than the vocabulary.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the one ordering or inclusion decision the rest of the plan turns on — then give the plan. No preamble, and don't restate my question back to me.
