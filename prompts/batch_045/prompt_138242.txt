You are a professional philosopher specialising in epistemology and the philosophy of language, with a particular brief on the norms governing speech. Please think the problem through carefully, and spend some time at the start brainstorming candidate requirements and the cases that motivate them before you settle on a final list: I would much rather have six requirements that have been pruned than twenty that have not been examined. Answer throughout in British English, and don't spend your word count on citations, bibliographies or a reading list — I know the literature and I want the argument.

I am writing the specification section of a paper on the epistemic significance of testimony, and I have realised that I don't know what I am entitled to ask a norm of assertion to do. So write me the desiderata: what any adequate account of the norm governing assertion has to deliver — which cases it must get right, which distinctions it must respect rather than collapse, which surrounding commitments it has to stay consistent with — with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and, for each, your verdict on whether it is non-negotiable or merely the prejudice of one camp. Since adequacy is relative to a purpose, let me fix mine: I need a norm that can do two jobs. First, it has to underwrite an account of what a hearer gains when a speaker asserts — why relying on an assertion is different from picking up a belief from overheard noise, and how a hearer's warrant can survive even when the speaker has in fact violated the norm. Second, it has to work as ex ante guidance for speakers who are not in a position to tell whether they know: the asserters I care about are institutional — a press office, an expert panel, an automated announcement system — rather than individuals in conversation. I take it that second job tells against the best-known candidate norm, and I want to see how much it really tells against it, not just that it does.

Here are the cases I have on my own list; give me yours before you look at mine, and tell me which of mine are genuine constraints and which are only demands I have smuggled in from a favoured theory. The Moorean pair ("the meeting is at noon and I don't believe it is") and the standard challenge data — that "How do you know?" is the canonical retort, that "but it's true, though" does not answer it, and that "you don't believe that" reads as an accusation of insincerity rather than a criticism of the assertion as such. The Gettierised and fake-barn asserter, and the careful asserter whose well-supported claim turns out false: the norm needs to be able to mark something as defective without marking it as blameworthy, and I want to know whether that distinction is a requirement or a repair. The selfless assertion — the friend who must tell a bereaved owner that the dog died on the table on the strength of hearsay she would normally discount. Assertion on merely statistical evidence: the administrator who can see that 499 of 500 tickets were paid and asserts, of this ticket, that it was. The author who asserts every sentence of her book and also asserts, in the preface, that at least one of them is false — does the norm require global consistency across a speaker's assertions, or is that a demand that only looks compelling if you already have belief in view? The spokesperson asserting for a committee whose evidence is distributed across members, no one of whom knows; the automated departure board or generated readout, where nothing believes anything at all; the pre-recorded statement or will, released after the speaker is dead and after circumstances have changed so that what was asserted is now false and known to be false by everyone, with no possibility of retraction, update or ordinary blame; the sealed diary and the assertion made in a language no one present understands, where there is no uptake. Hedging — why "I think it's probably p" lowers the bar, and whether it lowers it all the way to nothing. And the speaker who asserts with complete indifference to whether things are so, but who happens to believe it and happens to be right.

Then the two things I most want. First, tell me which single requirement excludes the most candidates, and say plainly which of the things people demand of a norm in this area are real and which are the shibboleths of a camp. Second, and this is the part I would read twice: is any subset of the desiderata jointly unsatisfiable — is there a pair or a triple that a norm cannot deliver together, so that the list itself becomes a result? I have a suspicion there is a trilemma somewhere in the vicinity of (a) explaining the knowledge-challenge and Moorean data, (b) being followable by a speaker who cannot tell whether she knows, and (c) staying invariant across stakes while still handling the statistical-evidence cases; if that is not where the real conflict lies, tell me where it is. Close with a short scorecard — a few lines each, no padding — for the knowledge rule as Williamson defends it, the truth rule, the belief rule, and the warranted-assertability or reasonableness rule. The criteria are the deliverable, not the scoring.
