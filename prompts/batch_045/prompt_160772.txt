I have no academic background — I'm a retired logistics manager who got hooked on this after reading a popular book about consciousness, and I've been chewing on it for two years, mostly in arguments with a friend of mine who has lived with severe chronic pain for fifteen years. The view I've ended up with is this: the badness of a pain is not a property of how the pain feels — not its intensity, and not even its felt unpleasantness considered as a quality of experience — but a matter of what I've started calling its practical grip, the degree to which the pain reorganizes what a subject can attend to, want, and get done. My main evidence is the phenomenon of pain asymbolia: patients who, after certain kinds of brain injury or surgery, still report the stabbing, burning, crushing character of the pain and can describe its location and intensity perfectly well, but say it no longer bothers them and no longer moves them to avoid anything. I want to say these subjects still have the sensation and have lost the badness, which shows badness was never in the feeling. I also lean on the looser case of people given strong opioid analgesia who say the pain is still there but no longer matters to them, and on a made-up mirror case I've been using on my friend, "reverse asymbolia": a subject whose felt unpleasantness is undiminished but whose grip has been severed, so the pain is screamingly awful to undergo yet changes nothing about what they do or care about. My claim is that this subject is not badly off in the way that matters. I take the further step that unpleasantness is bad only derivatively, as the ordinary mechanism by which grip gets imposed — so anything that produces the grip without the feeling (a future brain-computer interface, a non-conscious agent with the right practical architecture) would have something genuinely bad happening to it, and anything with the feeling but no grip would have nothing bad happening to it at all.

I've been asked to give a forty-minute talk with twenty minutes of questions to a pain support and advocacy group that meets monthly in a hospital library, and I'd like a prep sheet for the question period. The room will be about thirty people: mostly patients living with chronic pain and their partners, along with a pain-medicine physician who sits on the group's advisory board, the nurse practitioner who runs the meeting, and a clinical psychologist who does acceptance-based pain management and who has told the group more than once that the goal is not to abolish the sensation but to stop it running your life. So I'll get objections from at least four directions: patients who have spent years being told their pain is psychological and who will hear my thesis as one more version of that; a physician who will press the definitional point that if a state isn't unpleasant it isn't pain at all, and who will want to know whether my asymbolic cases are rare, heterogeneous, and possibly just confabulated reports; a psychologist who will say I've redescribed what her therapy already achieves and will ask what my view predicts that hers doesn't; and a nurse who will ask what any of this is supposed to change about whether we sedate a patient who can't report. I have a bad habit of talking too fast under pressure, so I want replies pitched at roughly ninety seconds of speech.

What I'd like from you is a prep sheet, not a defence of the view. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they would do if I handled them badly, each attributed to the kind of person who will raise it and phrased the way that person would actually phrase it rather than in philosophical jargon. For each: the short reply I should give out loud, and a note on where that reply is soft or where I'm quietly changing the subject. Tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes, before anyone asks anything, so that the room isn't defended against me from the start; tell me which single reply has to land if the talk is going to survive the question period; and tell me which objections have real force against the grip account as opposed to just being heat. Please also include, and help me answer, the question I am secretly hoping nobody asks: someone whose pain has forced them to give up work, sport, and a lot of their independence saying, in front of the room, that if badness is just grip then their pain apparently isn't bad any more now that they have stopped fighting it, and asking me whether I think they are suffering less than they believe they are.
