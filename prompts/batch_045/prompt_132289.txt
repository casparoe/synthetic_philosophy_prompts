I work on the sustainability team at a mid-sized food manufacturer — protein bars, pasta, a couple of frozen things. Part of my job is maintaining an internal sourcing scorecard that every new ingredient has to pass through: 30 or 40 decisions a year, each one gets about half a day of an analyst's time, one page of output, then it goes to a VP who signs off and occasionally to a retail customer or an NGO who wants to see the working. It already has columns for carbon, water, land use, and a vertebrate welfare column that's basically numbers × welfare range × confidence. Crude, but nobody fights about it.

The fight now is insects. We use cricket flour in one bar and black soldier fly larvae meal in a pasta, and we keep getting asked — internally, and by customers — whether insect deaths belong in the scorecard and with what weight. The case for is the scale thing: per tonne the individual count is somewhere in the high six figures (our own numbers, could easily be off by an order of magnitude), so a 1% or even 0.1% probability that they're sentient, times a small welfare range, gives an expected welfare cost that swamps the vertebrate column entirely. Every EV calc we run comes out saying insect numbers dominate everything else we do, that we should basically reorganise the supply chain around not farming insects, and also conceivably around reducing ant and termite populations on the land we source from — which is not something anyone here will put in a supplier contract.

I've read enough of the forum material on this to know the names for the problem — fanaticism, Pascal's mugging, cluelessness, moral uncertainty, the worry that wild insect lives are net negative so the sign of the effect flips depending on who you ask. One analyst has proposed an "anti-fanaticism cap" (no single category can move the total score by more than X) and another says that's just cowardice with a formula attached. "Apply your favourite decision theory harder" is not something I can put in front of a VP.

So: give me a procedure my team can actually run. Ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the thing to a human because the question is one of values or of sign-of-effect rather than of fact. Constraints: runnable by someone with no philosophy background in half a day; must not depend on any single contested empirical claim about insect sentience (tell me if that's impossible); has to be defensible to a skeptical CFO and to an animal-welfare NGO looking over the same page.

Then run it on three live-ish cases. (1) Swapping the cricket flour in the bar for pea protein. (2) Our larvae supplier offering to switch kill method from freezing to a gradual-chilling process at a cost premium — is that worth paying for? (3) Moving our plant protein to a certified "regenerative" source that skips insecticides but needs roughly 40% more land, where more land plausibly means more wild insects, whose lives may be net negative. I have a suspicion about which one gives an answer I won't like; tell me if I'm right.

Two more things. Mark the places where judgement genuinely can't be eliminated and where the procedure will misfire, and say plainly what it can't do. And separate what's actually settled among people who work on this from what's contested — I'd rather have an honest "this is a live dispute and here's the one-line version of each side" than a smooth consensus that doesn't exist. Also flag which empirical results, if they landed either way, would change the procedure rather than just the numbers going into it.
