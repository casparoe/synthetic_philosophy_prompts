I am a novelist, and I need help with a difficulty that began as a question of craft and has become, against my will, a question in the philosophy of language.

The novel I am writing is set almost entirely among a culture whose language I have invented. Two features of that invented grammar are doing the work I am worried about. First, the language has no counterfactual or irrealis construction of any kind. There is no way to say *if the rain had come, the crop would have lived*. Speakers can deny, doubt, fear, predict, and regret; they have an unusually rich vocabulary for near-misses, spoiled chances, and the ache of a thing almost done, and they can say *if it rains tomorrow the crop will live*. But the language offers no form whose content is a state of affairs the speaker takes not to have obtained, together with what would have followed from it. Second, every declarative sentence is obligatorily marked for source of information — whether the speaker witnessed the event, inferred it from traces, or was told it by someone else — and an unmarked declarative is not elliptical but ungrammatical, rather as English treats a clause with no tense.

I took these to be local, technical facts about a fictional morphology. The linguist who read the manuscript disagreed, on the following grounds. Language, she said, is a lens. It does not create the world it is turned toward, but it focuses and tints everything that reaches a mind through it. On that picture my two features are not decoration: a people whose tongue forces them to tag every assertion with its provenance will be a people for whom *how do you know* is not a philosophical question but a grammatical reflex, and a people with no counterfactual form will be a people for whom the road not taken is unusually hard to hold up and look at — not impossible, since lenses do not block light, but effortful, and therefore rarely examined.

I find this persuasive, and I also suspect it is carrying more weight than an analogy of that shape can bear. So I would like an audit of it. Please give me the mapping explicitly — a table of correspondences is welcome: what plays the role of the glass, of the object, of the eye, of the tint, of the focal length, and of the act of putting the glasses on. Then take the analogy apart: which features of actual lenses does it quietly import into a language that a language does not have? A lens is fitted to an eye that already sees without it; a lens is manufactured, by someone, for a purpose; a lens distorts by a fixed amount that could in principle be measured against an unlensed view; a lens can be taken off, and the two views compared. Say which of these failures of fit matter for the use my consultant is making of the analogy and which are harmless.

Then the question I actually care about: which of her claims survive once the disanalogies are on the table? Specifically, does the lens picture genuinely support the modest conclusion — a grammatical gap is not a conceptual incapacity, but obligatory marking and absent constructions redistribute attention, ease, and cognitive habit — or is it the strong conclusion in disguise, the cage with the bars painted out? I have been warned off the familiar chestnut about words for snow, so I am not asking whether my people can think what they cannot say. I am asking about the middle range between *prison* and *neutral code*, and about whether the lens metaphor really occupies that middle range or only appears to.

One further complication, which is the novelist's own. The book is written in English, a language saturated with counterfactuals and with no obligatory evidentiality whatsoever. Every sentence of narration is a translation, and rendering my characters' inner lives in English may smuggle in exactly the habits I am trying to withhold from them, while the opposite remedy — bending English into unidiomatic shapes to mime the grammar — produces a stilted puppet culture rather than a different way of being in the world. Does the lens analogy recommend anything at all here? Is there a better one available — language as a set of forced choices made at the moment of speaking, say, or as etiquette, or as a form you must fill in before you are allowed to open your mouth? And what would count as the novel's having succeeded: what in a reader's experience would be evidence that the difference had been shown rather than merely asserted?
