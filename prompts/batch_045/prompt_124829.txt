I am a high school senior writing an independent research paper on constitutional design, and my advisor keeps sending it back with the same note: before I can argue that one arrangement is better than another, I have to say what better means. I would like your help building that standard, because every version I write myself quietly assumes the conclusion I already like.

The purpose I want the standard to serve: a country is drafting a constitution from scratch and must choose how the executive and the legislature are to be related. The usual three options are a presidential arrangement, in which a separately elected head of state also heads the government and holds office for a fixed term; a parliamentary one, in which the head of government holds office only while the legislature is willing to tolerate them; and various hybrids in which a directly elected president shares executive power with a government answerable to the legislature. I already know these rough categories and don't need them defined. What I want is a specification: what a theory of executive-legislative design has to be able to do before it is good enough to give actual drafting advice to that country, as opposed to merely sorting existing countries into boxes.

Please write the list of requirements. For each one, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say whether you think it is non-negotiable or merely a preference that happens to be dear to people who already favor one of the three arrangements. I especially want your judgment on three things: which of the requirements that get asserted in this debate are real desiderata and which are partisan prejudices in disguise; whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, so that the list itself proves that no system can have everything (if so, tell me exactly which clique of requirements breaks); and which single requirement does the most work in ruling out otherwise attractive designs.

Here are the cases I have been worrying about, to use as raw material rather than as a checklist to copy:

One, people use the word stability for at least three different things - cabinets falling, constitutions collapsing, and policy lurching from one direction to another - and a theory that doesn't separate them will recommend the wrong thing. Two, there is the question of who the voter is supposed to blame: whether the arrangement makes responsibility clear enough to be used at the ballot box, or whether it lets each side point at the other. Three, there is the opposite demand: whether a government that has turned out to be incompetent, corrupt, or dangerous can be got rid of before its term runs out, and by whom. Four, whatever rule is chosen has to say what happens when the president's party does not control the legislature, and separately what happens when no party controls it. Five, it has to handle the clash of mandates, when each branch can plausibly claim to be the more recent or more direct voice of the voters. Six, it must not quietly assume good faith: assume the actors will exploit every provision to its limit, threaten elections they would lose, and dare the other side to blink. Seven, it has to work in the boring case as well as the dramatic one - routine legislation, budget deadlines, an executive who is merely mediocre rather than violent - because most of the life of any system is spent there. Eight, it should notice that the power to dismiss a government and the power to dissolve a legislature and call new elections are different powers, and that whether they are symmetric or asymmetric matters a great deal.

To keep the list from degenerating into a description of systems that already exist, please test it against three arrangements I invented myself and have not seen described anywhere. The first is the weakened veto: a president may return a bill, but the majority needed to override it shrinks each time the legislature passes the same bill again in the same session, so that persistence alone eventually carries it. The second is the mutual key: the head of government may call an early election only if the legislature, having dismissed them, fails within a set window to name a replacement who can command a majority - the aim being to make dismissal and dissolution genuinely reciprocal. The third is the decaying caretaker: when a government is dismissed, it stays in office with automatically narrowing powers, losing a scheduled slice of its authority every week that no successor is approved, so that the cost of deadlock falls on whoever is stalling. I want the desiderata to be strong enough to tell me what is genuinely wrong with these, if anything is, and at least two of the requirements on your list should be ones that thinking about these three cases is what brings into view.

Two further constraints. Please don't give me a country-by-country comparison or score the existing families against the list at any length - one or two sentences each is plenty, and I would rather have the criteria themselves argued properly, since that is the part I cannot do alone. And please argue from cases and from reasoning any careful reader could follow rather than from research findings, author names, or things I would have to look up; I have no access to a library from where I am writing, so citations and reading lists would be useless to me, and I would rather have an argument I can check myself than a citation I can't.
