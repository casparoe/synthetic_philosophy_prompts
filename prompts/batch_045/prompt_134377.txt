I write literary fiction, and a scene in the novel I am finishing turns on a small, exact effect: the reader is to grant a character knowledge of one thing, watch her competently deduce something further from it, and yet withhold knowledge of the conclusion — not because she is careless, self-deceiving, or irrational, but because of how the counterfactual structure of her situation happens to lie. I want to earn that effect rather than assert it, and I want it to be an instance of something genuinely defensible rather than a private intuition of mine. The account I want it built against is Nozick's tracking analysis of knowledge: roughly, that a subject knows a proposition, by a given method, when the proposition is true, she believes it by that method, she would not believe it by that method were it false, and she would believe it by that method were it true — with the method held fixed across the counterfactual shift. The analysis is known for refusing to let knowledge close under known entailment, and that refusal is the phenomenon I want on the page.

Please construct the case to the following specifications.

1. Realism. One scene, one location, an ordinary adult in ordinary circumstances, and no skeptical apparatus: no brains in vats, dreams, demons, simulations, duplicates, or fabricated environments, and no fake-barn or lottery structure. But the stakes must be real enough that whether she knows matters, because she will act on it before the scene ends.

2. She must know the premise. State P and the method M by which she believes it; identify the nearest worlds in which P is false and show that M would not there deliver the belief, and that in nearby worlds where P is true it would. Say explicitly how finely or coarsely you are individuating M, and flag that the verdict depends on that choice.

3. The deduction must occur. She must actually draw Q from P on the page, in a way a reader would find natural rather than staged, and P must genuinely entail Q rather than merely support it. Her method for believing Q is inference from P; tell me what becomes of the verdict if that method is instead described so as to include the method by which she knows P.

4. She must fail to know Q. Identify the nearest worlds in which Q is false, explain why they lie further from the actual situation than the nearest worlds in which P is false, and show that in them she would still believe Q. I take this asymmetry to be the engine of the case; if it is not, say what is.

5. She must also track the entailment itself, and I need to know whether she does so substantively or only because the worlds in which that conditional is false are extremely remote. If the latter, say so, and tell me whether I should be embarrassed to build a scene on it.

6. She must have an unexercised way of checking Q — a call she could make, a register she could consult — and must not use it. Tell me whether its mere availability restores knowledge on these conditions, and if it does, what the scene must contain in order to neutralize it without making her negligent.

7. She acts on Q, and the action is reasonable. Say whether the framework can hold reasonable to act on apart from known here, and what in the account does that work.

Deliver the scene itself, in prose, no longer than about five hundred words, since I need to see whether it can carry this weight. Then give me a compact apparatus: P, Q, M, the grain at which M is described, and, for each of P, the entailment, and Q, which of the four conditions holds and which fails, with the relevant nearby worlds described. Mark the single feature doing the work, and say what happens to the verdict if it is removed — in particular, if the nearest worlds in which Q is false were also the nearest worlds in which P is false.

Then give me the strongest attacks, and answer them: that the verdict is an artifact of how finely the method is described; that any account on which a locally undetectable but wildly unlikely falsifier destroys knowledge is skepticism with the science fiction removed; and that a conclusion drawn by someone who knows the premise and knows the entailment inherits whatever tracking the premise had. Tell me honestly which of these you think succeeds.

That second attack worries me most, because it may collide with my first constraint: I have banned skeptical apparatus, yet the mechanism seems to require a remote, undetectable falsifier. Is there such a thing as a non-skeptical closure failure on this account at all, or does every case of this shape become a skeptical case with the science fiction taken out? If the latter, say it plainly and give me the case that minimizes the resemblance rather than one that pretends to escape it.

Finally, a note on craft, since the scene will reach readers with no commentary attached: how it must be written so that a reader's attributions land where the apparatus says they should, what must be shown and what withheld. Specifically: whether the character's merely thinking of the remote possibility changes the verdict; whether making that possibility salient to the reader, by naming it in the narration, brings it closer and thereby restores the knowledge I am trying to withhold, which would be a case of the writing defeating the effect; and where, in the last resort, the novelist has to cheat.

I have no access to the literature and no wish to be directed to it: please do not rely on my recognizing any canonical example, and do not cite anything. The case has to be new and to carry its own justification in the terms I have set out.
