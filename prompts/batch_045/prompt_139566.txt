I'm finishing a novel whose narrator has spent fifteen years conserving painted scrolls in a museum store, and in the last chapter she has to read something at a memorial for her mother — a translator of Madhyamaka texts whose notebooks she has spent the whole book avoiding. The narrator doesn't share her mother's convictions and is allergic to anything that sounds serene, so what I need is a poem she could plausibly have written and that her openly skeptical sister could read aloud without wincing: no Sanskrit, no technical vocabulary, no incense, no mountains. What I'd like you to write is a pantoum — four or five quatrains, with the strict repeating scheme in which the second and fourth lines of each stanza become the first and third lines of the next, and an interlaced close such that the poem's last line is also its first — in which each of the first four stanzas is inhabited by one limb of Nāgārjuna's tetralemma: that a thing is, that it is not, that it both is and is not, that it is neither. The point is for the form to carry the doctrine rather than decorate it. Because the pantoum's lines keep coming back in changed company, each recurring line should be readable as the same line and as a different one, which is what I take the claim that things are empty — dependently arisen, possessing no nature of their own — to amount to; and the interlaced ending should read as a return that the fourth stanza has made impossible to read as a plain return. Two failures I want avoided: emptiness as nothingness, blankness, or indifference, and the fourth limb as a shrugging "it's all a mystery." The passage I keep failing at is the third limb — I can make "both is and is not" sound like paradox and like poetry, but not like something a careful philosopher could actually hold, and my beta readers say the poem currently goes mystical at exactly that point. (One note in her mother's margin that the narrator does read: the four limbs are not rungs, she has written, and the last is not the highest.)

After the poem, in a few sentences of prose, tell me which reading of the tetralemma you have committed the verse to — in particular whether you have treated the fourth limb as one more position alongside the other three or as a refusal to take any position at all, and whether "empty" in your lines means dependently arisen, conventionally designated, or something weaker you can defend — and flag the places where the poem leans on a contested reading, or where the rhyme bought you something the argument hadn't earned. Then say what would change the poem: is there a version of the emptiness claim, or an objection to it, on which the poem as you've written it would be the wrong poem, and which stanza would you rewrite first?
