Okay, I got roped onto our Center for Teaching and Learning's innovation-grant panel, and the chair wants a decision memo circulated by Friday. I'm the token philosopher and I ended up holding the pen, so I need something the rest of the panel can actually vote on.

Here's the application. A team of three (two from educational psychology, one from the business school) wants roughly 180k over three years for a "generation-aware pedagogy" program: a faculty workshop, a "learner profile" handbook, a dozen course-redesign stipends, and a pre/post evaluation using their own survey instrument. The pitch is that our undergrads are now almost entirely Gen Z, that this cohort has stable and measurable traits — shorter sustained attention, higher baseline anxiety, expectation of flexible deadlines and fast feedback, preference for digital-first and modular content — and that teaching should be systematically tailored to those traits. The panel is split right down the middle. Half think this is junk science with a budget attached: the categories are arbitrary slices, the evidence is cross-sectional self-report, and we'd be paying to write stereotypes into syllabi. The other half point out that faculty already run on tacit generational theories, so making them explicit, shared, and testable beats letting everyone improvise.

I have my own hobby-horse about whether cohorts are real kinds at all, and I recieved an email from our dean asking whether "generation" does any work that "person who was nineteen in 2021" doesn't. But what I need from you is the memo, not a seminar. So: draft me the panel's decision — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with reasons the applicants could respond to rather than just appeal from, and with a concrete time frame and any conditions spelled out at a level I could hand to them.

A few things I want it to get right, because I can already hear both sides:

First, the confound. Half of what the proposal calls a generational trait looks to me like it could be an age effect (people at nineteen), a period effect (people whose schooling was disrupted by the pandemic, which cuts across birth years), or a cohort effect proper. Tell me what the memo should demand by way of evidence to separate those, and what we should do if the applicants can't separate them within three years — is that a reason to fund the study or a reason not to fund the pedagogy?

Second, I don't want the memo leaning on a bad argument. Someone on the panel says the categories are fake because the boundary years are arbitrary and nobody can say whether a student born in late 1996 is a millennial. I think that's weak on its own — plenty of real classifications have fuzzy edges, and the cutoff years move by a couple of years depending on which outfit you ask. Say whether that's an objection worth putting in writing, and what the real objection in the neighborhood is.

Third, the feedback worry. If we train faculty that Gen Z students are a certain way, tell students about it in workshops, and hand out a profile, then the profile may partly produce the thing it describes, which would make their evaluation look like a success no matter what. Is that an argument against funding it, or just the normal way a social kind behaves? I'd rather the memo say something honest here than dodge it.

Fourth, the misfits. We have a lot of international students, transfer students, veterans, and people in their thirties in our undergrad population; several panel members worry that a cohort profile is either silent about them or actively wrong about them, and that teachers will apply it anyway because it's the only profile they have. What condition, if any, actually handles that?

Finally, give whichever side you come down against its best day in court — I need the losing argument stated strongly enough that its supporters on the panel feel represented, since I have to get five votes. And please don't pad it with references; the panel won't chase citations and I'd rather have the argument than a bibliography.
