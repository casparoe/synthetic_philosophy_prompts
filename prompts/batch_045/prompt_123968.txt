I'm in a little reading group on rationality and social epistemology (mostly grad students plus one postdoc), and after a guest talk on polarization two of the others got into an argument at the pub that I've been turning over since. I'm rewiting it from memory so the wording won't be exact, but the moves were roughly these. Marta was defending the line that persistent disagreement between epistemic peers is always a symptom rather than a position: (1) if two people share a prior, trust each other's reasoning and honesty, and it's common knowledge what each of them thinks the probability of some claim is, then those probabilities have to be equal — so if two such people are still apart, one of the conditions has failed, and in practice what failed is that somebody is being moved by something they haven't put on the table (loyalties, career incentives, considerations they'd be embarrassed to say out loud). "We'll just have to agree to disagree" is, on her view, a way of hiding that. (2) Yusuf said the common prior is doing all the work and no two actual people have ever shared one — they've lived different lives, so the theorem simply doesn't apply to real disagreements, and it therefore has no normative force at all. (3) Marta replied that the common prior is much less demanding than it sounds: any difference in priors over a shared set of possibilities can be redescribed as a common prior over a bigger space plus different private evidence, i.e. treat the fact that you started at 0.3 and I started at 0.7 as itself just more data each of us received. So "we had different priors" isn't an excuse that blocks the theorem. (4) Yusuf: if you can always rescue the common prior by inventing hidden signals, then the assumption can never fail, and an assumption that can't fail can't explain anything — so the theorem can't be the diagnostic she wants, it's at best a consistency constraint on a model. (5) Marta said the bite was never in the prior anyway, it's in the common-knowledge requirement: it's not enough that I know your number and you know mine, the numbers have to be common knowledge — I know that you know that I know, and so on without end — and what actually breaks down in real arguments is that tower, because people announce rounded, hedged numbers and never say what they'd bet at. That's a checkable lesson and it survives his unfalsifiability point. (6) Yusuf came back with two things: no real conversation reaches infinite common knowledge either, and the convergence result for back-and-forth announcement of numbers is only proved for finite setups with two agents and a fixed event, so we shouldn't assume it generalizes; and even granting the limit, all you get is agreement on that one event, which says nothing about whether they agree about anything else, and two people who both say 0.6 can still disagree completely about what to do. (7) Then Marta kind of half-conceded: the whole formalism assumes a single space of possibilities that both people's beliefs are defined over, and in the disagreements she actually cares about — whether a research program is dead, what counts as a cause of some outcome — the parties don't share a hypothesis space at all, since one person's live options include possibilities the other hasn't even represented, so there's no common space for a prior to live on and the theorem is silent exactly where the action is.
