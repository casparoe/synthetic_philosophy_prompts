I want a stress test of a position I keep defending in arguments about long-term policy — climate mitigation, pandemic preparedness, risks from new technologies. Call it Temporal Neutrality, and let me state it in two parts so that you can attack the seams.

First, the neutrality claim: the date at which a given person's well-being occurs is not in itself a reason to count that well-being for less. A positive pure rate of social time preference — the delta term in the familiar Ramsey-style decomposition of the social discount rate into a pure-preference term, a growth term, and a curvature term — strikes me as having the same status as a discount rate that varied with the recipient's birth cohort: not a parameter to be estimated but a claim in need of a defense, and the defenses of it that I've encountered seem to me to smuggle in something else.

Second, the contingency claim: neutrality does not entail a zero discount rate. Future benefits may never be received — the recipients may never exist, the institution or ecosystem that delivers the benefit may not persist, the project may be abandoned or reversed, the civilization may not survive. All of that licenses discounting, but only as expected-value discounting on the probability that the benefit is actually realized and actually lands on somebody. This is also, as I understand it, why the effective social discount rate should decline with the horizon rather than stay flat: if you are uncertain about the hazard rate at which benefits get interrupted, then the survival curve you get by averaging over the possible rates has a hazard that falls over time, so the further out you look, the lower the rate you should apply at the margin.

What I want from you is a map of the exposure of this position, not a survey of the literature and not a list of things to read.

Which considerations — arguments, formal results, empirical findings, implications I would find unacceptable — should move me, and which are merely awkward? Rank them from fatal to cosmetic. For each, say whether a defender of the position should concede that it counts against them or can reasonably dismiss it. I don't want a catalogue of objections that only converts people who already disagree with me.

Three candidate defeaters I want taken seriously. One: I've been pointed to an old impossibility result, which I think is associated with the name Diamond, about ranking infinite streams of well-being, and told that it sinks any form of temporal neutrality. I cannot reconstruct the argument and I may have the attribution wrong; reconstruct it if you can, and tell me whether it tells against neutrality itself or only against a particular way of aggregating that I could give up while keeping neutrality. Two: the non-identity thought — for most long-term policies, who exists later depends on what we do now, so there may be no fixed future people whose later well-being I am urging you to weight equally. Does that go to the heart of the neutrality claim, or only to the applications I care about? Three: the claim that location within a life matters non-instrumentally, that some goods are worth more for arriving at a certain stage of a life and that the shape of a life is partly constitutive of a person's good. If that is right, then a single person's well-being may not be a date-neutral sum in the first place, and the neutrality claim fails before we ever reach policy. This is the objection I find hardest and I am not sure I can answer it.

Then be honest about insulation. My worry is that the contingency claim is too accommodating: because some contingency story is always available, any observed discount rate can be retro-fitted with a hazard rate, and the position may predict nothing and risk nothing. If it has been qualified into unfalsifiability, say so, and say whether that is a defect in a normative claim of this kind or only what one should expect from one. If it does risk something, tell me precisely what observation or argument would make me abandon it. A test case, if it helps: spending a small fraction of current consumption now to avert a much larger consumption loss two centuries out, where there is a real chance the institutions needed to deliver the benefit will not last that long. If the position cannot yield a verdict there without my supplying further judgments, that is itself worth knowing.
