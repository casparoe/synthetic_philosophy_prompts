I've been reading popular books about how children pick up language entirely on my own — I have no academic background in any of this, just a stubborn interest — and I keep meeting the same argument, usually credited to Chomsky and often called the poverty of the stimulus. It seemed convincing, so I wrote it out in numbered steps to locate exactly where my discomfort sits, and the result worries me: when I run it past the case it was built for, it appears to prove that essentially nothing is ever learned. I don't want a reading list or citations; I want the shape of the mistake. Here is my reconstruction:

(1) By five or so, every normal child in a community has converged on a rich, specific system of rules — rules that go far beyond anything they were taught, that they apply to sentences they have never heard, and that they cannot state.
(2) The speech available to them is finite, full of fragments and false starts, and carries almost no information about which sentences are impossible: nobody supplies a list of ungrammatical strings, explicit corrections of form are rare, and children mostly ignore them anyway.
(3) For any finite batch of sentences, there are endlessly many distinct rule systems that produce exactly those sentences and disagree about every sentence not yet encountered. So the heard data never logically force the system the child arrives at.
(4) If the outcome is reliable, detailed, and not forced by the data, then something besides the data must be doing the forcing, and the only candidate on offer is the child's own innate, language-specific endowment.
(5) Nothing in (1)–(3) restricts the argument to the rules all languages share. Run it on the variable ones — whether the verb precedes or follows its object, whether adjectives sit before or after the noun, whether questions are formed by moving something or by adding a particle. For each of these, the data a given child hears underdetermine the answer in exactly the sense of (3), and for each of these the children of a community converge with one another.
(6) So the child must be innately endowed with those language-specific rules too: a bias that makes Japanese order inevitable for a child born in Osaka and English order inevitable for a child born in Leeds.
(7) But the very same child, swapped at birth, grows up with the other language; thousands of languages differ on exactly these variable bits; and the logic clearly does not stop at word order, since children also converge on the local greeting etiquette, the local money system, the local way of dividing up the colour spectrum and the local food taboos, which nobody wants to call innate.
(8) So either (4) is false and the argument establishes nothing at all, or it is true and "innate" has quietly stretched to cover every single thing a person picks up — in which case nativism wins by becoming vacuous.

I would much rather keep (1), (2) and (3) than (4). It really does seem to me that the input is scrappy and unlabelled, and that a finite pile of examples cannot logically single out one rule system from the endless crowd that fit them equally well. But (4) strikes me as the problem restated in the shape of a solution, and (6) through (8) I flatly reject. A friend says the trouble is that "underdetermined by the data" is doing two different jobs — once when the data are measured against every logically possible grammar, and once when they are measured against the few the child is willing to entertain — and that once you separate these, (3) and (5) are no longer making the same claim. That sounds sharp, but I cannot tell whether it is a diagnosis or just a way of declaring which side he is on.

There is one further case that makes the choice harder, and it is one I invented rather than read about, so please treat it purely as hypothetical. Imagine a small community whose adults communicate with a makeshift, inconsistent system of gestures — no shared order, nobody signing quite like anybody else — and imagine that their children, left mainly to play with each other, settle on something systematic, with a fixed order and consistent markers, which the adults then never fully master. If that happened, the children's system was present in no one's output, so it was not transmitted in the ordinary sense; but it also cannot be a setting pre-installed to fit the languages of our species' past, because it would be brand new. Does a case like that push you to reject a different step than the ordinary one, or is it simply (5) again with the variables re-described?

In at most 600 words: lead with the number of the step you reject and one sentence saying what goes wrong there; explain why that step looked as though it followed; say what rejecting it costs someone who wants the nativist conclusion, and what rejecting a different step instead would cost someone who does not; tell me whether the argument, once repaired, establishes anything real about innateness; and say whether my invented community changes any of the above. Assume I know the standard vocabulary and nothing beyond it.
