I spend most of my week as an educator at a memorial museum attached to one of the twentieth century's large-scale killings, and I'm finishing a master's thesis on how comparative claims about mass atrocity get policed in public discourse. I keep bumping into a pattern I cannot find a name for, and I want help carving it rather than labelling it. The rough shape is that comparisons are forbidden in one direction and compulsory in the other. When a visiting teacher asks my students which of two atrocities was worse, the question is treated as indecent and I have to deflect it; yet the same people who treat that question as indecent will say without hesitation that the event my museum commemorates was unprecedented, or the worst crime of its century, or a rupture after which nothing was the same. Those are comparative claims too — a superlative is a ranking — but they are not merely permitted, they are close to obligatory in commemorative speech; an exhibition text that presented the event as one atrocity among many would read as an insult. Something similar happens with the category *genocide*: campaigners fight bitterly to have their community's catastrophe counted under that term and resent it when it is withheld, which only makes sense if the term marks a high rank, and yet the same campaigners denounce the very idea of ranking atrocities.

There is a second asymmetry, along a different axis. Comparisons of kind are respectable scholarship — typologies, definitions, disputes about whether a given episode counts as extermination, as ethnic cleansing, as a crime against humanity — while comparisons of magnitude are the ones that get called obscene: a panel on classificatory questions draws no protest, whereas a table of death tolls draws an angry letter. But classification carries rank with it, since a category that groups compete to enter is not a neutral box. So the taboo looks like it attaches to a dimension of comparison rather than to comparison as such.

I've been calling this one-way comparativism, but I invented that, and it doesn't separate the two axes. What I want is the carving, not a label: which established concepts are the near neighbours here, and in exactly what respect does each one miss? I've considered and set aside two in plain terms — the competitive scramble between groups for victim status, and the deflection move where someone answers a criticism of their own side by pointing at the other side's worse crimes — since the first is about who wants the top slot and the second about evading blame, whereas what I'm after is the norm that makes comparison itself permissible upward and forbidden downward. If nothing established fits, I'd like a coined term with a definition that handles the boundary cases: a comparison between two harms inside a single community's own history; a comparison drawn by a survivor rather than by an outsider; a comparison framed explicitly as being about prevention rather than magnitude; and the same asymmetry well outside atrocity, where people refuse to rank a friend's grief or a revered figure's achievement while freely using superlatives about them. And I'd like your view on whether the norm can be stated as a non-self-serving principle at all, or whether any formulation of it will turn out to license precisely the comparisons its holder already wanted to make. Please don't give me a bibliography or tell me whom to read — I have no library access from here, and it's the analysis I'm after.
