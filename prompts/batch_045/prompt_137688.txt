I've got a 35-minute slot next week in our internal engineering-leadership program, twenty-ish newly promoted managers, and I want to build it around the dollar auction — the one where the prize goes to the highest bidder but the runner-up pays their bid too and gets nothing. Every one of them was handed, in the last month, a project that's already over budget and behind schedule, and nobody above them will say whether to push on or kill it, so this isn't hypothetical for them. Don't explain the auction to me; I know it. Help me plan how I teach it.

Two things make it harder than the usual version.

First, I don't want anyone walking out thinking the lesson is 'sunk costs are a fallacy' or 'don't get into stupid situations.' They can't un-enter a project they inherited, and in their actual jobs pushing on is sometimes exactly right. The distinction I care about is between the structural thing — a payoff rule where at every single node stepping forward looks better than stopping, for both of you at once — and the psychological thing, the 'we've already spent too much to quit' feeling, which can show up with no such structure at all and can also push people to quit too early. I want them to be able to look at a real case of their own and say which one is doing the work.

Second, I'm not sure I should run the plain version. Option A: standard, known one-dollar prize. Option B: sealed prize — I write a number between 50 cents and five dollars on a card, they can see the range, and I only reveal it after bidding stops. My worry about A is that someone smart-alecs a bid of 99 cents, or announces up front that they'd never go past a dollar, and the session turns into a lesson about greed or about that one guy. My worry about B is that it starts to feel like a gamble and the mechanism gets muddy. Tell me which to run, or tell me both are wrong and give me a third.

What I want from you:

- The order the pieces have to go in and why — what has to be in place before the first bid, and what depends on what. My guess is the payment rule has to land before anyone hears the word 'auction,' and that they need the other bidder's payoff in front of them before they think about their own, but argue with me if that's wrong.
- The one case or example that should carry the whole thing, and what it has to have and lack. It should read as their situation, not as an auction.
- The misconceptions to expect and exactly where in the sequence to head each off. The ones I'm anticipating: that this is just the sunk-cost fallacy with extra steps; that the fix is a budget cap or a pre-commitment (this one I think is the trickiest, because a limit you impose on yourself is revisable at exactly the moment it starts to bind — tell me if I've got that backwards); that once the bids pass a dollar both sides can see the other is being irrational so surely they'll stop; that it's really the winner's curse; and that backward induction hands you a clean answer about what to bid first.
- One question for the last five minutes that shows me whether they got the distinction and not just a sinking feeling. My instinct is to hand them one case where escalating is clearly correct and one where it's clearly not and ask what structural difference seperates them — is that the right test, and what would a confident-but-wrong answer sound like?
- What I should openly refuse to explain in 35 minutes, and say out loud that I'm refusing.

No game theory background, no slides beyond one whiteboard, and they're mildly skeptical of leadership training generally. They will definately try to solve the whole thing by being the reasonable adult in the room, so plan around that.
