hey — I'm running the last session of a grad reading group on early modern philosophy of perception next week, and we're ending on Molyneux's problem. I'm sick of the way it always gets done: someone restates the setup (man blind from birth, can tell a cube from a sphere by touch, gets his sight, can he tell wich is wich before touching them), then we trade empiricist vs rationalist answers for an hour and go home. I want to close with a poem instead and I'd like it to actually carry the difficulty rather than gesture at it.

Could you write me a sestina — six stanzas of six plus a three line envoi, so 39 lines, standard end-word rotation — with these six end words: cube, sphere, hand, eye, touch, light. In the voice of the man himself, right around when the bandages come off.

A few things I care about, in rough order:

- The rotation should be doing conceptual work, not just being decorative. The sestina is six fixed elements returning in a permuted order under a rule you can state in advance — which is close to what the question is about: given the same things arriving through a new medium, is the mapping already in him or does he have to work it out? Don't say that in the poem. Just let it be true of the poem.

- The twist I want in: the surgeon has put a third object on the table, a shape the man has never handled and has no name for. My reason: with only the two familiar objects, "that's the cube" could be pulled off by a nick in the wood he remembers, or by where they happened to be set down, or by which one felt heavier — success that looks like the answer but isn't cross-modal transfer. The test that would actually tell us something is the third object. So the poem's turn, in the last stanza into the envoi, should belong to that third thing, not to the cube and the sphere.

- He should never get to say the sentence he's being asked for. The words cube and sphere recur; the binding of either one to a thing he's seeing never happens in the poem. And no name-dropping: no Locke, no Berkeley, no Molyneux, no "empiricism." If a view like sight and touch sharing no common content is in there, it should be in the texture.

- In the envoi put cube and sphere together in the final line, so it reads like the answer arriving while you still can't tell which is which.

- Please skip the and-then-he-saw-the-world-and-wept register entirely. And it should be accurate enough that someone who knows the literature can say afterwards which reading of "could he tell" the poem is working with — matching, seeing-as, or answering straight off — and what kind of failure it would be if he fails. If you think the third object muddies that instead of sharpening it, say so and drop it, I'd rather have the sharper version.
