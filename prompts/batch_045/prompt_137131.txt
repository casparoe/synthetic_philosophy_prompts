I'm a backend engineer by trade, with no training in philosophy or law. I'm two years into a term on the Professional Conduct Committee of the Institute of Transportation Systems Engineers; I was appointed because the society wanted a practitioner on the panel, not because I have any background in ethics. We have to decide Complaint 24-11 at next month's meeting, and I'm the member assigned to draft the written opinion that we'll circulate, discuss, and vote on. I'd like you to draft it for me. I'm genuinely undecided, and both halves of the committee have said things I can't dismiss, so I'd rather have the reasoning than have you guess at the conclusion I want.

The rule we're construing is short. Rule 3.2 says members shall not knowingly make false statements of fact about a system they offer to a client, and shall not knowingly misrepresent its capabilities, limitations, or provenance. We've never had to interpret it. What we are really arguing about, as far as I can tell, is whether a member can violate it by saying something true.

The following facts are stipulated by both parties.

The member is the principal engineer at a fare-collection vendor that bid on a municipal transit contract. The transit authority's RFP asked, in its plain-language section, whether the system logs the travel history of individual riders and whether that history survives account closure. A technical annex to the RFP separately defined the phrase "travel history record" narrowly: a per-card itinerary row in the fare system's primary database.

The member's email answer to the authority's procurement officer was, in substance: the system does not log individual travel history, and fare transactions are processed without reference to the rider's identity. Both sentences are true on the architecture. There is no per-card itinerary table. Transactions write tap events to an append-only analytics stream keyed by card serial number, retained thirteen months; roughly sixty percent of cards are registered, and for those the card serial joins to a name and email in a separate registration table. Any engineer with warehouse access can reassemble a complete per-rider itinerary in about forty minutes of SQL, without approval from anyone. The member designed that pipeline himself. The week before he sent the email, he wrote in an internal channel that the less said about analytics retention the better, but that the team didn't have to volunteer it.

The procurement officer is a contracts attorney with no engineering background. The authority's own systems integrator, an engineer, was copied on the answer and raised no objection at the time; he later said he read "travel history" in the annex sense and assumed analytics was out of scope. Nine months after award, the authority published a rider-facing privacy page stating that the system does not track individual riders. The member did not draft it, but the wording came from his email.

The complaint has a second count. The vendor's proposal stated that the platform had been reviewed by an independent security firm, which is true: the firm was engaged, and its scope was the fare-gate firmware and the card reader protocol. Its preliminary draft flagged two access-control problems in the analytics pipeline as out-of-scope observations. The vendor terminated the engagement before a final report was issued, and the firm's contract bars the vendor from disclosing the draft. The member never said the review was complete, favorable, or covered analytics.

His defense on both counts is the same: every sentence he wrote is true; he used the RFP's own defined term; his statements went to people who either shared his technical dialect or had a copy of the annex; procurement is adversarial and asking follow-up questions is the buyer's job; and a rule that punishes true statements for what a hearer might infer would make liability turn on foresight the speaker can't fully control.

Four candidate formulations of the standard are on the table, and we have to pick one or draft a better one. Our staff attorney proposes a disambiguation test: a statement is deficient when the member foresaw that its audience would take it to convey something materially false and, if asked at the time, would not have agreed to add a clarifying sentence. I proposed an analogue from code review: a statement is deficient when a reasonable member of the audience must ask a follow-up that the speaker could have answered in the same breath, at essentially no cost. Another committee member proposes an interface framing: treat any client-facing statement like an API response, so that ambiguity is the producer's bug rather than the consumer's. The member's position amounts to a fourth: literal truth, with misrepresentation reserved for statements that are false or for outright fabrication.

Please draft the opinion as a committee memo, roughly a thousand to fourteen hundred words, in American English, with these sections:

Recommendation, one or two sentences, at the very top. Then the rule construction you're adopting, stated precisely enough that we could vote on it and apply it to next year's complaints. Then application to the two counts separately — they may come out differently, and I want to know if they should. Then a section on how the standard you adopt handles five pressure cases: statements that are true only within a technical dialect shared with part of the audience; true statements whose force comes entirely from an omitted qualifier; situations where the speaker is contractually barred from disclosing what would clarify the statement; a single statement that reaches both expert and lay audiences; and the adversarial-procurement context, where both sides expect the other to be guarded. Then the strongest version of the losing side's argument, written as a dissent one of our members could actually sign — the foresight-without-control objection is the one I find hardest to answer, and I want it given its full weight rather than conceded in a sentence. Finally, a short section separating what we can present as settled and uncontroversial from what is genuinely contested among people who know this area, so that we don't publish a contested reading as if it were the consensus position of the committee.

Please don't cite cases, statutes, treatises, or articles. We have counsel for that, and I want the argument rather than the authority, in plain language a working engineer can follow.
