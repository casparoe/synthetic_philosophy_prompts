I'm taking an upper-level seminar on rational choice — we've worked through the von Neumann–Morgenstern representation theorem, a few weeks on Savage, and one week on prospect theory — and after class last Thursday two classmates, Sofia and Elias, got into an argument about the Allais cases that they eventually asked me to referee. I declined, because I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning, so I'm bringing it to you instead. The cases: in the first pair you choose between $1M for sure and a gamble giving $5M with probability 0.10, $1M with probability 0.89, and nothing with probability 0.01; in the second you choose between an 0.11 chance of $1M and an 0.10 chance of $5M (nothing otherwise). Both of them, and most of the room, took the sure million in the first pair and the 0.10 shot at five million in the second. As they set it up, you can decompose the four options so that each pair compares the same 0.11-probability sub-lottery (a sure $1M versus a 10/11 chance of $5M) while the remaining 0.89 of probability mass delivers $1M in the first pair and nothing in the second — so the pattern is a violation of independence.

Their exchange, as close as I can reconstruct it: (1) Sofia said this just shows that most people violate a normative coherence condition, and that violating independence makes you money-pumpable, so the Allais pattern is an error to be corrected rather than a discovery about what people value. (2) Elias replied that the money-pump arguments for independence need more than independence itself — they need reduction of compound lotteries and some dynamic consistency across stages — and that an agent who stably holds the Allais preferences and declines to re-trade at the intermediate steps can't obviously be pumped, so the appeal to exploitation is doing less work than it looks. (3) Sofia said to forget pumps: independence is what licenses ranking two options by their reduced forms, and once you drop it your ranking depends on what would have happened in a region of the state space where the two options don't differ at all — structurally the same mistake as letting a sunk cost count. (4) Elias said that was restating the axiom rather than defending it, and that the substantive version of the anti-expected-utility view is that utility attaches to whole situations, so the value of a given sum of money can depend on how much of the outcome space was already settled before the draw; a preference for settledness, he said, is no more incoherent than a preference for arriving early. (5) Sofia then said to test that: replace the sure million with a 0.99 chance of a million, and the pattern reportedly collapses, which shows the motive isn't variance or security but certainty as such — a discontinuity at probability 1 — and a discontinuity of that kind looks like a defect in the agent rather than a feature of value. (6) Elias answered that a discontinuity in how probabilities are weighted isn't a coherence violation at all: rank-dependent models keep transitivity and stochastic dominance while giving independence up, so the most that has been shown is that independence is one axiom among several one can trade off, not that the Allais pattern is irrational.

Two things struck me that neither of them used, and I'd like you to fold them in. First, the cases as stated are silent about when the uncertainty resolves, yet in the first pair the safe option hands you the money right away while the risky one leaves you in suspense until the draw, whereas in the second pair both options resolve on the same schedule; if preferences are sensitive to resolution date, then either the Allais pattern isn't really about certainty (which would vindicate Elias) or the static description of the options is simply misspecified and the underlying preferences over outcome-plus-resolution-date might satisfy independence after all (which would vindicate Sofia). Second, the two of them kept sliding between Savage's framework of acts, states and the sure-thing principle and the Marschak-style framework of lotteries with stated probabilities and independence, without ever marking the switch, and I can't tell whether those are the same requirement, whether the Allais cases pressure them differently, or whether the switch is doing quiet work in some of the moves above. Please rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a brief justification, say who is right about what and who is more right overall, and then tell me how much each of my two wrinkles should shift the verdict. One caveat: Elias half-remembered an anecdote about Savage's own initial reaction to Allais's example and none of us could confirm it, so please don't rely on any version of that story unless you're actually confident about it — just say so and leave it out.
