I'm on a volunteer panel for our city council that divides up a fixed pot of money and a fixed amount of wall space between memorial projects — this year it's a Holocaust education programme, a plaque about the slave trade, something for a colonial famine, and a request from a community whose grandparents were killed in a massacre in the 1940s that almost nobody round here has heard of. I've sat through about a dozen of these meetings now and I can't work out what we collectively believe. Two things keep happening.

The first: we say, all the time, that we aren't in the business of ranking sufferings, and when a local journalist asked whether one of these was worse than another we shut the question down as obsenely tasteless. But the money actually gets allocated by a per-victim sort of formula — so much per name on the wall, travel costs for survivors scaled to how many, a small fund that pays out per head. That's a cardinal, roughly linear-in-deaths scale of badness, which is a stronger commitment than just saying A was worse than B. We also once floated splitting everything equally four ways and that got the angriest reaction of the whole meeting, which suggests the equal split gets heard as a verdict too, just a different one.

The second thing is the one I keep going round on and that I've never seen discussed anywhere. The taboo gets stronger the closer the two cases are. Nobody minds us saying that a famine was worse than a bar fight, or that the twentieth century was worse than the eighteenth. The fury is reserved for comparisons between two things that are properly similar — two genocides, two famines. But if the objection were epistemic, if it were that we can't get a grip on these things well enough to compare them, you'd expect exactly the opposite pattern: near cases are where comparison is easiest and most reliable. So the obscenity charge doesn't look like it's tracking difficulty at all. What is it tracking?

There's a related thing from the news, which may or may not belong in the same box: criminal sentences don't seem to scale with the number of victims anything like the way our budget does, and blame seems to run per-perpetrator rather than per-victim. If that's right then we've got two bits of public practice, money and punishment, doing the arithmetic completely differently.

So: reconstruct the theory of comparative badness that our committee's practice only makes sense under. Which parts of it are load-bearing and which are just what people happen to say? Could a straightforwardly aggregative view, an incommensurability view, or some expressivist view about what comparing does licence the the same behaviour — i.e. is the practice underdetermined? And what would we actually have to start doing differently under each of those? I'm after the argument rather than a bibliography, and I definately want to know where my own framing is the confused bit.
