So here's where I am. As far as I can tell the evolutionary debunking argument has two moving parts that people keep running together: first, a genealogical claim, that you can give a fairly complete causal story of why we hold the moral beliefs we do without ever mentioning whether those beliefs are true; second, a modal claim, that if the selective pressures had been different we'd have ended up with quite different moral beliefs, held with just as much subjective confidence, and that we have no vantage point outside the process from which to check whether we got pushed the right way. The back-and-forth I keep getting stuck in is all about whether those two can be combined into a real defeater without the argument also swallowing logic, maths or ordinary perceptual belief, and about whether the realist can wiggle out by offering an explanation that ties our moral beliefs to the moral facts via some third factor. I don't want to write about that, at least not head on. What I actually want to work on is what I've been calling the inheritance question: grant the debunker both claims for the narrow class of beliefs that have the cleanest selection story — kin altruism, reciprocity in small bands, disgust reactions around incest, that sort of thing — and then ask whether whatever defeat attaches to those beliefs transmits to the moral beliefs that have no such story, the ones about distant strangers mattering, or future people, or whether its wrong to shove a man off a footbridge to stop a trolley. There look like two honest answers. One is no: those further beliefs are reached by reasoning from commitments that have no selection story of their own, the genealogy just doesn't reach them, and moral philosophy is in the business of extending and systematising rather than of reporting intuitions, so the debunker has only shown something about a handful of parochial reactions. The other is yes: moral reasoning has to start somewhere, every chain of moral argument bottoms out in something we were shaped to accept, so contamination at the base contaminates the whole structure, and the realist's appeal to reflection is just the hope that a process built on a biased starting point can pull itself up by its bootstraps. I want the whole prospectus built around that fork.

There's a second wrinkle I'd like folded in, though I'm less sure about it, so tell me if it's a distraction. I've been distinguishing three things evolution might plausibly have selected for: particular moral contents; a general-purpose capacity to pick up, internalise and enforce whatever norms happen to be locally in force — a norm-uptake device, I've been calling it in my notes; and something messier, a talent for socially tuned self-presentation where the content of what we end up believing is mostly downstream of what plays well with the people around us. My hunch is that the debunking argument comes out different in kind depending on which of those is doing the work, and that some versions need the first for their punch but quietly need the second or third to explain why moral belief varies so much across cultures. If what got selected was flexibility rather than content, then our beliefs might be tracking something other than selection — local normative facts, or just local social facts — and that seems like it should change what the debunker is entitled to conclude. My supervisor thinks the whole thing reduces to whether reasoning can correct for a biased starting point, which might be right and worries me.
