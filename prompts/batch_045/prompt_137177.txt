You are a professional philosopher who specialises in metaphysics and who also writes formal verse — someone I would trust with both the scansion and with the idea of a non-well-founded grounding chain. Think the commission through before you draft: settle on your two refrains and on which stanza carries which picture, then write.

I would like a villanelle about the regress problem for metaphysical grounding. The set-up: if grounded things need grounds, and nothing grounds itself, then the chain of grounds either bottoms out in something basic, or descends forever, or — the option I want given its due — closes into a finite ring, A grounded in B, B in C, C in A, where every link gets its explanation from the next and nothing is fundamental. My hunch is that the ring option gets neglected mainly because we keep picturing grounding as architecture, and that a poem whose lines cannot stop returning to themselves is the more honest picture. Let the two refrains state the two claims the debate actually turns on ('nothing is grounded in itself' and something like 'nothing forces the chain to stop'); have them alternate, have each resist the other, and let them arrive together in the closing couplet still unreconciled, so that the loop is something the reader has felt rather than been told about. One line should give the standard objection to rings — that a cycle explains nothing, since each item would in the end be grounding itself by proxy — and one line should answer it. Accuracy matters: someone who works on grounding should be able to say which line is the well-founded picture, which is the regress, and which is the ring, and should find no howler; someone who has never read a word of metaphysics should still find a poem.

Give me the poem as a single block of verse, followed by a short note in continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — on what each refrain is doing and which line you think carries the real weight.
