I sit on the Nomenclature and Criteria Working Group for the third revision of our national psychiatric classification framework, and I am the member who keeps raising Hacking's looping effects, so the chair has asked me to stop raising objections and produce something the group can actually vote on at the November meeting. The item is a proposal to add a new category, persistent exertional intolerance syndrome, to the main body of the framework. Its proposed criteria are purely clinical: an episode of at least three months in which exertion reliably worsens symptoms, unrefreshing sleep, and complaints of slowed thinking, together with a clause requiring that the picture not be better accounted for by another diagnosable condition. There is no confirmatory test. The picture we have to work with is messy in a way that I think is characteristic of this sort of case. A draft of the criteria leaked roughly two years ago; since then a patient organisation of about forty thousand members has campaigned for inclusion, and the label is already in use by GPs, occupational health departments, insurers and schools, whether or not we vote for it. Our own clinic registry, some twelve hundred patients, shows that the cohort assessed after the leak reports a different symptom profile from the cohort assessed before it: more cognitive complaints, fewer exertional ones, longer reported durations, and far more people dating onset to childhood. Clinicians in the post-leak period also recorded the diagnosis after shorter assessments. Outcomes are mixed: people given the label report better workplace accommodation and fewer unpleasant investigations, but at twelve months they are no more likely to have returned to work, and a minority have plainly deteriorated on the graded-activity advice that tends to come with the diagnosis. A Nordic framework adopted a near-identical category some years ago; their incidence has since tripled, the phenotype has drifted in the same direction, and they have not withdrawn it. There is also a competing proposal on the table, backed by two of our members and part-funded by a company that sells the assay, to require a two-marker inflammatory panel instead, reported at about 72 per cent sensitivity in a single un-replicated study. The group is split three ways: full inclusion in the main body, inclusion in the research appendix only, or rejection. I am genuinely undecided, and I would rather the memo argued for one option than split the difference.

Two things I want to get right, and one I want to avoid. First, I do not think the looping observation settles anything on its own. The category will be shaping the behaviour of the people it classifies whether we include it or not, since the label is already in circulation; the question is which pattern of shaping we prefer, not whether we can opt out of shaping altogether. Second, I have found it useful to distinguish criteria whose satisfaction conditions shift as the label circulates from ones that are comparatively rigid, and I suspect the exclusion clause is among the most elastic parts of the proposal, because whether some other condition better accounts for the picture depends on how hard one looks, and having a ready-made label offers a convenient place to stop looking. What I want to avoid is any recommendation that turns on what Hacking himself thought: two of our members have been arguing about whether his account of travelling fugues in the late 1890s shows that a looping kind can be perfectly real or shows the opposite, and I do not want the memo to depend on settling that. Nor is it any use to the group to be told that all psychiatric kinds are socially constructed, or that the illness is real and that is the end of it; our clinicians and our two service-user representatives will simply stop reading.

Please draft the memo I will circulate, addressed to the working group, in no more than about twelve hundred words, with no references or citations — if you need to invoke evidence, use the registry and the Nordic comparison described above rather than attributing studies. Write it in a register that a busy clinician and a lay service-user representative can both follow, and give me a single named recommendation, with the date it would take effect and the date and body that would review it, rather than a menu. Use these sections, in this order and at roughly these lengths: Recommendation (up to 150 words, the bottom line, lead with it); The findings I am relying on (about 200 words, which of the facts above the recommendation turns on and which it does not); Why the looping does not settle the question (about 300 words, engaging the most sophisticated version of the worry rather than a stripped-down one); The strongest case for the option I am rejecting (about 250 words, argued well enough that its supporters would recognise it); Conditions, monitoring and review triggers (about 200 words, including what would count as evidence to withdraw or revise the category, and who would have to collect it); Note for the minutes from a dissenting member (about 100 words, the most honourable thing someone who votes against could record).
