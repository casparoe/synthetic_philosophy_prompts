I'm finishing up a PhD in political science (comparative, but I spend most of my actual time on informal enforcement and local ceasefires in civil conflicts) and I keep running into a pattern in my interview material that I cant find a word for, and I'm not sure whether that's because it genuinely has no name or because I'm reading the wrong literature.

Here's the shape of it. Two parties are in a repeated relationship where each side keeps cooperating roughly conditional on the other side having cooperated. Then something happens that means one side genuinely can't deliver: a bridge is out, a radio battery dies, the guy who was supposed to hand over the money gets held at a checkpoint for six hours. Nobody chose to defect, there's just an absence where a cooperative move was supposed to be. The other side can't tell "couldn't" from "wouldn't", reads the omission as a defection, and retaliates. The first side now sees a retaliation that, from where they're sitting, came out of nowhere, and retaliates back, and the thing unravels into permanent non-cooperation with both parties holding a completely clean internal ledger: neither thinks they owe anyone an apology, so none of the repair machinery people talk about — contrition, apology-and-forgiveness, the generous variants of tit-for-tat — ever gets triggered. And when the first side does say "I couldn't, I didn't choose to", that is exactly what someone who did choose to defect would say, so the statement carries no information. It shows up three or four times in my transcripts, and once, embarrassingly, in my own department: a co-author missed a deadline during a family emergency, I quietly slowed down on my end, we've basically never worked together since, and we both still think we are the aggrieved party.

A friend in IR said it sounds like the security dilemma, and I read around a bit and it's close but I don't think it's it — as I understand that literature it's about measures you actively take for defence that read as offensive preparation to the other side, whereas here the thing being misread isn't a move at all, it's a gap where a move should have been, and I think the everyone's-ledger-is-clean part also matters, because that's why none of the standard repair mechanisms fire. Someone else pointed me at the noise literature on repeated prisoner's dilemmas (mistakes, tit-for-tat echoing, contrite strategies), but that all seems to be about accidental defections — somebody did the wrong thing — and here nobody ever did anything wrong. And the psychology stuff about dispositional versus situational attribution seems to be about a single judgment, not about something that compounds over rounds.

So: is there an established concept for this, or for the family it belongs to? I don't just want a label, I want the carving — is this a species of the security dilemma, or of the noise/echo thing, or of something about unverifiable claims of inability (cheap talk, signaling), or is it its own failure mode with its own preconditions? And on the practical side, what should predict whether a pair survives one of these versus spiralling: does a long history of clean cooperation protect them, or does it make them more fragile, because there's more to lose and expectations run higher? If there's genuinely no term for it, coin one and define it, and tell me what would and wouldn't count as a case — e.g. does it still count if the "can't" was partly the agent's own fault, or if the other side could have verified it cheaply and just didn't bother?
