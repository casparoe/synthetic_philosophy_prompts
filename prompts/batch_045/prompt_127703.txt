I'm a junior double-majoring in statistics and philosophy, and this term I'm in an upper-level seminar on chance, evidence, and induction. Part of the requirement is that each of us leads one 75-minute session for the other seventeen people in the room. Most of my classmates have had one applied statistics course and one logic course. The last two weeks of the seminar have been spent on what probability statements are even about — long-run relative frequencies, objective single-case chances, or degrees of belief — and the room has split in a way that feels like temperament rather than argument. My session is in ten days, and I don't want to add a fourth position statement to the pile. I'd rather run a game.

Here's what I want people to walk out having felt, rather than having been told.

First: that for a one-off event it isn't obvious what "the probability" is a number about. The same event seems to admit three different questions — how often does this kind of thing happen, what is this particular setup disposed to produce, how confident should someone with my information be — and in clean cases all three return the same number, which is exactly why nobody notices there are three.

Second: that a quantity tracking the evidence can come apart from a quantity tracking the setup. I want them to meet a case where the physical chance is plainly one thing and the reasonable confidence is plainly another, and to have to decide which of those numbers is the one they'd actually bet at.

Third: that "how often does this kind of thing happen" has no unique answer, because the kind has to be chosen. I want them handed genuine frequency figures for one specific event from three nested classes and made to feel the choice as a choice rather than a calculation.

What I've sketched, and would like you to build into something runnable — or to replace with a better mechanism:

Three cases, priced one at a time. (A) A die is about to be rolled: symmetric setup, outcome not yet settled. (B) A die was rolled inside a sealed box before class: same die, same setup, but the outcome is already fixed and nobody in the room knows it. (C) A one-off claim about a named person in the room — I was thinking whether that person will still be enrolled here four terms from now, where I can pull real retention figures for the university as a whole, for students in this major, and for first-generation students, and those three numbers differ. Everyone writes down a number between 0 and 1 for each case.

Payoffs without money. My understanding is that a squared-error rule — you receive 1 minus the squared distance between your number and the outcome, so a confident correct call pays 1 and a confident wrong call pays 0 — makes it against your interest to write down anything other than what you really think. Is that right? Does it survive being run only once per case, where a lucky extremist beats a careful 0.6? And is there a payoff structure that also rewards whatever the frequency-based or chance-based intuition is genuinely good for, so the game doesn't quietly become an advertisement for one of the three views? That last worry is the one I care about most. I can see how to make evidence-sensitivity vivid in play; I can't see how to make the pull of "the chance is one-in-six and that isn't up for grabs" show up in play rather than only in my summary at the end.

One complication I want to stage. Before case B, I take a single student into the hall and let them look inside the box. Back in the room they write a number too. If the payoff rewards accuracy they will win, and if the number they write is 1 or 0 the class has to deal with someone who is certain about an event the rest of the room is calling one-in-six, and who has made no mistake at all.

What I need from you: exact rules and the order of operations; what I prepare in advance (index cards, envelopes, dice, a whiteboard, a laptop with a spreadsheet, no internet in the room); a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes with the debrief built into it rather than tacked on; the scoring formula in a version I can compute for eighteen people in about two minutes; and whether anyone needs a role other than "bettor."

Then the debrief: which moment in the game is meant to produce which of the three points above, and what I say when a moment produces something else. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — my guesses are that someone demands to be told the correct number, that the student who peeked refuses to commit, that case C gets swamped by what people happen to know about the person, and that a one-shot scoring rule rewards bluffing — and how I handle each without the last twenty minutes turning into a lecture.

Finally, be honest about what the game can't do. I suspect it can't manufacture the intuition that there's an objective single-case chance that evidence doesn't touch, that it can't show why long-run frequencies should constrain anything, and that a scoring rule only rewards honesty in expectation by your own lights. Tell me which of those I have to supply myself, roughly in how many sentences, and where in the session.

And if the three cases run short: what fourth case should I keep in my pocket? Ideally one where the chance itself is what's unknown, so that "what I'd bet at" and "what I think the chance is" come apart for a different reason than they do in case B.
