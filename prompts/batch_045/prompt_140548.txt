My girlfriend is doing her English teaching certification and asked me to help put together a discussion plan for the last act of The Crucible — her class is sixteen-year-olds and they've already finished the play — and I said sure, but I'm a philosophy PhD student and I can't help turning everything into my own stuff. What I keep circling is that the ending is basically one long argument about what saying something *does*. Danforth wants a signed confession, and it plainly doesn't matter to him whether the thing written on it is true; he wants the paper, the public act, the fact that it will be nailed to the church door. Proctor says the confession is a lie while he is signing it and then takes it back, and Danforth won't let the taking-back count. Earlier, Mary Warren's attempt to unsay her accusation against Abigail doesn't take either: the court hears her perfectly well and still doesn't hear it as a retraction, it hears it as further evidence. And there's the moment where Elizabeth, who in her own words never lies, lies exactly once, and the lie fails to do what she wants it to do even though everyone in the room understands perfectly well what she meant.

My instinct, since I wrote my undergrad thesis on Austin, is to read all of that in terms of felicity conditions and uptake: the court has quietly removed the conditions under which a denial or a retraction or a refusal counts as the act it purports to be, while leaving the words themselves audible. I have a vague memory of a feminist-philosophy literature on this — Langton? Hornsby? something like illocutionary disablement, or silencing, where the speaker's utterance is heard as noise but not as the speech act it is meant to be — and I'd like to run with it, but I want two worries addressed rather than one. First: is it even the right description? The girls aren't silenced in any ordinary sense — they talk constantly, they accuse constantly, and their accusations work all too well. It's specifically denials, retractions and refusals that fail. That looks less like being silenced than like being audible in only one register, and I don't know whether the framework has a way of saying that or whether I'd just be stretching it to fit. Second, I don't want to commit the usual interdisciplinary sin of using a play as a ready-made illustration of a theory I already had. So tell me where the play pushes back. My sense is that Miller is doing something the theory can't: Danforth isn't confused about whether Mary Warren means to retract, he knows exactly what she means and needs it not to count, which makes the failure look less like a broken felicity condition and more like a deliberate refusal of uptake; and Proctor's final refusal to sign is meant to be tragic and slightly mysterious in a way that "the illocution failed to achieve uptake" just doesn't capture. Say where that instinct is wrong too, and if there are distinctions in the speech-act literature — illocution versus perlocution, what counts as a misfire versus an abuse, whether uptake is a felicity condition at all — that I'm collapsing, use them on me rather than defining them at me.

And then the practical bit: if you had fifty minutes with a room of sixteen-year-olds, which two or three moments from the final act would you actually put on the board, and what question would you attach to each? I'd rather not end up with "here is a definition, now find it in the text."
