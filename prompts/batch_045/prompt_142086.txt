Draft the joint statement that a Devlinian legal moralist and a Hartian liberal could both sign at the close of a good-faith adversarial collaboration on a single question: may a stable democratic society use the criminal law to enforce a moral norm as such, in a case where no one is harmed, no one's rights are violated, no one is coerced for their own good, and no unwilling observer is exposed to the conduct?

The collaboration has agreed to work inside the following stipulations, and the document should treat them as settled rather than re-argue them.

One. The setting is the jurisdiction of Vale: affluent, legally stable, and culturally and religiously pluralist. Vale's members agree on a thin core of moral norms and disagree over a wide penumbra. There is no single comprehensive doctrine shared by all, but there are durable local majorities with a shared history.

Two. The test case is practice X: an adult, consensual practice valued by its participants, engaged in by roughly eight percent of the population. Seventy percent of Vale's residents report genuine repugnance at the thought of performing X themselves; fifty-five percent report repugnance at the thought of a neighbour performing it; and a substantial majority of those same people say they do not want participants punished. Call this the split-attitude profile. Both parties accept that the split-attitude profile is now the ordinary case in pluralist societies, and that it is the case on which the debate actually turns.

Three. Both parties accept the following evidence as correctly described: across eleven jurisdictions comparable to Vale that decriminalized practice X in the last thirty years, matched-control comparisons show no measurable decline in general social trust, in tax compliance, or in adherence to unrelated moral norms; a marked decline in the share of residents reporting repugnance at X itself; and a small but consistent spillover decline in reported repugnance at practice Y, which stood next to X in the local moral framework.

Four. Both parties accept that legal enforcement can erode moral conviction as well as express it: statutes that are widely flouted, or retained on the books and never prosecuted, tend to breed contempt for the law and to accelerate the decline of the norm they express.

Within those stipulations, the deliverable is the document itself, in four parts.

A numbered agreed-claims section: what both parties can sign. Include at least what each side concedes about the other's best case; what both accept about the difference between a society's having a shared morality and its enforcing one through law; what both accept about paternalism and about offense to unwilling observers being separate questions from the one under discussion; and what both accept about the limits of survey evidence as a measure of a living moral culture. These claims should be substantial enough that a reasonable outsider would learn something from them, and none should be phrased so thinly that it commits nobody.

A disagreements table. Columns: the issue; the moralist's own formulation; the liberal's own formulation; and a judgment, for each row, of whether the disagreement is substantive or merely verbal. Each formulation must be one its own side would accept as a fair statement of its position rather than a caricature, and must be specific enough that there is a possible world in which it is false. Expected rows include: whether the moderate version of the legal-moralist thesis collapses into the extreme version once the distinction between harming and offending is set aside; whether the repugnance reported under the split-attitude profile is the kind of moral condemnation the moralist thesis requires, or whether the concurrent unwillingness to punish shows that the shared morality has already ceased to condemn X in the relevant sense; whether the decline in repugnance at X and the spillover to Y confirm the disintegration thesis or merely redescribe decriminalization; and whether the majority's interest in preserving a moral culture across time is continuous with its interest in preserving a language or a civic tradition, or relevantly different because it is secured by coercing conduct.

A cruxes section. For each crux, state the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side; give the test, argument, or case that both parties agree would bear on it; and record each side's prediction in advance, written as a prediction rather than as a reassertion of its position. One crux should be the retrospective evidence in stipulation three, and the moralist should be permitted to develop the ratchet or threshold reading of it, while the liberal is permitted to press the charge that a thesis which survives every outcome only by retreating to a distant tipping point cannot justify coercion here and now. A second crux should be prospective: design a paired-jurisdiction natural experiment for a practice Z, in two jurisdictions alike in every respect the parties can agree matters, one of which decriminalizes and one of which does not; specify the measurements both commit to in advance, the timescale, and what each predicts. If some crux admits of no test both would accept, say so plainly instead of manufacturing one.

A short closing section stating, in the parties' joint voice, which of their differences they now believe would actually be moved by argument or evidence, which are settled by nothing they can presently foresee, and which turned out to be disagreements about words. Do not attribute views to named authors, do not cite or rely on the secondary literature, and do not summarize the history of the debate: construct the positions and argue them on their merits.
