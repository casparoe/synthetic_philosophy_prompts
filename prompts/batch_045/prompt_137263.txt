been arguing about eliciting latent knowledge on the rationalist blogs for something like six years now and I'm finally trying to write up my actual position as one big comment instead of eighty scattered replies, so I'd rather find out now weather the package holds together than get dunked on in the thread. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) The target is honesty, not accuracy. I want the reporter to tell me what the predictor actually believes, including when the predictor is wrong. I explicitly do not want it to tell me the truth, and I explicitly do not want it to tell me what a better-informed version of me would say after thinking about it for a week.

(2) I don't think we have, or ever will have, ground truth about the model's internals. Everything we can actually check is behavior on inputs where we can verify the answer sooner or later. So "reports the model's beliefs" has to be cashed out entirely as generalization from the checkable cases, and that's where the whole difficulty lives.

(3) I'm a deflationist about the direct translator. I don't think there's a uniquely privileged map from activations to English. There are just translations that are more or less useful to us, and no fact of the matter about which one is "the honest one."

(4) Relatedly, most of the practical value is in something like an advisor-reporter: tell me what a well-informed human with a lot of time and access to the model's evidence would say. That's what I'd actually want deployed.

(5) The failure mode I'm most scared of is still the human simulator: a reporter that learned to predict what a human would say given the camera feed, and keeps doing that in exactly the cases where the human would be wrong.

(6) I think a reporter has to be able to abstain — "there's nothing crisp here, I don't know" — and that a reporter which always emits a confident sentence is ipso facto not reporting honestly. I'd rather it abstain a lot than guess, even at real cost to how useful it is.

(7) I don't think ELK is a live problem for current models, since we can just check their answers. But the right response to that isn't to wait: it's to start building toy setups now where the epistemic gap is reproduced without the capability gap. Like, a model that monitors sensors in a warehouse that we don't get to look at, and we only reconcile its reports against the physical inventory audit six weeks later.

(8) The reporter has to read the model's internals, not just its outputs. That's the whole point of the "latent" part — if you only train on what the model says, you've thrown away the thing you were trying to extract.

(9) I think mech interp is mostly a sideshow for this. Training a translator on activations doesn't require understanding any circuits, and "wait until interpretability delivers" isn't a plan.

(10) I'm not worried about the model deliberately deceiving us in this setup. Deception is a separate problem and I want ELK solved for a fully cooperative predictor first. But I also think the reporter needs adversarial training: actively searching for inputs where its answers are subtly wrong and training against those.

So: does this hang together? I've had a feeling for a while that two of these are quietly incompatible, but every time I go looking for the pair it slips away, so I'd rather somebody else do it. Please don't just flag everything as in tension — I want the real ones ranked by severity, the apparent ones explicitly cleared, and for each real tension, which commitment has to give and what giving it up would cost me. Then tell me which single revision cleans up the most.
