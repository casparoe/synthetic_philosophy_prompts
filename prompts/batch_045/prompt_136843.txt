Write a parody of a government Notice of Proposed Rulemaking — docket number, background preamble, proposed regulatory text, and numbered questions on which the agency invites comment — in which a fictional regulator proposes to settle the question of whose values a general-purpose AI assistant should serve. Its solution is what the docket calls the controversiality rule: the assistant must detect any value judgment on which the relevant population is sufficiently divided and must then decline to act on it, remaining neutral between the competing views. Hard limit: 700 words. Write it deadpan, in an earnest bureaucratic register, with no winking at the reader.

The comedy has to come from getting the substance right, so the docket should be forced to specify things it cannot specify: how divided counts as sufficiently divided, and measured how; whose disagreement is counted (subscribers only, everyone the assistant's outputs affect, future people, animals, people who never touch the system); what makes something a value judgment rather than a disputed matter of fact; and what the assistant is to do when abstaining is itself a way of helping one side. Let the footnotes and the questions for comment quietly expose that the neutrality mechanism keeps turning back into a substantive moral choice — that the threshold at which a view stops counting as controversial is itself contested, that under the rule a view becomes easier to act on the more people already hold it, and that aggregating whatever survey the agency proposes runs into Arrow-style troubles and interpersonal comparisons of utility. The agency must never state these objections; a reader should feel them without being told. Someone who knows the debates about value aggregation and liberal neutrality should judge the target fairly rather than crudely described. Close with a comment deadline and a contact office whose name is itself a small joke.
