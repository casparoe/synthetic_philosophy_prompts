Hey, I've been going in circles on the species problem and I want to compare two ways of spelling out what it even means for an organism to be a member of a species. Heres the two ideas:

A — the ancestry idea: an organism belongs to species S iff it descends from the right bit of the tree of life, i.e. it's part of that historical lineage. Where you come from is all that matters; what you look like, who you mate with, and what niche you sit in are irrelevant to membership.

B — the cohesion idea: an organism belongs to S iff it actually takes part in the mechanisms that hold S together — mating with the others, sharing their gene pool, being shaped by the same selective regime / occupying the same adaptive niche. Membership is something you do, not just where you're from.

Please do three things:

1. Say where the two ideas actually come apart, and not just verbally. If you think they end up giving the same verdict in every possible case, argue for that instead.

2. Run both of them over these five cases and tell me which verdict looks right:
- A dispersing male leaves the group he was born in, joins a neighboring population that's normally counted as a different species, mates there, and his offspring are fertile and backcross into the new population.
- An all-female, clonally reproducing lineage that started out as a hybrid between two species maybe 50,000 years ago, has never swapped genes with either parent species since, and now occupies its own niche.
- A soil bacterium where something like a tenth of its genome over evolutionary timescales arrives sideways from unrelated bacteria rather than from parent to offspring.
- A ring of populations around a mountain range: every neighboring pair interbreeds freely, but the two ends meet up in one valley and don't.
- Two populations that split 400,000 years ago, look essentially identical, and make perfectly fertile offspring if you put them together in a lab, but in the wild they now use different hosts and basically never meet.

The thing that bugs me most: on both ideas it looks like no individual organism can ever be the first member of a new species — its parents are still the old species, and if it mates at all it mates with the old species. But some speciation seems to happen in a single generation (a polyploid plant individual, say, or one mutant that can self-fertilize). Can either idea be repaired so that a single organism can be the first of a new species, and what does that repair cost you somewhere else?

Feel free to name whichever real species concepts these two are cousins of if it helps, but I care way more about the argument then about getting the labels right. Oh, and please write in American English, thanks.
