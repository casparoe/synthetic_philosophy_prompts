Hi — I'm the research lead at a small eye hospital. Most of our caseload is kids who turn up late with dense congenital cataracts, usually somewhere between six and sixteen, we do one eye at a time and the kid is patched for a day or two afterwards. We get maybe fifteen of these a year. Every few months a journalist or a philosopher emails to ask whether we've ever "run Molyneux's test" — the old one: somebody blind from birth who knows a cube and a ball by touch gets their sight, and can they tell which is which at first sight, before touching. I'd like to be able to say something honest in reply. The last time we tried, we put out a press release I now think overclaimed, and I don't want to repeat that.

So what I want is a procedure, not a discussion. Something a nurse and I can actually run in a twenty-minute session on the ward: no eye-tracker, no lab, parents possibly in the room, the kid maybe bored or scared. Give me the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where we stop and void the whole run, and where it has to be handed to me or to the ethics board because it's stopped being a factual question. Please also say what to do when a session falls apart halfway — kid gets tired and stops cooperating, one of the aunties on the ward tells them what's on the tray, they grab before we've started — which parts are salvageable and which mean we start over next week.

A few things we've already run into that I want the protocol to handle, because they're exactly where our own arguments stall:

- We can't agree what counts as a response. Naming it ("ball"), or sorting it to a felt sample we've slipped them under the table, or just the shape of their hand as they reach, before contact. Two of us think the reach is the honest measure and naming is contaminated by having the words; two think the reach is a motor thing and shows nothing about recognition.
- A cube looks completely different depending on whether you see it face-on or corner-on — square outline in one case, six-sided in the other — so "matching what you see to what you felt" isn't matching one picture to one feel. We can put the objects behind a screen so only the silhouette shows, or leave the overhead light on so they get shading and corners. I genuinely don't know which version is the fairer test of the question.
- Lots of these kids had some light perception before surgery, so they're not as untested as the classic setup assumes; a fair number have nystagmus and come out around 20/200; some can't hold still. So there's got to be a gate we check before we even start, and a rule for when failing that gate means "void" rather than "no".
- Nobody can settle what "at first sight" means operationally. First hour after the patch comes off? First day? Before anybody has named anything out loud in front of them? We can re-test at a month if that's part of the answer.

Then run it on three cases. (1) A nine-year-old girl, one eye done, tested the morning after: her hand opens much wider as she reaches toward the cube than toward the ball, before she touches either, but she never names them and she can't sort them to the felt sample. (2) A twelve-year-old boy at his one-year follow-up, both eyes done, who instantly says "box" and "ball" for shapes he's felt before but that are a different size and made of different stuff from the ward ones. (3) Whatever case makes the procedure hand me an answer I won't like — I'd much rather have that than a protocol that only ever tells me what I was hoping for.

And be straight about what the procedure can't do. If the truth is that no run of this, however clean, settles the old question, say that, and say which step is the one that can't be repaired. Also tell me what our comms officer is and isn't allowed to put in a press release. No citations or reading lists, please — I have no library access out here and I'm definately not chasing references.
