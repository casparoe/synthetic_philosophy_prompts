I'm on the breeding advisory board of a mid-sized dairy co-op — I'm not a philosopher, I'm on it because I'm one of the larger members — and I'm also doing an evening course in philosophy of biology, which has made our own spreadsheets look strange to me. Here's roughly what we do. We dont really pick bulls by looking at cows any more. Every calf is genotyped at birth at a few tens of thousands of markers and gets a number out of it, a "genomic estimated breeding value", which as far as I can tell is just the summed estimated average effects of the chunks of genome it happens to carry. We choose the sires off that number before the animal has ever sired anything or even seen a cow; the top handful get used enormously, one bull can end up with tens of thousands of daughters through artificial insemination, and the rest are more or less culled. On top of that we do three other things that don't seem to be at the same level at all: we keep performance records on individual cows and we have a rule that we won't use a bull whose profile predicts miserable longevity or bad calving ease, we cap how much any one sire can contribute to the next generation because otherwise the breed's "effective population size" falls off a cliff, and we all pay into a national gene bank that freezes semen and embryos from rare local breeds that nobodys farms commercially any more.

So my question is what all of this presupposes about what selection is acting on, if it presupposes anything. On the face of it we've adopted a gene's-eye view as an engineering practice without anyone ever deciding to: the thing we evaluate, bank and optimise is a bundle of alleles, and the animal looks like a disposable vehicle for this year's draw from the gene pool. But my tutor keeps saying the gene-level, individual-level and group-level stories are different partitionings of one and the same causal process, so maybe counting alleles is only convenient bookkeeping and we're committed to nothing; and then the sire cap and the gene bank look like they only make sense if the breed or the population is a genuine unit that can do better or worse, which is the group-selection-flavoured thing I was told everybody had given up on decades ago. I'm less interested in being told which level is "really" the unit — I want to know what our practice commits us to. Which parts of it strictly presuppose the replicator / gene's-eye picture, which parts merely sit comfortably with it, and which parts an individual-level or multilevel picture would license just as happily? Suppose someone said we'd saved every allele of a rare breed in a freezer but let the breed die out as a living, breeding population — does our own practice give us anything to say about whether we conserved something or nothing? And if we genuinely took the individual animal, or the herd, to be the unit, what would actually change in what we do on Monday morning, and what would just be a different story told over the exact same decisions?
