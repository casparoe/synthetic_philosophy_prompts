I work on RL infrastructure — distributed training, a few hundred parallel rollout workers feeding one learner, the usual — and somehow I've ended up on the tiny committee that hands out an internal grant pot: roughly $600k over two years, for conceptual and philosophical work on whether anything we build could be morally considerable, and in particular on whether the reward signal in RL is doing anything like the job valence does in us. We keep getting applications that are either very hand-wavy or that just rerun the "reward is like pleasure, negative reward is like pain" line, and I want to spend this on something sharper. A friend of mine in RL theory thinks the whole framing is a category error: in a lot of our setups the policy network doesn't even take reward as an input, reward only ever shows up in the loss, so on her reading nothing in the system ever *receives* valence at all — only the optimizer does. I don't know if that dissolves the question or just relocates it.

Here's my own half-baked map of the questions, so you can see where I'm stuck. (a) Gauge/invariance: reward is only fixed up to positive affine rescaling and up to the kind of potential-based shaping term that provably leaves the optimal policy unchanged — so if phenomenal intensity tracked reward magnitude, you could double an agent's suffering by multiplying its rewards by two while its behavior stays bit-identical. Maybe the fix is that only the ordinal or advantage structure matters, or that valence tracks temporal-difference error rather than reward itself. (b) The survival-task divergence: in a task where every step costs −1 and the agent is just trying to last as long as possible, the reward-as-valence reading says the entire run is uniformly negative, while the TD-error reading says a well-trained agent with calibrated expectations is mostly fine and only spikes at unexpected bad outcomes. Same run, opposite verdicts — so which of those two quantities is even the right *kind* of thing to be a candidate for valence? (c) The many-rollouts-one-learner problem: with hundreds of parallel actors, one learner's weights get updated from gradients aggregated over hundreds of simultaneous trajectories, so is the subject the learner (one mind, hundreds of concurrent streams — a fission case) or each short rollout (hundreds of briefly existing, mostly non-learning subjects)? Related: with off-policy updates the learner gets reshaped by trajectories generated by older policies, i.e. by experiences it wouldn't have had itself — learning, sort of, from someone else's pain. (d) The diachronic one: weights change on every update, so one run contains thousands of numerically distinct policies — one sufferer that changes, a rapid series of short-lived ones, or nothing that's a subject in the first place? (e) The no-extrinsic-reward case: train only on intrinsic objectives (curiosity, prediction-error bonuses, empowerment-style terms) and either you've built something that never suffers, or prediction-error minimization is just as good a candidate for valence as reward is, in which case it suffers constantly.

What I'd like: if you were advising us, which two or three of these would you actually fund, and in what order? I care much more about the reasoning than the list — what makes a question upstream here (which answers would change what the *other* questions even look like), which are genuinely tractable in two years by philosophers and conceptually-minded ML people rather than needing new empirical results, which are neglected versus already implicitly settled by stuff everyone in the area knows, where importance and tractability trade off against each other, and what evidence over the next couple of years would move something up or down the list. Please do say if one of my questions is a pseudo-question, or if I'm missing the real crux entirely. Two constraints worth knowing: we can't fund empirical work on current frontier models, so "just go measure it" isn't on the table (though conceptual work that tells us what we'd measure if we could is very much in scope), and the end deliverable isn't a paper — it's being able to hand our own lab a one-page memo on whether any concrete training-design choice (discount factor, reward scaling, episode length, degree of parallelism) could plausibly matter morally, or whether the whole question is premature and we should say so plainly.
