I've never studied any of this — I work as a QA tester and on weekends I mess around with a hobby project, a little "bug simulator": a few hundred agents with tiny neural nets, evolved to forage in a grid world. I've gotten kind of obsessed with a question I can't shake, and I let myself get talked into giving a twenty-minute talk about it at the monthly "AI and society" meetup at the public library next week. No slides, just me talking, then questions.

Here's the rough thing I want to say, in my own words (I'm sure some philosopher already has a version of this, I genuinely don't know who and I don't want to pretend I've read anything): people keep asking whether a simulation "really" feels anything, as if there's a fact of the matter hidden inside the box. But the same physical box can be seen as running loads of different computations depending on how you label its states — so the question isn't "what is this machine doing" but "which computation are we entitled to attribute to it." My thesis: we should attribute the one that earns its keep. That is, out of all the computations you could read the box as implementing, the one that matters morally is the one that lets you predict and control the thing more cheaply than any rival description — and its welfare counts once per pattern, not once per copy of the hardware carrying it.

Two cases I'm building the talk around. First, a lazily-evaluated simulation: the engine only computes the internal cell values that something downstream actually reads, so most of the "neural" states are never computed at all, and the creature behaves identically. I want to say it matters just as much as the eager version, because the uncomputed states still figure in the best explanation of what it does. Second, a triple-redundant setup — three processors running the same creature with majority voting, like they do in spacecraft. I want to say that's one creature, not three, because it's one pattern instantiated three times. If I'm right, bolting on redundant hardware doesn't multiply how much the thing matters, and skipping unread computation doesn't reduce it.
