Analyze the controversy over whether the Large Hadron Collider could destroy the Earth as an instance of Pascal's wager, and then tell me what the episode does to the wager itself.

The facts, so that nothing here turns on recall: in the years before the LHC began operating in 2008, critics argued that its collisions might produce stable microscopic black holes or hypothetical strange matter, or otherwise trigger a doomsday scenario. CERN commissioned an independent safety review — issued in 2003 and updated in 2008 — which ruled out any such scenario, relying substantially on the observation that collisions of far higher energy occur naturally and routinely, since ultra-high-energy cosmic rays have been striking the Earth, the Moon, and other bodies for billions of years without ill effect. The report was endorsed by outside committees and published in a peer-reviewed journal. The collider was switched on regardless. A broadly similar scare, with a broadly similar rebuttal, had surrounded the Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider roughly a decade earlier.

I know the wager literature's standard moves and don't need them rehearsed: the dominance argument, the many-gods objection, the trouble that infinite or merely astronomical stakes cause for expected-utility reasoning, the question whether the argument is addressed to belief or only to action, and the extension of the same structure to small-probability/huge-payoff cases generally. What I want is the mapping done carefully, and then a verdict that runs in both directions.

Three distinctions I would like you to use, since I don't think the standard discussion draws them:

1. Call a wager *sealed* if no course of action available to the agent could produce evidence bearing on the hypothesis before the payoff is determined, and *self-testing* if the recommended action is itself the test — the agent learns only by taking the risk, and the catastrophic outcome is precisely the outcome in which learning is impossible. Pascal's hypothesis is sealed. Say which the collider case is, and why that difference matters for the argument's force.
2. Pascal's bettor is the one who pays the cost and the one who would collect the prize. In the collider case the decision is made by an institution, on behalf of a population that had no say and that is not the primary beneficiary of the science. Ask what the dominance argument becomes when the decision-maker externalizes the downside, and whether the wager has anything to say about legitimacy or consent at all — or whether it is simply silent there.
3. The cosmic-ray argument supplies something Pascal lacked: a partially run experiment, an empirical anchor that shrinks the probability for the enumerated channels. But it only addresses channels someone thought to enumerate. Ask whether the hypothesis space here is closed or open, and whether the many-gods objection therefore bites harder, softer, or differently than it does in the theological case.

Then the analysis proper. Lay out the options, the stakes, and what would have counted as evidence, and say what a strict dominance argument would have recommended about switching the machine on — noting that on its face it seems to recommend not switching it on, which almost nobody thought was the right answer, and explaining why. Consider whether the episode shows that the wager needs a *no symmetric counter-wager* condition: here, as with many real cases, not acting carries its own open-ended risks, so that both horns of the decision host unenumerable catastrophic hypotheses. Say where the theory illuminates the episode — including what work the phrase no conceivable threat was actually doing — and where it misdescribes it or falls silent. Close by saying whether the case is a confirmation of the wager's critics, an anomaly, or simply outside the argument's scope.

Two standing requests. First, separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested: for example, treat as contested rather than established any particular reading of what Pascal's argument is an argument for, and flag the disputes about infinite or fanatical stakes rather than resolving them by assertion. Where you are unsure of a scientific or historical detail, say so instead of supplying a plausible one. Second, write in American English.
