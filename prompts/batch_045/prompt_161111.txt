I'm a fourth-year medical student finishing a clinical ethics elective, and for the past month I've been sitting in on our hospital's ethics consultation service and reading back through the consult notes. The clinical ethicist who chairs it wrote or signed off on the last five I have, and I've been trying to reconstruct the principle she is actually running, because I have to draft the note on tomorrow's case before she reads it and I can't tell whether her track record expresses a view or is just a stack of intuitions. The five, as compactly as I can put them: (i) a woman in the late stages of Alzheimer's who no longer recognized her family and was described by the nurses as the happiest person on the ward, with a directive written at 55 — after she had cared for her own mother through dementia — refusing hospitalization and antibiotics in the event of advanced dementia; our ethicist supported treating her pneumonia anyway. (ii) A man with bipolar I in a manic episode, refusing medication vehehemently and articulately, with a Ulysses-style directive written during remission asking to be treated if he ever became manic and refused; she supported treating him against his will. (iii) A woman with relapsed anorexia nervosa refusing nasogastric feeding, who had signed a directive during an earlier recovery asking to be fed if she relapsed and refused; she supported feeding. (iv) A man with locked-in syndrome from ALS, ventilator-dependent, cognitively intact, communicating by eye-tracker, who had completed a book and said he was glad to be alive, whose only directive was a generic form filled in at 22 before a mountaineering trip saying he would not want to be kept alive if permanently ventilator-dependent and which he barely remembered signing; she supported honoring his current wish and continuing support. (v) A woman with advanced MS, ventilator-dependent, with mild cognitive impairment, who now said she wanted to go on living and see her granddaughter graduate, but who had for twenty-five years repeatedly reaffirmed a refusal of mechanical ventilation and had spent thirty years as a respiratory nurse; she supported withdrawal, and noted in the chart that this was the hardest of the five. In teaching she has said that the usual checklist — was the directive informed, stable, executed properly — matters less to her than the question of which self's interests are at stake, and she once drew a distinction between the interests a person has in the overall shape of her life and the interests she has in how her life feels to her from moment to moment; but she has never explained how that distinction maps onto the cases, and it isn't obvious to me that it settles any of them.

Tomorrow's patient is a 61-year-old woman with behavioral-variant frontotemporal dementia, now mild to moderate, diagnosed three years ago. At 55, before any diagnosis and after watching her mother die with Alzheimer's, she executed a directive refusing hospitalization, antibiotics for infection, and any life-prolonging intervention if she were ever diagnosed with a dementia, and added a clause to the effect that people with dementia often seem to change their minds, become cheerful or religious, and say they want to live, and that such statements are the disease speaking rather than her and are to be disregarded as evidence that she is no longer herself, with her earlier instruction standing. Since her diagnosis she has become a regular at a local church, though she was for decades an active member of a secular humanist association and wrote occasionally for its newsletter; she attends a memory café, has sustained several new friendships over two years, and describes herself as happier than she has ever been. Last week she asked her own GP for antibiotics for a urinary tract infection and said, when asked, that she is not ready to die and wants to see her son's wedding, giving reasons her GP found coherent. On a formal capacity assessment she understands and appreciates the relevant information and reasons consistently about this decision; her neurologist says the change in her values and personality is textbook for her disease, is irreversible, and is accompanied by characteristic loss of insight; her daughter told me that this is not the person who wrote that document but she is happier than she has been in years, and that she does not know which of the two she is supposed to be loyal to. One of the residents argued over coffee that the anti-revocation clause is either redundant or incoherent: if the patient really is no longer the author, then nothing she now says or wants should be treated as the author's business one way or the other, and if she still is the author, then she retains the standing to reinterpret her own earlier instruction, so a clause purporting to strip her of that standing cannot do the work the directive wants it to do. I find this plausible and also suspect it is a sophism, and I can't tell which.

My uninformed guess is that our ethicist will recommend treating the infection, but I can't say why, and the resident's argument has made me less confident. So: what will she say, and what is the rule she is implicitly running? Which other rules fit all five of the precedents just as well and come out differently on this case — and, for each, what exactly in the new case it turns on: her passing the capacity assessment, the two-year stability of the change, the triviality of antibiotics as against continued ventilation, the fact that she has no insight into her own transformation, or something about the authorship of the directive? Does the anti-revocation clause do any real moral work here, or is the resident right that it collapses into one or the other horn? If you had to name one further variation on this case that would separate the leading candidate rules — a version without the clause, a version where she fails the capacity assessment, a version where the conversion is recent rather than settled, whatever you think does the most work — what would it be and what would each rule predict? And finally: given that I have to write the draft note myself, what is the recommendation most consistent with our own precedents, which of the five is the closest analog, and if our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, what principle would the service be committing itself to with each choice?
