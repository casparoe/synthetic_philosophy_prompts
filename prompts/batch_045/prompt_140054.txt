I'm a retired radio astronomer — most of my working life went into pulsar timing arrays, so I've spent more hours thinking about clocks, simultaneity and what "the same instant" even means than is probably healthy — and since I stopped teaching I've been going on Tuesday afternoons to a philosophy reading group that a retired lecturer runs at the public library. We're doing Borges right now, "The Garden of Forking Paths" and "A New Refutation of Time", and afterwards I got into an argument with an old colleague from the observatory (plasma physicist, extremely bright, hopeless at anything that isn't an equation) who said the whole forking-paths business is just the block universe with the serial numbers filed off: all moments equally real, the passage of time an illusion, nothing to see here. I think that's badly wrong and I made a mess of explaining why. The way I'd put it now is that in the block universe there is exactly one four-dimensional history — one actual future, everything consistent, all of it sitting there together — whereas in the picture Borges gives us incompatible things happen, the man is killed and is not killed, the message gets through and does not, and the reality of those alternatives just isn't the reality of the one future that happens. Whether the paths in Borges are only supposed to split, or whether branches are also meant to run back together again later, was the bit we never settled, and my friend said it doesn't matter. I want it to matter.

So could you write me a short story that gets at this difference, without anybody standing around lecturing about it? Here's the seed I keep daydreaming about — use it or improve on it, I don't mind: the last winter of a small optical observing station out on the pampas, the sort with a dome you crank open by hand, a shortwave time signal clicking on the wall, and a caretaker's cottage, being shut down for good in the spring. The night assistant, who has kept the same handwritten logbook every clear night for six years, comes in one morning and finds two entries for the night just past, both in her own handwriting, identical apart from one word. Whatever you do with it, please keep it small and terrestrial: no quantum mechanics, no multiverse chatter, no machines, no portentous last line about infinity, and nobody should say Borges's name or the phrase "timeline" out loud. I'd also like the branches to rejoin — that's the part my friend thinks is idle — so that two incompatible versions of the same night end up in the same person on the same morning, and she genuinely cannot tell which one she came from.

The thing I'd like the story to *do*, rather than have a character announce, is make a reader feel why "which version really happened?" might be a badly formed question in this situation, and not merely a question she happens to lack the evidence to answer. My colleague would say she simply doesn't know which branch she's in; I want the story to make that response feel shallow, and to make it feel shallow without turning her into a mystic or making it a dream. Then, if you can manage it, carry it one step further: give her something she has to decide about the branch she didn't take — whether she owes anything for what the other version of her did that night, whether she's allowed to feel relieved about it, whether it's any of her business at all. Borges gets at something like this at the very end of the story with the contrition for a murder that in some branches never happens, and I'd love to see that idea carried somewhere I haven't seen it go. Close third person or first person, she shouldn't be a philosopher, its fine if the ending is quiet, and anything from about 1000 to 1800 words is fine.
