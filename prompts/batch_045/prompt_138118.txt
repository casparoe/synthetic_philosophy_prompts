I sit on the internal review board of a research institute where any one of eight senior researchers may unilaterally authorize a certain class of high-consequence experiments; the rest of us learn about it afterward, and the downside, if there is one, is borne by everyone. We are split over what rule to adopt. One faction argues that our situation is just the winner's curse from common-value auction theory in a new costume: the outcome is settled by the most optimistic estimate in the group in the same way that the item goes to the highest bidder, so the problem is purely a selection effect on noisy private estimates of what is, at bottom, one shared value. From this they conclude that we need no veto and no unanimity rule at all, only a correction each researcher applies privately: authorize only when the experiment would still look worth doing conditional on one's own estimate being the highest in the group, the way a well-advised bidder shades. Another faction reads the same analogy as supporting a hard near-unanimity requirement. I am not sure either reading is earning its keep.

Please audit the analogy. My own worries are that the actor in our case does not pay the price the winner pays, that we have no track record from which to calibrate how wrong we each tend to be, and that what we disagree about may be values rather than a single common quantity being estimated — but I cannot tell how much any of that matters.

Structure your reply in these sections, in this order:

1. The mapping (about 150 words, a table of correspondences is fine): what in the auction plays the role of each researcher, the shared value, the private estimate, the winning bid, the overpayment, and the other bidders.
2. Where the analogy genuinely holds (about 100 words): the features the two cases share that do the explanatory work.
3. The disanalogies that matter (about 250 words): three or four, ranked by how much they affect what the analogy can support, and separated from disanalogies that are merely decorative.
4. What the auction frame quietly imports (about 100 words): structural assumptions about payoffs, repetition, error distributions, and shared aims that our institute may not have.
5. Verdict on shade, don't veto (about 150 words): whether the no-veto conclusion survives, whether it survives for the reasons its defenders give, and what the conditional-on-being-most-optimistic test would actually require here.
6. A better analogy (about 100 words): one comparison that carries the same core insight without the misleading features, or a note on why none is available.
