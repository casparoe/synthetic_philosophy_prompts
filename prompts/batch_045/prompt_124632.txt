I spent most of last night arguing with a friend — a machine-learning theorist — about whether the no-free-lunch theorems have anything to do with Hume's problem of induction. Her position: the NFL results settle the old problem, negatively and once and for all. Average over all possible target functions and every learner does exactly as well as every other, so there is no such thing as a better inductive bias in general; there is only a bias matched to a domain. The philosophical puzzle, on her view, was a confusion generated by not noticing that the question 'which inductive method is best?' is incomplete until you name a distribution over environments. My position was that this does not dissolve the problem but relocates and sharpens it, because the interesting question was never about the uniform average. It is whether anything can be said, in a way that is not merely a restatement of what we were trying to justify, about which restrictions on the space of environments are admissible — and about what makes a given restriction the right one.

We got nowhere, partly because we were arguing past each other about what would even count as a satisfactory answer. So before I go back to her, I want the specification rather than a survey.

Write me the desiderata that a theory of inductive inference would have to meet. Fix the purpose narrowly: I want a theory that is good enough to tell a research group which inductive bias to adopt for a concrete inquiry of theirs, and to tell them why that answer is not just an expression of their preferences. A theory that only redescribes existing scientific practice, or that only explains why no general answer is available, does not do the job I care about — though if you think nothing can do that job, say so and say what the strongest available replacement is.

The weight should be on the requirements, not on scoring views against them. For each requirement give the case or consideration behind it, and mark it as one of three things: non-negotiable, a genuine desideratum that could be traded off against others, or a prejudice of one camp that should be struck from the list. I want at least the following handled, and you should add what I have missed:

- Whether the theory has to explain why the uniform average over target functions is the wrong benchmark rather than the honest one, and what it has to say to someone who insists the uniform average is the only neutral place to start.
- What fixes the grain at which a domain is individuated. NFL-style averaging gives different verdicts depending on how coarsely you carve up the space of environments, and a theory that lets you pick the grain to get the answer you want has not told you anything. Call this the domain-grain requirement if you like.
- The transport question: when does track-record success in one domain license carrying a bias into a neighbouring one, and what has to be true of the two domains for that to be legitimate?
- Whether resources matter. The uncomputable universal-prior style of answer buys its convergence guarantees at the price of not being implementable by anyone; is feasibility a desideratum for a theory of inference, or merely an engineering constraint on its applications?
- Whether the justification a theory offers for a bias may presuppose the reliability of the very kind of inference at issue. I take it something like a bootstrapping or vindication-style argument is the only thing on offer here, but I want you to say whether a non-circular requirement belongs on the list at all, and at what cost it has to be relaxed.

Two candidate desiderata of my own that I want you to rule on, because I suspect one of them is my prejudice and not a real requirement:

1. Recoding invariance: the theory's verdicts should not flip when the observation stream is re-encoded in a computationally equivalent way — relabelled, bit-flipped, or rewritten in a predicate system like the one Goodman used to make green things come out grue. My worry is that the NFL results themselves seem to show this cannot hold in full generality, since for any learner that does well somewhere there is a relabelling of the world that makes it do badly; so perhaps I am demanding the impossible and should drop it.
2. The requirement that the theory be able to order two hypotheses that fit all the data so far equally well, without appealing to the data. If a theory can only rank predictive rules by their past record, it seems to me to have abandoned the part of the problem that was about projection rather than description.

Then, and this is the part I most want: identify any subset of your requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied. If the list is internally inconsistent, that inconsistency is itself the interesting result — say which requirement you would drop, what the drop costs, and whether the surviving list still does the job I fixed above.

Finally, a short scorecard — I mean short, a few lines each — against your own list for the following, understood as families rather than as particular authors' views: Bayesian or universal-prior accounts, simplicity or minimum-description-length accounts, error-statistical or severe-testing accounts, and approaches that answer the problem by pointing to the causal or physical structure of the environment. I mainly want to see which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates.

Two constraints on how you go about it. Argue from the structure of the problem and from cases, not from who has said what: I do not want citations, a bibliography, or a reading list, and I do not want the argument to depend on the details of some paper I would have to look up. And I want your epistemic posture made explicit: attach to the document a note saying how confident you are in the list as a whole, and single out the one claim in it you are least sure of, with the consideration that would most quickly change your mind about it.
