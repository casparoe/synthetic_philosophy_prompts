Running a 90-minute session on moral luck at my city's rationalist meetup next month — usual crowd, maybe 15 people, most of them have been reading the blogs since the late oughts, they throw around "expected value" and "update" unironically, and they get visibly twitchy the moment anyone says the word "desert." We've got a whiteboard and no projector. Don't explain moral luck to me — I know the shape of it. What I need is a plan for how to get that specific room to actually feel the problem instead of dissolving it in the first five minutes.

So: the order the pieces have to go in and why (what depends on what), the one case that should carry the whole session, and the derailments to expect with the point in the sequence where I should head each one off. The derailments I'm most worried about, roughly in the order they'll arrive: (a) "this is just process vs. outcome, you evaluate the decision under uncertainty, EV handles it, next topic" — which I think is basically right about forward-looking blame and completely steps over the backward-looking part; (b) "we only track outcomes because they're evidence about the agent's hidden riskiness, so this is a base-rate-neglect story, not a fairness story" — I actually want to invite this one and then break it on purpose with a stipulated-omniscience version of the case, because my guess is that the intuition that survives that is the real payload of the session; (c) "desert isn't a coherent thing, drop the concept, done"; (d) "if determinism is true then constitutive luck swallows everything and the control principle is either vacuous or eats itself, so what are we even doing here" — I want that one parked at a definite spot rather than either answered or banned.

My half-formed take, which I want the session built to converge on if it holds up: there are two different activities that both get called "blame" — a forward-looking, incentive-shaping one that decision theory does handle cleanly, and a backward-looking one that tracks whether the agent's actual will was at fault — and the puzzle is that the backward-looking one is the one that can't be squared with "you only get credit for what's inside your control," but is also the one nobody in that room will actually give up. If that fork is where the session should land, tell me the sequence that gets us there; if it's a bad fork, say so and hand me a better one.

Also please give me: the carrying case with an explicit spec of what it has to have and what it has to lack (I want outcome luck as pure as I can get it — no hidden difference in character, no hidden difference in riskiness — so that nobody can sneak the evidential reading back in through the example), one question at the end that would tell me they've got the problem rather than just a verdict, and an honest list of what I should flat-out refuse to get into at this level. I'm guessing the free-will literature and legal sentencing practice are the two to name-and-refuse, but you tell me. Nobody's doing assigned reading — assume they know the standard drunk-driver-ish cases from cultural osmosis and literally nothing else, and don't give me names to drop, I'll skip them anyway.
