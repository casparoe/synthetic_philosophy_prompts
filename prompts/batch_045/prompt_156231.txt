I'm a retired experimental physicist — forty years of condensed matter, most of it on things you can hold in your hand — and for the last two years I've been sitting in on a philosophy of physics reading group at my old department. I've been asked to give them a short talk on Newton's bucket, and I would like to close it with a poem rather than with a summary slide or a list of unanswered questions.

What I have in mind is a sestina: thirty-nine lines, six stanzas of six plus a three-line envoi, in loose iambic pentameter, using the standard end-word rotation (each stanza's end-words being the previous stanza's in the order 6-1-5-2-4-3), with all six words gathered back into the envoi, two to a line.

The form is not decoration here. In a sestina the same six words keep returning in a new arrangement each time: nothing is added, nothing is removed, only the relations among the terms change. That is very nearly the relationist's claim about the bucket — that the difference between the bucket rotating and the bucket standing still while the cosmos wheels around it is a difference in arrangement alone. So I'd like the six end-words to be items in the scenario rather than abstractions: bucket, water, rope, void, stars, curve. If one of them won't carry its weight, replace it with another concrete thing from the set-up, but please don't substitute words like motion, frame, or relation — the point is that the vocabulary stays fixed and only its ordering moves.

For the run of the poem, roughly this: the bucket at rest with a flat surface; the rope twisting, the water lagging behind; then the water climbing the sides at just the moment when it has stopped moving relative to the bucket, which is the observation that does the real work; then the reading on which it is rotating with respect to space itself; then the reply on which what it rotates with respect to is the whole material universe, so that a bucket alone in an otherwise empty world would show no curve at all; then the stalemate; and the envoi drawing the six words together without adjudicating.

Two constraints on accuracy, if you can manage both. First, get the physics and the dialectic right: the concavity is greatest once water and bucket are co-rotating, and that is why rotation relative to the bucket cannot be what explains it; the substantivalist's extra posit is rotation with respect to space; the relationist's is rotation with respect to the distant matter. Someone who actually works on this should recognise the argument as theirs rather than as a caricature of theirs. Second, don't settle it — no rescue by way of general relativity, no quiet indication that one side was right all along. The closing lines should leave both readings standing, and a reader who knows nothing of the dispute should still come away having read a poem rather than a riddle.
