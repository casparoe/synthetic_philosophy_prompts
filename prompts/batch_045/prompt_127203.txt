I should say at the outset that I have no academic background in any of this. I am a retired building society manager, and for the last nine years I have been a volunteer trustee of a small city museum in the East Midlands with about forty thousand objects and a staff of six. For the past two years I have been reading about colonial-era collections and restitution on my own — mostly popular books and whatever I can get through the public library — because our collections committee has been asked to go through eleven objects acquired before 1950 and produce a written recommendation on each, and I do not think any of us knows what we are doing. The big cases everyone has heard of, the Benin Bronzes and the Parthenon Marbles, are not much help to us: by now both sides have a settled script, and our objects are not like that. They are ordinary things that arrived by ordinary routes and then sat in a storeroom for eighty years.

What I would like is a procedure my committee can actually run: the questions to ask, in the order to ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, where the procedure stops and says no or yes on its own, and where it has to hand the decision over to people because the question is one of values rather than facts. It should fit on two sides of A4, because it will be read by four trustees who have not read anything on the subject and by a city council culture committee, and it may be quoted in the local paper. We have half a day per object, no budget for more than a day of a solicitor's time, and our solicitor is not certain our governing documents permit us to dispose of anything at all.

There are seven difficulties that have come up in our own argument and that I want the procedure to force us through rather than let us skip.

One: an object whose paper history simply stops — and the gap in the record falls exactly on the years of a documented mass looting, even though there is no evidence about our particular object. Who carries the burden, and what would discharge it?

Two: cases where more than one party in the country of origin wants the same object: the national museums service, the court of the traditional ruler, a state government that has built a museum near where the object came from. Is returning it to the wrong one a return at all, and how would we decide who has standing?

Three: an object that was exchanged for something — a documented transaction, at the time, between an adult and an adult — where the community's descendants say the object was not the kind of thing anyone had the right to sell, whatever was handed over for it.

Four: return that ends the object's life as a museum object: things that would be buried, re-secreted, or used until they wear out, so that the argument about preserving them for everyone has no grip on the people asking for them.

Five: an object that is a piece of a dispersed whole — one of a pair, a fragment of a dismantled shrine — where the rest is in five other museums. Does that change what we owe, and does our returning it make it harder or easier for the others to do the same?

Six: objects that have acquired a second life here. Ours have been in this city for a hundred years, they turn up in local writing, and a community association whose families came from the region in the 1960s brings its children to see one of them on Saturday visits and wants it to stay. I want to know whether there is any claim at all that runs from the receiving side, and how much it weighs.

Seven: the case where the law says we may not do the thing we have concluded we should do. I would rather the procedure separated may we from should we cleanly, and told us what the honest alternatives are — long loan, acknowledgement, a replica — and which of them is a real answer and which is a way of keeping the thing that mattered.

Please then run the procedure on three of our objects, about a page each. The first is a cast brass head in the style of the court of Benin, bought at auction in London in 1962 and given to us in 1978, with nothing in the file between 1897 and 1962. The second is eleven ancestral skulls and two carved memorial figures from the highlands of what is now Papua New Guinea, collected in 1901 by a missionary-surgeon from this city, whose notebooks record that he handed over two steel axes for them, and whose descendants say they should be buried. The third is a carved wooden figure from the Cross River region, given to us in 1935 by a colonial officer who recorded that it was handed to his patrol during a campaign against a secret society, for which the national museums service has now asked, and which our local Nigerian-British community association has asked us to keep here on long loan, partly because their children see it and partly because they do not trust the storerooms it would go back to. At least one of the three should come out against what I would like: I would personally find it very hard to refuse the association, because these are our neighbours, and if the procedure says that their request is the old argument about our superior care in a friendlier voice, I would rather be told that plainly than have it smoothed over.

Finally, be frank about what a procedure like this cannot do: where it will misfire, which distinctions it has to drop to stay usable by people like us, and which parts of the question can only be settled by a vote of trustees who are guessing. Please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, footnotes, or case citations — I have no library access beyond the public one and no way to check any of it, and what I need is something we can run in a room. And please write it in British English.
