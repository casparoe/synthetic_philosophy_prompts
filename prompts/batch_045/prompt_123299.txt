I've been reading Paul Grice on my own over the past few months — I have no philosophy background, I just picked up the lecture manuscripts after an argument with friends about whether the text a large language model produces actually means anything. For background: Grice was born in Birmingham, gave the William James Lectures at Harvard in 1967, and died in Berkeley in 1988, so he never saw any of this, and I'd like you to reconstruct what he would have said, grounding it in the machinery he actually built rather than in generic pros and cons about AI. My friends split three ways. One says his analysis of speaker meaning in terms of nested intentions settles it: no intention, no meaning, so a model means nothing at all. Another says the Cooperative Principle and the maxims were always a rational reconstruction of communicative practice, so they apply to whatever we are trying to interpret, intentions or not, and we should just read the model as if it were a cooperative agent. A third says the real speaker is the company that deployed the system, since it is the only thing with aims. I noticed in an encyclopaedia entry that Grice's work is now cited in research on animal communication, which made me wonder whether his framework was already under pressure from signallers that presumably have no Gricean intentions, and whether the AI case is just that pressure in a new form.

Three cases I keep getting stuck on, and I'd like his verdict on each. First: a customer-service assistant at a utility company can only emit sentences from a fixed menu of a couple of hundred pre-approved templates, and a learned policy selects whichever template has historically produced the highest satisfaction score. Nobody chose a sentence in order to convey anything, yet customers reliably come away having been told their refund is approved when the template emitted was something like a cheerful Let me look into that for you. Note that the inference here seems to depend on which alternative templates were available on the menu and were not chosen — a contingent fact about system design rather than about language — which strikes me as relevant to whether it is detachable. Second: an assistant that is barred from confirming or denying something, and which responds to a direct question with a long, polished, apparently relevant paragraph that never actually answers it; everyone reads that as a yes, and their reasoning seems to run from the assumption that the system is being evasive rather than from the assumption that it is being cooperative. If the operator adds a disclaimer saying that evasion does not mean yes, does that cancel the reading, or does the fact that it survives cancellation push it into some other category? Third: someone prompts a model to produce a sentence, reposts the output as their own, and later says they never said it, the machine did. Here one agent has the intention and another produces the words. Is there a speaker meaning, and if so, whose?

For each case, please say which Gricean category he would put it in — genuine conversational implicature, some other item in his taxonomy, or a case where he would say nothing is implicated at all — and actually run his own diagnostics rather than just asserting the label: whether the content is calculable from the assumed cooperative footing, whether it is cancellable without contradiction, whether it is detachable. Where the answer turns on some technical feature of his account I may not have appreciated — his distinction between flouting a maxim and merely violating it, the layering of utterer's meaning over utterance-type meaning and sentence meaning, the way he treated saying as constrained by conventional meaning, or his methodological razor against multiplying senses — please say which feature is doing the work and why. Then: which of my three friends would he have found most nearly right, which single commitment would he abandon first if he thought the cases forced a revision (the intention-dependence of speaker meaning, the rational grounding of the maxims, or the razor), and where, if anywhere, do you think the way my friends and I have set the question up is itself confused? Please write in British English, and please don't pad the reply with references or a reading list — I have no access to a library and it wouldn't help me see the argument.
