You are a professional philosopher who has published on free will and moral responsibility and who has served on several PhD comprehensive exam committees. Before writing anything, please spend a few sentences thinking about where the real crux of this topic lies and what a merely competent answer would look like; then draft the exam materials.

I co-teach my department's graduate seminar on moral responsibility, and this year I'm on the committee setting the comprehensive exam. Last year's question on Galen Strawson's Basic Argument failed as an assessment: almost every candidate reproduced the regress — we do what we do because of the way we are, so being truly responsible for what we do requires being truly responsible for the way we are, which requires having made ourselves, which requires that we already were some way, and so on — and then stopped. Nothing separated the candidates who understood the argument's structure from those who had merely memorized the phrase causa sui. So I want a question built around a specific hinge, and I'd like it built around an objection I've been trying to develop. Roughly: call it ampliative self-creation. Grant Strawson everything about causation by prior character and grant that no one can be causa sui. Still, an agent's acts can reorganize her own subsequent character — reducing akratic conflict, installing second-order policies, making her motives more unified and more transparent to her — so that later acts express her to a greater degree than earlier ones did. Model that as a scalar quantity, degree of self-determination, which rises over time without ever reaching 1; then the regress is not vicious but benign, since what is demanded is not an uncaused first link but a sequence whose later links are increasingly the agent's own. The Strawsonian reply I keep running into is that amplification from a base of zero stays zero: if the earliest links are not truly yours, no amount of subsequent reorganization makes a later link truly yours. My intended reply is that this objection assumes the relevant quantity is conserved under transmission — that unification merely recombines what was already there — whereas organization can be genuinely new, since a commitment or a policy is a structure not present in the motives it organizes. I am not confident that last move works, and I don't want the exam to presuppose that it does; if you think my formulation has a fatal flaw, say so in a short note before the question and then set the question around the strongest version of the idea you can defend, flagging what you changed.

What I need: one exam question, answerable in sixty minutes and worth twenty marks, that cannot be passed by summarizing the Basic Argument, that forces candidates to say what would have to be true for the regress to be vicious rather than benign, and that separately asks whether a scalar, self-amplifying notion of responsibility would deliver the kind of desert Strawson claims is impossible or whether it changes the subject into something Strawson could happily accept as a non-desert-based surrogate. Two further constraints: the argument is supposed to work whether or not determinism is true, so the question should not let candidates drift into the consequence argument or luck objections, and the marking scheme should penalize an answer that treats ampliative self-creation as just another compatibilism about determinism. Then give a model answer of roughly seven hundred words and a marking scheme: five must-hit points with marks totalling twenty, a one-line note for each on how candidates typically get it wrong, and a short closing paragraph on what distinguishes an A answer from a B. Assume candidates sit the exam without library access: don't require recall of any particular paper, and please don't add citations or a reading list to the model answer.
