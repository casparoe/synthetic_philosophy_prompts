I'm second-marking a midterm for an undergraduate political philosophy course and want an independent read on one answer before I finalize the grade. Below is the question as it was set and one student's response.

The question:

(a) State the three principles of Nozick's entitlement theory and explain, in your own words, how the Wilt Chamberlain example is supposed to embarrass patterned principles of justice. Be explicit about what work the word voluntary is doing, and distinguish Nozick's point from the crude point that everyone ends up better off.

(b) Now consider a variant I will call the Marrow Flats case. Marrow Flats is a company town: one firm owns the mine, the housing, the shops, and the basketball club. Workers are paid in scrip that can be spent only at the company's own stores and on the company's own entertainments, and their rent is deducted before pay. On game night each worker freely drops a quarter into the box at the gate for the right to watch the town's star play; nobody is threatened, and each of them, asked afterward, says they would rather watch the game than spend the quarter any other way. After a season the star is rich and everyone else is as poor as before. Apply the entitlement theory to this case. Does anything in the chain from the original acquisition of the land to the quarter in the box fail Nozick's conditions? If so, which principle is supposed to deal with that failure, and does having to invoke it weaken the anti-patterning moral that part (a) was supposed to establish? Say exactly what the Lockean proviso does and does not do here.

The student's answer:

The entitlement theory has three principles: the principle of acquisition, which lets a person appropriate unowned things provided the Lockean proviso is met; the principle of transfer, which lets holdings move by consent; and the principle of rectification, which corrects past violations of the first two. Justice in holdings is wholly historical: a distribution is just if it arose through just acquisition and voluntary transfer, whatever pattern it happens to display. Patterned and end-state principles, by contrast, judge a distribution by how it looks at a moment: strict equality, the old socialist maxim about ability and need, utilitarianism.

The Wilt Chamberlain example turns this against them. Take any favoured patterned distribution D1. Let a million people each drop a quarter into a box for the privilege of watching Chamberlain play. The result is D2, in which Chamberlain holds a quarter of a million dollars. Every step was voluntary, so by the entitlement theory D2 is just; but no patterned principle can accept both D1 and D2. So a state that wants to keep the pattern must forbid capitalist acts between consenting adults, and must go on interfering forever. The argument is not the crude one that D2 serves everyone's welfare; it works even if we suppose the fans would have enjoyed the beer more, since what matters is how the holdings arose rather than how anyone feels about the outcome. What matters is only that no one forced them, and that each holding therefore came about in the approved way.

The Marrow Flats case changes nothing. The fans were not dragged to the gate; each preferred the game to the alternatives open to them, and the star's holdings are built out of an original acquisition of unowned land that satisfied the proviso plus a long series of voluntary transfers. There is therefore nothing here for the principle of rectification to correct. The scrip and the company housing are unpleasant, but the Lockean proviso is not offended by them: the proviso asks only whether anyone is made worse off by the transaction, and the workers plainly are not, since without the company's offer they would be worse off still. So the entitlement theory holds D2 just in Marrow Flats too, and the moral of part (a) survives untouched: once the background is just, liberty upsets any pattern, and equality could only be preserved by stopping the fans from spending their own money as they choose.

What I need from you: a mark out of 100 and the specific comments that justify it. Reply as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Item 1 should give the mark and the one-line reason for it. Items 2 through 4 should cover, in order, what the answer gets right, the conflation or unsupported step in its treatment of the Lockean proviso, and what it misses about the Marrow Flats chain and about rectification. Item 5 should say what would change your mind: which reading of Nozick, or which further stipulation about Marrow Flats, would make this answer better than you have marked it, and where your own reading of the case is most likely to be wrong.
