I'm taking this slightly weird independent study on medieval Jewish philosophy (mostly me, one grad student, and a lot of coffee), and last night I was argueing with two friends from my reading group about Maimonides on animal sacrifice and we definately kept going in circles. Here's the knot: in the Guide for the Perplexed (Part 3, chapter 32), Maimonides gives that famous accommodationist story—he says it is impossible to go suddenly from one extreme to the other, that people cling to whatever worship they were raised with, and that in those days the common custom was sacrificing animals in temples with images, bowing to those images, and burning incense before them, while the really religious folks were serving in temples devoted to the stars or to created beings and imaginary unreal things. So God, acting like a wise physician, did not command the Israelites to give up all those familiar modes of service; instead He transferred them to His own service—build a sanctuary, set up an altar, offer sacrifices, bow, burn incense—but now directed to Him alone, with the result that the traces of idolatry were blotted out and the existence and unity of God were firmly established without deterring or confusing minds by abolishing the only worship they knew. He even says it would be as shocking then as if a prophet today told us to serve God only in thought, with no prayer, no fasting, no asking for help in trouble.

The problem my friends and I couldn't get past is that this sounds like sacrifices are a regrettable concession, a kind of spiritual training wheel for a people who weren't ready for pure intellectual worship—so why does Maimonides turn around in the Mishneh Torah (Laws of Kings and Wars, chapter 11, first law) and say that in the days of the Messianic king the observance of all the statutes will return to their previous state, that we will offer sacrifices and keep the Sabbatical and Jubilee years according to all their particulars, and that this Torah, its statutes and its laws, are everlasting and not to be added to or detracted from? If sacrifices were only a fading crutch meant to wean Israel off idolatry, the ideal final state should ditch them, not restore them forever. To try to get unstuck, we invented a distinction (don't laugh) between two kinds of non-ideal practice: a 'fading crutch,' which is a practice whose entire point is to disappear once its therapeutic job is done, like training wheels that must come off or a cast that gets removed when the bone heals; and a 'permanent scaffold,' which is still not the highest end and is still only justified by human psychological limits, but which even a perfected community retains because taking it away would be impossible, or because it has become the load-bearing structure for the rest of the legal system, or because its continued presence doesn't actually compete with the higher worship for those who have attained it. We called the first reading of Maimonides the fading-crutch reading and the second the permanent-scaffold reading.
