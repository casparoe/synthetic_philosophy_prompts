I'm one of the volunteer officers of a housing co-op with about 180 member households, and I've landed in the middle of an argument about how we make collective decisions. I'm a retired accountant — I look after the books in the evenings — so procedural questions are the sort of thing I notice, but I have no formal training in any of this. We currently elect our board by plurality and decide other business by simple yes-or-no votes, and a few of us have been pushing to move the board elections to ranked ballots. On our members' forum someone closed the thread with what he plainly took to be a conversation-stopper: "every voting system can be gamed, that's literally Gibbard–Satterthwaite, so it doesn't matter what we pick." I'm fairly sure that leap doesn't follow, but I can't locate exactly where it breaks, because the slogan itself seems to me to slide between several quite different claims.

So what I'd like is a numbered menu I can take back to the forum: the distinct precise things that one-liner could be asserting, from the near-tautological — no voting rule is perfect on every criterion anyone might care about — up to genuinely strong ones: that for any non-dictatorial rule, at some configuration of preferences, some voter could get a better outcome by misreporting; that manipulation is frequent and easy enough that real voters will in fact do it; that all rules are manipulable to roughly the same degree, so no reform could help; that the problem covers coordinated groups and not just individuals going it alone. For each reading, please say in a line or two whether it is a platitude nobody would dispute, the actual content of a theorem, a plausible but unproved strengthening, or simply false — and, which matters most to me, whether that reading would really entitle my co-op member to conclude that our choice of rule makes no difference. Where a reading is the genuine article, please spell out the assumptions it leans on — how many options are on the table, what kind of ballot voters submit, whether the rule is allowed to randomise, whether every option has to be capable of winning, and anything else load-bearing — in a form I can check against our situation.

Two wrinkles from our own case that I'd like handled explicitly, since I suspect they are where his argument is weakest. First, our second kind of vote is structurally different from the board elections: once a year we set the per-household maintenance levy, and everyone seems to have a single favourite figure and to be less happy the further the figure moves away from it in either direction. A member suggested we simply adopt the middle-most proposal rather than averaging or running a runoff, and it feels to me as though nobody could gain by exaggerating under such a rule — but I can't tell whether the slogan is even meant to cover a case like that, or whether it only bites when preferences are messier. Second, someone else proposed settling close board elections by drawing lots among the two candidates with the most first-place votes, on the theory that if the outcome is partly random there is nothing to manipulate. Please tell me whether "every system can be gamed" has anything to say about a rule with chance in it, and be careful to separate what actually follows from what merely sounds plausible.

Please approach this as a social choice theorist who has been asked to advise a real organisation, and think the space of possible readings through carefully before settling on your list rather than stopping at the first disambigulation that seems reasonable. Where you give verdicts, I'd like you to flag your confidence, and to separate what is settled — the mathematics itself, which I assume nobody disputes — from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this: for example, how often strategic voting actually occurs in practice, whether immunity to manipulation should be traded off against other properties we might care about, such as electing someone who would beat every other candidate head-to-head or letting people express more than a bare ranking, and whether it is realistic to hope that our own preferences fall into the tidy shape that would take us out of the theorem's range in the first place. If you think the slogan is being misused in our thread, say so plainly — but also say which reading the person deploying it most likely had in mind, and whether that reading survives scrutiny.
