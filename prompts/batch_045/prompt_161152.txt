So there's a postdoc who runs the decision theory reading group I go to, and over the last few weeks she's given verdicts on a whole string of choice-without-preference cases. I'm trying to work out what rule she's running. Her calls so far:

- Two cans of the same soup, same brand, same price, same best-before date: take either one, nothing more to say.
- Buridan's ass, two bales of hay equally good and placed symmetrically, and it'll starve if it keeps dithering: it may go for either one; starving is bad luck, not irrationality.
- Two restaurant meals you like exactly equally, except one comes with a slightly nicer garnish: she says that tiny difference is enough and you've got to take the garnished one. She definately rejected the idea that small improvements are too small to matter normatively.
- Two jobs, one pays way better and the other is in a city you'd be much happier in, no common scale between them: either is permissible, but not because they're equal — they aren't comparable, and there's no further fact of the matter about which is better. She calls these hard choices and calls the soup ones ties.
- Two apartments equally good, you're agonising, then a third apartment that's clearly worse gets listed and suddenly picking one of the first two feels easy: she says the third option changes nothing normative, the relief is just a feeling.
- Coin available, two equal options: flipping is fine but not required, deliberately picking either is fine too.
- Two equal options, and for a tiny cost (one minute of reading reviews) you could find out which is actually better: she says getting the information is optional, not something you owe.

Now the case I want to bring next week. Two career paths that are incomparable in her sense rather than equal — a well paid stable job in a city you'd hate, and a precarious artistic one you'd probably love. No deadline. A fair coin is available. You could also hand the decision to a friend who knows you well and would just pick one for you, no better informed than you are. Extra wrinkle: you're fairly confident that whichever one you end up in, you'll come to care about it and prefer it afterwards, and you don't think that's self-deception, you think its just how people work.

What's she going to say? May I just pick either one? Am I required to flip, or to let the friend decide? Is there something I'm supposed to do myself — commit to one, own it, stand behind it — that I'm not allowed to hand over to a coin? I dont mainly want the answer, I want the rule: what rules fit her seven verdicts about as well as each other, what does each of them predict about this case, which one do you think she's actually running and why, and what single further case would I put to her to tell those rules apart. If you think her verdicts are already inconsistent, or that one of them is doing more work than the rest, say that too.
