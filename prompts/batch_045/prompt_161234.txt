The case type they were arguing about: a name is introduced into a community and passed around, everyone who uses it associates it with a certain description, and it then turns out that the description is satisfied by a different person from the one the name was originally attached to. Does a speaker's use of the name pick out whoever satisfies the description, or the person the name traces back to? It has been reported that answers to questions like this vary between Western and East Asian samples, though Amara and Devin disagree about how well the replication record supports that. Please treat what the data ultimately show as an open question and concentrate on the methodological argument.

The exchange, more or less as it happened:

(1) Amara: The philosophers' evidential base is culturally unrepresentative — mostly their own armchair plus Western undergraduates. And if answers to a case vary systematically with culture, then either most respondents are simply wrong, in which case we need a non-question-begging way of telling who is wrong, or there is no shared fact for the intuition to be tracking at all. Either way the evidential use of intuition should be downgraded: intuitions are data about our concepts, not about the subject matter.

(2) Devin: The unrepresentativeness charge only bites on surveys that generalise from a sample to a population. Philosophy isn't polling. It uses intuitions as exercises of a competence, the way a linguist uses a speaker's verdict on a sentence. Variation then only shows that some respondents fail to exercise the competence — the way variation in people's sums shows arithmetical error, not arithmetical relativism. Unless you can show that the deviating respondents both understood the case and weren't in error, the variation is noise, not evidence against the method.

(3) Amara: That's the expertise defence, and it's circular: competence gets certified by producing the answer the philosopher already accepts, and unlike arithmetic there's no independent calibration standard — no correct answer available by another route. And here specifically, the deviating answers look like they track a different feature of the story: who the community in the story would hold responsible, or what the story's own speakers would say. That suggests the respondents grasp the case perfectly well and simply read the question differently. So the error hypothesis isn't the default.

(4) Devin: If they're reading the question differently, then there's no disagreement in the first place: it isn't one proposition receiving conflicting verdicts. Merely verbal variation can't be evidence that intuition is unreliable — the fact that a word has two senses doesn't show that people disagree about anything. To run a debunking argument you need same question, different answer. You haven't shown that.

(5) Amara: Agreed — which is why it should be tested. My proposal: after the case judgement, ask respondents to paraphrase the question in their own words and to say which feature of the story they took to be decisive. Suppose the protocol returns three things: within a single coded reading the residual variation shrinks sharply but not to zero; the distribution of readings is itself strongly patterned by culture; and philosophers land on one reading and treat it as the question. Then your move backfires, because the choice of reading is doing all the philosophical work and that choice is itself culturally located. Call it reading drift: the disambiguation your defence needs is not a neutral procedure but a culturally situated act. Even purely verbal disagreement can therefore be evidentially damaging.

(6) Devin: But any disagreement whatever can be redescribed as a difference of reading, so your protocol would "debunk" intuition use even where intuitions are perfectly reliable — and a method that could never in principle be vindicated isn't a method. Someone must have the prerogative to fix the reading. We stipulate that the phenomenon being theorised is the one the tradition cares about — the one where the name traces back through use — and then the lay variation is variation about a different phenomenon, and irrelevant.

(7) Amara: If you stipulate the reading, the intuition becomes ornament. The original argument's whole force came from the untutored judgement that the name reaches past the description. Stipulation concedes the substance of my conclusion while keeping its vocabulary. And the prerogative can't be unilateral: the point of appealing to what we all intuitively say was to settle a question we'd otherwise just beg.

(8) Devin: Then take the middle way. Treat intuitions as defeasible inputs into reflective equilibrium rather than as a tribunal: pre-register the intended reading, sample across populations, and let cross-population stability do the weighting. And the protocol needn't be damaging everywhere — if you run it on the side-effect cases, where an agent foresees a side effect that is either good or bad and people's verdicts on whether it was brought about intentionally flip with the valence, the pattern may well prove robust across cultures. That would make debunking local rather than global: you'd have earned the right to lean on intuition in some domains and been told to stop in others, which is a research programme, not a crisis.
