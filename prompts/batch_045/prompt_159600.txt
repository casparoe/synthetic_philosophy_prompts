I'm on the clinical ethics committee at our hospital trust (big NHS teaching hospital) and I'm supposed to circulate a written opinion before Thursday's meeting, so please draft it for me.

The case: Alan, 44, fifteen months out from a severe brain injury, assessed repeatedly at the bedside with no signs of awareness, diagnosed as being in a vegetative state / unresponsive wakefulness syndrome. His family want us to run a test offered by a visiting research group from another university — an EEG measure they call the "broadcast index". Their pitch is straight out of global workspace theory: a stimulus counts as conscious when it sets off a late, widespread, sustained burst of recurrent activity, "ignition", that makes the information available at once to lots of downstream systems — memory, attention, report, action. The index is meant to detect that without needing any behaviour from the patient. They validated it in healthy volunteers, where it separates stimuli people say they saw from ones they didn't about nine times in ten; it falls during deep sleep and under anaesthesia; and it runs high during REM sleep, which they read as picking up dreaming.

Two results are on the table for Alan. At rest his index is low. When the researchers play repeated spoken instructions — imagine moving your right hand, that kind of thing — it jumps into the range they associate with conscious perception, even though he does nothing observable. Nothing in his imaging rules awareness in or out either way. The group is also spinning out a company around the index, which the clinical team are not slow to point out.

So the committee has to decide four things, and I need a recommendation on each, with who does what by when:
(1) do we run the test again outside a research protocol, and if so on what basis (repeat it twice more? second opinion from another lab?);
(2) does a positive result license a "presumed experience" protocol — analgesia before turning, suctioning and so on — given that the nursing staff worry about over-sedation and chest infections;
(3) does it license a time-limited trial of some brain–computer-interface communication attempt, and how long before we call it off;
(4) how much weight, if any, should it get if his clinically assisted nutrition and hydration ever comes up in a best-interests decision.

One subgroup has floated a two-tier framework: access-type evidence (the index) triggers only reversible steps like a communication trial and analgesia, while anything as serious as a CANH decision would need evidence of experience, which on their view we can never actually have. I need you to say whether we should adopt that two-tier split or collapse it into one standard, and why.

The objection I half share: global workspace theory is arguably a theory of access — of what's globally available for report and control — and Alan can neither report nor act, so a marker calibrated on healthy people's button presses may just be registering a brain doing the processing with nobody there for it to appear to. The thing we care about is whether there's something it's like for him, and this marker may not touch that. The objection I also half share on the other side: we never get direct evidence of phenomenal consciousness from anyone, so demanding more than a broadcast marker here applies a standard we apply to no other patient, and the asymmetry of the errors is brutal — miss consciousness and you either let him die or put him through months of unanaesthetised procedures.

Please deal honestly with whichever of those you end up ruling against, and say what we should do for Alan specifically, not just what our policy should be in general. Don't cite papers or name studies — we can't check the literature anyway, and half the committee won't recieve the reading in time.

Write it as the opinion itself, starting on line one — no preamble, no "this memo considers", no restating the question back at me — with the bottom line in the first sentence and the reasoning after it. About 800 words, British English throughout.
