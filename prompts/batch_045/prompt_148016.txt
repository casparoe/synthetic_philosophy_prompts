I work in operations for a mid-sized freight company — mostly building driver schedules and assigning routes — and for several years now I have been arguing with my brother-in-law about who actually deserves what at work. He thinks the whole question is easy: pay and praise should track results, and everything else is an excuse. I think results are the wrong thing to look at, but I cannot say what the right thing is, and whenever I try to write down a principle it either swallows his view whole or collapses into mush. I have been reading around on my own (no philosophy background, so please translate jargon), and I have gotten as far as a distinction I have seen drawn between institutional desert — what a company or a prize committee has reason to award by its own rules — and something called basic desert, the thing that would make it fitting for a person to get credit or blame apart from any institution handing it out. I suspect my verdicts keep sliding between the two, but I am not sure. Here are my gut verdicts on a set of cases, including two I keep going back and forth on, and after them the principle I have been trying out.

1. Nepotism. A manager gives her son the plum account; he then does the work genuinely well and the account does great. My verdict: he deserves less credit than the colleague who would have done the job at least as well and was passed over, even though the results were identical and even though he worked hard once he had the account.
2. Natural gift. A singer is born with an unusual voice, trains brutally for fifteen years, and becomes excellent. My verdict: she deserves the acclaim fully, with no discount for the fact that the raw material was pure luck. This sits badly next to case 1, because in both cases an unearned advantage was causally necessary for the result, and in both cases the person then worked hard.
3. Lucky territory. Two salespeople of equal skill and effort work identical hours; one closes a huge deal only because her territory happened to contain a booming new development. My verdict: she is entitled to the commission under the company's rules and it would be wrong to claw it back, but she does not really deserve credit as a salesperson for the difference.
4. Accidental hero. Someone carelessly drops a heavy plank, which jams a mechanism and stops a vehicle from hitting a child. My verdict: no credit at all, not even a little, despite the outcome and despite the fact that his action made all the difference in the world.
5. Inseparable team. Five people build a product where every one of them was indispensable but nobody's individual contribution can be pulled apart from the others — the value only exists in the combination. My verdict: an equal split feels right, and I am suspicious of a formula I read about, from cooperative game theory (the Shapley value, I think), for dividing a joint surplus by averaging each person's marginal contribution over all the orders in which they could have joined. That formula looks elegant but I cannot tell whether it is describing what fairness requires or just what a certain kind of bargaining would produce.
6. Effort without aptitude. Someone works extremely hard at a technical job and produces mediocre results, while a colleague who coasts produces excellent ones. My verdict: the hard worker deserves something — recognition of a kind — but not the bonus, and I am unsure whether the something is credit or merely encouragement, given that his capacity for sustained effort is itself largely a product of how he was raised.
7. Retroactive defeat. Someone does a genuinely great piece of work and is later revealed to have treated people appallingly in private, or uses the work's fruits harmfully. My verdict: I do not want to say the credit for the work is cancelled, but I also do not want to say the person is owed the same esteem. I cannot get these into one sentence.

The principle I have been trying out is this: a person deserves credit for an outcome in proportion to the difference their choices made to it, counting counterfactually (what happened with them acting as they did, against what would have happened without them), discounted by any advantage they did not earn — but only where the advantage was the kind that someone else could have had instead of them — with costly effort counting as a separate, smaller desert base of its own. Case 4 suggests the counterfactual part is wrong or at least incomplete, and I cannot say what replaces it.

What I would like: the principle, or the two or three best candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself, with the case that motivates each clause and the cost of each clause (what it commits me to that I might not like). Build me a table with the columns case, my verdict, verdict under your principle, match (exact, partial, or none), the load-bearing feature of the case that produces that verdict, and whether your principle needed repair to get there. I would also like your principle run on two cases I have not listed: a designer who produces excellent work using a heavy AI assistant that does most of the drafting, and a person who gives up a promising career to care for an injured sibling for a decade — does the sacrifice itself add to what she deserves, or is that the wrong question? And tell me whether your principle is symmetric: does whatever handles credit also handle blame, or does it only work on one side?
