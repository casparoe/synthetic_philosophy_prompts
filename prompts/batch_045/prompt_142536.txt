Been turning something over for a while and I don't have a word for it, hoping someone here does.

Take tipping. In New York it's basically compulsory: the waiter is owed it, you're a bad person if you stiff them. But there's a way of tipping that somehow lands worse than not tipping at all — working out the exact percentage on your phone, saying "that's the customary rate, yeah?", handing it over like you're settling an invoice. Same money, same hands, and people react as though you've insulted them. Compare the person who leaves the same amount without thinking about it, who is completely fine.

Second case. I'm British, recently somewhere with a much looser queueing culture. Back home, letting someone go ahead of you at the bus stop is a tiny courtesy; where I am now it's more of a scrum and you hold your ground. When I do the local thing I feel fine. When I do the local thing *as* the local thing — a small mental "when in Rome" before I do it — I feel slightly grubby, and I don't think that's just leftover Britishness. And I've seen the mirror image: a visitor here who very visibly adopts the local way of doing it and reads as condescending rather than respectful, even though her behaviour is exactly what everyone around her is doing.

So the thing I want a name for: conventions where what you owe isn't the behaviour, it's the behaviour done without it occuring to you to do otherwise. Which means the person with most reason to think about what's required — the visitor, the one who looked the norm up before travelling — is structurally incapable of getting it right, and the more carefully they comply, the worse they do. And it isn't read as mere awkwardness; it's read as a moral failing, a kind of contempt. I don't think a plain signalling story captures it, because on that story the observable behaviour is all there is to get right, and here the observable behaviour is identical in the good and the bad case.

Does this have a name? Someone once told me Kant has a distinction about doing the right thing for the right reason, but from what I remember his version says the opposite of what I mean — that acting *because* it's required is the good case. So if there's something established, say how it differs from that and from the general wrong-reasons stuff, and where its edges are: does it apply to all conventions or only some (tipping yes, driving on the left no — why?), is this really obligation or just etiquette wearing obligation's clothes, and what is the visitor supposed to do about it. I don't want a label dropped on it, I want the carving. English spelling please, I can't be doing with -ize endings.
