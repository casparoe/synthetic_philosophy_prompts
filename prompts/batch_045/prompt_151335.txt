I'm on a team drafting a deployment safety case for a system we expect to be considerably more capable than any model we can trust and run as a monitor. The ground rule we've been given is that no measure in the case may presuppose that the deployed model is aligned: each one has to be justified on the assumption that the model is trying to sabotage us in ways we would not notice, and would notice our countermeasures. In practice we keep arguing about which measures belong in the case at all, and I've noticed that my own gut verdicts don't line up with each other. Here they are.

1. The untrusted model writes the artifact; a weaker trusted model reviews it before anything runs. Control. Obviously in.
2. The same, except that the untrusted model also writes out step-by-step reasoning for the monitor to read. I want to call this control — my colleagues treat it as continuous with case 1 — but it nags at me.
3. Monitoring only the reasoning stream, with the final artifact checked separately or not at all. Not control. My gut says this leans on the reasoning being a faithful report, which is close to assuming honesty.
4. The untrusted model produces many candidate artifacts; a weaker trusted model selects the least suspicious one and makes only minimal edits to it. Control, and I'd rank it above case 1.
5. Resampling: run the untrusted model many times on rephrased versions of the input and discard outputs that disagree with the rest. I go back and forth. There is no worst-case story at all — an adversary that wants to sabotage consistently is free to sabotage consistently — and yet I'd still want it in the stack.
6. Asking the untrusted model how confident it is, or whether its last output was sabotaged, and deferring to the trusted model when the answer sounds worrying. Not control. That is asking the suspect.
7. Training the model to report its reasoning faithfully and then relying on those reports. Not control. That is alignment work wearing a control badge.
8. A white-box deception detector: a classifier over internal activations, trained to separate deceptive from non-deceptive processing using models fine-tuned to be deceptive on demand. My gut files this with case 1. Then I notice that the training signal came from models that did what we asked, including when what we asked was to be deceptive, and I can't say why this isn't case 3 with extra machinery.
9. Structural measures: no network access, sandboxed execution, rate limits, tripwires that halt a run when its pattern of tool use looks wrong. Control, but cheap control. I don't think any of them survive a model that can plan around them.

Two pairs are doing the real work in my head, and I'd like you to concentrate on them. First, cases 1 and 3: in both, a weaker trusted system reads something the untrusted system produced — so why is one an evidential assumption about the untrusted system and the other apparently not? Second, cases 3 and 8: in both, something other than the output is being read as evidence about the output — so why does my gut separate them, and is that separation defensible?

The test I have been using, and now think is broken, is this: replace the untrusted model with a hostile human expert of equal capability and equal information, including full knowledge of the measure in place, and ask whether the measure's guarantee survives. Under that substitution almost everything collapses — the expert writes subtly backdoored code the weak reviewer passes, games the candidate-selection process, defeats the activation classifier — and I am left with case 9, which says nothing about what to do with the model's actual outputs. So the test is too strong; but too strong is not a diagnosis, and I don't have a repair.

The repair I keep circling is to distinguish guarantees conditioned on the adversary's capabilities from guarantees conditioned on the adversary's dispositions. Roughly: a measure counts as control iff the difficulty of defeating it is a question about what the adversary can do rather than about what it wants, is willing to do, or would admit to doing — iff you can state the condition of failure as a capability threshold you could in principle test (can it write a backdoor that survives review by a model at capability level X?) rather than as a claim about its motives, its honesty, or its cooperation. But I'm not confident in this cut point, and I suspect it relocates the problem rather than solving it: any capability threshold has to be estimated from the behavior of systems that are, for all we know, cooperating, and estimation is an average-case exercise even when the guarantee being estimated is a worst-case one.

What I want from you is the following. Give me a principle — one, or at most two rivals — stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a measure I haven't considered, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it and no clause added merely for tidiness. Then give its verdicts on the cases I haven't listed here: the untrusted model writing the monitor itself, subject to a trusted model's sign-off; two copies of the untrusted model cross-examining each other's work with a trusted model judging the transcript; a payment or scoring scheme under which sabotage that gets caught is made expensive for the model; honeypots, where the model is handed an easy opportunity to sabotage during evaluation and deployed only if it declines; a tripwire whose trigger the model is deliberately kept ignorant of; letting the untrusted model decide which of its own outputs get audited; and passing the untrusted output through the trusted model to be paraphrased before review, so as to strip out any covert channel between the two systems. Then tell me honestly which of my nine verdicts the principle forces me to give up, and in particular how it settles cases 2 and 5. Finally, tell me how much of the resulting classification rests on empirical facts my team could actually measure over the next few months — name the facts and say which way each one would move a measure across the line.

I'm not after a survey of the control literature or a taxonomy of techniques. I want the cut point: where it sits, why there rather than one step up or down, and what it costs me to accept it.
