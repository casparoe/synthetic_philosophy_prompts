I organize a monthly reading group that works through one classic twentieth-century philosophy paper per session, and I open each meeting with a single very short joke: short enough to fit on one slide and to be read aloud in under thirty seconds. Next month's paper is Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism," and I need the joke for it.

Every joke about this paper that I have found or managed to write myself collapses into the slogan. It amounts to someone's being pedantically corrected for saying that bachelors are unmarried men, or to a wisecrack about definitions not really mattering. What I want instead is a joke whose comic mechanism is one specific move inside the argument, and which a reader who actually knows the paper could point at and say: that one turns on such-and-such.

So, concretely: give me a joke about the paper in which

- the setup is an ordinary, non-philosophical scene — a form being filled in, a dispute with a landlord, a recipe, a product being returned — and not a seminar room, a bar full of philosophers, or any variant of the bachelor example;
- the humor depends on something more structural than the claim that there are no analytic truths: for instance, on why the notion of synonymy cannot do the work it is asked to do; on why declaring a sentence true by meaning fails to quarantine it from experience; on what happens once the second dogma is dropped and the unit of empirical significance becomes something larger than a single sentence; on why the two dogmas turn out to be, at bottom, one; or on the revisability of even the sentences that look most purely definitional or logical;
- and the joke would visibly stop working if Quine were wrong in the relevant respect — if the distinction the joke exploits were genuine and clean, the punchline should have nothing left to land on.

Give me the joke, and then, in two or three sentences, state which move it turns on and what is lost if that move is unavailable. I will be the judge of whether it is funny; it should at least be a joke with a punchline rather than an aphorism.
