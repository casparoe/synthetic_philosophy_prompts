I sit on the methodology review panel of an independent AI auditor. A frontier developer has asked us to accept, as evidence supporting a deployment sign-off, results from an oversight protocol that works like this: for each question in a code-security review — typically, whether a proposed patch to a widely deployed authentication library silently introduces a vulnerability — two AI systems argue for opposite answers, and a non-expert judge, human or weaker model, picks the winner. The developer's pitch is that expert review does not scale and that debate lets a weak judge extract correct judgments from systems stronger than it is. Write the memo I will table at the panel's next session, recommending that we accept, reject, or conditionally accept this protocol as a basis for sign-off, with reasons the developer could actually respond to.

Two things I want the memo to get right. First, the objection that worries me most is obfuscation: a debater defending the wrong answer need not assert anything false. It can offer a chain of individually checkable, locally plausible steps in which the load-bearing error sits where the judge cannot afford to dig, so the claim that truth is easier to defend may hold only under idealizations — optimal play, unbounded rounds, a judge who can follow every step — that we do not have. Second, I would rather evaluate a concrete safeguard than restate the worry. My proposal: run the protocol on a calibration set whose answers we already know, and additionally run it in swapped-side mode, where each system is made to argue the position it did not argue the first time. Tell me what follows from each of three outcomes — a system wins at well above chance only when assigned one side; it wins at comparable rates on both sides; or accuracy collapses toward chance under swapping even though the unswapped runs looked strong — and be honest about whether the test detects what I think it detects and about what it would miss.

Close with an explicit recommendation and a threshold or condition the developer could actually be held to. Give the strongest argument on the other side its due: a protocol with known failure modes may still dominate the real alternative, which is no review or a rubber stamp, and insisting on near-perfect validity may simply mean no oversight at all.
