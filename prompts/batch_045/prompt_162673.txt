hey — I'm in a seminar this term on memory and public space (philosophy dept, cross-listed with history) and for the final paper we have to come up with our own principle about what to do with commemorations and then defend it against cases. Before I write it I wanna find out whether my gut verdicts even fit together. So here are my verdicts, mine only, a couple I'm shaky on:

1. Equestrian statue of a Confederate general on the courthouse lawn in a mid-size southern city, put up in 1911 by a local "heritage" organization during Jim Crow, in a city that's now majority Black. Take it down. And a plaque is not enough — every plaque compromise I've seen feels like a dodge.

2. Bust of some colonel in a park. The inscription is unreadable, nobody has any idea who he was, everyone just thinks of it as "the soldier statue." Someone digs up that he was a slave trader. My gut: literally nothing needs to happen. It isn't honoring anybody, nobody receives it as honoring, it's park furniture at this point.

3. A scientist who basically founded his field and also drafted the state's forced sterilization law, under which a few thousand people were sterilized. There's a statue of him and a building named after him. Statue: keep it, with a genuinely brutal plaque. Building: rename. Yes I know those two look inconsistent, that's kind of the point.

4. Elementary school named after a local guy who ran an Underground Railroad stop and who also campaigned hard to keep Irish Catholic immigrants out of the country. Keep the name. He's being honored for the one thing, the other thing is separate from it, and the thing we honor him for actually cost him something.

5. Street named after a merchant whose fortune came from goods produced by enslaved people, and who also paid for the city's first free public library, which is still named after him. Rename the street, keep the library's name.

6. Statue of a colonial governor, pulled down by protestors a few years ago, now reinstalled inside the history museum with wall text about the famine he presided over. Fine — actually better than leaving it in a warehouse.

7. Statue of a 1930s labor organizer who defended the Moscow show trials in print and never walked it back. Remove. Shaky on this one.

8. Tomb of a medieval bishop inside a cathedral; the guy led a pogrom. Leave it alone — it's a tomb, it's been there six hundred years, it's basically architecture now. But the memorial plaque to him that was put up in the same cathedral in 1931: take that down.

So 1 and 2 are the pair I can't get under one principle. If the problem with the general is the message the object sends now, then 2 is fine and 1 is fine — but then 1 has nothing to do with the general, and it bugs me that on that story we're taking his statue down for reasons that don't involve him. And if the problem is that he doesn't deserve to be honored, then 2 should come down too, and I don't believe that.

The rule I keep circling is something like: a commemoration is wrong to keep when (a) it still successfully communicates honor to some real audience, and (b) what the person is honored for is connected to the bad thing they did. Plus our TA keeps banging on about scarcity — that there are only so many plinths and school names in a city, so leaving one up is also refusing that space to somebody else — and insists that belongs inside the principle rather than as a separate thing to weigh. I can't tell if that's a third clause or if it eats one of the other two.

Can you hand me a principle, or two or three competing candidates, stated tightly enough that I could actually use it, with a note on which case motivated each clause? And be straight with me about which of my verdicts it can't save. I also want it to rule on cases I haven't decided, not just on my list:

- a city's "Founders Day" holiday named for a guy who made his money in the slave trade, where nobody thinks about the name anymore and it's mostly just a day off and a parade;
- a scholarship fund named after a donor who was never convicted but was credibly accused of abusing kids — the money's already been spent, and renaming means the family pulls the money and there's no scholarship at all;
- a high school that was renamed away from a slave trader and toward a civil rights leader who, it later comes out, was abusive to his wife;
- and the one my seminar group fought about: the same general's statue, but the only people who now care about it are the neo-Nazis who hold rallies around it. Our reading made a big deal of the difference between the original dedication — a speech act done once, in 1911 — and what the object is doing now, including the possibility that a monument gets re-dedicated by the crowds who rally around it even though nobody in charge ever said anything. Does that rallying make the case for removal stronger, or does it make it a different case entirely?
