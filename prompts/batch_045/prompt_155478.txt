I'm a philosophy PhD student working on the epistemology of mathematics, and this term I'm running the discussion sections for Computation and Mind, a mixed-enrollment course — about fifteen students, mostly second-year computer science majors with a scattering of philosophy majors, all of whom have had discrete math and a formal-languages course where Turing machines were simply defined and then used as a tool. Nobody has ever asked them why we are entitled to identify that definition with the informal notion of a procedure a person could carry out by rote, so right now I expect they'll think I'm asking them to doubt something they've successfully treated as a definition all year. I have one seventy-five-minute section, a blackboard, and no handout. The topic is the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis. I don't need the thesis explained to me; I need a plan for getting them to see that there is a real question here at all.

Here is roughly where I've gotten to on my own. I want to separate three things they currently run together: the mathematical thesis (that the independently motivated formalisms — Turing machines, general recursive functions, the lambda calculus, register machines — all pick out the same class of functions), the physical thesis (that nothing implementable in the actual world outdoes that class), and the psychological thesis (that human mathematical capacity doesn't either). I also want to separate two very different-looking reasons for accepting the mathematical thesis — the sheer convergence of the formalisms, and the fact that decades of attempts to describe a stronger but still mechanical procedure have quietly collapsed back into the same class — and then ask why neither looks like evidence that would move someone who wanted a physical hypercomputer. I've been calling the first the licensing role of the thesis: it's what entitles anyone to move from computable-by-my-favorite-formalism to computable, full stop. I've been calling the second the definitional-retreat problem: every candidate counterexample gets reclassified as not really a procedure rather than counted as a refutation, which makes me suspect the whole thing has quietly become unfalsifiable. My supervisor suggested framing the thesis as a calibration principle — not a prediction you test but a stipulation that lets an entire apparatus of results about computability speak at all — but I don't know whether that framing survives contact with students who have never seen a physics-flavored counterexample.

So: give me the teaching plan, not the explanation. What has to be on the board, and in what order, before the question of the thesis's status can even look like a question rather than a quibble about words — I assume the informal/formal asymmetry has to come first, but tell me if something else has to precede even that. What single concrete case should carry the session: I'm inclined toward a specifiable but non-Turing-computable function (the halting problem, or the Busy Beaver function) paired with one candidate physical device that would decide it — either an accelerating machine performing a supertask or a machine exploiting relativistic time dilation — so tell me which of those two devices to actually draw, what the carrying example must have (concretely describable, mechanically specifiable, not smuggling in an oracle) and what it must lack, and why the example you pick is better equipped to carry the epistemic point than the obvious alternatives. Then the misconception map: which misunderstandings to expect in a mixed computer-science-and-philosophy room, and where in the sequence each one should be headed off rather than merely corrected after the fact. My guesses are that they'll say that Turing proved it in his paper on computable numbers, that quantum computers are about to break it, that calling it a thesis just means nobody has gotten around to proving it, and — among the sharper students — that the physical version is simply very well-confirmed empirical science, which is the one I'm least sure how to answer. Include a question I can ask at the end that would tell me whether they've grasped the structure of the dispute rather than learned to repeat the slogan that it's a thesis and not a theorem, and tell me plainly what I should refuse to settle in seventy-five minutes and say out loud that I'm refusing. If the calibration-principle framing will mislead this particular audience, say so and give me what to put in its place.

Format: exactly eight numbered items, each two or three sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. Make item 8 your confidence note: how confident you are in the plan overall, and which one of the preceding items you are least sure about, and why.
