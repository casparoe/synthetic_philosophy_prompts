I design and maintain knowledge-graph schemas for a living. My team runs a middle layer — internally we just call it the registry — that merges legacy databases from separate departments behind one typed interface, and it has a small number of primitives that were forced on us by that history:

- **Declared kinds**: named classes that a data owner asserts a record into. Membership is stipulation rather than computation, and a declared kind can perfectly well be empty.
- **Derived kinds**: classes defined by a predicate over records, computed at query time, with no owner and no act of assertion behind them.
- **Aspects**: named slots on a record. An aspect can repeat, so a single record may carry COLOR at index 1 and index 2 with different fillers, for two patches of one painted object; and an aspect's filler may itself be a record, so a single aspect occurrence can be reified as a node with an identifier of its own.
- **Co-tickets**: a separate table added during one merger, whose rows assert that two aspect occurrences in different records are the same occurrence. We invented it because two departments had independently recorded the same patch of paint and we needed to stop their records from double-counting it.
- **Identity-bearing aspects**: a flag on the schema marking aspects a record cannot lose and still be merged as the same record across sources. We use it for record linkage and for nothing else.

Beyond that, my vocabulary is a data modeler's and a working programmer's: type versus token, intension versus extension, nominal versus structural typing, subtyping and inheritance, interfaces and implementations, validation against a schema, duck typing, nullable and repeated fields, reified relations, and the difference between *is an instance of* and *is a subtype of*. I have some second-hand acquaintance with type theory — universes of types, higher kinds, dependent types — but I would not call myself fluent in it. I have no metaphysics, and I do not want a glossary of one.

What I have been reading around the edges of lately is the problem of universals — the old question of what makes a red ball and a red sunset both red, and whether the answer is that there is a single item, redness, which both of them somehow have. As I currently understand the field, the live options are: transcendent realism, on which the universal exists whether or not anything instantiates it; immanent realism, on which the universal exists only in its instances, is wholly present in each of them, and so is multiply located while remaining one thing; class nominalism, on which being red is membership in the class of red things; resemblance nominalism, on which the red things simply resemble one another and that resemblance is all the unity there is, together with the familiar worry that resemblance classes do not stay closed under resemblance; and trope theory, on which each red thing has its own individual redness, a particular rather than a universal, with some versions going on to build the object itself out of a bundle of such particulars. There is also a distinction I keep tripping over between sparse and abundant properties — the world's own joints versus our gerrymanders — which I gather cuts across the others rather than sitting alongside them.

Here is what I want. Restate each of those positions as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I have just given you — declared and derived kinds, aspects and their occurrences, co-tickets, identity-bearing flags, type/token, intension/extension, nominal and structural typing, subtyping, validation — and then give me the residue report. For each position: what translates cleanly, what translates only if you quietly distort it, and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all. Where a translation only works because you have imported an assumption my system does not have — treating a kind as something located in the records it applies to, or as something that makes those records resemble one another, or as something whose existence is not up to us — mark that as smuggling rather than passing it off as translation.

Then run all five translations against four cases from my actual work, and tell me where they disagree about what the registry is doing and where, to my surprise, they agree:

1. A declared kind with zero instances — RADIATION-SPILL-INCIDENT, defined and owned but never asserted into — next to a derived kind whose predicate no record currently satisfies.
2. Two derived kinds defined by different predicates that happen, as things stand, to return exactly the same records: every record in one department's data carrying a heart also carries a kidney.
3. One record carrying COLOR twice with different fillers, and two records in different departments whose aspect occurrences are joined by a co-ticket.
4. A kind-of-kind registry: kinds that are themselves records, so that a kind can appear as an instance of another kind.

Finally, the verdict I actually care about. My suspicion is that the registry is metaphysically neutral in a slightly cheap way: everything in it is either an assertion by someone or the output of a predicate someone wrote, so the question of what *in the world* makes any of it correct never arises inside the system at all. If that is right, then the interesting question is what my vocabulary is missing that would let the question arise — and I have a rough guess that it is modality, location and part-whole structure, causation, and explanation, none of which the registry has any primitive for. Tell me whether that guess is right, and then tell me the harder thing: which parts of the metaphysical dispute do not survive the translation because my vocabulary is deficient, and which do not survive because the original claim was less clear than it looks — because a spatial metaphor like *wholly present in each instance*, or an explanatory one like *grounds the resemblance*, was doing more work than anyone noticed.

I am not asking for references or further reading; I cannot chase them. I want the translation, worked through, and I would rather have eight hundred dense words than two thousand thin ones.
