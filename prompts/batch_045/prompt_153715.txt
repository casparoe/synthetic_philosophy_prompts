so a guy in my philosophy of history reading group walked me through this and I cant find the bad step.

(1) Singular causal claims are contrastive: "C caused E" says that C, rather than the salient alternative to C, made the difference to E.
(2) Making a difference just is counterfactual dependence: hold the rest of the background fixed, and if C hadn't obtained, E wouldn't have.
(3) The big outcomes historians care about are overdetermined. Grant, for the sake of argument, that the usual story about 1914 is right: drop the Sarajevo assassination and you still get a great-power war; drop the alliance system and still; drop the German mobilisation plans and still. Remove any one factor and something war-like arrives anyway.
(4) So no single factor passes (2). So by (1) no single factor caused the war.
(5) Only the whole background, or the disjunction "at least one of these factors obtained", passes (2) — and no history book says that.
(6) And he adds: citing a factor that made no difference couldn't explain anything anyway, because the whole content of "C caused E" is the information that without C, no E. If that information is false, the citation is empty.
(7) So either every singular causal claim historians make is false, or their explanations are empty gestures, or (1) and (2) have to go.

I'd rather keep (1) than give it up — its the only thing we've read that explains why "what caused it?" isn't the same question as "what happened?". But (4) looks like it follows straight from (2) and (3), and (3) is the historians' own claim, not his. I also suspect (6) is doing more work than he admits, though I can't say why.

Which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you — especially if you still want to say both that the assassination was a cause and that the war was overdetermined? And tell me what would change your mind about the diagnosis: which step would you switch to, and under what argument.
