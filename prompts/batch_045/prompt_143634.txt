I got into this a few months ago after hearing it mentioned on a podcast, and then arguing about it for weeks in a group chat with my brother-in-law (he's an actuary, and he keeps saying "its just a wash" in a way that I think is too quick), and I've ended up with a set of things I believe about the two-envelope thing that I want checked before I write them up as a blog post. I've got no formal background in any of this, I'm a night-shift dispatcher and I do my reading on my phone between calls, so please don't assume I know the standard moves. Here's where I've landed:

(1) Closed envelopes, before you look: switching and staying are exactly equally good. Not "we can't tell which is better" but genuinely a wash — the policy "always switch" gives you exactly the same spread of outcomes as "always stay", so there's nothing to be gained and nothing to be lost. This is the one I'm most confident about. (2) Open envelope, but with a setup I actually know: suppose I'm told the smaller amount was picked uniformly from $5, $10, ... $50. Then I switch on the small amounts and stay on the big ones — seeing $5 I switch for sure, seeing $100 I definitely stay. And I don't think that fights with (1): the money I make in the cases where switching is right is exactly cancelled by the money I lose in the cases where it's wrong. My brother-in-law thinks (1) and (2) contradict each other; I think he's wrong, and I want to know precisely which of us is confused. (3) My diagnosis of why the paradox feels compelling: the step "whatever I see, the other envelope is 50/50 to be double or half" isn't a free assumption, it's secretly a claim about how money is spread out — roughly, that every order of magnitude is equally likely — and that can't be a real probability distribution, it doesn't sit still. (4) But then here's my actual practical verdict: real money does seem to be spread over lots of orders of magnitude and I don't think the right answer should hinge on a precise prior I couldn't possibly have, so in practice — whatever amount you see, switch. Everyone tells me this is the wrong bit, but (3) feels like it should support (4) rather than sink it, since (3) says the whole question is what your prior looks like and mine is diffuse. (5) Method: I want to go by plain expected money. No diminishing marginal utility, no risk aversion — at these amounts that feels like a side issue, and bringing it in seems like changing the subject. (6) My switching rule: I switch exactly when I think it's more likely than not that I've got the smaller envelope; if I think I probably already have the bigger one, I stay. (7) A variant I made up because I couldn't sleep: instead of showing me the amount, the host just tells me "your envelope has between $20 and $80 in it". My verdict is that this is symmetric, it gives me no reason to switch, a range doesn't single out either envelope. I'm least sure about this one.

Two different people have now told me this set can't all be true and neither of them would say which part, which is driving me up the wall. So: audit it. Which pairs genuinely conflict, ranked by how severe the conflict is, and for each one what exactly would have to give and what that would cost me — I'd rather know which claim is the expensive one than drop something cheap. Also tell me which apparent conflicts aren't conflicts at all and why, especially whether (1) and (2) really do fight, whether (4) is saved or sunk by (3), and whether anything in my list commits me to a verdict on (7) that I haven't given. Please don't just tell me the paradox has been solved somewhere in the literature or that I should go read up on it; I want the tensions traced between these seven claims specifically, with the arithmetic shown where it matters.
