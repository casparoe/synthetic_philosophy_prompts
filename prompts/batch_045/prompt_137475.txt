My friend Dana has a little newsletter where she grades old predictions — a recurring bit she calls the vindication board or whatever — and I've been reading her verdicts for years. I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background, so I dont have the vocabulary for any of this, but I've got a standing argument with a coworker and I want to know what Dana's actual rule is, not just what she'd say.

Her verdicts, as best I can reconstruct them:

- Malthus, late 1700s, population grows geometrically and food only arithmetically, so famine and misery: she says wrong, not early. Her reasoning as I remember it: food per person went UP for two centuries after him, and the only reason people still call him right is that they keep sliding the deadline.
- The 1968 population-bomb stuff, mass famines predicted for the 70s and 80s: wrong. She also brings up the bet its author made with an economist around 1980 about whether a basket of metals would get more expensive over ten years, which he lost.
- The Limits to Growth book, early 70s, business as usual leads to overshoot and collapse sometime this century: she says genuinely undecided, ask her in 2050, and she won't be moved on that.
- Hubbert the geologist calling a peak in US oil production around 1970: right, and she's generous about it — it peaked about when he said. But she adds that only the narrow numerical call gets credit; the follow-on stuff about society having to reorganize around scarcity gets nothing.
- Y2K: she says the alarmists were right, and her argument is that nothing happened because people spent a stupid amount of money fixing it, so "nothing happened" isn't evidence against them. She's stubborn about this one.
- Any prediction of the form "X is thirty years away" that gets remade every decade — fusion, oil running out, AGI: zero credit. Stopped clock, not falsifiable, end of discussion.

Here's the case I actually care about, because it's the one I know something about. In the early 90s the networking people looked at a 32-bit address space and exponential internet growth and said, in effect: we're going to run out of IP addresses around 2010, and its going to be bad, the internet can't keep growing past that. What happened: the central pool did run dry around then give or take a year, the regional registries trickled out over the next several years, NAT absorbed most of the pain, IPv6 has been about to take over for roughly fifteen years now, and addresses now trade on an actual market for actual money. And — the thing my coworker and I disagree about — nothing visibly broke. Traffic kept growing. Nobody I work with noticed.

My coworker says: obviously vindicated, it's the cleanest Malthusian setup there is, fixed stock and exponential demand, and they basically nailed the year. I say: the arithmetic was right and the real claim — that growth would hit a wall — was wrong, and what we got instead was a price and a bunch of plumbing underneath it.

So: what would Dana say? More usefully, what are the two or three rules that fit her verdicts above equally well, where do those rules come apart on this specific case, and which one is she most likely running? I care much more about that than about the yes/no. And if her track record genuinely doesn't settle it, what's the one further case you'd want her to rule on to find out?
