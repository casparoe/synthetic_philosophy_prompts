I've been reading Aristotle's Politics and Nicomachean Ethics on my own, in translation, with no philosophy training beyond one school class years ago. Two claims of his about money have lodged in my head: that acquiring goods for a household has a natural limit, so that wanting more money without limit is a corruption of a skill rather than a skill; and that the most unnatural way of getting wealth is lending at interest, since a coin exists to be handed over in exchange and not to produce more coins — the Greek word for interest, as I understand it, literally means offspring. I can't settle whether that last point is a real argument or a pun wearing a real argument's clothes, and I'd like to hear it argued out properly.

Write a dialogue between two friends who have each thought about this independently and have ended up on opposite sides. One defends Aristotle: a genuine medium of exchange is a convention for measuring and settling trades, so anything that grows merely by being owned has quietly stopped being money and become a disguised share in someone else's labour or property. The other argues that this only looked true in a world of stamped metal, and presses two test cases — a loan of seed grain, which really does multiply, so why is interest on it supposed to be unnatural; and a hypothetical currency whose units are claims on a self-reproducing herd, one head today redeemable for more heads next year simply because the herd grows.

Both speakers should be well read on the point at issue and equally able, and neither should win by caricature: each has to answer the other's strongest formulation rather than restate their own. Let them push through to what exactly Aristotle would have to establish — something about the function of money, or about who performs the work that makes the increase possible — and let the conversation end with both of them clearer about where the genuine disagreement lies, even if neither gives ground. Around 900 words.
