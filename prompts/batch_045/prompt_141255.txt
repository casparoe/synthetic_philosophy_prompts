I work as an analyst to the Nosology and Entitlements Committee of a national health service. The Committee keeps the Schedule of Diseases, and the stakes of listing are concrete: a listed condition brings mandatory coverage of diagnosis and treatment with no cost-sharing, while a condition the Committee declines goes into a separate prevention budget, where screening is discretionary and patients pay a co-payment. The governing statute defines a disease as a departure from the normal functional organization of the human organism that, in the environment its bearers actually inhabit, is harmful or materially raises the risk of harm. Nearly every dispute we have is a dispute about how to read that sentence, and the Committee has never said how it reads it.

I am writing the recommendation memo on a new petition. I do not need a literature review and I do not want citations; I can find those myself. What I need is the rule-space: the rules consistent with our own precedents, what each predicts here, and which further case would tell them apart. I am less interested in which rule is philosophically best in the abstract than in which one our track record commits us to, and in what we would be committing ourselves to by each available decision.

Our precedents, all reasonably recent and all with reasons given:

1. Asymptomatic essential hypertension — blood pressure persistently above a fixed threshold, no symptoms at all, roughly double the ten-year cardiovascular risk, cheap and effective treatment. Listed. Harm, the Committee said, may consist entirely in elevated risk; present suffering is not required. Note that this threshold is absolute, applied identically at every age.
2. Age-related bone loss and presbyopia in people over sixty-five — near universal, measurable loss of function, treatable, sometimes painful in their consequences. Not listed: changes incident to the normal course of ageing. The Committee compared each patient to an age-matched reference distribution and said explicitly that it would not adopt a reading on which most of the elderly population is diseased.
3. Dental caries in adults — present in something like nine adults in ten, caused largely by a dietary novelty in evolutionary terms, productive of pain and tooth loss. Listed. The statistical objection was answered by saying that the reference population is itself environmentally abnormal, so that high prevalence within it is not evidence of normality.
4. Sickle cell disease, homozygous — listed. Sickle cell trait, heterozygous — confers malaria resistance, asymptomatic except under severe hypoxia. Not listed: a statistically unusual variant is not by itself a departure in function.
5. Persistent multi-symptom exertion syndrome — severe fatigue and pain, real disability, no identified mechanism, no biomarker, diagnosis by exclusion, modest response to graded therapy. Listed provisionally, over a dissent holding that with no demonstrated dysfunction this is at most an illness and not a disease. The majority reasoned that a condition causing this much suffering and responding to clinical attention warrants the standing of a disease.
6. Biomarker-positive, cognitively normal preclinical Alzheimer's — not listed; classified as a risk state, on the grounds that the biomarker is neither necessary nor sufficient for later dementia and that no intervention has been shown to alter the course.
7. Profound congenital sensorineural deafness — listed as a disorder of the auditory mechanism, with a notation, added at the insistence of a signing community, that listing pathologizes a mechanism and implies nothing about the person, their language, or their community.

The petition now before us asks that we list constitutive cortisol hyper-response: a cortisol awakening response above a fixed threshold, with a polygenic score as a confirmatory test. It is asymptomatic. Bearers score slightly higher on alertness measures, and a subgroup — endurance athletes, shift workers, trial lawyers — report that it advantages them in their work. In a twenty-year prospective cohort it carries roughly 1.4 times the rate of type 2 diabetes, hypertension and cardiovascular events after adjustment for body mass, smoking and socioeconomic position. Nothing in the mechanism has failed: the axis responds, it down-regulates, its set point is simply high. Comparative and anthropological work suggests that the high-response phenotype was the ancestral human norm and was advantageous under high pathogen load and heavy physical labour, and that the low-response phenotype became common only after urbanization. A cheap generic drug lowers the response into the bottom quartile and, in an eight-year trial, cut the composite event rate by about a fifth, with mild side effects. Because the entire distribution shifts upward with age, the threshold is exceeded by roughly 9 per cent of twenty-five-year-olds, 20 per cent of forty-year-olds, 45 per cent of fifty-five-year-olds and 82 per cent of seventy-year-olds. The cohort study was funded by the drug's manufacturer; the trial was not. An association of self-described hyper-responders opposes listing, invoking the notation in precedent 7 and arguing that they are being medicalised for being well adapted to demanding lives. The petition asks for listing at all adult ages; classifying the condition instead as a risk factor would mean the prevention budget, cost-sharing and no mandated screening.

Concretely, I would like:

1. A menu of the rules that fit all seven precedents — at least a biostatistical rule keyed to statistical deviance of function in an age-matched reference class; that rule with an abnormal-environment clause of the kind the caries decision relies on; a harmful-dysfunction rule requiring an actual failure of a mechanism rather than a setting; a harm-and-treatability rule of the sort the exertion-syndrome majority appears to use; a risk-based rule with a predictive-validity requirement of the sort that blocked the biomarker case; and any pluralist or procedural rule that accommodates the set better than any single one of these. For each, say which precedents it has to work to accommodate and where the strain shows.
2. What each rule predicts about this petition, and in particular whether it predicts different verdicts at twenty-five and at seventy. Our precedents already use an absolute threshold in one place and an age-relative one in another, and I want to know whether that inconsistency is doing real work here or whether we can leave it undisturbed.
3. Which precedent is the closest analog, and your best guess at the recommendation, with the reasons for preferring it to the alternatives.
4. Because I expect the precedents to underdetermine this, an account of what the Committee would be adopting under each available option — listing at all adult ages, listing only below some age, declining and classing it as a risk factor, or listing provisionally as in precedent 5 — in terms of how normal functional organization, the environment its bearers actually inhabit, and harm or risk of harm would then be read.
5. The further case that would best separate the rules that survive, chosen from among the cases we could actually get: we can only take petitions for conditions with an agreed biomarker or accepted diagnostic criteria, though we can commission a review on our own motion. Say why that case would not already be settled by what we have decided.

The Committee reads memos rather than papers, so please keep it in prose and lay the candidate rules out as a list I can put in front of them.
