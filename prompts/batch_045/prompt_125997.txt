Write me an essay of roughly 1,000 words that settles a question I have been going in circles on for a while: whether the pattern of choices in Ellsberg's urn case can be rationally defended once you stipulate that no adversary is in the picture. Assume your reader already knows the standard setup and the argument that the pattern is inconsistent with subjective expected utility via the sure-thing principle; do not spend words rehearsing them, and do not lean on who said what or on citations. Argue from the cases.

The thing I want the essay to sink its teeth into is a pair of urns I have been using to isolate what ambiguity aversion is actually a reaction to. Both urns hold 90 balls: 30 are red, and the remaining 60 are black or yellow in some proportion. In the Wheel urn, the proportion was fixed by a fair wheel with 61 equally spaced stops, spun in your presence, with the outcome hidden under a cloth that no one has lifted: not you, not the experimenter, not anyone. In the Committee urn, the proportion was fixed by a committee that met in private; you are told, and have no reason to doubt, that the committee had no stake in your bets, did not know which bets you would be offered, and had no information about black and yellow that you lack. In both urns your evidence is symmetric between black and yellow and across the possible compositions, so in both your credence that a drawn non-red ball is black should be 1/2. The only difference is that in the Wheel urn your uncertainty runs through a chance setup you fully understand, and in the Committee urn it does not.

So the essay has to tell me whether that difference is rationally load-bearing, and it should work through three options rather than picking one immediately.

First: if it is load-bearing, what exactly does the extra weight attach to? The chances themselves, as opposed to your credences? Once credences are matched, is there any work left for the distinction between risk and uncertainty to do, or does aversion here reduce to a preference for beliefs with a certain pedigree?

Second: if it is not load-bearing, then in the Wheel urn you are declining a bet with positive expected value measured against chances you yourself accept, and the Committee urn is no better off. That pushes toward treating the whole pattern as a psychological effect with no normative standing. I have been told that the aversion largely evaporates when the ambiguous bet is evaluated on its own rather than alongside an unambiguous one, and that it tracks how competent you feel about the subject matter of the bet. If that is right, say what it shows about the normative status of the pattern; if it would not settle the question even if true, say why.

Third: the option I cannot shake, which is that the pattern expresses no ranking at all but an incompleteness. On this reading the agent has no determinate preference between betting on black and betting on yellow, forced choice reveals nothing about what they ought to choose, and the charge of irrationality is misdirected. If this is the best treatment, defend it and say what it does to the standard arguments on both sides.

The essay also has to deal with the two serious defenses, and with whether they reach these urns. One is the strategic defense: aversion to unknown probabilities is sensible because in real life unknown probabilities are usually produced by agents with opposed interests, and if the urn might have been loaded against you then caution is not paranoia. I grant the adversary; both of my urns are stipulated to have none. Does a disposition that pays against adversaries carry any normative weight where there are none, or does it only explain where the disposition came from? The other is the robustness defense: decision rules ought to be sensitive to how well supported a probability estimate is, since a single prior erases the difference between knowing the chances and having no idea, and since your model of the situation may itself be misspecified. Test that defense against the Wheel urn, where there is nothing unspecified left to fear, and against the regress worry that hedging within a set of priors you have yourself specified cannot be a hedge against your model being wrong.

Give me a verdict, tell me what the agent should choose in each of the two urns on your view, and justify it against the strongest version of the alternatives rather than the weakest.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning: no preamble and no restating of the question.
