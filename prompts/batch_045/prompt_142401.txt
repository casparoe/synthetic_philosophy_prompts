I am editing a volume of adversarial collaborations in value theory and practical reason, and the contribution I still need is on hard choices. Please draft the joint statement that the two camps described below could both sign after a good-faith collaboration. No bibliography, no citations, no titles of papers, no appeal to what anyone has published: define every position in your own terms, and let the arguments carry the weight.

Set up the phenomenon first, and keep the setup as part of the document. By a hard choice I mean a case in which the agent has full information about her options, has reasoned as well as she can, is not impaired by inattention, weakness of will, or confusion, and yet the deliberative difficulty survives everything that further information or further reasoning could do: neither option seems better, nor worse, nor equally good. Both camps accept that hard choices in this sense are real, that they are not merely choices we find difficult, and that a hard choice is rationally resolvable in a way that tossing a coin is not.

Camp one is the Parity Camp. Its members hold that the options in a hard choice stand in a fourth positive axiological relation, on a par, which is neither betterness, worseness, nor equality: the options are genuinely comparable, each is a live rational competitor of the other, and yet the trichotomy of better, worse, and equally good does not apply. Because that relation is not transitive and does not by itself rank the options, the resolution cannot be a matter of discovering which option is better; the agent must instead put her own authority behind one of the competing considerations, and in doing so she creates for herself a reason that did not exist before the resolution. Camp two is the Incomparabilist Camp. Its members hold that no positive axiological relation holds between the options of a hard choice: not the three traditional ones, and no fourth one either, so that saying the options are on a par is a misleading way of saying that a relation fails to hold. They agree, however, that the options are nonetheless genuine rational competitors, that the resolution is a full-blooded exercise of rational agency rather than a coin flip, and that it is not a matter of discovering a ranking. What they deny is that the exercise tracks or creates comparative value.

Deliver the document in four parts.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims, eight to twelve of them, each substantive rather than diplomatic: claims a committed member of each camp could endorse and a committed defender of what the two camps call the easy-choice view could not. They should include the shared account of what has to be true for a choice to be hard, the shared rejection of the claim that the three traditional relations exhaust the cases, the shared view that resolving well is neither arbitrary nor a discovery, and the shared view that some resolutions are criticisable, so that the will or the commitment is not unconstrained.

Second, a disagreements table. For each row give the issue; the Parity Camp's formulation in the wording that camp would accept; the Incomparabilist Camp's formulation in the wording that camp would accept; whether the disagreement is genuine or merely verbal; and, if it is genuine, the crux it reduces to. Where you diagnose a dispute as verbal, say precisely which term is doing the work and what substantive question is being fought under cover of it.

Third, a cruxes section, at least the following five, each with the case or argument both camps accept as bearing on it and each camp's prediction recorded in advance. A crux is a sub-question such that settling it would move at least one camp.

1. The value-span model. Stipulate, as neutral machinery both camps may use, that an agent's values at a time are represented not by a single ordering but by a set of admissible precisifications, each a complete ordinal ranking of the options, where the spread of the set encodes the imprecision in her values; call two options spanned when some precisifications rank one above the other and some the reverse. Add, as both camps insist, that no precisification is the correct one independently of the agent's resolution, so the imprecision is in the values and not in her knowledge of them. The crux: does the model, so read, deliver comparability that is not trichotomous, or does it merely redescribe incomparability in a new vocabulary?

2. Robustness under small improvement. Take a hard choice between two careers and add to one option a benefit so trivial that in any ordinary comparison it would be irrelevant: a slightly shorter commute, a marginally larger office. The crux is whether hardness is robust under such improvements, and, if it is not, where along a series of ever larger improvements a determinate ranking first appears and what explains its location.

3. The second-chance case. An agent resolves a hard choice, lives the chosen life for a decade, and is then offered the other option again at no cost and with nothing else changed. The crux is not whether she would choose again, since both camps predict she might, but whether a comparative judgment of the form this is better for me than that has become apt where it was not apt before. Add the variant in which she resolves inwardly days before the choice must be exercised and the world changes in between so that the options become determinately ranked, and say what becomes of the earlier resolution.

4. The staircase. There is a series of options such that each adjacent pair presents a hard choice while the first is determinately better than the last, and the agent can move one step at a time. The crux is whether every step can be permissible while the whole descent is not, whether rationality therefore requires resolving at the level of a policy rather than a step, and where such a requirement would come from.

5. Menu sensitivity. Two hard options are joined on the menu by a third that is determinately worse than both but resembles one of them along the dimension on which that one is stronger. The crux is whether what it is permissible or rationally required to do with respect to the original pair changes with the menu, and, since the agent can often add such options to her own menu, whether menu sensitivity admits a reductio or is a feature.

Fourth, a short section on defeat conditions: for each camp, the argument, case, or finding it would count as evidence against its own view, stated specifically enough that the other camp accepts it as a genuine defeat condition and not as a rhetorical concession.

Write the whole thing as a polished document of roughly 1400 to 1800 words, in a register both camps could put their names to: no hedging, no it depends, no verdict on who is right. If you conclude that the two positions coincide extensionally for all first-order normative purposes, say so in the agreed claims and state what the residual dispute is then about, whether the metaphysics of value, the theory of reasons, or the explanation of the phenomenology of choosing. If you think one of my five cruxes is unproductive, replace it with a better one and say which it replaces and why.
