I'm in a tiny reading group on confirmation and induction, and our seminar leader has been working through the raven paradox by ruling on cases one at a time. She always rates an observation on the same scale against the hypothesis *all ravens are black* (call it H): strong, weak, negligible but real, or nothing at all. She is very firm that negligible but real is not the same as nothing at all, and she keeps saying that if you collapse those two you have thrown the paradox away rather than solved it. Her verdicts so far:

1. You close your eyes and point at a random object in the museum collection; it turns out to be a raven, and it is black. Weak — a single instance never does more than nudge a universal generalisation.
2. Same random pointing, but the object you get is somebody's white trainer. Negligible but real.
3. Random pointing again, you get a black umbrella. Nothing at all.
4. A raven that turns out to be white. H is false, game over, no scale required.
5. A sealed cabinet downstairs holding exactly 12 objects; the curator's catalogue, which we are told to trust, says exactly 3 of the 12 are non black, though not which ones, and says nothing about how many are ravens. You draw one object at random and it is a blue ceramic ashtray. She said: weak, but at the very top of weak and definitely not negligible — you could see this one in the odds if you were betting. She stressed that the cabinet is small and closed and that the catalogue fixes how many non black objects there are.
6. A student texts her a photo of a black raven instead of her going to see one. Same as seeing it; how you found out doesn't matter, only what you are now in a position to know.
7. You draw an object from the shelf where only the black ones are kept, and it turns out to be a raven. She said: confirms, by the same amount as an ordinary black raven, and left us to work out why that is not the same as the shoe sort of case.

Thursday's case, which she has said will be on the exam: I go into a shoe shop, so I know before I look at the colour that whatever I pick up is not a raven, and the trainer I pick up is white. Everything else is as in case 2 — open-ended world, no catalogue, nothing fixing how many non black objects there are.

I have to hand in two pages predicting her verdict. So: what will she say, and on which point of her scale? What is the rule she is running, what other rules fit cases 1 through 7 just as well, and what does each of those say about Thursday's? Which of the earlier verdicts is the closest analog, and is that the one she will actually reason from? And what further case should I put to her that would separate the surviving rules?

My own attempt was some version of the old Nicod-style thought: an observation counts only if it was still open whether the thing was a raven at the moment you took in its colour. That fits 1 to 7, but it also makes Thursday's case a flat no, and I can't tell if that is the right answer or just the answer I was hoping for. Someone else in the group muttered that the whole thing turns on whether the rival hypothesis is supposed to keep the total number of non black things fixed or just add non black ravens on top of them; I didn't follow that at all, and if that is what Thursday's case is secretly about I would like to know. I was also going to offer the cabinet version as my discriminator — same 12 objects, same catalogue entry saying exactly 3 are non black, but I take my object from the shelf marked footwear, knowing it is a shoe, and it turns out blue — on the grounds that the fixed total forces the issue. Does that actually separate the rules, or does it collapse the distinction I am trying to test?
