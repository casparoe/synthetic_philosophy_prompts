i'm helping draft the first chapter of a handbook for people who actually have to *judge* selection schemes — a university admissions panel, a compliance body looking at a company's board, a tribunal ruling on a reservation category — and i keep going round in cirlces because everyone i talk to has a different idea of what the question even is. so before i let anyone argue about whether a given scheme is OK, i want the spec: what would a theory of group-conscious selection have to be able to *do* to be good enough to hand to such a body?

Not a theory of justice in general. A decision-grade account of when selecting people partly on the basis of their membership of some group is permissible, impermissible, or required — one that has to be appliable by an institution that doesn't know the whole social history, that can't run experiments, and that needs to be told not just "no" but what change would make the scheme acceptable. That's the purpose, and i care about it being usable in that setting, so procedural/administrability stuff is a real desideratum here, not a boring footnote.

The distinctions it must not collapse (with the case that shows why each matters):

- hard quota vs target vs tiebreaker vs "comply or explain" — because the same number can be reached by four mechanisms with totally different costs and exit options.
- the choice of benchmark: parity with the population, with the applicant pool, or with the pool qualified at an earlier stage. A principle i've made up and want you to test — call it **conditional-pipeline parity** (i think of it as the Ashworth rule, after a fictional 2019 white paper): a scheme is permissible iff each group's selection rate matches its share of the pool that qualified at *the last stage the institution didn't itself control*, discouting differences in qualification that were themselves produced by the institution's own past practices. Is that even coherent? Does it demand something any real institution could compute?
- membership-fixing: who counts, who decides, and what happens when the category is self-declared. **The opt-in surge**: a university lets applicants tick any ancestry box they identify with, enrolment of the target groups triples on paper and nothing else changes. Is the scheme better, worse, or the same?
- **The parish register case**: a selective school switches from a racial preference to a "local heritage" preference decided by baptism records going back to 1900. In this made-up town that tracks ancestry almost perfectly. Every fact about who gets in is unchanged. Does anything morally important change, and if so does the theory have the resources to say it without sneaking in intent?
- **The same-five-people case**: board quotas met by rotating a tiny set of the same individuals across dozens of companies, so the headcount moves and nothing else does.
- **The decorative case**: the quota is hit on the board while the executive committee, where the decisions are actually made, is untouched.
- **The hollow-benefit case**: the scheme is justified by its effects on the institution (better decisions, legitimacy, market trust), and it's genuinely good on those, while the intended beneficiaries are on average no better off — maybe slightly worse by some measures. Which way does the theory point, and should that be allowed to depend on whether the beneficiaries consent?
- **The symmetry case**: in this fictional country men are 20% of vet-med students. Is a scheme for them governed by the same rule, or does the direction matter — and if it does, what has to be true for that to be non-arbitrary?
- **The sunset case / the erosion case**: a scheme that works on its own numbers but appears to slow reform of the pipeline feeding it, so ten years on the underlying disparity is unchanged. Also: when, if ever, is a scheme *required* to have an expiry date?
- **The intersection case**: someone disadvantaged along two axes at once. Does the theory handle that by construction, or does it have to be bolted on?

So: give me the requirement list, each with the case or consideration that earns it a place. Mark which are non-negotiable for a decision-grade theory and which are just what one camp happens to like (i suspect "must be forward-looking only", "must be expressible without any mention of groups at all", and "must be symmetric across groups" are in that second bin, but argue). Flag any pair you think can't be jointly satisfied — especially the administrability requirement (an institution shouldn't have to adjudicate contested identities or people's inner lives) against taking intersectionality seriously — and if some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, say so, that's the bit i most want. Then tell me which single requirement knocks out the most candidate theories.

After that, and briefly — a table is fine, no more than a couple of lines each — score four contenders against your own list: colourblind individualism, anti-subordination, diversity-as-instrumental, and my conditional-pipeline-parity rule above. Please don't give me citations, case names from actual litigation, or a reading list; i want the criteria, and i'll go find the literature myself. Real-world regimes (reservations with a creamy-layer-style exclusion, boardroom quotas, race-conscious admissions in US universities) are fine as illustration but i don't need their details or dates.
