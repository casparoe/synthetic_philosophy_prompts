ok so I keep going back and forth on what species even are, and I figure the honest way to do this is to just write down my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and see whether anything unifies them. here they are, in no order:

- the ring case: populations strung around a barrier, every neighbouring pair interbreeds fine, but the two ends come back together at the far side and dont. I say: one species. I really dont want to draw a line somewhere in the middle of that chain.
- chihuahuas and great danes. one species, obviously, even though the mating would need mechanical help.
- two populations of drab little frogs that look basically identical to me, live in the same stream, but dont interbreed in the wild and differ at a bunch of genetic markers, though they make perfectly fertile tadpoles if you cross them in a bucket. I say: two species.
- an all-female lineage of lizards, no males, no mating, just cloning itself for tens of thousands of years. definitely a species.
- soil bacteria that swap genes sideways with everything, so that the gene trees for different genes tell totally different stories. species, sure, but the edges are negotiable and I'm fine with that.
- chronospecies: one unbroken lineage, sample it at the start and 300,000 years later, and those two endpoints would not interbreed. I want to say two species, even though nothing ever split.
- a mountain range splits a population in two for 40,000 years, then the climate shifts and the two sides come back together, interbreed completely, and are one population again. I want to say it was never two species, not even briefly.
- the same kind of organism assembled twice independently: hybridisation between different parent species produces, in two separate places, populations that are genetically distinct in origin but end up interbreeding with each other freely and doing the same ecological job. I say one species, and I have a feeling that answer is going to cost me.
- a cell built in a lab by chemically writing a genome from a computer file and booting it up inside an emptied-out cell of something else; it replicates fine and its genome is a near-copy of a known species. I want to say it is not a member of that species, and I cant say why that matters to me.

This is the case I actually lose sleep over: on a small island, a hybrid between two imported plant species becomes fertile and starts breeding only with its own kind; seven generations later it looks different from both parents, lives in a different patch of the island, and wont cross with either parent. I keep flip-flopping between new species already and not yet, ten more generations first.

So: is there one principle that gets most of these, and where does it give up? Two pairs I suspect are the real trouble: the ring case and the chronospecies case are both endpoints that cant interbreed, and I answered one species to one and two to the other; and with the dogs and the frogs my verdicts run opposite to interfertility in both directions. If your account has to overturn one of my calls, tell me which is cheapest to give up and what I lose by giving it up. Then use whatever principle you land on to settle the island hybrid for me, and name one case it commits me to that I would probably hate. Dont just hand me a list of the standard named species concepts and say it depends which one you pick — I want a principle, where it breaks, and what it does with the unlisted cases.
