I spent a career in experimental physical chemistry, mostly phase equilibria, and in retirement I have been reading about concepts and how they stretch across cases. I want to know what my own working concept of a *phase* commits me to, because I use the word in ways that do not obviously add up, and I would like a principle rather than a survey of usage.

My verdicts, given plainly. Ice Ih and the high-pressure form ice III: two phases. Diamond and graphite: two phases, and the fact that diamond is metastable at room pressure is no objection. Two grains of the same crystal differing only in orientation: one phase, not two. A nematic liquid crystal: a genuine phase, since there is a real transition, a change of symmetry, and reproducible properties on the other side of it. Silica glass: I write *glass phase* in a blend diagram, but I do not think it is a phase in the same sense, because its properties depend on how fast you cooled it. A supercritical fluid: I say *the supercritical phase* routinely, and I would defend that as more than convenience, even though no transition line separates it from ordinary liquid. Glass and the supercritical fluid are the pair I cannot reconcile: they look alike in being regions I name that are not bounded by a transition, yet I am comfortable with one and not the other. A simulation of hard spheres that orders into a crystal with no attractive forces at all, and a driven many-body system that settles into responding at twice the drive period — the so-called time crystal: I am inclined to count both as phases, without being able to say why. A fifty-five-atom cluster flipping between an icosahedral shape and a disordered one: I want to call those two phases and suspect this is just slippage. The phase of a signal, the phase of the moon, phase one of a trial: not the same concept, though I notice the pull of the analogy.

Is there a principle that sorts the legitimate extensions from the copycat ones? I would like each clause tied to the case that motivates it, an honest statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and its verdicts on two cases I have not settled: a single-component liquid with two distinct amorphous forms separated by a sharp transition, and a flock of self-propelled particles that spontaneously moves in one direction and keeps doing so. Finally, tell me whether the looseness in my field's use of the word is disciplined analogical extension or mere convenience — that is, whether I should stop saying *glass phase* in print.
