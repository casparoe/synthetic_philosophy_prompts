I'm on the board of a tiny county historical society and we're rewriting the plaque about the big 1890s fight over the mill dam here. Two things make it messy. The dam's backers won in court and their company basically became the town — but almost everything written down at the time, the letters and diaries and the newspaper that fought them, came from the side that lost, because the winners' own papers went downstream in the 1913 flood. Decades later it was the winners' grandchildren who paid for the archive, wrote the finding aid, and put up the old plaque.

At our last meeting somebody shut down a long argument with 'history is written by the victors' and then used it for two separate things: (a) our surviving record must be slanted toward the winners so we can't trust any of it, and (b) more strongly, there's no real fact of the matter about who was in the right, only whichever story got told. Both feel wrong to me but I cant say why.

Can you lay out the different precise claims that line could be making — about who does the actual writing, who gets preserved and archived, whose framing survives, who gets believed, whether the outcome somehow makes the truth — and for each say whether it's trivially true, contestable but defensible, or just false, and whether it would actually get my colleague (a) or (b)? Especially: in a case like ours, where the losers wrote most of it and the winners only curated it afterwards, which of those readings even apply? A list I can take to the next meeting would be ideal.
