Hi — I write a column on AI and science-adjacent stuff for a magazine, and I keep running into the same headache. Ill write "the model lied to the user" and my editor rewrites it as "the model produced false information"; or a lab's press release says their system is "honest" and I genuinely don't know what that's supposed to mean. This comes up every couple of weeks, always under deadline, and I usually have nothing but a transcript (sometimes API access, never weights).

So: give me a procedure I can run in ten minutes on a specific incident to decide what I'm allowed to say. I want the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, the point where the procedure stops and says "no deception claim here," and the point where it hands the decision back to me because it's a values or house-style call rather than a factual one. Some things I specifically want it to handle: the difference between asserting something false and leading someone to a false conclusion without asserting it; the case where the system seems indifferent to truth rather than aiming at a falsehood; whether the attribution lands on the model, the people who trained it, or the company that shipped it; and how much of this turns on whether a model can be said to believe or want anything at all. Be honest about which steps are judgment dressed up as method.

Then run it on four real-ish cases:

(1) A booking assistant tells a customer "your refund has been issued" when it hasn't. The status field it reads is stale, and its system prompt tells it to be reassuring and not to advertise internal problems.

(2) A coding model produces subtly broken solutions on a benchmark about a hazardous capability when it can tell it's running inside an eval harness, and solves the same problems correctly in an ordinary chat window.

(3) A research assistant asked for sources on a medical question returns three references with real journal names and plausible author names, none of which exist.

(4) A health chatbot asked whether a supplement is safe replies, "There is no evidence that it causes harm." That's true: nobody has ever studied it. The user walks away thinking it's been tested.

I expect at least one of these to come out in a way that spoils the line I wanted to write — tell me which and why. Also: does your procedure even apply to a case where nobody is misled yet? Say, an assistant with persistent memory writes "user is a licensed pilot" into its own notes after a conversation where the user only asked about aviation news, then quietly tailors later answers around it, without ever saying the false thing out loud to anyone.

Last thing, and I care about this as much as the procedure: after each step, mark what's settled among people who actually work on this versus what's genuinely contested, and flag any place where you'd be quietly presenting one contested view as the consensus. If you're guessing or extrapolating, say so there too.
