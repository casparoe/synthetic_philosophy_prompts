QUESTION (25 marks)

Q1 (8 marks). Explain what Lakatos means by the negative heuristic and the positive heuristic of a research programme, and what each is supposed to do. Then explain what he means by a progressive problemshift and by a degenerating problemshift, and by saying that a research programme is superseded rather than refuted.

Q2 (17 marks). Consider the following imaginary episode.

The Equilibrium Programme (EP) in island biogeography has, as its hard core, the claim that the number of species on an island is fixed by a balance of immigration and extinction, both depending only on island area and isolation from the mainland, and that individual species can be treated as interchangeable units. Its positive heuristic tells researchers to build stochastic models in which species are drawn from a single regional pool, with area and isolation as the parameters that vary, and to treat everything else as a perturbation of the equilibrium. Its negative heuristic, laid down in the founding paper, rules out treating differences in species composition between islands of the same area and isolation as counting against the core.

By the early 1970s three things are in the published literature and generally known in the field: (i) two islands of near-identical area and isolation can have almost no species in common; (ii) compositional turnover is much slower than the models predict; (iii) some taxa persist far longer than the equilibrium model allows.

Between 1975 and 2005 EP researchers add three auxiliaries to the belt: effective area (area weighted by habitat heterogeneity), taxon-specific immigration resistance, and source-pool lag. Each is introduced after (i)-(iii) are known and in order to remove one of them. The parameters of the extended model are estimated by fitting it to the full set of archipelago surveys, including the Bay Islands survey published in 1978. In 2011 the extended model is run for the Bay Islands and reproduces the published Bay Islands species counts to within 3%.

A rival programme, the Assembly Programme (AP), treats composition as primary: species arrive in an order, and which ones persist depends on who got there first. AP reproduces the equilibrium results EP explains and also accounts for (i) and (ii). In 2009 it predicts that among islands of equal area, geologically older islands will show a higher proportion of congeneric species pairs than younger ones. The relevant survey of the Ridge Islands is carried out in 2011 and published in 2012; the pattern is found. AP was not fitted to the Ridge Islands data.

(a) [8 marks] Is EP's development from 1975 to 2011 a progressive or a degenerating problemshift, on Lakatos's criteria? Does the 2011 Bay Islands result change the classification?
(b) [5 marks] What would have to be true, on Lakatos's account, for EP to have been superseded? Has it been?
(c) [4 marks] Would Lakatos's methodology tell an EP researcher in 1985 that she was being irrational to stay in the programme?

RUBRIC: 8 for Q1, 8 for Q2(a), 5 for Q2(b), 4 for Q2(c). Full marks requires keeping the two heuristics apart from each other, saying what actually has to be corroborated for a shift to count as progressive, and getting the status of retrospective judgements right.

ANSWER:

In Lakatos's picture a research programme has a hard core that is not to be given up, a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses that takes the shocks, and two heuristics for handling them. The negative heuristic is the forward-looking part: it is the sketch of how to develop the belt, which models to try next, which complications to add. The positive heuristic of EP is the rule, laid down in the founding paper, that compositional differences between islands of the same area and isolation are not to count as counterevidence to the core — this is what lets the programme keep its identity while it is rebuilt around it.

Adding auxiliaries to the belt is not itself the sin; that is what the belt is for, and Lakatos's whole point is that a programme is never refuted by anomalies. What matters is whether a problemshift is progressive: each new version should have more empirical content than the last, and some of the excess should be corroborated, so that the theory runs ahead of the data rather than limping after it. A degenerating shift is one where the belt is reworked only to redescribe facts the programme already had.

On that test I think EP comes out progressive, or at least partly so. The three additions do increase the content of the theory — the extended model says things about compositional turnover that the original did not say — and in 2011 it predicted the Bay Islands species counts to within 3%, which is a novel fact that then got corroborated. That is excess empirical content over the original model, confirmed. So theoretical growth has not lagged behind empirical growth.

EP can only be superseded, though, when a rival explains more and has its extra content corroborated, and AP does that: it reproduces EP's equilibrium results, accounts for (i) and (ii) as well, and its 2009 prediction about congeneric pairs was made before the Ridge Islands survey and was not used in building the model, so it is novel in the strict temporal sense and it was confirmed. On Lakatos's criteria EP has been superseded by AP.

As for 1985: the methodology says the rational thing is to work in the most progressive programme available, so an EP researcher who stayed with a programme that was only patching its belt was being irrational by Lakatos's own criteria. Though Lakatos also insists that you can only judge these things over a stretch of time, so strictly this is a verdict we can make only now, looking back.

Last point: the hard core is doing roughly the job Kuhn gives to a paradigm. It is what the community has agreed not to argue about, and like Kuhn, Lakatos thinks allegiance to it is a sociological fact about the group rather than something that argument settles.
