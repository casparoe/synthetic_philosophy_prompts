So I've got roped onto the assessment panel for the composition pathway at the conservatoire where I teach, and we have two cases this week that we can't agree on, and I'm the one who has to write it up by Friday.

Case one: a second-year, "J", submitted eight short pieces for ensemble. What J actually did was record about forty of their own sketches over two years, train a small generative model on them, then spend weeks walking around that model's latent space, pulling out outputs, arranging the ones they liked for live players and editing them. No dispute that J built the corpus and the model themselves. The fight is about whether navigating and selecting from a space you built counts as composing. J can tell you exactly why the corpus was built the way it was and why they threw out most of what came back, but cannot tell you why one particular coordinate produced that chord — and neither can anyone else, that's sort of the nature of the thing.

Case two: a third-year, "P", submitted six pieces made with a commercial preset library and a script that randomly nudges a handful of parameters and keeps anything under three minutes. P chose the final six from roughly nine hundred outputs. And musically — we listened blind — P's are better. The panel ranked them higher and that dosen't help anything.

Our regulations say the work must be substantially the student's own, and that the student has to be able to account for there musical decisions. Half the panel wants to fail P and pass J, on the grounds that J chose the space and P just mined somebody else's. The other half says that's incoherent: everything is variations on a theme inside a space you didn't invent, so if recombining within a fixed space isn't composing then most of the repertoire isn't either, and in any case P's pieces are better.

Here's the bit I actually want help with. There's that old Hofstadter knob-twiddling idea — creativity isn't conjuring something from nothing, it's finding the right few high-level dimensions to vary in a space of variations on a theme, and the interesting creative act is often deciding what the knobs *are* rather than turning them. I half want to hang the memo on that. But I can tell it cuts both ways, and I don't think it hands me a criterion I can actually apply to J and P rather than a nice story I tell afterwards about a decision I'd already made.

So draft me the decision memo. Bottom line on each case — pass, fail, or pass with conditions, and if there are conditions, what they are and by when — and reasons that the rest of the panel and the two students could genuinely argue with instead of just having handed down to them. Take the "recombination is all composition has ever been" objection seriously and tell me where it does and where it doesn't land. And seperate out, explicitly, which of what you're telling me is uncontroversial and which is a live fight among people who actually work on this stuff: don't smooth a contested claim into a consensus just because it makes the memo tidier. If the honest upshot is that the knob story doesn't decide these cases at all and that we're really ruling on something else — what a degree is meant to certify, or what "account for your decisions" was ever meant to test — say that, and recommend on that basis instead. A page and a half, two at most.
