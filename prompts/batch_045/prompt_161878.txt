I'm a retired stratigrapher, forty years of deep-time climate records, and I've drifted into philosophy through a reading group of other retired science people. We've been arguing about whether this century is a hinge of history — the idea that a handful of people alive now can shift the whole long-run trajectory — and we can't figure out what we actually disagree about, because everyone uses the label for something different. Could you build us a decision tree to sort that out, something we can work through in a two-hour session?

What I'd like:

- Start from a node we'd all grant (either that the long-run future holds vastly more of whatever matters than the present does, or that almost nothing we do now will still matter in ten thousand years — you pick the more honest entry point) and end at named leaf positions.
- Every branch node should be one claim that genuinely splits the space, phrased so someone can answer it without knowing where it leads. No asking are you a longtermist?
- Keep how much our actions change what happens strictly separate from how much our actions change what future people will care about, or which standard the future gets judged by. We keep conflating those and I think half the argument is that.
- Give its own branch to whether the window is closing because of institutional and technological fragility, a narrow period of danger, versus because of sheer numbers and value density downstream. One of our group says those are the same question; I think they aren't, so flag it if your tree can't tell them apart.
- Seven to nine leaves, including the time-of-perils view, the view that the big future matters but our influence on it washes out or has an unknowable sign, the slow-down-and-deliberate view, and the view that the whole framing is a category error about how deep time works. That last one is my soft spot — argue against it fairly, don't just note it exists.
- Mark any combination of answers nobody seems to actually occupy, with one line on whether it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Where a leaf has no settled name, call it unnamed and describe it instead.

No reading lists or citations, we can't follow them up anyway, and wich names go with wich view matters much less to us than the shape of the space.
