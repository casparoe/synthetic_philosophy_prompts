I'm the grad-student rep on my university's coverage review committee for the student health plan, and since I'm the closest thing to a philosopher on it people keep pushing the hard ones at me. Our rule of thumb is that the plan pays for treatment and not for enhancement, and we've never written down what that means. Here's our track record:

- We pay for stimulants for students with an actual ADHD diagnosis, including one who told us straight out that what it mostly does is help her get through finals.
- We refused the same meds for a student with no diagnosis, even though she had a GP's letter and her grades demonstrably went up on them.
- We paid for LASIK for a student with severe myopia and refused it for one with mild myopia who needed uncorrected 20/20 for flight training. His need was real and glasses fixed it fine; our reasoning was that we don't pay for career advantage.
- We paid for a cochlear implant for a student deaf from birth, over a letter from a Deaf student group saying deafness isn't an illness, she wasn't suffering, and she'd never asked to hear.
- We refused to fund a tDCS headset for language learning, and we also refused a memory-training course for a student with entirely normal memory who just wanted to be better at a language she volunteers with. There was no competitive angle there at all, so that refusal wasn't about advantage.
- We pay for prevention without blinking: vaccines, PrEP, the malaria pills for the field course.
- Growth hormone, yes for a diagnosed deficiency, no for a healthy student who was just short.
- We paid for an SSRI for a student with diagnosed depression, even though her doctor said it left her concentrating better than she had before she got depressed.

New one, and I have to vote on it Thursday. A second-year student with no diagnosis of anything has been sleeping four to five hours a night for months because she's the main caregiver for a sibling with a chronic illness. She's asking us to cover a wakefulness drug for two semesters. Her GP's letter says the daytime sleepiness is normal physiology, not a disease; that the drug would put her alertness back around where it was before the caregiving started, which was unremarkable either way; that at a standard dose she might end up slightly sharper in the afternoon than she used to be; and that without it she'll probably fail the year.

Please don't send me to the literature, I've read plenty of it, I want your read. Give me the verdict you'd actually defend, not a menu of options, and tell me how confident you are and what would move you off it. If you think our precedents are simply inconsistent, say that and tell me which one we should overrule. Sections, roughly:

- The rules we might be running — about 250 words, each stated sharply enough to be checkable
- What each rule says about her — about 200 words, including the wrinkle about whether normal is indexed to her circumstances
- Closest precedents — about 150 words, which ones pull which way
- What I should vote — about 200 words, committed
- What we'd be signing up for — about 150 words on the principle each choice commits us to
- The case to bring next — about 100 words on the one application that would actually settle which rule we're on

One more thing I keep going back and forth on: if we say yes to her, do our own precedents commit us to saying yes to someone whose sleep loss is entirely self-inflicted, e.g. a guy who games until 3am because he wants to? Say whether the rules you've extracted would split those two, and if they do, what exactly they're tracking.
