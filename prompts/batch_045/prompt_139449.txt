I manage a team of about thirty people, ages 19 to 67, no academic background at all, and Ive been reading about generations on my own for a couple of years because I keep hearing "Gen Z won't do X" or "the boomers are like Y" and I genuinely cannot tell whether I'm learning something or whether somebody sold me a horoscope with extra steps. Before I go any further with it I want to know what I should be demanding of an account of this stuff.

So: what would a theory of generations have to actually do to count as any good? I don't mean "is it true", I mean the shopping list. What does it have to explain, which cases does it have to get right, which distinctions does it have to keep instead of smushing them together. And for each one give me the case or the reason it's on the list, not just the requirement by itself. Then tell me where two of the requirements pull against each other — which ones probably can't be satisfied at the same time. If that turns out to be the answer then that's the answer and I'd rather find out now.

Here's the stuff I've been chewing on. Tell me which of these are real requirements and which are just me being grumpy.

The three clocks thing. When I measure something in a given year, a person's birth year is just that year minus their age. So "they're young", "it's the times we're living in" and "it's their generation" all have the same shape and the data by itself can't tell them apart. Seems to me a theory has to be upfront about what extra assumption it's bringing along to break the tie, because if it isn't, it's not a theory of generations, it's a theory of whatever it brought along. I think there's a name for this in statistics but I can't remember it.

The sharp line problem. The cutoffs are sharp but the causes aren't. Somebody born December 1996 is a millennial and somebody born January 1997 isn't, and everyone agrees nothing happened in those five weeks. So either the line is a discovery — there's an actual seam there — or it's a decision, a convenience, like time zones. A theory has to say which. And I think it also has to survive me wiggling every boundary by two or three years: if the whole story falls apart when the lines move, the story was about the lines.

The adjective test. "Millennial" keeps looking like shorthand for a bundle: grew up with the internet, came of age around a crash, debt, whatever. If the word only ever does the work the list of adjectives already does, it's a nickname for a pile of causes, not a kind. So what work does the generation itself have to do on top of the experiences? And the flip side — a rich kid and a poor kid born the same year: how much spread inside a generation is allowed before the word stops earning its keep?

The feedback problem. Tell people they're a generation and they start acting like it, the pattern shows up in the data, everyone says see, it was real all along. I can't decide if that's a debunking or if it's the same as a recession being real because we're all acting like it's happening. A theory has to take a position on whether that loop makes the thing more real or less, and I want to know which choice costs it more somewhere else.

The export test. These labels feel American to me. My team has people who grew up in Korea and Nigeria and they don't line up with the birth year windows at all — the thing that shaped them was something that happened in their country, not someone else's bracket. Is that a problem for a theory or just a fact about which labels got popular? A theory has to tell me whether a generation needs to be global to be real, and if not, what makes my 1997 line better than a "came of age during the war" line.

Two more things. Which single requirement knocks out the most popular accounts? I suspect it's the three clocks one but that might just be because it's the one I actually understand. And be honest about which requirements are taste rather than requirements — I notice I want a theory to say something I could turn out to be wrong about, and I also want it to cover the way people actually use these words, and I have a feeling both of those can't be on the list at once.

The purpose, so you know what "adequate" means here: I want it good enough to decide whether to stop using these words about my team entirely, or whether there's a careful way to keep using them. So the standard is — does this account let me say something true and useful about a specific person I manage, or is it only ever true of averages and useless for that.

If it helps, a couple of lines at the end on how the usual takes do against the list — the "shared events made us" one, the "it's just age and times" one, and the "it's made up but it has effects" one — but keep that short. The list is the part I want.
