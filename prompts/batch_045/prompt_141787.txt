I've been voluntold into running a 45-minute session on Goodman's new riddle of induction for the ML reading group at work — about a dozen people, all perfectly comfortable with cross-validation and distribution shift, none of them with any philosophy, whiteboard but no projector. Half of them will walk in assuming this is either Hume-with-a-twist or just a cute way of saying you overfit and should go collect more data. I dont need the riddle explained to me; I need a plan for explaining it to them, and I need the dependency structure more than anything: what has to be on the board before grue can even be stated (my guess is they have to first grant that the generalization that every emerald examined so far has been green is a paradigm of a well-supported projection, so that nothing later looks like general skepticism about the future), and in what order the pieces go so that it lands as a problem about which predicates are projectible rather than as a problem about whether induction works at all.

Then the carrying example. I'm tempted to throw out the emeralds entirely and do it in their idiom — two featurizations of a data stream that agree on every row they've been fit on and come apart at row N — but I worry that invites "just wait for more data" and "the true function is whichever one generalizes" way too early. Should the example be the classic emerald case, the featurization case, or something else, and what does it have to have and, more importantly, lack? My instinct is that it must not come with any causal or mechanistic story attached (no impurity in the crystal, no odd lighting, nothing that explains the color) and that it must not let them appeal to the fact that green has been used more often than grue, but I'd like that instinct checked.

One wrinkle I've been playing with and can't settle: a version where the switch isn't a fixed calendar date but is tied to the first occurrence of some event whose timing is unknown, so that waiting to see which predicate wins isn't even available in principle before the divergence. I can't tell whether that makes the riddle sharper or whether it makes grue non-projectible for some boring unrelated reason — something about the predicate depending on the order of observations rather than on a time. Is that variant worth using, and where, or should I stay with the fixed-time version and handle "just wait and see" as one of the misconceptions instead?

Misconceptions I'm expecting, which I'd like pinned to exact points in the sequence rather than just listed: (a) collapsing it back into Hume, so that the conclusion becomes that induction is unjustified full stop; (b) "it's just overfitting, use a simplicity prior / minimum description length" — which I think needs the move where green gets defined in terms of grue and bleen exactly as well as the reverse, so that syntactic simplicity is vocabulary-relative; (c) "we have actual physics here, the reflectance of the stone is settled, there's a mechanism" — I genuinely don't know whether to treat that as a solution, i.e. projectibility cashed out as lawlikeness or mechanism, or as a change of subject, and I'd like advice on where in the hour it should surface; (d) "entrenchment is just word frequency, and isn't it circular anyway" — which is where I expect Goodman's own positive proposal to be weakest, and where I'm happy to say so out loud. Anything big I'm missing, and in particular anything an audience with ML backgrounds specifically would form that a philosophy audience wouldn't?

Finally: one closing question that would actually tell me they'd got it rather than that they'd been following along (I was thinking of asking them to grue-ify a feature in one of our own pipelines so that the grue version has exactly the same track record as the original, and say what they'd now predict), plus a short honest list of what I should refuse to explain at this level and say out loud that I'm refusing — I'd rather leave them wanting than fudge it.
