I lead a small platform team that builds internal developer tooling, and I have no formal training in philosophy — I apologize in advance for any mangled vocabulary. I need a classification instrument, not a literature review: I cannot check citations, my team will not read them, and half of what I have seen on this topic reads like marketing anyway. Please work from the structure of the design space rather than from what anyone has published, and where a region of the space has a label I might recognize from engineering blogs (oracle-style, copilot, bounded agent, autonomous agent, and so on), use it only if it maps cleanly onto a leaf; otherwise mark the leaf "unnamed" and do not invent a source for it.

The argument I am trying to settle: we keep calling some of our systems "tools" and others "agents," and the word is doing three different jobs at once. Marketing uses "agent" for anything with a loop in it; our security reviewers use it for anything holding credentials; a couple of people on the team use it for anything with a goal it carries across invocations. The result is that every design review turns into a definitional argument, and the review requirements (threat model, kill switch, audit log, sign-off gate, named liability owner) get assigned by whoever argues hardest. I want to sort systems by what they actually do.

Deliverable: a decision tree, eight to ten branch nodes and eight to twelve leaves, that takes a reviewer from "here is a system's design doc" to a named position on whether it is tool-shaped or agent-shaped and what governance that position implies.

Constraints on the nodes:

Every internal node must be a question a senior engineer can answer from a concrete design doc without knowing which leaf it leads to — no node may be "does it have agency?", "is it autonomous?", or anything else that just restates the label. Where a node cannot be phrased that way, say so rather than forcing it.

Build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of names: state the independent axes you think the space really has, say how many combinations they permit, and then locate the actual positions inside that space. I have a candidate set of axes I want either used or explicitly argued against: whether the objective is per-call and fully specified versus persistent and underspecified; whether the system has discretion over means even when the goal is fully determined; whether it holds credentials that let it cause irreversible change; whether its permission set is closed for the duration of a run or can be expanded by the system itself (installing a package, creating an account, spawning a sub-agent, spending money that was not pre-allocated); and what happens when the human stops paying attention — my own candidate for the deepest cut is a failure-direction test, since a tool fails safe when you stop calling it while an agent fails unsafe when you stop supervising it, and that looks more diagnostic than anything about how capable the system is. I also want tested a reversibility-budget variable (magnitude of irreversible side effect per unit of human attention), a substitutability test (if you swapped the system for a human contractor given the same brief, credentials and budget, and the surrounding governance did not change, it is an agent whatever you call it), and the question whether gratuitous agency counts — if the same output is achievable with the loop, the credentials or the persistence stripped out, was the agency ever load-bearing?

Cases the nodes should be built out of, at least most of them: a linter that suggests edits but never writes; a CI bot that opens pull requests but cannot merge; an unattended research job with a thirty-minute wall clock, a fixed compute budget, web access and no side effects beyond a report; a support system that may issue refunds up to a small limit and email the customer without per-case approval; an incident-response bot holding credentials to restart services and edit production configuration; a coding agent permitted to install dependencies and create cloud resources; a scheduling assistant holding a user's mailbox with permission to send mail in their name; and a system permitted to delegate work onward, whether to another agent with its own budget or to a human hired through an API.

Leaves: each should carry a name or "unnamed," one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and one sentence on the engineering consequence — what review it triggers and which controls are non-negotiable. These must appear: pure instrument with no side effects; side-effecting tool gated on per-action human approval; the murky middle of a fire-and-forget job with closed scope and no new affordances, treated explicitly rather than collapsed into either neighbor; supervised agent with authority inside a pre-allocated budget and checkpoints; wide-authority agent whose corrigibility and reliable interruptibility are supposed to compensate; agent that may acquire new affordances or delegate recursively; agent acting under a principal's authority and held to outcome standards rather than process standards; and a deflationary leaf for the view that the tool/agent distinction does no real work and only capability plus authority matter, with the tree showing what routes there.

Two further things I need: note which combinations of answers are unoccupied and briefly diagnose each (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored — I suspect a batch job with irreversible authority and no persistence is genuinely unoccupied and that is telling), and flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate rather than pretending it can. Finally, tell me which single cut is doing most of the work, and whether the tool/agent distinction is a binary, a spectrum, or two axes that my team keeps conflating.
