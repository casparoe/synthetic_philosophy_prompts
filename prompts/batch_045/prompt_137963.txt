hey — I've been sketching the rule system for a made-up polity (call it Vantara) and I've drifted into holding an actual philosophical position about it that I want stress-tested. here's the setup, then the position, then what I want from you.

Vantara starts with one rule:

R0 (the Grant): "The Assembly may add, repeal, or amend any Rule by simple majority."

and then acquires a few more, in this order:

- R1 (the Ratchet): "No Rule may be amended except by a two-thirds vote of two consecutive Assemblies." — passed by simple majority under R0.
- R2 (the Padlock): "R1 may not be repealed or amended." — passed under whatever R1 requires.
- R3 (the Bootstrap): "An amendment is valid only if it is consistent with the Rule set that exists after the amendment takes effect."
- R4 (the Rewind): a later amendment declaring itself to have been in force since the founding.

plus two situations: a package ballot where amendment P (repealing R1) and Q (lowering all thresholds to 40%) pass together and each is only desirable if the other also takes effect; and a case where R1 is adopted while some other proposal is already halfway through the ratification process.

my position: nothing here is really circular. every apparent paradox of self-amendment reduces to ordinary sequential amendment under a two-step picture — (i) you assess the validity of an amending act only against the Rule set in force at the moment of the act, (ii) if it's valid you install the new set, and only then does it govern future acts. self-repeal, self-entrenchment, and changing the amendment rule are all just steps. two corollaries I also hold: entrenchment that doesn't protect itself is always leaky in two steps (repeal the padlock, then repeal the thing it padlocked), and whenever a case still looks paradoxical it's because someone has quietly conflated validity with durability or smuggled in retroactivity.

what I want: what would count against this? specifically,

- which of my clauses or cases actually break the two-step picture, and which does it handle fine (say so honestly, including the ones it handles easily);
- for each case that hurts, what would have to be true — about legal time, the moment of enactment, how we count votes, or where the amendment power comes from in the first place — for it to be a genuine counterexample rather than a puzzle;
- whether the picture secretly depends on a second-order rule it can't itself amend ("always validate against the rules in force at the time of the act"), which would mean it reinstates the exact fixed point it was supposed to dissolve. I think this is my real exposure, but I might be wrong about that;
- what a defender of my position should concede as counting against it, as opposed to what a critic would say;
- whether I've defined "valid" in a way that makes the whole view unfalsifiable, and if I have, whether that's actually a problem for a view of this kind.

I'll be moved most by a case where my picture gives no answer at all, rather than one where it gives an answer I find unintuitive. no citations or reading lists, and please don't duck into "well, the literature says" — argue from the cases above and any you invent.
