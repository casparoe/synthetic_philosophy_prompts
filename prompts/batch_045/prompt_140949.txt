I am a PhD student in philosophy writing a short position statement on whether trained neural networks really have representations, or whether talk of representation is only a useful façon de parler about systems we do not yet understand. I want the package audited before I commit to it. Here is where I have landed:

(1) Feature realism. There is a fact of the matter about which features a trained network represents, and this fact is not fixed by what interpreters find useful, by what is convenient to visualize, or by which decomposition we happen to choose. It is a substantive, interpreter-independent fact that our attributions could in principle get wrong.

(2) Basis anti-privilege. No basis of activation space is metaphysically canonical: not the neuron or "standard" basis, not the directions recovered by dictionary learning / sparse autoencoders, not principal components. Two decompositions can be incompatible as sets of directions and yet both be perfectly good descriptions of the same network.

(3) Superposition. Networks standardly represent many more features than they have dimensions: features correspond to directions that are not mutually orthogonal, and the network tolerates a nonzero amount of interference among them because the features are sparse.

(4) The linear representation hypothesis. Features are, at least approximately, linear directions in activation space rather than, say, regions, manifolds, or essentially nonlinear codes.

(5) The causal-efficacy criterion. A candidate feature is a real feature of the network only if the network's own downstream computation actually reads it out. This is what I rely on to settle the "grain" problem: when a finer-grained dictionary splits one coarse feature into several, or when one dictionary element's variance gets absorbed into others, I want to say that the grain at which the network itself is sensitive settles which of these are the features, and that a decomposition element nothing downstream consults is not a feature of the network at all.

(6) Interventionist evidentialism. Representation claims are warranted only by interventional evidence — patching, steering, ablation that changes behavior in the way the hypothesis predicts. Merely observational or correlational evidence, such as a linear probe's accuracy, is at most suggestive and never sufficient.

(7) Anti-propositionalism. It is a mistake to ascribe beliefs, desires, or propositional attitudes to models. Interpretability is not in the business of recovering "what the model thinks"; where such talk appears it should be read as shorthand for behavioral or counterfactual dispositions.

One distinction I have been leaning on in the draft: the difference between a feature's causal profile — the inputs that activate it and the downstream effects it has — and its geometric realization — the particular direction or set of directions in which it is implemented. I use this to argue that (1) and (2) are compatible: realism is about causal profiles, and one causal profile can have many geometric realizations.
