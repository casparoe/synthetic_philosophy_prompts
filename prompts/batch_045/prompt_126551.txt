we run a philosophy-and-film night at the local library and we're doing It's a Wonderful Life again in december (yes, seasonal, yes, half the room is there to cry). I don't want it to be just a cry this time, so I've been trying to read the thing through the lens of supererogation, and I keep flip-flopping. Give me your reading and push back on me where I'm wrong.

Here's the bit I can't settle. George spends the film giving things up — college, the trip, the honeymoon, the money from the building and loan — and we're clearly meant to see a man going well beyond what he owes anyone. But the movie also loads up a counterfactual: the Pottersville stretch is basically an argument that if George hadn't done all that, genuinely terrible things happen, people die, the town rots. That seems to me to cut against the whole category. If the alternative is that bad, then it wasn't optional at all, it's what he had to do, and treating it as beyond-duty looks like a way of laundering an obligation into a favour. Is there a coherent way to hold both halves? Does the film quite take a side on the standard worry that supererogation collapses into obligation once the stakes get high enough?

Second thing, and this is the part I haven't seen anybody raise: a lot of what George "sacrifices" isn't only his. He hands over their honeymoon money, keeps the business barely alive instead of taking a real job, and Mary and the kids eat most of the cost — the drafty house, no savings, the whole bit. As I understand it, the cost condition on supererogation is normally built as a cost the agent himself bears, and the praise is tied to that. So what happens to the category when the loss lands on people who never volunteered for it? Does that make the acts less supererogatory, more, or what? Does it show the category has a third-party problem nobody notices because the textbook cases are always a lone agent with his own wallet?

Third: the ending pays him back. The town fills the basket, the house gets fixed, Clarence gets his wings. Does retroactive reward undo it? I don't mean the crude version where he was in it for the money; I mean whether an act can stay beyond-duty if, looking at the life as a whole, it wasn't really a net loss for him. And relatedly — my own coinage, tell me if it's dumb — the ratchet: one generous act raises the bar so the next one is just expected, and by the end George isn't freely giving anything up, he's the guy everyone calls. On that reading the film isn't a portrait of supererogation at all, it's a portrait of supererogation eating itself.

What I actually need: which two or three scenes you'd point the group to (I can only show short clips); where you think this reading is forced and the film resists it; and whether the film gives the debate anything the arguments don't — I'm thinking about how much of George's arc is a good man who is quietly furious about being good, and whether resentment and burnout have anything to tell us about the limits of the category. Don't just tell me George is a saint and sainthood is supererogatory, I can get that off the back of a cereal box. And no reading suggestions, I won't read them.
