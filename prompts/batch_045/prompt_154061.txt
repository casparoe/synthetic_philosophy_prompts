I work in the central services of a mid-sized Swiss canton, four of us, and a big chunk of what we actually do is triage. Something lands on us -- a request from a group of municipalities, a motion in the Kantonsrat, a question from Bern, occasionally some standard our exporters have to match if they want to keep selling into the EU -- and we have to say which level should be carrying this: municipal, cantonal, federal. About two a week during session season, half an hour to forty minutes per file, and the output is one page of plain German or French that goes to a Regierungsrat who signs it and who will not read anything longer than a page. Right now everyone on the team invokes subsidiarity and it settles nothing, so I want an actual procedure.

The reason it settles nothing is that the standard two-part test -- the higher level may act only if the lower one cannot adequately achieve the objective, and it could therefore be better achieved up here by reason of scale or effects, which is roughly how the EU states it, with the German version tying it instead to uniformity of living conditions and legal and economic unity, and the US having almost no legal version of it at all, just the rhetorical one about states as laboratories -- collapses exactly where we need it. So don't restate the two-part test at me; build me something that survives contact with these three problems.

First: the "can they" half of the test is partly endogenous. Whether a municipality can do a thing depends on whether it has been allowed to do that kind of thing before. Competence that sits unused atrophies: the one person who understood the system leaves, the maintenance contract lapses, a neighbouring municipality builds the tool and everyone else just buys access from them. So a test that asks "can they?" will ratchet everything upward over time, because every time we answer yes at the higher level we make the lower level a bit less able next time. I want a step in the procedure that catches this explicitly -- something like asking whether the unit would still fail if it had been doing comparable work for ten years, or whether the failure is self-inflicted and by whom. Call it what you like, but it has to be a real gate and not a footnote.

Second: spillovers, including the awkward ones. The classic case is the lower unit that can act but pushes costs onto people outside its own electorate, or captures benefits paid for by others. There is also a mirror case that bites us constantly: a municipality that can "afford" something only because the money isn't really theirs -- federal grants, matching funds, cantonal equalisation -- so the sufficiency answer is an artifact of who happens to be paying, not of capability. Give me a question that separates those two, since in my experience the second one is where our files quietly go wrong.

Third: the levels don't nest the way the principle assumes. The right unit for deciding, for paying, for delivering and for setting the standard are frequently four different answers, and I want the procedure to let them come apart and to say who chooses when they do. And then there's the regress I'd rather you didn't dissolve with a joke: whenever we say "this one belongs to the municipalities," we are saying it from up here, at a desk in the canton. Tell me where that actually bites in our kind of work and where it's harmless.

For each step I want to know what counts as a sufficient answer given that we cannot do research -- the evidence we really have is things like "this municipality has never run anything larger than a road salt budget" or "they share a clerk with two others" or "the IT supplier is dropping support next year." And I want it marked, honestly and not buried, where the procedure just stops with a no and sends the file back down, versus where it has to hand the decision to the Regierungsrat because the question is a values question rather than a factual one -- whether uniformity across the canton is itself worth paying for, whether a municipality keeping its own thing is worth some measurable inefficiency. Those two I can't answer for him and I don't want the procedure pretending otherwise.

Then run it on three live-ish files. One: five municipalities in a valley want to jointly buy and run an extra treatment stage for a contaminant showing up in the wells; the contamination crosses municipal boundaries; two of the five refuse to join and one of those two is where the plume starts. Two: a cantonal shared platform for civil registries, about forty municipalities, every one of them on its own contract, a few on software whose support ends soon, per-resident cost varying by a factor of about six. Three, and this is the one I'm dreading: our own unit spent most of last year drafting a cantonal licensing regime for short-term rentals, caps set per municipality, one register, cantonal enforcement. I have a feeling the procedure is going to tell me this one isn't ours, and if it does I want to see that worked out straight rather than massaged, because I have to defend it in front of the person who comissioned it.

Finally, tell me what the procedure can't do: which kinds of files it will systematically get wrong, and whether the ratchet point means we should be giving the lower level a presumptive bonus that the two-part test as normally stated doesn't give it.

And please: continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, the way you'd write an email to a colleague. Don't cite anything at me either -- I can't look things up from here and I have no use for a reading list; I need something I can run before lunch on a Thursday.
