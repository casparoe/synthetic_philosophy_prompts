Not sure this is the right place to ask this, but here goes. My younger brother got diagnosed last year with a genetic condition — he's fine right now, but he'll probably need expensive treatment and won't be able to work full time from his forties onward — and, in a completely unrelated way, I ended up on the committee at my office that hands out a small hardship fund. So I've been reading about luck and justice in whatever order the internet hands it to me, which is not a good way to learn anything. The sentence that keeps ending our arguments is "nobody should be worse off through no fault of their own." One colleague used it to say we obviously have to help pay for another coworker's daughter's treatment for a rare condition, and then at the very next meeting someone else used the same sentence to argue we should give nothing to a guy who took a risky contract job abroad, skipped the firm's insurance top-up to save money, and now has enormous medical bills: bad outcome, but his, she said. Both of them think the slogan plainly supports them and are baffled that the other one doesn't see it.

I can't tell how much content that sentence actually has, and the more I poke at it the more it looks like four or five different claims wearing one coat. So: could you lay out, as a list I could actually bring to the next meeting, the different precise things it might be saying? I mean the stuff like — is "worse off" about resources, happiness, opportunity for welfare, capabilities, something else; is it a claim about how a person compares to other people or about how they compare to some threshold or baseline; does "fault" mean morally culpable, or does taking a risky gamble count all by itself, or does it mean the person could reasonably have been expected to avoid it; and is the claim that unchosen disadvantage has to be fully wiped out, or only that it's one reason among many that can be outweighed by cost or by other people's claims. For each reading, say whether it's more or less trivial, whether its actually defensible, and — this is the part I really need — which reading each of my two colleagues is leaning on and whether that reading really does the work they want it to do. I've also seen people sort luck into "brute" and "chosen" kinds, which sounds useful but I can't shake the feeling it just renames the problem.

Here's the bit I've been toying with, tell me if it's dopey: a reading where "fault" is indexed to what was reasonably foreseeable at the time of the choice, so that someone who took a sensible gamble that happened to go badly counts as unlucky and is owed something, while someone who took a reckless gamble that happened to go fine is "at fault" but not worse off, so nothing is owed to them and nothing is owed back from them either. Does that hold together? And what does it say about a case we haven't got to yet: one of our salespeople made a fortune on a genuinely risky career bet, and his kid now grows up very comfortable, which under some of these readings looks like pure unchosen advantage that ought to be clawed back, and that strikes me as a reductio of something but I don't know of what. Finally, does whichever reading survives tell me anything about my brother, or about someone whose entire appetite for risk — and so every gamble they'll ever take — was shaped by growing up poor?
