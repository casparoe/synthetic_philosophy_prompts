I work at a tiny shop that audits the update rules of autonomous forecasting agents, and we have this internal group—we call it the Diachronic Coherence Working Group—that issues yes/no rulings on whether a proposed credal policy is rationally permissible. They take van Fraassen's Reflection principle as a baseline: roughly, if an agent is certain that at a later time it will assign credence x to hypothesis H, then its current credence in H, conditional on the claim that its future credence will be x, ought itself to be x; and if the agent is certain of that future credence, this in practice means its unconditional credence now should already be x. Everyone agrees there are limits, though, and the Group's precedents are as follows. An agent starts at 0.3 on H, will run a clean experiment, and will update strictly by conditionalization, ending at 0.7 on success and 0.1 on failure; its current credence conditional on each possible future credence matches those numbers, so it satisfies Reflection, and the Group approved. An agent at 0.4 on H knows it will be given a confidence drug that, regardless of the evidence, will force its future credence to 0.9; this agent refuses to defer, staying at 0.4 unconditionally and keeping its credence in H given a future credence of 0.9 at 0.4, which violates Reflection, but the Group still approved, presumably because the future credence is produced by a distorting non-evidential process. An agent is currently biased at 0.8 on H and knows that bias-removal therapy, with no new evidence about the world, will later leave it at 0.3; this agent does defer, now setting its credence to 0.3 to match its certain future credence, and the Group approved that as well.

Next week's case is an agent that starts at 0.6 on H. It is certain that an hour of careful a priori reasoning, again with no new empirical evidence, will leave it at 0.2, and it currently believes this reasoning is fully reliable and will make it more accurate; but the agent's planned policy is not to defer, staying at 0.6 now with its credence in H given a future credence of 0.2 also at 0.6, which violates Reflection. I have to guess whether the Group will approve or reject. I'm less interested in a straight verdict than in the rule they seem to be running, because as far as I can tell at least two or three rules fit the first three precedents: one rule says Reflection only constrains future credences produced by learning new evidence, so for any non-evidential process such as a drug, therapy, or reasoning the agent is free to defer or not as it likes; a second says Reflection applies exactly when the future credence results from a process that, by the agent's current lights, makes it more accurate, so deference is required for evidence, therapy, and reliable reasoning but not for a bias-adding drug; a third says Reflection always applies unless the future credence is produced by a known truth-distorting influence like the drug, which is the only exception. Please tell me what each of these predicts about next week's agent, which verdict is your best guess and why, which precedent is the closest analog, and what principle the Group would be adopting if it approves versus if it rejects.

Finally, suppose the Group rules the way you guess. What further case would you put to them next to tell apart whichever rules survived, especially any that agreed on the reasoning case? For instance, how would you distinguish a rule that requires deference whenever the future self will be more accurate from a stricter rule that requires deference for every future credence except those produced by a distorting influence? I'd want a concrete next case: an agent with a certain starting credence, a specified future process, and a specified policy of deferring or not, that would split those rules.
