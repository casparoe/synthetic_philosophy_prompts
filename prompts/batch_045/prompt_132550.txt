I sit on the procurement panel of a city housing authority, and I need a specification rather than a survey of the literature.

The system we are buying. Each applicant to the waiting list is assigned a need score between 0 and 1 by a model. Anyone above a cutoff is fast-tracked to a full assessment; everyone else waits. The cutoff is re-set each quarter with the budget, so no single operating point is fixed in advance. There are three applicant groups, call them R, S and T, that differ in size, and genuine urgent need occurs in roughly one applicant in six in R, one in three in S and one in ten in T. The score is positively but imperfectly informative in all three groups, and its distributions overlap heavily. Outcome labels are recorded only for applicants who were fast-tracked: if you are left on the list, we never learn whether you were in fact in urgent need. Applicants also come to understand roughly how the score works and can spend time or money improving the answers that feed it.

Your task. Write the requirements list: what any fairness criterion for this system would have to deliver in order to be worth writing into a contract that we can actually audit and enforce. For each requirement give the concrete failure case that makes it a requirement, and mark it as non-negotiable, as genuine but negotiable, or as a prejudice of one camp dressed up as a principle. Argue from the arithmetic of the failure cases and from small explicit numerical examples that you construct; do not settle anything by naming a theorem, a result, a paper or an author, and do not give me a bibliography or reading list.

Demands I want you to put on the list and then stress-test, using these reference labels:

D1 All-cutoff validity: the criterion holds at every cutoff we might set, not only at the one in force this quarter.

D2 Auditability under selective labels: the criterion can be checked, at least approximately, from data in which outcomes are observed only for applicants who were fast-tracked.

D3 Stability under adaptation: the criterion still means what it says after applicants have adjusted their answers to the cutoff, and it does not invite an arms race that only the better-off can afford.

D4 No reward for worse predictions: the criterion is not satisfied by a constant score, by randomising independently of the score, or by fast-tracking almost nobody; and adding a feature that makes the score more accurate for every group cannot lower measured fairness.

D5 Contestability: an individual who is told their score and their outcome can tell whether they personally were treated unfairly, and can make that complaint in terms the panel can adjudicate.

I also want you to rule on three demands I keep hearing in the room, saying which of them are genuine requirements and which are commitments of a particular faction: that the score must not use group membership or anything that tracks it; that the same cutoff must apply to all three groups; and that a given score must carry the same interpretation in every group. Alongside these you may draw on, but need not use, the familiar candidates: equality of false positive and false negative rates across groups, calibration within group, equality of predictive value, but-for invariance of the score under a change of group membership, and closure of the criterion over every subgroup definable from our own records.

Deliverables, in this order.

First, a table with exactly these columns: ID | Requirement | Failure case that motivates it | Status (non-negotiable, genuine but negotiable, or camp prejudice) | Which other requirements it conflicts with | What it rules out. Give every requirement an ID; use the labels D1 to D5 where they apply and mint your own for the rest.

Second, the sets of requirements that cannot all be satisfied together. For each such set, give the argument in two or three sentences plus the smallest numerical example you can construct that shows the conflict, built from the base rates and score behaviour I described. Flag the conflict you take to be deepest, and say why it is deeper than the others.

Third, a JSON object, keys in snake_case, with this schema: unsatisfiable_sets, an array of objects each with ids (array of ID strings), argument and example; non_negotiable, an array of IDs; camp_prejudices, an array of objects each with id, camp and why_not_genuine; most_excluding_requirement, an object with id and reason; recommended_package, an object with kept (array of IDs), dropped (array of IDs), rationale and enforcement_test, where enforcement_test is one concrete audit procedure a regulator could run each quarter with the data we actually hold; least_confident_claim, an object with claim and what_would_change_it.

Commit yourself. Do not present the menu and leave the panel to choose: name the package you would put in the contract, say which requirements you are sacrificing and why the sacrifice is the right one, and own the consequences for the groups that lose out. Then identify the single claim in your answer you are least certain of, and say what would change your mind about it. Write throughout in British English.
