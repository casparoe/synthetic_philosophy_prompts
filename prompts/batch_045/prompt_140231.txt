I host a university philosophy podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher defending what she calls differential conciliationism. The view has three moving parts: (1) in a disagreement between acknowledged epistemic peers you owe the other person's judgment real but less-than-equal weight, because you have non-inferential access to your own evidence, and treating the two of you symmetrically would, on her account, double-count evidence you already have; (2) the size of the required shift depends on how much of the relevant evidence is, in her phrase, already on the table between you, so that after full disclosure of first-order reasons it shrinks almost to nothing; (3) what shifts is credence rather than allegiance to the argument: you may lower your confidence while continuing to think you had the better argument.

I argued about this with a friend late into the night and came away convinced the view is either conciliationism in name only or else unstable, and I want the interview to establish which. She is also practised at meeting hard cases with some version of "that is a feature of the view rather than a commitment I need to give up," and I want questions that make that reply progressively harder to fall back on.

Draft eight questions, in the order you would ask them: at least one each on the symmetry problem for (1), given that the peer can say exactly the same thing about their own evidence; on whether (2) collapses the view into steadfastness in precisely the informed, long-running, fully disclosed disagreements philosophers actually care about; on whether the credence/reason split in (3) is psychologically and normatively stable, and what the peer is meant to take from a confidence you have lowered while still defending; on applying the view to disagreement about the view itself, including to a peer who accepts (1) but denies (2); on contested peerhood, where the other person denies that you are their peer on this sub-question; on the many-peers case, where ten peers have all heard and rejected your argument, and on whether the required shifts accumulate or are screened off, and why; and on disagreement with your own earlier or future self. End with one question about what could count as evidence against the view.

Return a JSON object with two fields. "questions": an array of eight objects, each with "question" (the wording), "target" (which of claims 1 to 3 it presses, or meta), "likely_reply" (her strongest answer, one sentence), "hinge" (the single assumption that reply rests on), "follow_up" (the question that attacks that hinge), and "deflection_available" (true or false, whether the feature-not-commitment reply is open to her here, plus a one-clause note on why). "sequencing": two or three sentences on why this order makes her usual deflection harder with each question.
