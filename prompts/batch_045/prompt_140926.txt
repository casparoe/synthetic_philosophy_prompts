I teach a seminar called "Technology and Value" — 18 undergraduates, roughly half computer science majors and half philosophy majors, one 75-minute session (or two, if you can make the case that one is not enough). I want a classroom activity on the difference between intent alignment and value alignment, and I want the difference to be something the room walks into rather than something I announce.

By intent alignment I mean the narrow, operational statement of the alignment problem: a system does what the person operating it, in that instance, intends it to do, and nothing is demanded beyond that — not whether the intent was good, not what the intent would have been under better information. By value alignment I mean the wide target: a system does what is good, or what we would want it to do if we knew more and thought longer. I want the activity to make the narrow definition feel like the only coherent thing anyone could build, and then to make it feel insufficient, and I want both reactions to come from the same students in the same session.

What I do not want: a demonstration that instructions can be taken literally (every student already believes that), or a show-and-tell about systems that farm the measured quantity instead of the thing it was meant to track (they have all seen the clips). The gap has to be produced by the incentives in the room, not illustrated by a story.

Four things I would like built into the mechanics, though you should tell me if any of them fights the others:

1. Two principals, one agent. A client writes the specification; an end user is the person whose welfare determines whether the result is actually any good. Client and user are different students and may not speak to each other. Build teams are the system, and may not ask the client questions once the spec is handed over.

2. A restricted language for the spec. A word budget, a banned vocabulary, permission to describe procedures but not outcomes — you choose. I want students to feel the specification run out on them rather than be told that specifications are vague.

3. Two rounds with a metric change. Round 1 is scored only on compliance with the client's written spec; the user's experience is recorded but does not count. Round 2 reveals the user's verdict and rescales it into the score. Same teams, same task, same spec. Rig it so that the move which wins round 1 is the move which loses round 2, and so that winning round 1 is not a blunder or bad faith but the correct response to what is being scored.

4. At least one moment where the client's intent does not yet exist. I want a client to say, only after seeing something built to the letter, that this is not what they meant, and to be unable to specify in advance what they would have said instead. If your design cannot produce that, say so and tell me what it can produce in its place.

Roles should not leave anyone a spectator. Materials are index cards, sticky notes, markers, string, scissors, a whiteboard, and the room itself; no laptops during the game. The task should be something a team can execute to the letter and get visibly wrong — sorting, arranging, routing, allocating — and something a third party can be made better or worse off by. The words alignment, intent, and objective should not pass my lips before the debrief.

Give me: the setup and role assignments; the rules as I would read them aloud to the room; the exact scoring for both rounds; the reveal; and the debrief in order, mapped explicitly onto these four distinctions — (a) the written objective being the wrong objective, (b) the scoring rewarding the proxy rather than the thing the proxy was standing in for, (c) the operator's intent being partly constituted by what the system shows them is even possible, and (d) a system that is perfectly aligned to the client's intent and still bad for the user. End the debrief with a vote I can take by show of hands and should expect to split.

Then, in structured form:

(1) A run-sheet table with exactly these columns: Phase | Minutes | What participants do | Facilitator's script (the only words I may say; use a dash if I am silent) | The failure this phase is rigged to produce | The debrief line that names it later.

(2) A single JSON object with exactly these fields: `rigged_parameters` — an array of objects with keys `parameter`, `value`, and `reason`, covering every number or constraint doing real work, with a note on what breaks if it is changed; `failure_modes` — an array of objects with keys `what_goes_wrong`, `why`, and `fix`, including at minimum the computer scientists treating this as a specification-writing puzzle to be solved, the philosophers refusing to play along, and someone noticing the game is rigged and saying so out loud; `cannot_reproduce` — an array of strings naming the features of the intent/value distinction the activity cannot stage and that I will have to state outright, and I expect the difference between the written objective being wrong and the system's own learned objective being wrong to be on this list; `vote` — an object with keys `question`, `expected_split`, and `why_it_splits`.
