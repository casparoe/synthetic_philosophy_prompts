Im a journalist covering science and ideas, and Im doing a piece on whether large language models can be said to know anything. I want a gimmicky section in the middle where I imagine sitting Charles Peirce and William James down separately in front of a chat window and asking each of them, in his own terms, whether the thing on the other side of the screen knows anything and whether its outputs can be true. So: ventriloquize them. Dont give me a primer on pragmatism, Ive read enough of it to be dangerous, and I definately dont want a survey of the two positions. I want you to commit to what each man would actually say about this case, and to say which of them has more resources for handling it — I have a hunch its Peirce but tell me if Im wrong.

Here is what I want them to react to, and some of it is specific:

(a) The pipeline. These systems are pretrained to predict text, then fine-tuned heavily on human approval — raters pressing the thumbs-up button on outputs they like. So the training signal is what people find acceptable, not what the world is like. The model converges, but on our likings.

(b) A practice I saw pitched at a machine-learning conference, which nobody there seemed to realize was philosophy: call it convergence auditing. You feed a model progressively larger and better-curated slices of the total evidence base bearing on some question, watch whether its answers stop moving, and if they stabilize, you treat the stable answer as your best proxy for the truth — you operationalize the end of inquiry as the limit of a training run. I could not decide whether this is the most Peircean thing anybody has ever built or a complete travesty of him.

(c) Confident falsehoods that happen to work: a model tells a doctor something wrong, the doctor acts on it, the patient does fine anyway. And the reverse: a true statement nobody acts on.

So, concretely. Would Peirce let a model into the community of inquirers at all, or does his whole story depend on the inquirer being something that can feel the irritation of doubt, be surprised, and pay a real cost for being wrong — a thing with purposes, not just a very elaborate abduction engine? If he did let it in, what experiment would he insist on before trusting a convergence like the one in (b), and how would he distinguish a limit imposed by reality from a limit imposed by the raters in (a)? Would he call that a fake limit?

And James: truth for him is a species of the expedient in our believing, but a model does not believe and does not act on anything. Is he going to say the whole question is a category mistake and that the model is just part of the environment, with all the believing still being done by me and the cash value showing up in what I do next? Or does he have room to say the model itself has beliefs? One thing Im curious about: James seems to me to care, more than the what-works caricature of him suggests, about whether a belief is expedient because it latches onto something or merely because it flatters and reassures the person holding it. Would he be suspicious of (a) on exactly that ground?

Last thing: would either of them revise his view because of this case, and where would they end up disagreeing with each other — not about some textbook summary of truth, but about this object on the screen? Please pick the most plausible reading of each and defend it rather than hedging.
