I'm auditing a graduate seminar on historiography this term — I'm the only person in the room who isn't a historian — and last night I had a long, increasingly circular argument with a friend who is finishing a dissertation on nineteenth-century German historical writing. The object of the argument was one sentence, from the preface Ranke attached to his first book in 1824. In the English translation I've been working from (a rendering printed in an 1896 Sewanee Review article on Ranke), it runs: To history has been attributed the function to judge the past, to instruct ourselves for the advantage of the future. Such a lofty function the present work does not attempt. It aims merely to show how it actually took place. A few lines later the same source gives: Rigorous presentation of the facts, however conditional and lacking in beauty they may be, is without question the supreme law.

My friend reads the famous sentence as essentially negative. On her account its energy is all refusal — a historian declining the magisterial office his predecessors had claimed — and the word "merely" is a rhetorical shrug aimed at rhetoricians, not a substantive thesis. So, she says, Ranke is not offering a theory of truth about the past at all; he is laying down a rule of the guild: don't pass sentence, don't edify, show. I objected that on that reading the clause everyone remembers does no work at all, and that in any case the "supreme law" sentence is itself a normative demand, not an abstention, so the preface is less modest than it performs. She then made a move I couldn't answer. The weight, she said, is entirely on the German adverb behind "actually" — eigentlich, built on the stem for "own" — so that the phrase asks for the thing as it is in itself rather than as later ages have made use of it: not appearance versus reality, but the matter itself versus later appropriations of it. She cited the later line she'd been working on that week, in which Ranke says each epoch is immediate to God and its worth rests not on what issues from it but in its own existence, "in ihrem Eigenen selbst" — in what is properly its own.

Here is where we deadlocked. I put a case to her: a sixteenth-century envoy's dispatch says he is defending Christendom, while the archives the historian assembles make it fairly clear he is protecting a dynastic revenue stream he never mentions. On the "in itself" reading, does "how it actually took place" include the envoy's own sense of what he was about, or does it mean the configuration the historian reconstructs and that the envoy would have repudiated? If eigentlich means the thing apart from the uses made of it, then the agents' own self-description is also a use, and the formula seems to license overriding them; if it means the thing as it was for the people who lived it, then reconstructing it apparently requires a scheme of our own, and Ranke sounds much closer to his twentieth-century critics — that the facts of the past always reach us already selected, and that the shape of a narrative is imposed rather than found. She replied that my either/or is anachronistic and Ranke would not have recognised the choice; I replied that a formula which cannot answer the question has less content than its reputation implies. Can you help me understand the passage properly: what kind of claim is it making, what work the adverb is doing, how it sits with the "supreme law" sentence, and which of our two readings the text can actually bear — where each of us is overreading, and what we are both missing about what a preface like this one is doing? I would much rather have the grammar of the claim laid out than a survey of what later writers said about it.
