A friend of mine — a musician rather than a philosopher, but the sort of person who reads intellectual history on holiday — and I spent most of an evening arguing about Tolstoy's book What Is Art?, which she had just finished and which I have never read. She emailed me her summary afterwards, and since I don't have her email open and can't look anything up, I'll paraphrase rather than quote. As I remember it, she said: that Tolstoy defines art as the transmission of feeling, so a work is art only to the extent that it infects its audience with the feeling the artist himself had; that he then turns this into a test of value, so that how widely and strongly a work infects is how good it is, which is why he ends up rejecting most of the European tradition — Shakespeare, Dante, Beethoven's late works, the symbolists, and his own novels — as counterfeit; that the reason so little survives is a second and stricter religious test, on which art must express the Christian perception of the age, so that what he really wants is art with religious subject matter, edifying stories about brotherly love; that he is a thoroughgoing aesthetic egalitarian who would rather have a peasant's dance song than King Lear, on the grounds that ordinary people can understand the one and not the other; and that he reaches all of this by simply discarding the concept of beauty, which he thinks is nothing more than a name for whatever the cultivated classes happen to enjoy. She also takes the whole thing to be refuted by a single objection: that on his own criterion a catchy music-hall song infects far more people than anything he praises, so either he has to call the song great art or he has to smuggle his religious views in by the back door.

I argued back, and I think badly, and ended up defending a reading I more or less invented in the course of the argument: that his two criteria are doing quite different jobs, with infection functioning as a sincerity test — a sign that the feeling was genuinely lived through rather than manufactured by someone working to a commission — and the religious-perception criterion doing the separate work of ranking feelings by what they do to the bonds between people. On that reading, the music-hall objection misses its target, because wide infection would only ever have been evidence of sincerity and never a measure of worth. But I genuinely can't tell whether I reconstructed something Tolstoy actually built into the book or merely made him tidier than he is, and I suspect that somewhere in the argument I slid from what he says to what would be defensible for him to say.

So I'd be grateful for an accuracy audit I can act on. Please go through her summary claim by claim and tell me, for each, what is faithful to the book, what is a simplification a careful populariser could get away with, and what Tolstoy explicitly denies or would have rejected — including whether any of it collapses into one slogan a distinction he insists on. And I especially want your judgment on three things: whether the music-hall objection is one he has the resources to answer or one he is really stuck with; whether my two-criteria reading is fair to him or a rationalisation that quietly drops something he cared about; and whether, if I concede to my friend next time we meet, there is a version of what she said that I could honestly agree with. This is for settling an argument, not for a paper, so please reason from what you know of the book itself and don't send me to sources or references.
