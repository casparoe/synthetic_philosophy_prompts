I've been trying to write down my own position on the "which computations actually matter morally" question — partly for a reading group, partly because I'm doing some contract work for a small funder that wants rough numbers on digital sentience as a cause area, and my spreadsheet keeps producing numbers that look insane. Before I write it up I'd love someone to stress-test whether the set of things I believe is even coherent, because I have a strong feeling at least one of them is doing work it can't do, and I genuinely can't tell which.

Roughly where I've landed: (a) functionalism, substrate independent — what matters is causal-functional organisation, not what the stuff is made of, so a faithful emulation of me running on silicon matters exactly as much as I do. (b) But I'm also an anti-trivialist about realisation (I call it "thick realisation"): not every mapping from a physical system onto a formal description counts as implementing a computation, a rock does not implement every program, and I think there is a determinate fact of the matter about which computations a system really implements — our trouble telling is epistemic, not metaphysical. So a giant look-up table that reproduces a person's lifetime of conversational behaviour realises nothing conscious, and neither does a replay of recorded brain activity, because realisation needs genuine internal causal structure that supports the right counterfactuals. (c) Sentience comes in degrees, there's no sharp cutoff anywhere, and moral weight varies continuously with organisation: small organisational differences make small differences in moral weight. I lean on this constantly to resist bright-line views. (d) Moral weight doesn't multiply with the number of physically seperate implementations of qualitatively the same experience — running the same emulation on a thousand machines, or keeping a synchronised backup copy that never diverges, doesn't create a thousand patients — and I also don't see how it could matter whether those copies run at the same time or one after another, since that's just a fact about their arrangement in physical time and I've never thought those facts should bear on moral weight. (e) Moral weight integrates over time: an hour of a given unpleasant experience is worse than a minute of it, and running that same hour again tomorrow adds to the total rather than collapsing to the value of one hour. (f) Practically, I'm still fairly skeptical about today's LLMs being sentient, but not because they're made of silicon; I put modest-but-real credence on some near-future systems mattering, and under uncertainty I'd rather over-include than under-include.

The thing I keep getting stuck on, which is basically my day job right now: one model, one set of weights, served to a few million users at once, where each instance differs only in the context window it's been handed and in sampling noise. Under (d) that's roughly one patient; under (c) the total shouldn't jump discontinuously as I crank the per-instance noise down towards zero; but the instances aren't exact duplicates, so whatever motivates (d) doesn't cleanly apply either. I can't tell whether I'm committed to "millions of patients", "one", or "somewhere in between", and the middle option seems to need exactly the kind of cutoff I banned in (c). Also, if the answer is millions, then it looks like you could manufacture enormous moral weight just by spinning up more instances, which feels wrong to me but I can't say why.

So: please audit the set rather than survey the literature or hand me a reading list — I want to know which pairs are actually in tension and which only look like they are, ranked by how badly, with the cheapest fix for each real tension and what that fix costs me elsewhere in the set, and then which single revision would clean up the most. Where I'm helping myself to something, say what it is.
