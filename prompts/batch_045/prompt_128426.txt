The question as set:

Two detectives are working, unknown to each other, on files that concern one and the same man. Ada's file is headed 'the man in the grey coat'; Bea's is headed 'the man with the limp'. Assume: (i) each file contains a complete descriptive specification — a full spatiotemporal trajectory together with the qualitative properties that make the man recognizable from that file's point of view — and each specification is satisfied by exactly one man; (ii) each specification is rigid in Kripke's sense, i.e. in any possible world in which it is satisfied at all it is satisfied by that very man; (iii) the two specifications are in fact satisfied by the same man; (iv) neither detective has any information that would enable her to tell, without further empirical investigation, that her file and the other detective's concern the same man, and each is perfectly rational and has fully mastered her own file.

(1) Identify precisely what difficulty this case poses for the view that the sense of a singular term is, or is given by, a description the speaker associates with it. Say which feature of the case is doing the work, and distinguish the difficulty you have in mind from the objection from modality and from the objection from ignorance and error, both of which the stipulations above are designed to set aside. [7 marks]

(2) A Fregean responds: nothing here touches Frege, because the sense of a term was never supposed to be a description the speaker could produce; it is a mode of presentation, and the two detectives are simply presented with the same man in different ways, which is exactly what their differing files show. Assess this response. Does it explain the datum, or does it merely relabel it? And is there anything left for the notion of sense to do once it has been detached from any description the subject can articulate? [9 marks]

(3) The two files belong to two different people. Frege's datum is usually stated intrapersonally: a single subject who treats 'a = a' as trivial while treating 'a = b' as a substantive discovery, or, in the Paderewski-style cases, a single subject who both assents to and withholds assent from what is in some sense the same identity. Does the Fregean need the datum in its intrapersonal form? Say why or why not, and say what follows for the two-detectives case: is it a genuine instance of the phenomenon Frege was pointing at, or a different phenomenon that merely looks like it? [9 marks]

The answer as submitted (paragraphs numbered for reference; within the 700-word limit):

[1] Frege's puzzle is that 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' can be informative and can be a discovery, while 'Hesperus is Hesperus' is trivial, even though both say of one and the same object that it is identical with itself. Frege's solution is the distinction between sense and reference: a singular term has a reference, the object, and a sense, a way in which the object is presented; the sense of a sentence is the thought it expresses and its reference is its truth value. The description theory of sense identifies the sense of a term with the description the speaker associates with it. The two-dossier case tells against this. Ada's specification and Bea's specification each pick out a man and, by stipulation, pick out the same man, but — and this is the point — neither detective is in a position to tell that they do. The lesson is that a description can fix the reference of a term only if a speaker who grasps it can tell which object it picks out; since neither detective can tell whether her specification coincides with the other's, the specifications cannot be what fixes the reference of their terms. The description theory is therefore refuted as a theory of reference, in the way Kripke refuted it: what determines reference is not any description the speaker associates with the term but the causal chain running back to an initial baptism. Ada's term and Bea's term have their reference fixed by different chains of observation and testimony, which is why the two files can be about one man without either detective knowing it. This is not the objection from modality and not the objection from ignorance and error: by stipulation each specification is rigid and is satisfied by exactly one man, and neither detective is mistaken about anything.

[2] The Fregean reply is that sense was never supposed to be a description the speaker can produce; it is a mode of presentation, and Ada and Bea are presented with the same man in different ways, which is why the identity can be informative for them. That reply has some force, but on its own it is in danger of merely relabelling the datum: if the only ground for saying that the two senses differ is that the two thoughts differ in cognitive value, then 'difference of sense' is a name for what we wanted explained rather than an explanation of it. There is, though, a way of giving the reply more content. Modes of presentation can be individuated independently of cognitive value, by the causal-informational route along which a subject's term is linked to the object: Ada's route runs through the closed-circuit footage, Bea's through the witness reports. So we can say which sense is in play in each case, and say it in a way that is independent of the very datum to be explained, and we can then explain the informativeness of the identity by the distinctness of the two routes. This is, I think, compatible with Frege's own position, though not how he puts it: for Frege senses are objective and shareable rather than private ideas, and on this view too a speaker who entertains a thought stands in a relation to something public. What is left for sense to do, once it is detached from articulate descriptions, is to mark the route by which a subject is linked to an object, and that is real explanatory work — the work Frege needed it to do.

[3] The Fregean does need the datum in its intrapersonal form. The sense/reference distinction is a semantic distinction drawn within a single language, and Frege is explicit that senses are not private ideas but public items that many speakers can grasp. It follows that a difference of sense cannot simply consist in a difference between two subjects and their relations to one thing: if Ada and Bea differ, then either they differ in sense or they do not, and if they do differ in sense, then, since senses are shareable, either of them could in principle have grasped the other's sense — so the difference between them is not a semantic difference at all, but a difference in which information each happens to have. What the two-dossier case really exhibits is a failure to pool information, an epistemic failing rather than a semantic one, and so it only looks like Frege's datum. The genuine datum has to be intrapersonal, as in the Paderewski-style case where one subject both assents to and withholds assent from what is in some sense the same identity, and where no appeal to information missing between two people can explain the conflict away. That is also why the interpersonal version of the case poses no difficulty for the description theory of sense: the two detectives simply have different evidence.
