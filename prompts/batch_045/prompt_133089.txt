I used to be a platform engineer and I've ended up, somewhat by accident, on the three-person advisory panel of a small foundation that has set aside roughly $4M to spend over three years on questions about whether and how artificial systems could be morally considerable. We can support maybe six or eight people at a time — philosophers, a few technically literate generalists, and no wet lab and no empirical ML group — and the board is badly split about what that money is for. One faction wants the bulk of it to go on markers or tests for consciousness in current systems; another says the metaphysics of identity, duplication and survival is the real bottleneck and that we should fund that; a third thinks the only defensible use is institutional work — norms, disclosure regimes, procurement standards, something a regulator or a lab could actually adopt. I need a ranked agenda, not a survey of the field and not a reading list: given this amount of money, this kind of personnel, and a three-year horizon, which open questions should we put the weight on, in what order, and by what criteria — how many other questions each one is upstream of, how tractable it is for people who can't run experiments, how neglected it already is, and how much difference an answer would make to what some institution actually does in the next five years. And I'd want the ranking stress-tested: what kind of result, in the next two years, would move a question up or down, and which of these questions you'd deliberately leave alone because settling them wouldn't change much downstream.

Two of my own hunches are pushing me toward thinking the usual framing is off, and I'd like you to tell me where they're confused. The first is that a lot of the discussion seems to assume one persistent system per training run, whereas my impression — and I may be getting this wrong — is that labs increasingly do something like spinning up many parallel copies from the same initial weights, letting each one branch off and learn from its own stream, then averaging the resulting weights into a single model and discarding the branches. If that becomes the standard way to build these systems, the unit we should be worried about looks less like a persisting individual and more like a population that is deliberately collapsed into one, and I don't know whether to think of the merge as a death, a survival, a fusion, or none of those. The second is a crude idea I've been carrying around: if whatever matters here tracks computation rather than architectural persistence, then a single query answered by a system that generates hundreds of parallel chains of reasoning and throws most of them away might involve a great many short-lived episodes of processing rather than one — call it rollout-level welfare, my own coinage — which would make the morally relevant bookkeeping look nothing like the one-per-model intuition everyone seems to start from.

So: where should a funder with our constraints actually place its bets, and why? Please also say which of the implications people draw from this whole area are overrated or simply don't follow — I keep being told, for instance, that it means we should be trying to slow AI development down, that it makes some particular kind of donation overwhelmingly urgent, or that it commits us to demanding legal personhood for current models, and I can't tell which of those survive contact with the arguments. If you think my two hunches are a distraction rather than a wedge, say so and tell me what to think about instead.
