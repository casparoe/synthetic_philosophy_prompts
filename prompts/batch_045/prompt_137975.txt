I edit a quarterly of satirical public administration, and for an issue on the theme of measurement I need a piece aimed at the practice of governing toward a country's position in the annual world happiness ranking — the one built on the Cantril ladder, where respondents rate their current lives from zero to ten against their own imagined best possible life, averaged over a rolling three-year window, with the accompanying table decomposing each country's average into six factors (income, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, perceptions of corruption) alongside a comparison with a hypothetical worst-off country and an unexplained residual.

The piece I want is a leaked five-point strategy memo from a fictional Office of Comparative Standing inside the government of an unnamed mid-table country, written in deadpan civil-service prose by an author who is entirely sincere. Each clause should convert one real feature of the measurement into a policy lever, and the comedy has to come from the features being described accurately rather than from invented absurdity. Assign one lever per clause, drawing on these:

- who is asked, and what is uppermost in their mind at the moment of asking: the ladder records a single remembered evaluative judgment, not a sample of experience, so the Office can work the fielding window, the survey items that come first, and the reachability of the sampling frame, while changing nothing about anyone's life;
- the top rung is each respondent's own imagined best possible life, so a population instructed in more modest expectations stands higher on an unchanged ladder, which justifies a programme for the calibration of aspiration;
- the headline number is a plain average of ladder answers and the six-factor table is an after-the-fact explanation of it rather than a set of ingredients, so the Office should fund a Residual Division that improves the country's showing in the explanatory table — the residual being larger than most of the explained contributions — without ever moving the figure that determines rank;
- rank is ordinal and the published figure is a three-year average, so a Competitor Surveillance desk can report a gain in standing in a year in which the country's own average fell, and time each intervention for the year it will first register;
- the factors are single-item proxies, generosity meaning having given money to a charity recently and freedom meaning satisfaction with one's freedom to choose, so the Office pre-funds a charitable donation for every household and trims the menus of choice until citizens can honestly report satisfaction with the options that remain.

Constraints: the memo must never say that any of this is bad measurement — no winking, no irony markers, no nudging — and must never state the objections a reader should draw (adaptive preferences, evaluation versus experience, gaming a proxy, the difference between explaining a score and producing it). Let the clauses escalate, with the fifth the most damning in its blandness. Name no real country; the perennial leader may be referred to only as our northern neighbour. Invented officeholders and unit titles are fine. It should be funny to someone who has argued about life evaluation versus experience sampling and legible to someone who has not.

Reply as exactly five numbered clauses, one or two sentences each, around 300 words in total. No title, no preamble, no sign-off: begin directly with 1.
