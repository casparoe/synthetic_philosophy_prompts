I'm co-teaching a grad seminar on formal epistemology this term with my colleague Dana, who's formally in the math department and is a much more commited Bayesian than I am — I'm the one who keeps dragging everything back to bare metaphysical questions she finds boring, and she's the one who brings those grocery-store cupcakes to every meeting. We've just finished two weeks on updating on uncertain evidence, and I've realized I have no idea what rule she actually thinks is correct. She won't state one in general terms; whenever I push her she says something like "the cases tell you what to do" and then just does the case. So I've been reconstructing it from her verdicts, and next week is the last session before she flies off to a conference, and I'd like to have it pinned down before then. (My sabbatical application is due Friday and I keep working on this instead, which tells you where my priorities are. Also: please don't give me a reading list or point me at papers — I can look those up myself. I want the reconstruction.)

Her running example goes like this. An urn is either the red-heavy one (80% red balls) or the blue-heavy one (20% red), 50/50 which. One ball gets drawn and you squint at it under bad light. Let E be the proposition that the ball looks red: your prior gives you P(E) = 0.5, plus 0.8 for the red urn given E and 0.2 given not-E. The rule she keeps using is the one where you move your credence over the evidence partition first and then read off the hypothesis by holding fixed the probability of the hypothesis inside each cell — so if your experience leaves you 0.8 on E and nothing else is going on, you end up at 0.8 × 0.8 + 0.2 × 0.2 = 0.68 on the red urn. Her verdicts so far: (i) that clean case, she says 0.68, no hesitation. (ii) Same 0.8 shift on E, except you've also just learned that the lamp has a red gel over it and that the gel goes up only on the days when the blue urn is the one in use: she refuses to run the update on E at all, says the very conditionals you'd be holding fixed are the ones that just got changed, calls the 0.68 "an artifact of the partition," and lands in the mid-0.4s — much closer to where you started — once she writes down a bigger space with a variable for the gel and conditions on the look in there. (iii) Same 0.8 shift, but there's no story at all: nothing you can point to changed, you just find yourself at 0.8, and you have no opinion either way about whether your color vision is degraded. She shrugs and says 0.68 anyway. (iv) You're not sure you even saw the ball — you might have blinked, and the 0.8 arrived by some fuzzy route: she says the input isn't well formed, go find the proposition you actually got evidence about (something in the neighborhood of "it visually seemed red to me") and condition on that with a likelihood that handles the blink case, you can't just feed the number for E into the update. (v) Two shifts, one at 10:00 when you look and one at 10:05 when you heft the ball: she says do them in the order they happened, and adds that doing it the other way round gives a different answer, so temporal order is what breaks the tie.

The case I've written for next week, which she hasn't seen, is this: same urns, but now you heft the ball and squint at it in a single motion, so it's one experience rather than two, and it leaves you simultaneously at 0.8 on looks-red and 0.75 on feels-heavy. Heaviness is linked to the urn as well (70% of the balls in the red urn are heavy, 30% in the blue one), and the look and the heft are independent of each other once you know which urn you've got. I can see at least three or four rules that fit her verdicts on (i) through (v) equally well, and they come apart here: one says pick whichever shift is the real evidence and ignore the other, one says apply them one after the other, one says find the single distribution closest to your prior that respects both numbers at once, and one says throw the numbers out and go build a model of the experience. What I want is: what will she actually say — with a number, if her rule delivers one, and a flat "she'd refuse to give a number" if it doesn't; which of those rules you think she's running given (i)–(v) and why you'd prefer it over the others that fit just as well; which of the verdicts gives you the least traction in telling them apart; and one further case I could put in front of her next week that would separate the surviving rules instead of just re-running this one. I'd also like to know which of the four I'd be committing myself to if I decided to write it up as my own view, and which of them is the most embarrassing to be caught holding.
