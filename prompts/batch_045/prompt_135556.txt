I'm a teaching assistant for an upper-level seminar on democratic theory, and I'm building a two-page handout on the boundary problem — the question of who belongs to the demos — for a class session next week. Every treatment I've seen, including the survey chapters my students have read, just arrays the familiar named principles side by side and stops there, which lets students pick the label they like instead of being forced to bite the bullets that come with it. So I want the instrument built in the opposite order: begin from the diagnostic questions and the space of answer combinations they permit, and only then drop the actual doctrines into the cells, noting which cells turn out to be empty and whether that's because the corresponding view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored. The deliverable is a decision tree plus a short note on how to run it as an in-class sorting quiz: a path of roughly ten questions from a starting commitment to a leaf, each question phrased as a claim about a concrete case that a student can answer before knowing which doctrine it leads to, and a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto leaves. Please don't frame any branch as "which principle do you find plausible?", and don't give me a bibliography, a reading list, or author attributions — I've read those already, and what I need is the structure. If a position has no settled name in the literature, call it "unnamed" and describe it in a sentence rather than inventing a label or attaching a name you're not confident about.

The recurring case should be this one, invented for the handout and used at every node so the questions stay comparable: a regional body, call it the Estuary Authority, governs flood control and water allocation across a river delta, and its decisions bind resident citizens of the delta districts; long-term non-citizen residents who have lived and worked there for fifteen years with no route to citizenship; farmers upstream in a neighbouring province, outside the jurisdiction, whose irrigation gets cut off; a downstream city in another country that absorbs the surge when the Authority opens the spillway; resident children under sixteen; people who would move into the delta only if the Authority builds the new levee, so that their stake is created by the decision itself; residents born fifty years from now, if the Authority chooses the groundwater option instead; a district that has petitioned to secede; and finally a traveller who is inside the territory for one week and subject to its laws while there. Diagnostics I want the tree to handle, in whatever order and phrasing you think is cleanest: whether mere causal affectedness is sufficient or whether subjection to enforcement is also required; whether being subject to coercion is sufficient even with no stake in the outcome; whether the affectedness must clear some threshold of significance and who would set it; whether stakes that the decision itself brings into existence count; whether future people are in the demos, and what that does to the idea of an actual assembly; whether any competence or agency condition gates inclusion and whether it competes with or overrides affectedness; whether the boundary is the same for every decision or varies issue by issue; whether the current demos may decide its own membership, and what to say about the resulting regress; and whether inclusion is owed as a matter of right or justified instead by what it does to the quality of the decision, which is where I want sortition-based and competence-based answers to enter rather than as a footnote.

Two further things. First, please place two principles I've been toying with, and tell me honestly if each collapses into a leaf you already have (in which case say which one and why) or occupies an otherwise empty cell (in which case say whether that cell is empty because the view is unattractive, unstable, or just unoccupied): the captive-constituency principle, on which what grounds a claim to a say is not affectedness or coercion but the prohibitive cost of exit, so that denizens are in and the one-week traveller is out; and the relational test, on which the relevant question is not who is affected by outcomes but who can be wronged by being excluded from the procedure, irrespective of whether their inclusion would change any outcome. Second, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot actually separate — I suspect the affectedness and coercion principles will come apart only in cases like the downstream foreign city and the passing traveller, and I'd rather have that limitation stated plainly and given a tie-breaking question than have it smoothed over. One sentence per leaf on its most awkward commitment, please, and write the whole thing in American English.
