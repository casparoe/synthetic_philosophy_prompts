Hey — I run a tiny decision-theory reading group (five of us, grad students plus one econ postdoc) and we're doing a session on gambles that have no expected value. I want to open it with a scripted dialogue that two of us read out loud and then fight about, so it has to be genuinely balanced: both speakers sharp, neither winning by fiat, no one saying "well that's just your intuition."

The gamble, spelled out so nobody has to look anything up: flip a fair coin until it lands heads; if the first heads is on toss n you receive (-1)^(n-1) · 2^n / n dollars — so +2, -2, +2.67, -4, +6.40, -10.67, ... Each outcome contributes ±1/n to the would-be expectation, so the series is the alternating harmonic one: it converges to ln 2 ≈ 69 cents if you sum it in the natural order, but its positive and negative parts each diverge, so the payoff has no mean and the expectation (in the Lebesgue/integration sense) simply doesn't exist. Both speakers grant all of that.

The part I want them to actually fight over is a case I made up, call it the arcade: you don't get one shot, you can play as many times as you like at 69 cents a throw. Here's the background I want them to use — if you think any of these claims is wrong, put a one-line correction before the dialogue starts, I'd rather know:

- Over N plays the average payoff converges in probability to about 69 cents, but the convergence is pitifully slow, error on the order of 1/log N.
- It does not converge almost surely (it can't — the payoff has no mean, and the converse of the strong law rules it out), and you can see why: the single largest payout among your first N plays is on the order of N dollars, so the running average keeps getting kicked by jumps that don't shrink.
- Whether "stop as soon as you're up a million" turns the arcade into a money pump is exactly what they should disagree about, and the answer seems to hinge on whether you need an unbounded line of credit to run that strategy (I suspect you do, and that the unbounded credit is doing all the work, but don't let me settle it for them).

Two speakers:

Dana holds that the right fix is to value such gambles by the limit of their truncated expected values — 69 cents here — and that this isn't gerrymandering, because every non-crazy truncation agrees: cut off the game after N tosses, ignore payoffs above $M, ignore outcomes rarer than ε, all three land on ln 2. She also gets to use the weak-law fact above: 69 cents is what you actually average, in the only sense of "actually average" that's on offer.

Kit holds that "undefined" is the answer and not a placeholder: the number you get depends on which regularization you pick, the long-run argument that was supposed to justify maximizing expectation is precisely the thing that breaks here, and the honest fallback is stochastic dominance plus capping plus refusing to price gambles the theory can't price. Kit's best card: take a Pasadena-shaped gamble and cap its downside at, say, -$1M, and you've converted a gamble with no expectation into one with expectation +∞ — so anyone still maximizing expectation is now obliged to pay any finite price for the capped version. Kit thinks that's a reductio of the whole framework; Dana has to deal with it.

Last third of the dialogue: make it concrete. A grantmaker with one pot of money is choosing between (a) funding some field whose payoff distribution is Pasadena-shaped — heavy tails with tail index around 1, unbounded upside and unbounded downside in roughly balanced measure, no mean — (b) funding work whose main effect is to cap the bad tail of (a) at some finite level, and (c) a boring intervention with a well-defined +5. Have them converge on this case and disagree about what she should do.

Format: 1,000–1,500 words, minimal stage directions. Requirements: each speaker has to state the other's strongest point in a form the other accepts before replying to it (no talking past each other); at least one real concession from each, not a fake one; at least one moment where they notice they agree on all the math and still disagree about what follows; and it ends with each naming the one consideration — a case, a principle, a result — that would actually move them, not "more evidence." Keep it spoken, a bit scrappy, defintely not two encyclopedia entries taking turns.
