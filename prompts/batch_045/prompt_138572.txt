I'm taking an undergrad IR theory seminar and we just did a week on nuclear deterrence — Schelling, Kahn, the whole MAD thing — and I think I've talked myself into the conclusion that the doctrine is self-refuting, but my TA says I'm conflating two things and refuses to say which, which is incredibly annoying. Here's my argument. A deterrent threat only works if the other side believes you'd carry it out. But by the time you'd have to carry it out — the inbound missiles have launched, your cities are already gone, there's nothing left to protect — retaliating is pure revenge: it guarantees the destruction of whatever is left of your own society through fallout and the other side's remaining warheads, and everybody knows that, and knows that everyone knows it, and so on. So the threat cant be credible. My professor calls this the credibility cascade: any threat needs a further threat to make carrying it out rational, and that regress never bottoms out in anything a sane person would actually will, so the only place it can stop is a genuine willingness to be annihilated — i.e. actual irrationality, not the theatrical kind everyone summarizes as the rationality of irrationality. But then the conclusion looks much stronger than the standard line: the only leader who can really run a nuclear deterrent is one who is clinically crazy, and a rational person's arsenal deters precisely nobody. Which in turn means the Cold War was forty-odd years of pure luck, and that people who say the long peace is evidence that deterrence works are just telling a just-so story to justify keeping the weapons. My roommate says the fix is easy, that a secure second-strike capability is what makes retaliation rational because you still have warheads and you're preventing further strikes — but that seems to sneak in limited counterforce war-fighting, which is the exact destabilizing thing MAD was supposed to rule out, so I dont think that rescues anything.

Anyway: where does this go wrong, if it does? Is my TA right that I've mixed up two separate claims, and if so which ones — is the mistake in the regress itself, or in the idea that credibility has to bottom out in willingness, or in the jump from "deterrence is paradoxical" to "so it was all luck"? And if the luck story is actually defensible, what's the strongest version of it, and how does it answer the person who says we ran the experiment for decades and got zero great-power wars? I'd rather have the confusion diagnosed than be told I'm roughly right.
