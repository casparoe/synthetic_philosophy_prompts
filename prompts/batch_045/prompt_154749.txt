Hi — I cover science and ideas for a living, and once a year the local MSc programme makes the hacks sit a methods exam, which I'm the one setting. This year I want one question on Hempel's raven paradox, but I dont want the model answer to be "non-black non-ravens confirm via the contrapositive, lol" — every student writes that in their sleep.

Here's the setup I want to build it around. Two ecologists on an island with a closed population of exactly 200 ravens are testing H: all ravens on the island are black. Protocol A: visit nesting sites and check the colour of every raven found. Protocol B: pick a random point, walk a 1 km transect, and record every non-black object seen, noting whether it's a raven. Under B someone logs a white plastic bottle washed up on the beach. Call an observation's *counterfactual yield* the set of observations the protocol that produced it could have produced instead — so under B the bottle's yield includes turning up a non-black raven, which would refute H, while under A it doesn't. Question: does the bottle confirm H? Does it matter which protocol produced it, or whether H is about the 200 island ravens rather than all ravens everywhere?

Can you give me (1) the question as I'd actually hand it out, 90 minutes, 1000 words max; (2) a model answer of roughly 600 words; and (3) a marking scheme out of 20 built around four or five must-hit points, with marks per point and a line on how people usually fumble each one? The must-hits should cover at least: why the puzzle isn't just an artefact of the equivalence condition; what counterfactual yield is supposed to do and where it breaks down; and why the closed-population version behaves differently from the unrestricted one. I want an A answer to say something about what confirmation is *for* — prediction, intervention, or just moving probabilities — rather than reciting Nicod and Hempel and stopping.
