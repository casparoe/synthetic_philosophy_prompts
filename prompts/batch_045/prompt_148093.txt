You are a professional philosopher who works at the intersection of formal epistemology, philosophy of action, and computational complexity theory, and who has also mediated a number of adversarial collaborations — so you care a great deal about formulations that neither party can dismiss as a strawman. Before you draft anything, spend a few minutes brainstorming: for each claim you are tempted to write down, sketch the strongest objection each side would make to the wording, and then revise until you think both would sign. Only then produce the document.

I convene a fortnightly reading group in a cognitive-science department, and for three sessions we have gone in circles on the same quarrel, which I want you to turn into the text the two sides should have written. Call the first side the **Tractability Constraint theorist**: she holds that a norm of reasoning or decision that no computationally feasible procedure can meet is not a viable candidate for the normative theory we should use in psychology and epistemology, and that Bayesian updating and expected-utility maximization, taken literally, are therefore best replaced by norms that respect the agent's computational budget (resource-rational, ecological, satisficing-style norms). Call the second side the **Norm Autonomist**: he holds that normative requirements are not answerable to achievability, that complexity results are theorems about algorithms on worst-case inputs and are largely silent about the instance distributions agents actually encounter, that average-case, parameterized and approximation results routinely dissolve apparent hardness, and that his opponent's replacement norms covertly depend on the very optimality standards they reject, since someone still has to compute the resource-rational policy. Take the following as shared premises both parties grant, so that no one has to argue for them: exact probabilistic inference over unrestricted graphical models is NP-hard, and there are known classes of models for which even approximate inference is hard; worst-case and average-case complexity can come apart; and "no free lunch"-style results mean that no single method dominates across all possible instance distributions. Please do not cite papers or assemble a bibliography — I want claims and formulations, not references, and where you would normally reach for a citation, say instead what the claim is and how confident you are in it.

The deliverable should have three parts. First, a **numbered section of agreed claims** (aim for six to ten, and make each one substantive enough that a reader learns something: not just "complexity results are relevant somehow," but claims about the formalism, about what a normative theory is for, about what the hardness results can and cannot establish, about what would count as the other side's view being right). Second, a **disagreements table**, with one row per crux and columns for: the crux stated neutrally; the Tractability Constraint side's own preferred formulation of its position on that crux, in wording a partisan would accept; the Norm Autonomist's own preferred formulation; a test, case, or argument both sides agree bears on the crux; and each side's prediction recorded in advance about what that test would show. Among the cruxes I expect you to treat — add or substitute if your drafting turns up better ones: (i) which measure over instances is normatively relevant, the "ecological" distribution the agent actually faces or an adversarial distribution, and who bears the burden of describing it; (ii) whether intractability functions as an *excuse* for noncompliance, as a reason to *weaken* a norm, or as a reason to *replace* a norm's content, and whether agreement about the first of those already settles anything; (iii) whether the content of our normative requirements is hostage to unsettled mathematics — what each side should say if P were proved equal to NP with small exponents and constants, or if some inference task common in a real environment were proved hard on the actual ecological distribution; (iv) the meta-level regress, since deciding how to allocate a bounded computational budget is itself a computationally hard problem, so the Tractability Constraint side may owe us an account of how the regress stops and the Norm Autonomist may owe us an account of why an unachievable ideal still guides; (v) whether "feasible" means polynomial-time or means within the agent's actual budget, given that a fifty-millisecond latency deadline is not an asymptotics question at all. For diagnosing (i) and (ii), it may help to try out a diagnostic I have been toying with and say whether both sides could accept it: call a norm *intractability-resilient* if, for every plausibly ecological distribution of the agent's decision problems, there exists a polynomial-time procedure that comes within ε of meeting the norm on instances drawn from that distribution — and note where the diagnostic itself begs questions (who fixes the distribution, who fixes ε, and whether the procedure must be findable by the agent rather than merely exist). Two cases I would like used as shared tests, rather than invented ones: a decision-maker facing a hard latency deadline whose instances are empirically easy but worst-case hard, together with a variant in which the instance distribution is actively adversarial to her method; and an empirical comparison in which resource-rational and Bayesian predictions diverge for a real task, with each side writing down in advance which pattern of results would count against its own view. Finally, a short closing section in which you say, explicitly, which parts of the quarrel you concluded are merely verbal or about emphasis and why, and which are genuine and would survive any amount of clarification.

As you go, attach a brief confidence marker to each agreed claim and to each row of the table, and close with a short paragraph headed "least certain point" in which you single out the one place where you think you most likely distorted a position, or where the collaboration would in fact have stalled and you had to legislate as mediator. Say plainly where you are extrapolating beyond what the two positions strictly commit their holders to, and where a real defender of either side would reach for an empirical result that neither you nor I can look up.
