Write a straight-faced news feature — headline plus about 600 words — for a made-up trade magazine (Emerald Industry Weekly or something like it), datelined a few weeks before the switch date in Goodman's grue problem: midnight GMT on 1 January 2050. In this world grue and bleen are the entrenched, professionally certified predicates, and green and blue are the gerrymandered upstarts that a fringe of 'color realists' insists are the real natural kinds. Gemologists certify emeralds as grue, insurers have written bleen clauses, the ISO standard says every emerald on record becomes bleen at switchover, and a standards commitee is finalising the relabelling protocol.

Every joke has to come from actually getting the new riddle right, not from gemstone puns: all emeralds are grue and all emeralds are green are supported by exactly the same evidence so far, no one more grue emerald observation tips it toward grue, the fight is about which predicates are projectible rather than about what the data are, and Goodman's own way out is that projectibility tracks entrenchment, which is mostly just a record of which predicates we have done a lot of projecting with before. That last bit is the real joke: the trade has institutionalised entrenchment — votes on which predicates officially count as entrenched, grandfathering clauses, a certification exam in correct grue usage. Include one quote from an exasperated color realist and one from a standards bureaucrat.

Then push it somewhere the textbook case doesn't go: invent one genuinely awkward predicate or product — grue insurance that only pays out if emeralds stay green, a predicate keyed to how many observations have been made rather than to a calendar date, a switch date the committee keeps postponing — and let the piece end up on the actual worry, that nothing in the evidence settles how we are going to describe the future.
