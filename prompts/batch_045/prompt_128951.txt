I write about science and ideas, and I'm currently working on a long feature on whether culture genuinely evolves — whether the Darwinian vocabulary of variation, copying, and selection does explanatory work when applied to ideas, or whether it is a metaphor that has quietly stopped earning its keep. I don't want another exposition of the meme's-eye view. I want to test it against a case, and I want the case to be a film: the 2023 Barbie movie. That is not meant as a joke.

Barbieland, as the film opens, is a population with almost no heritable variation: near-identical copies, a repeated day, no conflict, no history. Into it come two kinds of transmission, and I want them treated differently. First, Gloria's distress, which arrives in Barbie not as a copied template but as an acquired psychological state that somehow crosses from one mind to another and produces thoughts of death, flat feet, and cellulite. Second, Ken's importation of patriarchy, which spreads through Barbieland with extraordinary speed, arrives in badly degraded form, reorganizes property, housing, and the constitution, and leaves both its carriers and its hosts worse off.

So, three things I need from a reading.

One: the Gloria channel looks Lamarckian rather than Darwinian — an acquired characteristic transmitted directly, no copying bottleneck. Is that a genuine objection to Darwinian theories of culture, or does cultural evolution already house such cases, and at what cost to the analogy?

Two: Ken's variant is the textbook case where a variant's transmission success and its hosts' welfare come apart, and it seems to spread less by imitation of a model than by restructuring the environment that will select what comes next — cultural niche construction. Does niche construction belong inside a Darwinian theory of culture, or is it a rival research program wearing Darwinian clothing?

Three — and this is the distinction I would like you to work with, because I have not seen it drawn anywhere: I want to separate what I'll call occupancy success, a variant's doing well by colonizing more hosts, from leverage success, a variant's doing well by becoming rare or even vanishing after it has permanently altered the selection pressures on everything that follows, rather like a ratchet or an enabling constraint. Does the film's resolution — the Barbies deliberately rewriting their world rather than out-reproducing the Kens — dramatize leverage rather than occupancy? If so, is leverage success something memetics can represent at all, or is it precisely the kind of cultural change the theory is blind to?

Finally, be honest about where the reading fails. The film's moral is authored, deliberate change; its own status as a studio artifact engineered for propagation is an awkward exhibit for any theory of viral ideas; and its ending may be closer to a sermon than to a selection process. Tell me where a memeticist would be cheating. And if the film does add something that the literature on cultural evolution doesn't, say what it is — that's the paragraph I'd most like to be able to write.
