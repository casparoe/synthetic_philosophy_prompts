I'm co-teaching an undergraduate seminar on toleration and its limits next term, and I want to pair the paradox of tolerance — the argument associated with Karl Popper's *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945), and the more guarded versions in Rawls's *A Theory of Justice* and Walzer's *On Toleration* — with Margaret Atwood's *The Handmaid's Tale*. I'd like a reading of the novel through that argument: not a hunt for illustrations, but an honest attempt to see what each does to the other. A colleague of mine, who teaches the same material, insists the pairing is a category error, and I want to know whether she's right.

Here is why she might be. Popper's remedy is triggered by a speech-act marker: the intolerant, he says, are recognisable by their refusal to meet us on the level of rational argument, denouncing argument itself and teaching their followers to answer with their fists. But Gilead, as Atwood dramatises it, does not win an argument; the Sons of Jacob assassinate the President and most of Congress and suspend the Constitution. That suggests a distinction I've started using in my own notes — between conversion-intolerance, which grows by persuading within open institutions and eventually votes the opening closed, and coup-intolerance, which seizes the levers and rewrites the rules — and the uncomfortable possibility that platform-denial, the standard Popperian remedy, is aimed squarely at the first while being both too late and too broad for the second. My colleague's own proposal, which she calls the asymmetric ratchet, is that toleration should extend to an illiberal movement's expressive activity but never to its access to the machinery of the state: speech yes, levers no. Test that against the novel's pre-history — Serena Joy's career as a media personality arguing for women's domesticity long before Gilead, the incremental freezing of bank accounts and revocation of employment, the training of the Aunts — and tell me whether the ratchet actually bites where Atwood says the damage was done, or whether the novel's real emphasis falls somewhere the ratchet can't reach (on complicity, on the clerks and doctors and neighbours who implemented it, on the fact that nobody who mattered was ever forced).

There is a second, sharper problem I want you to work on, which I think of as the epistemology of the threshold. Rawls allows the tolerant to act only when they sincerely and with reason believe that their own security and the institutions of liberty are in danger; but Offred's flashbacks are structured so that no single step crosses that line — each measure is small, legal, temporary, and retroactively normalising — and the threshold is visible only in retrospect, which is precisely when suppressing anything is no longer possible. Popper's paradox may therefore be less a logical puzzle about self-defeating principles than an epistemic one about acting on a danger you cannot yet demonstrate. I also want you to take seriously the novel's closing framing chapter, set in 2195 at an academic conference where a male keynote speaker discusses Offred's cassette recordings with detached, faintly jeering professionalism: that scene stages a second-order version of the paradox, in which a tolerant scholarly culture extends not power but rehabilitation and a kind of amused regard to the intolerant. Does it vindicate Popper, or does it show that the licence to be intolerant toward intolerance has no stable stopping point?

Please give me: three to five scenes you'd put on a seminar handout and what each is supposed to show; the strongest version of my colleague's objection that the novel isn't about liberal toleration at all but about patriarchy, and that Offred's remembered pre-Gilead world was never the tolerant order the paradox idealises; and where you think the reading simply fails — where the novel resists being made into a case study. Work from memory of the text and say plainly where your recollection is uncertain rather than inventing details; no citation apparatus or further reading is needed. I'd especially like a closing answer to this: does Atwood dramatise something about toleration's limits that the philosophical arguments, for all their care, cannot say — and if so, is it an insight or just the vividness of fiction?
