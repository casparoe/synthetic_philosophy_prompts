hey — so I'm a 3rd-year PhD student (ML, mostly preference learning and RLHF-adjacent stuff; my advisor is more theory than empirics) and I've somehow ended up as the token technical person on the advisory board of a small funder. They've got around $2M to spend over three years, and they've decided to spend it on the *conceptual* side of inferring what people want from what they do — IRL, reward inference, preference learning, whatever you want to call it. Explicitly not more benchmarks, not more scaling, and they can't fund compute, so anything that needs a cluster is off the table. Realistically it's about five or six postdoc-years, maybe a couple of senior people part-time, and they want to be able to say at the end that they changed what people think is worth working on, not that they produced twelve papers.

What I keep going in circles on is what to actually put in the call. Some candidates I've been kicking around:

- the identifiability stuff. If behavior is generated by something roughly like a boundedly rational agent, the reward is pretty badly underdetermined: lots of rewards fit the same behavior, and whole families of transformations of a reward give identical predictions. So what exactly is the estimand? Is "the human's reward function" even a coherent target, or should we be estimating something else — what they'd choose with better information, what they'd endorse on reflection, some counterfactual-choice target? And how do you decide between those without already knowing the answer?

- what I've started calling the inference-aware demonstrator problem: once people know their behavior is being used to train a system (labelers, users who've read one news article about algorithms), the behavior starts encoding their *theory of the learner*, not just their preferences. Clicks get partly strategic, partly performed. Is this a real problem for the whole framework, or a measurement annoyance you can design around?

- whose behavior counts. The machinery is built for a single agent, but deployed systems act for a population, and the people generating the data usually aren't the ones the system's actions land on.

- misspecification. If the human isn't rational in the way the model assumes, you get a confident, wrong reward, and I don't think anyone has a good story for telling that apart from success.

I defintely don't want a survey and I really don't want a reading list — I want a defended agenda. Which of these (or something I'm not seeing) should they prioritize, in what order, and on what criteria: how upstream is it of the rest, is it tractable in three years by theorists and philosophers with no compute, is it neglected, and how much downstream practice would actually change if it got answered? Which are overrated, or don't really follow from the setup at all? What evidence over the next couple of years should move things up or down the list? And I'd also like to hear what you'd tell them is a flat-out waste of the money, with the reasoning for killing it.
