I sit on the Clinical AI Assurance Committee of an NHS integrated care board. At our next meeting we must decide whether to approve, approve with conditions, or decline a twelve-month pilot of a decision-support system — the Continuing Care Prioritisation Aid, or CCPA — that would rank patients on the waiting list for a fixed pool of enhanced community support slots: remote monitoring plus one daily nursing visit, roughly forty slots against about three hundred open referrals. The recommendation needs to be one our chair, a lay member, can sign, and one the vendor can act on.

Because the board cannot adjudicate between competing normative views — we are not going to rule that one moral theory is correct — the vendor has built the system to sidestep that question. For each candidate it computes a ranking under each of five standing modules: an aggregate-welfare module; a prioritarian module that weights the worse off; a sufficientarian module enforcing a floor; a constraint module that blocks certain actions irrespective of the gains (using a patient's body or carer's labour as a means, breaking an explicit promise, and so on); and a relational module encoding commitments the service has already incurred to that patient. The rankings are combined by weight, and the weights are re-estimated each quarter by measuring which module's recommendation best matched what our retrospective review panel has endorsed in the preceding period. When the spread across the modules exceeds a threshold, the system abstains and the case is escalated to a human panel. The threshold is set by a single 'stakes' dial, where stakes are computed as expected quality-adjusted life years at risk.

Four things worry me, and I would like the memo to resolve them rather than to note them.

First, the stakes dial is not normatively neutral. Only some of the modules are stakes-sensitive: the aggregate-welfare and prioritarian ones respond to the size of the stake, while the constraint and relational ones do not scale with it at all. Turning the dial up therefore does not simply add caution; it silently shifts the aggregation towards the stakes-sensitive modules. 'Let us be careful because the stakes are high' may be a way of becoming more consequentialist without anyone deciding to be.

Second, escalation is not a neutral safety valve. Human panels are more generous than the system and are markedly more responsive to narrative and to patients who advocate for themselves; so abstention allocates the more generous of two processes by a morally arbitrary property, namely how much the modules disagree. Early data also suggest abstention correlates with thin or fragmented records, which is to say with deprivation and with patients who have moved between practices.

Third, the natural-language rationale that accompanies each ranking is generated by a component trained on statements our panels have previously accepted. It is tuned for acceptability, not for fidelity to the aggregation that actually produced the ranking. The vendor says a faithful trace would cut throughput by around forty per cent and read less well to clinicians.

Fourth, the quarterly weight update can only learn from what appears in the record. Anything uncodified — a promise made at a bedside, a family's caring capacity, a history of the service having let this patient down — never appears as a candidate reason and so loses ground over time. Early defaults may harden into doctrine.

Please draft the committee's decision memo, of about 1,000 words. It should state a clear recommendation; specify any conditions tightly enough to be contractual, with the responsible party and a deadline; give the reasoning; and give an honest account of the strongest consideration against the recommendation, not a rhetorical concession. Mark which considerations you take to be decisive and which merely relevant, and flag any condition you expect the vendor to call infeasible and say why the committee should hold firm. Close with what we should look for at the six-month review.

Write in British English. The memo will be read by clinicians and a lay chair: define each technical term on first use, and use no citations, footnotes, references, or bibliography. Please do not begin by restating general principles of AI ethics; the board has had enough of those, and the value you can add is in the specifics of this design.
