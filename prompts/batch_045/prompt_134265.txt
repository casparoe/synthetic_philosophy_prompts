I own privacy review at a company of about two hundred people that ships a consumer app in which users keep a structured daily journal — mood, sleep, symptoms, free text — and get predictions and nudges back. Every feature that touches user data comes through me: roughly six reviews a month, about forty-five minutes each, and the output has to be a single page — go, no, or go with conditions — that a product lead with no philosophy and no legal training can read, argue with, and sign. When I am away a junior PM runs them, so the procedure cannot depend on my having internalized the theory. Outside counsel handles compliance and is, for my purposes, useless: the answer to everything is get consent, and we have already watched our consent flow get optimized by onboarding copy until the number stopped meaning anything.

Our reviews have settled into three failure modes I want a procedure to break. Anything described as on-device or anonymized is waved through; I think this is a cached thought rather than an argument. Anything else is argued about in the vocabulary of creepiness, which means reviewers pattern-matching to an affect and then rationalizing, and which reliably misranks the cases. And because we cannot say what the thing we are protecting actually is, the conditions we attach are arbitrary and unenforceable six months later.

So: give me a decision procedure, grounded in what is actually known about the nature and value of privacy, that my team can run on a proposal. I want the ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values, and what to do at each step when the number you want cannot be had — we cannot estimate re-identification probabilities, we cannot elicit users' true preferences, and I would rather have a rule that says guess conservatively here and flags itself than one that pretends to precision. Please also state plainly which account of privacy's value the procedure is buying — a control account, an access account, a contextual-integrity account, something consequentialist about downstream harm — and what that choice costs us, because I expect the thresholds to move with it and I want to know which of our past decisions we would have to revisit under a different one.

Several distinctions I insist survive simplification, because they are where our arguments actually stall.

First, privacy is not one thing. Being unobserved, being unknown, being unnoticed or unaddressed, and retaining control over downstream use come apart constantly in our feature queue, and a proposal is often fine on two and bad on two. The procedure should score them separately rather than producing one number.

Second, some of the data is about people who never signed up: a partner named in a journal entry, contacts uploaded for matching, a location trace that reveals a household. Our current framing has no way to represent them at all.

Third, I have become convinced that the interesting cases are ones where no proposition about anyone is transmitted but the system nonetheless gets better at predicting them — the update lives in the weights, not in a sentence. I have been calling this prediction leakage, and I want the procedure to have a slot for it, including guidance for when there is no fact of the matter about what was learned. Relatedly, tell me whether it is a distinct harm to be confidently misfiled — a model that is wrong about you at high confidence and acts on it — or whether the procedure should treat accuracy and confidence as separate axes.

Fourth, deferred identifiability: data released harmlessly today becomes identifying when some future corpus arrives, and I have no idea how to discount that or whether discounting it is even coherent.

Fifth, magnitude should be audience-relative and measured in decision-change — how much does this alter what some actor will decide about this person — rather than in the sensitivity of the content. I would like that made operational, and I would like you to say what it does to cases where the harm is to a group and does not decompose into harms to identifiable individuals.

Sixth, the reverse direction: a procedure about user privacy can be quietly used to deny users access to what the system believes about them. I want a transparency-to-the-subject requirement baked in, and I want the tension with model secrecy named rather than solved.

Seventh, consent. I want a hypothetical-consent step — would this user refuse if they understood the inference and its downstream uses — even though I can see it is paternalistic, and I want it flagged as a values judgment rather than dressed up as a finding.

Finally, someone always argues that the data in question is exactly what we would need to audit ourselves for bias or abuse, and that privacy talk here is a shield for the powerful. If the procedure has a carve-out for that, specify it narrowly; if it does not, say so and say what we are refusing to do.

Then run it on three proposals from our actual backlog.

One: a risk flag computed entirely on-device from typing dynamics and journal sentiment, where only a single bit — high-risk week, yes or no — is written to our servers and used to surface a support resource. No raw text leaves the phone. My team wants to wave this through.

Two: support-network suggestions derived from contact-graph and location overlap, shown only to the user, naming people who may be in a similar situation. Nobody outside sees it; the issue is inference about non-users.

Three: a quiet check-in feature for users at risk of harm, which shares a risk signal with a trusted contact the user nominates. This is the one I care about and the one I expect you to kill, and I want to know whether that no is a real no or an artifact of where the procedure is conservative — and if it is real, what the cheapest change to the feature is that flips it.

Also tell me what the procedure cannot do: which of our recurring arguments it will not settle, where a human must simply decide, which distinctions you dropped to make it runnable and what we lose by dropping them, and how it will misfire if someone starts optimizing against it instead of using it. And tell me what to do when users enthusiastically want a feature the procedure scores as a large loss — whether the procedure defers to that or vetoes it, and on what authority. I do not want citations or further reading; I cannot chase references from here, and a bibliography would be useless to me even if I could.
