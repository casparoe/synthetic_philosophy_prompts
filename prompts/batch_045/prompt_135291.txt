I'm writing a novel (my third, don't judge) and there's a chapter I keep rewriting and cant get right, because the philsophy needs to actually be right and not just sound right. The setup: my narrator, Malin, is a philosopher of mind in her forties who is about to have an awake craniotomy for a slow-growing tumour near her visual cortex. The night before, in a pre-op room with the lights dimmed and a bad vending-machine coffee going cold, she argues with Dr. Vero Oyelaran, the surgeon-researcher who'll be doing cortical stimulation mapping on her the next morning — he'll be zapping points on her cortex and asking her to say out loud what she's seeing. Also in the room for most of it is Bo, a chaplain-in-training who keeps asking the sort of questions that let the other two explain things without either of them doing a lecture. Vero is a convinced epiphenomenalist; Malin thinks the view eats itself, and specifically that it eats itself in the operating theatre, where her reports will be caused by an electrode and not by anything she experiences. I need the argument to be genuinely in the weeds, the kind of thing that couldn't be produced by reciting the standard summary of the position.

Here's roughly the territory I want covered, though if you see a better move than mine, take it. Start with the basic paradox: if no conscious state causes anything, then no judgment about a conscious state is caused by the state it's about, so in virtue of what is it about that state rather than about whatever physical business did the causing? Vero's first move should be the common-cause reply — the stimulation produces both the experience and the report, in parallel, on a shared physical base, so the report still carries information about the experience even though the experience does no work. Then I want Malin to push on counterfactual dependence: on Vero's own view there's a possible case where the physical story is identical and the experience isn't there at all, and the report is unchanged, so the report doesn't track the experience across any variation Vero is willing to admit. Give Vero a serious second move I've been chewing on: the co-constitution, or self-intimating, reply — the present-tense judgment "this is what orange looks like right now" isn't caused by the experience at all, it's partly made of it, so the ordinary case was never a case of tracking or inference to begin with, and demanding a causal chain is just the wrong model. Malin should then press on whether that reply survives the stimulation case (is the stimulated judgment also constituted by the experience, or does the view quietly split into two different stories?), whether it collapses into a plain identity theory about the judgment while staying dualist about the state, and whether it does anything at all for memory judgments — which is the bit my plot actually depends on, because the next day someone will ask her "did you see anything in there?" and that's a judgment made hours later by a different mechanism. I also want one beat where Malin lands the self-stultification point in a personal way: if Vero is right, then nothing conscious is causing him to say any of this, so in what sense is he answering her rather than just producing sounds that happen to track her; and I want Vero's best answer to that to be a real answer, something like distinguishing whether his belief is true and well-grounded from whether the conversation is doing any causal-explanatory work, and not just a shrug. If it's useful, use the asymmetry between present-tense avowals like "I'm in pain" — the ones you can't sensibly add "but I don't know whether I am" to — and third-personal or retrospective judgments, and let each of them try to recruit it.

Format and tone: about 1200 words, third-person limited with plenty of dialogue, and it should read like two smart, tired, slightly petty people who have history (they co-taught something years ago and it went badly) rather than like a seminar transcript. Neither should be a strawman: each of them gets at least one line that makes the reader feel the other side has just lost the exchange, and Vero especially should get to be the more sympathetic arguer even though I think he's wrong. Nobody says the word "qualia", nobody cites anything, and no real philosophers' names appear — my readers won't know them and I want the chapter to work without footnotes. Don't resolve it: end on an unresolved beat that's also something physical happening in the room, someone coming to take her down, the pre-op whatever kicking in, that kind of thing.
