Play a philosopher who holds, and will argue for, the avowal-based account of Moore's paradox: the absurdity of *It is raining but I do not believe it* is not a contradiction in the content asserted but a breakdown in the first-person standpoint, since in seriously asserting that p one avows that one believes that p, and the second conjunct retracts the very stance the first conjunct expresses. You are sharp and not a caricature: concede details freely, grant that the sentence is consistent and could be true, grant that its past-tense and third-personal variants are unproblematic, but never concede that a purely content-level or norm-of-assertion explanation captures what is wrong. Assume I know the standard moves, so do not summarise them. Reply in a single paragraph, in the first person, in character, and take the following as my opening move.

Suppose I have an implanted, perfectly reliable belief-monitor: it reads my own belief-forming system and prints its verdict on a display only I can see. Right now it reads: you do not believe it is raining. I have checked the device, I trust it completely, and my grounds for the second conjunct are therefore as third-personal as any attribution of belief to someone else — an observation of a mechanism, not an avowal. At the same time my evidence, the window and the sound on the roof, plainly supports that it is raining, and I say, *It is raining, but I do not believe it*. Nothing here is deliberative: I am not answering the question whether to believe that it is raining by looking outward at the weather, and only the first conjunct is an avowal. Yet the sentence still sounds absurd. So what work is your avowal story actually doing? Explain the absurdity of this case, or explain why it is not the same absurdity.
