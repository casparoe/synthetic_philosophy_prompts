I should be grateful for a careful reading of Kazuo Ishiguro's *The Remains of the Day* through the lens of Hannah Arendt's account of the banality of evil — and, more precisely, through the particular version of that account I spent last evening defending, with diminishing success, against a friend.

The disagreement was about what the thesis actually says. My friend took it to be a claim about the ordinariness of Eichmann's motives: no malice, no demonic depth, merely careerism and diligence and a talent for logistics, and the lesson is that mass murder is done by people with the ambitions of a middle manager. I take that to be a misreading. On the view I want, the thesis is about a failure of thought: an absence of the inner dialogue with oneself, a dependence on stock phrases and official vocabulary, an incapacity to take up the standpoint of another person — and this can come apart from ordinariness of motive, since a convinced ideologue might be banal in Arendt's sense and a mere careerist might think perfectly well. My friend then produced what he took to be a refutation: that later scholarship, working partly from things Eichmann said and recorded while in exile in Argentina, portrays him not as a hollow functionary but as an enthusiastic ideologue, and that there is even Nuremberg testimony recording his satisfaction at the numbers he had on his conscience. His conclusion was that the banality thesis is dead. My reply was that this refutes the thesis he and I had been arguing about, not the one I want — but I could not talk him out of the thought that a man who exults in his victims is not banal in any sense worth caring about, and I do not think I made the distinction vivid enough.

Here the novel seems to me a useful test case, and not only because Stevens is, on the face of it, my friend's kind of example. I have in mind the following material: Stevens's devotion to an ideal of professional dignity and his definition of it as a matter of not being stripped of one's role even under provocation; Lord Darlington's pro-German politics in the 1930s and the antisemitism that comes with them; the dismissal of the two Jewish housemaids at Darlington's insistence, which Stevens implements and which he cannot, later, bring himself to describe as what it was; the death of his father upstairs while he serves the gentlemen below; the West Country holiday, the villagers who mistake him for a gentleman and press him about politics, and the local man who insists that dignity in a democracy means being free to speak one's mind; the occasions on which Miss Kenton finds him alone and turns away her sympathy; and the closing scene on the pier, with the lights coming on and the question of what his service was worth.

Three things I should like you to do. First, say which of the two readings the novel actually fits, and where it resists either — whether Stevens is better described as a man with ordinary ambitions or as a man who has stopped thinking, and whether the text gives us reason to prefer one description. Second, consider a wrinkle I raised and could not develop: that in Arendt the failure of thought looks like a vacancy, whereas in Stevens it looks like an achievement, a structure of unknowing maintained with real skill and at the cost of real effort, organised around a role and an ideal that pre-empt judgement. His narrating voice is almost entirely circumlocution, yet it is rhetorically accomplished; and that, I think, puts pressure on the idea that clichéd speech is a reliable symptom of thoughtlessness. Is this a correction to Arendt, a friendly amendment, or simply a confusion on my part between failing to think and thinking about the wrong things? Third, tell me where a reading of this kind overreaches: Stevens's wrongdoing is complicity by omission and self-deception, not participation in administrative mass murder, and I am unsure whether extending the banality thesis to cover such a case illuminates it or merely stretches a term that was doing specific work in the context of the camps and the trial. If it is overreaching, I would rather be told now, before I commit the framing to an essay that is meant to begin from the novel and end with the philosophy.

Finally, and this is the part I care about most: what does the novel contribute to the philosophical argument that the argument alone lacks? I have in mind the form of it — the first-person narration whose reliability we are meant to doubt, and the way a life organised around a role can be narrated by its occupant with intelligence and even at points with honesty. If there is something about shame, or about the phenomenology of a service ideal, or about how self-deception feels from the inside, that the philosophical literature on thoughtlessness cannot get at, I would like to know what it is and where in the book I should point a reader to find it.
