The context: I spent three hours last night arguing with a friend — a moral philosopher who works on the history of utilitarianism — about whether Bentham's principles have any sexual ethics in them at all. Her claim, which she defended with some energy, was that Bentham's treatment of sexual matters is essentially a demolition project: he strips away the appeal to religious prohibition, to antipathy and disgust, to "unnaturalness," to the offence given to spectators who merely disapprove, and to the supposed corruption of character, and what is left over is a permission slip. On her reading, once you have subtracted everything Bentham himself regarded as a bogus ground for punishment, no residue of mischief remains, and Benthamite sexual ethics collapses into "anything goes between competent adults," with the only genuine prohibitions (coercion, deceit that matters, harm to dependants) being ones that are not about sex at all. I disagreed. My claim was that there is a principled Benthamite line, but that it is invisible in his own day because nearly all the mischief attached to sexual irregularity in his day was extrinsic — a product of bad laws and of educated antipathies — and that you only see the line once you strip that extrinsic mischief away and look at what survives.

So I want you to adjudicate, using a scoring device I proposed (and which my friend attacked as question-begging, so you should tell me whether it is). Score each case on a 0–100 scale of total Benthamite mischief, but score it three times, in three columns:

Column A — the mischief as things actually stand, in an unreformed society of Bentham's own time, with existing laws and existing antipathies in place.
Column B — the mischief that would remain if the law had already been reformed along Benthamite lines, so that no legal punishment, prosecution, or threat of exposure-to-the-law attaches to the act.
Column C — the residual mischief that would remain if, in addition, the antipathies were dissolved: that is, if nobody were pained by the bare thought or report of the act, though people could still be pained by being made to witness it against their will, by being deceived, by being coerced, or by the frustration of expectations they reasonably hold.

Score every case on the stipulation that the act becomes fully known to everyone who has any stake in it (so no mischief is hidden by secrecy alone), but that no legal sanction follows. Rank the cases by Column C, since that is the column my argument depends on. Here they are:

H. An act in which one party's pleasure depends essentially on the other's being deceived about who they are; the deceived party would refuse if undeceived; the deception is never discovered and the deceived party reports the encounter as pleasant.
