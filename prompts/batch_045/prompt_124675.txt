Give me one concrete example of a case where the polite, indirect move is clearly the *worse* move than just saying the thing out loud — and worse for a reason that isn't just "lying is lying, so don't." I've been going back and forth on whether white lies are actually wrong and every discussion circles the same two stock cases (the ugly gift, the terminal diagnosis), so no medical stuff and no dinner parties.

Here's the shape I want, and the bits I keep getting stuck on:

- The bad news is something the hearer already half-knows or could check in five minutes, so the evasion isn't buying them ignorance. What *is* it buying, and for whom?
- The hearer is good at reading indirection — they decode the hedge. So I want you to be careful about what they end up believing once they have: do they now believe the false proposition less, do they believe something extra about how the speaker regards them, and does the indirection itself become part of the message?
- There's a third person in the room with the opposite directness norm (think the blunt-Dutch / indirect-Japanese contrast, or any pairing you like). Being direct to the hearer is then rude to the bystander, and being indirect to spare the bystander is condescending to the hearer. Whose norm is supposed to govern, and what's the argument?
- Don't let the example work by the lie getting found out later and causing a fuss. I want the indirect version to be worse *right there in the moment*, even if nobody ever mentions it again.

Then in a few lines: does your example suggest the white lie was never really about the hearers feelings at all, that its real job is deniability or comfort for the speaker? And if it does, does that spread to the ordinary small cases, or is mine just a degenerate setup?
