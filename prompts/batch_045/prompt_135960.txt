I am writing a novel, most of which takes place inside the legislature of a fictional country whose assembly is chosen by lot, and I want the constitutional logic of that assembly to be defensible rather than merely atmospheric: several chapters turn on points of procedure, and I would like the arguments my characters make to survive a reader who knows this material. Before I fix the design, I would like an audit of the commitments it embodies. I set them out below roughly as I hold them. I do not know where the trouble is, and I am not especially attached to any one of them.

1. The lot is the only procedure that is fair to citizens as equals, because it gives each of them the same prospect of exercising power at some point in their lives. Elections are not merely imperfect instruments but unfair in kind: they distribute office according to other people's judgments, and those judgments are shaped by advantages that are themselves unequally distributed. I hold this independently of outcomes — if it turned out that allotted assemblies legislated worse than elected ones, I would still prefer sortition.

2. The assembly should be a cross-section of the population: a mirror of the cleavages that actually shape political judgment — occupation and income, region, language, race, gender, age. The rationale is not symbolism. Which interests get voiced, and which considerations any proposal has to answer, depends on who is in the room.

3. Selection should therefore be stratified: quotas fixed in advance for the relevant categories, with random selection within each.

4. Service must be voluntary. Conscription into office strikes me as incompatible with the equal citizenship that sortition is supposed to realise, so anyone drawn may decline, at no cost and without having to give a reason.

5. The case for the assembly is also partly epistemic, not only a case about fairness: a diverse cross-section deliberating under the right conditions reaches better decisions than an elected chamber. And I want the assembly to hold final legislative authority, not an advisory role.

6. Three hundred members, one three-year term, no possibility of a second. The real work is done in twelve standing committees of nine, drawn by lot from the whole house, which set the agenda and draft everything that reaches the floor. Inside the assembly there are no parties and no caucusing outside the chamber; the bar on re-eligibility is meant to make campaigning for a second term pointless, and with it the faction-building I take to be the main defect of elected legislatures.

7. I want to say that this arrangement is more democratic than an elected legislature — not that it is something else entirely that happens on the whole to work better, but that "democratic" has the same sense in both cases and that sortition scores higher on it.

There is also a case I need to be able to write. In one chapter the assembly enacts a measure that a referendum would have rejected by a clear margin, and a character defends it as legitimate. That defence has to be available to me. But I notice it may pull against (2): if the point of mirroring is that the people's interests are structurally present in the room, what is the standing of a decision the people themselves would have voted down? I cannot tell whether this is a real conflict or whether I have conflated two different things that mirroring might be for.

What I want is an audit, not an exposition. Go through the set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, ranked by severity rather than listed as equals. For each real tension, say what would have to give, what the cheapest repair is, and what that repair costs me elsewhere in the set. Tell me too which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection and why — I suspect at least one of my worries is spurious, but I cannot locate it. Finally, name the single revision that would clean up the most, and say whether any of the repairs would cost me a scene I need: the reluctant member who has to be dragged into the chamber, the committee of nine that quietly settles the fate of a region, and the floor fight that no party whip can bring to a close are all load-bearing.
