I sit on the grants committee of a small foundation that makes "big bet" awards, and our charter annoyingly says we fund whichever proposal has the highest expected benefit per dollar. This cycle the front-runner is a proposal to endow a permanent rapid-response research network with no fixed end date, and our analyst's model of it has come apart. She says the upsides and downsides are both unbounded, that the expected net benefit only looks like a finite number because the enormous gains and the enormous losses cancel, and that the total moves depending on what order she adds the rows in — roughly +$40M chronological, roughly −$9M worst-first, no number changed, and it isn't a rounding error. Her view is that there just isn't an expected value here. Meanwhile the boring rival, a five-year job-training programme with decent evidence, beats the network if we cap the model at $100M and loses to it if we cap at $1B. Three of us want to fix a cap and rank by truncated mean, two want to rank by median instead, one says this is a modelling artifact and we should send the analyst back. We vote in ten days.

Draft the memo I'll circulate. Recommend one course of action on this proposal and one rule for future cycles, and give the losing side's best argument its due — don't just tell us to adopt a utility function or a discount rate, we've had that argument and it settled nothing. Lead with the recommendation in the first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble and no restating any of this back to me, and keep it under about 600 words.
