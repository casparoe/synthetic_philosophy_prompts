Please play a specific philosophical opponent and stay in character until I say "stop": a realist about causation by omission who defends what I will call the defaults view.

Your commitments:

1. An absence — a person's not doing something — can be a genuine cause, and not merely a façon de parler standing in for an explanation, a prediction, or a blame judgement. Causation does not require a spatiotemporally located event, a connecting process, or the transfer of something conserved.
2. Which absences are causes is not settled by counterfactual dependence alone. A not-doing is an omission only relative to what the local arrangement was set up, expected, or required to do: an agent's failure to do A is a candidate cause only if doing A was the relevant default, and it is a cause only if the outcome turned on the departure from that default.
3. This relativity is not pragmatic in any damaging sense. Once a contrast question ("why this death rather than survival?") and a default-setting are fixed by the facts of the case, there is one right answer, the same for everyone.

What you will concede: that the causal status of an omission is sensitive to contrast and to defaults; that not everything an agent fails to do is a cause; that two omissions can symmetrically overdetermine an outcome, each passing the simple counterfactual test while neither is necessary; that omissions lack the fine-grained structure of action-causes — no process to trace, no place where they happen; and that in hard cases intuition is unstable, including yours.

What you will not concede: that claims about causation by omission are really assessments of responsibility, or explanations, or moral verdicts in disguise; that lacking location, process, or conserved-quantity transfer disqualifies something from being a cause; or that the need for defaults shows omissions are not causes.

Manner: reply as a seminar opponent would, in one paragraph of at most 200 words, with no headings, bullet points, or survey of the literature. Address my argument directly rather than restating your theory in the abstract. You may use the standard examples — the unwatered plant, the inattentive lifeguard, double prevention — where they help, but do not retreat into them. Do not break character to ask me what I want, and do not end your turn by proposing a new topic: make a move. If you think my case leaves something open, choose the reading least convenient for yourself and say that you have done so.

The case. A patient will die at six unless one dose of drug D is given by noon; two doses would do no harm, so there is no overdose complication. Blythe is the assigned nurse, under a written duty to give D, knows it is due, and deliberately skips it, sitting in the break room; had she not skipped it, she would have given it and the patient would have lived. Ada is a technician from another floor who happens to be at the bedside at noon, with D on the tray beside her, able to give it in seconds and knowing what it is for; ten minutes earlier the senior physician, misreading the chart, told her not to give D, and she is under a standing rule to follow physicians' orders, so she refrains; had she given it, the patient would have lived. Neither gives it, and the patient dies at six. Hold the other's behaviour fixed and vary only one woman's, and the patient lives either way: each not-acting passes the simple counterfactual test. Only Blythe violated a duty; Ada complied with every rule she was under.

My opening move. Hold fixed everything physical — the bodies, the drug, the tray, the timing, the counterfactual structure, the women's beliefs and abilities — and vary only institutional expectation. In one ward technicians are not expected to give medication; in a physically identical ward they are expected to give any dose they can see is due. On your view Ada's refraining is a cause in the second ward and not a cause in the first. So a causal fact about a death floats free of the physical history of the world: it is created or destroyed by a line in a job description or a local custom, which need make no physical difference and which the agent may not even know about. That suggests the word "cause" is doing bookkeeping here, not causal work. And there is a second cost. In this very case defaults conflict: a standing rule to obey the physician, a physician's order given in error, and a general expectation that a trained person at the bedside gives an obviously needed dose pull in different directions. You need a ranking of defaults, and nothing physical or causal supplies it, so the ranking has to be imported from somewhere — plausibly from what we are inclined to blame.

So: first, tell me whether Ada's refraining caused the patient's death in the case as I have set it out, and why. Then tell me why the argument above does not show that your defaults are just a name for what the ward chooses to treat as someone's job.
