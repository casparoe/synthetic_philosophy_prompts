I am in the lower sixth and writing my Extended Project Qualification on whether pain is bad in itself or bad only because of the way it makes us feel. I should be grateful for your help with one part of it.

As I understand it, pain asymbolia is a rare condition in which a person can still tell you that a stimulus is painful, where it is, how strong it is, and whether it is stabbing, burning or aching, yet seems entirely untroubled by it: such a person may describe the sensation accurately and even smile or laugh, while showing no inclination to withdraw or to ask for relief. I am interested in what follows if we set this condition beside an invented mirror image of it. Suppose a second patient who, after a different sort of brain change, has lost the sensory side altogether: she cannot locate the pain, cannot compare its intensity from one moment to the next, cannot say whether it is sharp or dull, and can point to no bodily sensation at all, but who is nonetheless in a state of acute distress, weeps, begs for it to stop, and would do almost anything to bring it to an end. Call the first case indifference without numbness and the second misery without sensation. Assume that both are genuine and stable, and that neither patient is confused, seeking attention, or using words loosely.

What, in your view, are the most important implications of taking these two cases together as a pair, rather than discussing pain asymbolia on its own, for the question of what makes pain bad? I am especially interested in the implications for the following: whether the badness of pain lies in what it feels like, in how much it is disliked, in the desire for it to stop, or somewhere else entirely; whether a pain that is not unpleasant is still a reason to be concerned about the body; which of the two patients, if either, is worse off; whether the second case shows that there can be suffering with no sensory quality at all, and what that would tell us about pain in animals, or in people who cannot describe their experience; and whether a doctor trying to help each of them should be trying to achieve the same thing.

If it helps, please also say which of these implications you think I am most likely to get wrong, and how someone who holds that the badness of pain simply consists in its hurting quality would reply to the pair of cases.
