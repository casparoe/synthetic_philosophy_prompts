I'm a journalist — I do a column on science and ideas — and I've been sitting on a Fermi paradox piece for about eight months that I keep not filing, because I have a feeling my own position doesn't quite hold together and I can't find the seam. Would you audit it before I commit it to print?

Where I think I've landed:

1. There is a real puzzle. I don't buy any of the deflationary moves: we've only been listening for a few decades, space is huge, the odds of intelligence could simply be low, we wouldn't recognise their signals anyway. As I understand it, a civilisation even slightly ahead of us — even at a small fraction of light speed, or just rearranging stars for usable energy — would be obvious galaxy-wide by now, and the galaxy has had billions of years to produce one. So the silence is evidence: absence of a signal, given everything else we think we know, is informative rather than just a gap.

2. I reject every version of the story where they are already around and deliberately keeping quiet — zoo, quarantine, we're a nature reserve, we're not ready yet. Unfalsifiable, and suspiciously well adapted to surviving any counterevidence.

3. My favourite explanation is that technological civilisations reliably wreck themselves not long after they get industrial-scale energy: war, engineered pathogens, runaway tech, ecological collapse, take your pick. The filter is ahead of us or right about at us, not behind us.

4. And that's why I want to write the piece as a piece about us rather than about them. The silence has policy bite: if the filter is ahead, then reducing existential risk is what the evidence actually points at, and that's the argument I want the column to make.

5. I think deliberate broadcasting is fine and the don't-shout crowd is being paranoid. The galaxy looks empty; and anything that could come and hurt us wouldn't need an invitation, since it would already know we're here from the composition of our atmosphere or our city lights.

6. I also want to say in the piece — and I half believe it — that the likeliest way this actually resolves isn't a clean narrowband signal but something already sitting on a hard drive: an archival all-sky survey, a weird stellar light curve nobody flagged, some object in our own solar system nobody looked at twice. The evidence may already have been collected and simply not noticed.

7. Anthropic and observation-selection reasoning does nothing for me. Arguments of the form we should assume we're typical observers and then the puzzle evaporates definately feel like changing the subject.

8. Two odds and ends. I'm happy to predict that any civilisation which survives long enough ends up filling its galaxy, while also thinking that billionaire multiplanetary talk is mostly ideology and has nothing to do with the paradox — that pairing is the bit I'm most suspicious of in my own draft. And I want nothing to do with ufology, which I think is a journalistic sinkhole, even though I also think the extraordinary-evidence standard gets used as a lazy way to wave off inconvenient results.

So: does this hang together? Please don't just tell me everything is in tension with everything. I need the real conflicts ranked by how badly they hurt, and for each one, what specifically I'd have to give up and what it costs me in terms of the argument I'm trying to make to a general reader who will notice hand-waving. If something only looks inconsistent, say that too and say why. My instinct about which commitment is load-bearing has been wrong before, so don't take my own suspicions in (8) as a hint. And if there's a single revision that cleans up the most at once, tell me which one it is.
