Last November I lost a race for our city council to a self-funded opponent who spent roughly eight times what our campaign did, nearly all of it in the final three weeks on mailers and digital advertising we had no way to answer. Since January I have been one of five people on a new municipal commission asked to draft campaign-finance and lobbying rules for the city, and I have discovered that I cannot state clearly what was supposed to be wrong with what happened to me. A friend, who is sympathetic but a much better arguer than I am, has given me an argument that I think ends somewhere neither of us wants to go, and I cannot locate the step that breaks.

He asked me first to state the principle I was relying on, and then reconstructed my position as steps 1 to 3 and attacked it from step 4 on:

1. A political decision is defective to the extent that the official making it is responsive to something other than the reasons there are for it and the interests of the people it affects. Call this the responsiveness principle. It seems to me to be the only thing that distinguishes taking a bribe from reading a well-argued letter.
2. Money in politics is the paradigm case of the failure: what officials do tracks who gives and how much far more closely than it tracks the merits of the question or the distribution of voters' interests, and the interests of large donors systematically diverge from those of the electorate as a whole.
3. So money in politics is a defect, and the volume of it in our system is a serious one.
4. To make step 1 precise, separate influence that works through the content of what is communicated — arguments, evidence, testimony about what constituents need — from influence that works independently of content, by changing the official's prospects of re-election or her prospects afterwards. Call these channels persuasion and leverage. On this reading, step 1 condemns leverage and permits persuasion.
5. But the separation does not hold. Money buys polling, research, advertising and paid canvassing, which are persuasion as much as anything a union or a newspaper does; and a union phone bank, a congregation's mobilization, a party's threat to primary someone, or an endorsement that moves thousands of votes is leverage as much as a check is. Every real case mixes both channels, and mixes them in the same proportions whatever the source of the influence. So step 4 cannot be made to work.
6. So step 1 must stand in its bare form. But then it condemns everything that produces unequal responsiveness: a newspaper with a large circulation, a union with many members, a celebrity with an audience, an expert with relevant knowledge, a congregation that turns out its members. None of these is something we want to regulate, and the last two we want more of.
7. So unequal responsiveness is not itself the defect. Step 1 must be read down to this: decisions should be responsive to reasons, and influence that works by supplying reasons — better arguments, better evidence, wider audiences — is permissible however unequally it is distributed.
8. And money in our system mostly does exactly that. It pays for the polling, the studies, the advertisements and the canvassers that carry arguments to voters and to officials.
9. Therefore money in politics is not corrupting except where it is exchanged for a specific official act, the bribery case, and there is no principled basis for regulating anything else.

I cannot accept step 9. On that view a donor who funds ninety per cent of a challenger's budget, names no price, and asks for nothing in particular is doing nothing wrong; a foreign corporation buying airtime in our elections is doing nothing wrong either; and the only thing my commission should be drafting is a bribery statute. That seems to prove far too much.

I tried replacing the persuasion-versus-leverage test with a swap test: would the official have taken the same position if the money had gone to the other side instead? It did seem to separate the legislator who changes her mind after a briefing from the one who changes it after a fundraiser, but in the ordinary case it returns no verdict at all, because donors pick candidates who already agree with them, so the swap is something that never happens and I cannot say what would have followed if it had. My friend says the test merely restates the intuition I am trying to justify.

Which step do you reject? I would rather keep steps 2 and 3 than keep step 1 exactly as I stated it, if I have to choose between them, but I want to know what that choice costs me: tell me why the step you reject looked fine when I read it, and tell me whether what survives can still condemn what happened in my race without also condemning the newspaper endorsement, the union phone bank and the expert's testimony. I have to defend something to four other commissioners next month, so I need the reason and not just the verdict.
