You're a professional philosopher who works on theories of well-being. Take this slowly: brainstorm two or three candidate diagnoses of the argument before you commit to one, and be explicit about what each one costs.

A friend of mine has been going on for weeks about a view he calls tranquilism: how well someone's life goes depends only on whether they're in a state of craving (wanting, unease, wanting something to stop) or in suffering; positive things — pleasure, joy, love, achievement — have no value of their own and matter only as the quieting of craving. Last night he gave me an arguement that I think was meant to support it, and it seems to me to run off a cliff instead, but I cant locate the step. Reconstructed:

(1) Nothing makes a life go better or worse except what it's like for the person from the inside.
(2) The experiences that make a life go worse are exactly the ones the person wants to stop: pain, unease, unsatisfied desire.
(3) Wanting a state to stop is itself a craving, so a life goes worse exactly when it contains craving, and someone with no craving isn't suffering.
(4) So the only way an experience can be good is by quieting craving, and how good it is equals how much craving it removes.
(5) The phenomenology seems to back this up: relief from intense craving is among the best experiences there are, and it gets better the more intense the craving was — just what you'd expect if pleasure just is the quieting of craving.
(6) So the value of an episode of relief is an increasing function of the craving it relieves, with no ceiling.
(7) Then for any calm, craving-free life there is a better one: take that same person, induce in them an arbitrarily intense craving, and then completely relieve it. The bad is the craving; the good of the relief is, by (6), arbitrarily large.
(8) So you can improve someone's life without limit by torturing them and then handing them relief, and a life of addiction and fixes can beat the sage's tranquility. Which is mad.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather keep (1), (2) and (3) than accept (8), so don't just tell me tranquilism is false and be done with it. If the flaw is a slide between two readings of one of the steps, say which step and what the two readings are; if the argument is fine and the real choice is which premise to drop, tell me which one is cheapest to give up and what you lose by giving it up — including whether the friend has to stop saying that relief is one of the best experiences there is.
