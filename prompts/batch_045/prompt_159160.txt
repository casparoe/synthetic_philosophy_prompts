My sister works in QA at a plant that fills and freeze-dries some temperature-sensitive biologic, and we got into a huge argument about her job at dinner last night and I can't let it go. The way she describes the rules: every batch has to stay inside a narrow cold range, data loggers record the whole journey, and if there's a recorded excursion outside that range the batch gets destroyed or diverted. Crucially, you cannot get it back by testing it. The potency and purity assays come back fine, every vial looks and measures just like a batch that gets released, and the answer is still no — there's some motto over there about how you can't test quality into a batch. At the same time, the release decision for an unexcursioned batch rests entirely on tests: assays on a handful of vials, often on a surrogate marker rather than the thing anyone actually cares about, plus accelerated stress data from *other* batches extrapolated forward. And two pallets from the same batch, one that rode near the truck door and one from the middle, can get opposite fates even though nothing anyone can measure distinguishes them now.

Then three wrinkles came up later in the argument and this is where I properly lost the thread. (1) Some firms now use what they call a stability budget: excursions get converted into a cumulative dose-like number with a threshold, so a batch with three small ones is treated exactly like a batch with one medium one, and the number is computed from a model rather than from any measurement of the batch. (2) A rival outfit does re-challenge: after an excursion they pull samples, put them through an aggressive accelerated stress test, and release the batch if the samples survive — so there, testing *can* overrule the history verdict. (3) For one product the stress conditions in that re-challenge are themselves what cause the molecules to clump, so the test can only ever be run on a stand-in lot, never on the batch you're actually shipping; and the release assay itself is run cold in a stabilizing buffer, i.e. in conditions chosen partly so that the failure mode everyone is worried about won't show up in the tube. A friend in a metaphysics reading group muttered something about "finks and masks" when I told her this and I waved it off, but now I think it might be the exact thing.

So: what does this bundle of practices presuppose about what it even means to say a dose "would protect someone if injected"? Is that being treated as a real feature of the batch, something it can have while nothing ever happens to manifest it, or as just a summary of what we predict will happen? Does the practice have to say that a batch's history matters over and above its current measurable properties, and that a batch can have a property whose manifestation is prevented by the very test meant to detect it? Is the bundle even consistent with itself, given that it refuses to let tests overrule history in one place and lets a proxy measurement or a model stand in for the batch in another? Tell me which parts strictly presuppose a view and which merely fit one, where the whole thing is underdetermined so that several rival theories would license the same rules, and what a regulator would actually have to change if they switched to a view on which "would protect" is nothing over and above the batch's current state plus the laws, or to one on which there are no such properties at all and there's only a hazard rate and a decision about acceptable risk. Give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, no intro and no conclusion.
