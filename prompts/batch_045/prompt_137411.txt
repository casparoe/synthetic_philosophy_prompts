I'm assembling a short teaching dialogue for a graduate seminar on metanormative theory, and I'd like you to write one for me on the question of free-floating rationales: whether there can be normative reasons that exist without being anyone's reasons. The idea I want dramatized is roughly this — when we say that there is reason for the suffering to stop, or that a promise left unkept matters, it is not obvious that the reason has to be *for* somebody; maybe some reasons have, so to speak, a deleted addressee, and the two-place fact "N has reason to φ" is only the special case in which a free-standing normative fact happens to get taken up by an agent. I want the dialogue staged in an empty seminar room at the end of a long session, with two co-teachers who have just finished arguing about this in front of the students and are now continuing without an audience: Nadia Ferrante, who holds that a reason is essentially owned — that reasons are, by their nature, reasons *for* an agent, and that the impersonal "there is reason to…" is at best a way of saying that some agent has reason, or that the fact would ground a reason for anyone suitably placed — and Emil Vasa, who holds that at least some reasons float free and that ownership is an extra, contingent feature that a reason acquires when someone stands in the right relation to it. Please give them equal dialectical standing: each should be able to state the other's position in terms the other would accept before replying, and each should make at least one real concession — a case where their own account delivers a verdict they find unattractive.

They should agree explicitly on the ordinary cases at the outset, so that the disagreement is located precisely rather than manufactured: both accept that there is reason to keep a promise to a living friend, and both accept that this has something to do with her expectations, her claims, or her good. The pressure should come from the hard cases, and I'd like at least these four to be worked through rather than merely gestured at: first, the orphaned bequest — a dying friend extracts a promise to finish a translation she knows no one will ever read, and then dies with no beneficiaries, no readers, and no survivors who care, so that nothing is left of the reason except the request itself; second, fission — a person makes a promise, then splits into two successors each equally and partially continuous with her, so that the reason seems either to belong to neither or to be too large for either; third, the empty world — a world containing nothing but intense, prolonged agony and no agents at all, where Emil will want to say there is reason for the agony to cease and Nadia will want to say there is badness but no reasons; fourth, what I think of as the capture dilemma, which I'd like Nadia to press hard: any free-floating reason that shows up in someone's deliberation seems thereby to be taken up and become hers, while any that never shows up anywhere seems normatively idle, so that a free-floating reason looks like it must be either absorbed or inert and never actually free-floating in practice. If the conversation gets there naturally, I'd also like them to consider whether reasons can be *inherited* — whether a reason can pass down a chain of custody the way a debt can be assigned to a new creditor, or whether the chain simply breaks when the original holder does.

Two further threads I want woven in rather than bolted on. The first is the weighing problem: if free-floating reasons exist, do they enter the same deliberative balance as Nadia's agent-relative ones, and if they do, do they not simply swamp every personal project, and if they don't, in what sense are they reasons at all? Alongside this, Nadia should raise the shadow worry — that in any world containing agents, every free-floating reason is already shadowed by somebody's agent-relative reason to promote what matters, so the extra reasons change nothing — and Emil should say what would have to be true for a free-floating reason to come apart from its shadows in a way that makes a first-order difference. The second is whether the dispute is substantive or verbal: both should say what kind of consideration would settle it, what would count as evidence, and whether, if no possible case can separate the two accounts' first-order verdicts, they should conclude that they have been disagreeing about the word "reason" all along. Please keep it free of citations, author names, slogans and bibliographic gestures — argue from the cases and from what reasons have to do, so that a smart undergraduate with no background can follow it — keep stage directions to a minimum, aim for something readable aloud in about ten minutes, and close not with a resolution but with each of them having extracted from the other a single sharpened question they cannot yet answer.
