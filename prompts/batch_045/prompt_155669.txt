I write software for a living and read about statistics and the philosophy of science on the side, so I know the standard Bayesian machinery — priors, likelihoods, updating by conditioning — but I have no formal philosophy training, and I keep running into a distinction I cannot name. Here are the three situations I cannot keep apart.

First, my own work. I have a dataset, I plot it, I notice a kink, and then I write a model with a term that produces the kink. The model fits beautifully. I do not feel entitled to say the data confirmed the model, because I chose the model after looking at the data. In my field the remedy is mechanical: hold out data the model never saw and check it there.

Second, a case from physics. A theory is built partly to account for a fact that astronomers had known for decades — the residual, unexplained precession of Mercury's perihelion, recognised as an anomaly since 1859 and still unexplained when general relativity arrived — and that accommodation is routinely counted among the strongest evidence for the theory. The shape looks identical to my first case: the fact was sitting there first, and the theory was shaped to fit it. But nobody reaches for a held-out set, and the fit is treated as genuine support rather than as cheating.

Third, the formalism, as I understand it, says the second case should not work at all. If I already know a fact, I assign it probability one, and conditioning on something of probability one cannot move any probability. So an already-known fact cannot raise the probability of any hypothesis, however elegantly the hypothesis explains it. On that telling, case one and case two come out the same way, and both come out badly, which is the opposite of what the physicists and my own instincts say.

So my question: is there a name for what I am circling, and is it one thing or several?

Is whatever makes case one illegitimate the same thing that makes case three a problem, or are these two separate complaints that merely look alike in a formalism that only tracks probabilities?

My own hypothesis is that the variable that matters is not whether the fact was known before the theory existed, but whether the fact was in front of the theorist while the theory was being built — whether I had that data point on my screen when I chose the functional form. Someone who fixed the model first and only then looked at the data would be entitled to count it, even if the data had been sitting in a filing cabinet for fifty years. Is there an established concept for that distinction, and does it line up with whatever the philosophy literature calls the problem in case three?

A related guess, which I lack the vocabulary to state properly: perhaps what does the work is not how probable the fact was to me, but how probable it was that a theory of this kind would manage to fit it at all. My model's kink term could have fit anything, so the fit tells me little; general relativity could easily have failed to reproduce the Mercury residual, and that it did not is the news. Is that a different thing from the first distinction, or the same thing in other words?

Finally, does my train/test split capture any of this? It seems to me to measure predictive accuracy on unseen cases, which is a different virtue from being supported by a fact: a model can predict well while being wrong about why.

I do not want references or a reading list. I want the carving: what the phenomenon is, what it is not, where its edges are, and which nearby concepts attach to which of my three cases. If part of what I am describing has no established name, say so and give me a working label with a definition, plus the boundary cases that would settle whether it applies.
