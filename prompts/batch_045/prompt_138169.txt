I'm retired — spent about thirty-five years as a protein crystallographer, and the last eight sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the university, so philosophy is a hobby rather than a job and I'd rather have reasons than a literature. This term we've spent most of our sessions on natural kinds, and I've come away with thesame feeling I used to get at conferences where the same three people made the same three arguments for twenty years. I want a diagnosis of the standoff, not a verdict on who's right.

Here's the shape of it as I see it. One side says the sciences really do carve nature at its joints: the chemical elements, the elementary particles, maybe water, where there's an underlying structure that explains all the surface behaviour, so induction over the kind isn't luck. Another side says look at how few of our working classifications are like that — species, diseases, personality disorders — the boundaries shift when our interests shift, and what looked like a joint was a line we drew. And then there's a middle view I've become oddly fond of, where a kind is just a cluster of properties held together by some causal mechanism, so the kind is real and induction over it works without there being any single property that every member has to have.

My own hypothesis, and I want it treated as a hypothesis and knocked around: this is a verbal dispute wearing the costume of a substantive one, because the camps are using "kind" to pick out different things — a class that licenses induction, a class with a shared hidden structure, a class with an essense, a lineage — and once you say which one you mean, half the arguments on both sides stop engaging. The analogy I keep coming back to is from my own lab: when two purification protocols kept giving different products and people argued for years about which was right, it usually turned out they were isolating different things and the argument was about reference, not about chemistry.

But I don't want to just assume that, and there's a case from my own field that makes me doubt it. We grouped proteins into families by sequence and fold. The groupings were enormously useful and predictive — you could infer function across a family — and yet every few years someone redrew the boundaries, and nobody thought the old boundaries had been wrong, just less useful for the question then being asked. That looks like successful induction over something with no fixed edges at all. And here's the case that really bothers me: in astronomy every element heavier than helium counts as a metal, so oxygen is a metal. Both classifications support plenty of induction. So does the question "is metal a natural kind?" have an answer independent of which science is asking it — and if the answer is "it depends on the science," hasn't the antirealist already been conceded half of what they wanted?

So: is this a verbal disagreement, a substantive one, several different questions sharing a name, or a hidden empirical question in disguise — whether nature happens to contain lots of tightly clustered property-bundles or very few, which strikes me as something you could in principle get evidence about? And given whichever diagnosis you give, what would actually count as progress: a formal result, a case that forces the distinction, giving up the single question? Or is this the sort of thing where no progress should be expected, and if so why? Please argue for your answer rather than survey the positions, and go light on the jargon.
