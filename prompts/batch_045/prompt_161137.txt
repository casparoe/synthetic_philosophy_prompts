For forty years my research consisted in measuring relaxation times in disordered materials; since retiring I have been reading my way into philosophy of science, and in particular into the old dispute about whether the higher-level sciences can be reduced to physics. Out of that reading and my own habits of thought I have assembled a position I have not found stated anywhere, and I would like to see whether it can be made to stand up.

The position turns on a distinction. Call the *counterfactual slack* of a description the breadth of the class of distinct lower-level states of affairs over which causal claims stated in that description remain true. A claim such as "the specimen's being below its critical temperature is why it carried current without resistance" has a great deal of slack: it holds however the carriers happen to be paired, however the lattice happens to be disordered, however the impurities happen to be arranged. The corresponding microphysical description, being maximally specific, has none: alter it and you have simply described a different microstate, for which the microdynamics yields a different successor. My suggestion is that counterfactual slack, rather than specificity of description, is the mark of causal relevance — and that if this is right, several things follow at once.

Please argue for the following thesis, in that spirit:

1. A microphysical cause of some event does not compete with, and does not exclude, the higher-level cause of the same event; being more specific is not being more causally relevant. The argument that reducible higher-level properties are causally drained by their realizers tacitly treats maximal specificity as a virtue, when in fact specificity is exactly what destroys the counterfactual generality that makes a causal claim explanatory, manipulable, and projectible.

2. The phenomena usually cited against reduction — the failure of higher-level kinds to be derivable from lower-level ones, their multiple realizability, the fact that wildly different micro-constitutions flow to the same macroscopic behaviour — are not evidence of irreducibility but predictions of a correct microphysics. When a macro regularisation is obtained only by taking a singular limit, the resulting higher-level theory is not deducible from the lower-level one by way of bridge principles, yet the lower-level theory is precisely what explains why a whole basin of microstates shares one counterfactual pattern and why that pattern has the finite domain of validity it has. Reduction should therefore be reconstrued as *limit-explanation*: the reductionist owes us an account of why the higher-level counterfactuals hold and where they break down, not a derivation of them.

3. Consequently, the autonomy of the special sciences is better explained by reductionism than by any rival, and the apparent conflict between "emergence" and "reduction" is an artefact of an outdated model of reduction as deduction.

In making the argument, please deal with at least two objections: first, that counterfactual slack is merely epistemic, so that a higher-level causal claim is only a disjunctive summary of micro causal claims and inherits whatever relevance it has from them, in which case the drainage intuition returns in a new costume; and second, that limit-explanation is a promissory note, since in practice we cannot carry out the limiting procedure for the systems we care about, so that the reconstrued reduction is unfalsifiable. Finally, please say plainly which step of the argument you take to be weakest and why — I would rather have the weak joint identified than have the whole thing presented as more secure than it is.
