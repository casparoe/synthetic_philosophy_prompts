I teach a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and once a year I devote a session to van Fraassen's Judy Benjamin case; once a year the discussion ends exactly where it started, and I am now writing an overview chapter in which I have to say something honest about what the state of the question actually is. I set the case up as follows. There are four cells — Blue Headquarters, Blue Second Company, Red Headquarters, Red Second Company — with prior probabilities 0.3, 0.2, 0.4, 0.1, so that the Blue/Red odds are even while Headquarters is likelier in Red territory (0.8) than in Blue (0.6). Judy is dropped, loses her bearings, and gets through on the radio; depending on which version of the handout I give out, the message is either the disjunction that she is in Blue territory or in Second Company territory, or the conditional that if she is in Red territory then she is in Second Company territory. Read materially these are true in exactly the same three cells and false only in Red Headquarters, so conditioning on the content yields the same posterior either way — 1/2, 1/3, 0, 1/6 — and her confidence that she is in Blue territory jumps from 1/2 to 5/6. Yet regularly about half the room insists that only the disjunctive version licenses that jump, and that the conditional version is news about the Red region alone and should leave P(Blue) where it was, giving 0.3, 0.2, 0, 0.5. The literature has by now produced a whole menu of rules that seem to vindicate one or the other or something in between — Jeffrey-style updating under various rigidity conditions, minimum cross-entropy or relative-information minimization, Lewis-style imaging, distance-based updating on conditionals — and as far as I can tell no argument in the field has moved anybody in decades.

My own hypothesis, which I would like tested rather than confirmed, is that the case as stated is radically underspecified and that the competing rules are not competing norms of inference at all but different silent completions of the same incomplete story. Suppose the message is produced by a relay officer with a fixed list of admissible messages and known chances of emitting each true one, and suppose Judy conditions on the message received rather than on its truth-conditional content. Then her posterior depends on the emission chances; and the uncomfortable observation a graduate student of mine made is that for any posterior whatsoever with support in the three surviving cells one can find emission chances that produce it by plain Bayesian conditioning, so that the entire published menu of answers — the 0.3/0.2/0/0.5 answer included — is reproducible by strict conditioning under some protocol. If that is right, then at the level of the single case there is nothing to choose between the rules; the real disagreement, if there is one, must concern the general policy rather than the instance — whether updates commute, whether order matters when several messages arrive in sequence, whether some rigidity or minimal-change constraint is a requirement of rationality or merely a heuristic, whether a rule that is sensitive to the form of a message can be diachronically coherent. And the standard vindications of conditioning, the Dutch-book and accuracy-domination arguments, cannot get a grip on any of this, since they presuppose that evidence arrives as a partition of propositions, which is precisely the point in dispute.

So here is what I want, and I would rather have a diagnosis with reasons than a survey of positions: is this a substantive normative disagreement about how to update, a verbal dispute conducted over the words evidence and learns, or several distinct questions sharing one name — a semantic question about what the conditional in the message means, a hidden factual question about the communicative protocol that produced the signal, and a formal question about which global desiderata an update policy can jointly satisfy? Which of these is doing the real work, and does any one of them become well posed once the others are bracketed? Take my protocol reconstruction seriously enough to say whether it dissolves the debate or merely relocates it, and tell me whether there is any principled way to select a canonical protocol, or a protocol-invariant residue, or whether the honest answer is that the admissible posteriors form a class with no distinguished member. Finally, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here — a representation theorem, an accuracy or domination result generalized to evidence that is not a proposition, an invariance or commutativity principle, an empirical result about how people actually read such messages, or an argument that the question should be abandoned as ill-posed — and if no progress should be expected, say why. I need to be able to tell my students, and my readers, whether this is a hard problem or a confused one, and I do not need citations or a reading list; assume I know the usual rules and argue with the numbers above.
