I'm a postdoc in evolutionary biology, and I've been asked to circulate a short "where I stand" statement ahead of a reading group on levels of selection. Before I commit it to a page, I want the package stress-tested. Here is roughly where I've landed:

(1) Only a faithful, long-lived replicator can accumulate the products of cumulative selection, because organisms and colonies are transient and do not persist across generations as the same entity. So in the beneficiary sense, genes are the units of selection; organisms and groups are at most vehicles or interactors.

(2) Contextual partitions and gene-level partitions are alternative bookkeeping schemes over the same total change, and when both are specified correctly they agree. So the question "does selection act at the group level, yes or no?" has no empirical content; it is a choice of description.

(3) There is nonetheless a real, empirically answerable question whether a given collective trait is instantiated only by the group — a colony's division of labour, say — and whether groups carry heritable variance in fitness at their own level. Discovering that is empirical work, not notational preference.

(4) One should choose the decomposition whose terms track the actual causal structure of the system. A partition that lumps causally heterogeneous causes together is a bad partition even when it predicts well.

(5) Case A, idealized from my own system: an obligate, strictly vertically transmitted host–endosymbiont pair in which a nutritional pathway is split across two genomes — the host carries the first two enzymatic steps, the symbiont the third, and neither genome alone yields the product. I want to say the pair is a single unit of selection, and that this is the causally faithful description rather than a convenience.

(6) Case B: the same system with occasional horizontal transmission of the symbiont, where a strain spreads by biasing host investment toward symbiont-bearing propagules at a cost to host survival. I want to say there is a genuine conflict of levels here, and that the level at which selection acts has shifted relative to Case A.

(7) The mid-century rejection of for-the-good-of-the-species explanations was a substantive empirical correction, not a change of notation. Relatedly, I take the major transitions — multicellular organisms, eusocial colonies — to involve a real historical transfer of fitness from the lower-level units to the higher-level unit, not merely a redescription of the same process.

(8) The Price equation is the right framework for all of the above; and I also think it is a bookkeeping identity that explains nothing until its terms are given a causal interpretation.
