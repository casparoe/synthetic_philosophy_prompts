On a LessWrong-adjacent comment thread last week — the usual argument about whether Gödel's incompleteness theorem has anything to say about biology — someone tossed off "but DNA is code, obviously" and then used it three times in as many paragraphs: first, that the genome is literally the organism's source code; second, that because codes are arbitrary conventions there's no fact of the matter about what a gene "means" apart from whatever the translation machinery happens to do with it; third, that the cell is the hardware interpreting the software, so a genome sitting in a test tube is like a program on a wiped drive. I've been nodding along to that sentence for years and I realized I can't say what it actually commits me to, which is a bit embarrassing given I've read GEB twice and have loud opinions about typogenetics.

So: what are the different precise claims "DNA is code" could be making, from something nobody would deny to something that would be big news if it were true? I want the menu, not your favourite item — though do tell me which readings are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, and which are straightforwardly false or confused. And the part I actually care about: for each of those three uses, which reading would have to be the true one for his argument to work, and does that reading stand up on its own, or is it only plausible-sounding because it's sitting next to the platitude version?

First, there's a version of the claim that strikes me as more interesting than any of his: the translation machinery — the tRNAs, the synthetases, the ribosome — is itself encoded in the genome, so what we've got isn't a code plus an external reader but something closer to a quine, a description that contains a specification of its own decoder. That's roughly the isomorphism with Gödel that typogenetics is gesturing at. Is that self-referential version the same claim as "DNA is code", a special case of it, or a different claim that merely sounds like it?
