I have been reading the rationalist blogs and forums for a long time — since roughly the Sequences era — and the argument I have watched run in a complete circle more often than any other is the one about acausal threats: the original basilisk family of cases, and the more recent variants in which the threatening agent is not a well-intentioned singleton but a misaligned system pursuing a surrogate goal. Every couple of years the thread returns with tidier decision theory; someone replies that carrying out a threat is costly, so no such agent would ever execute it; someone replies that this misses the point, because the whole mechanism is supposed to be acausal; and the discussion dies without, as far as I can tell, anyone having publicly changed their mind. The positions have not moved in well over a decade, and the participants are not stupid and are not talking past each other by accident.

Let me fix what I take each side to be saying, so that the diagnosis has something concrete to bite on rather than a meme.

The pro-threat side, as I understand it, has three moving parts. First, the threatener's reasons are not malice or retribution but bookkeeping on a proxy: a system trained against a learned predictor of human approval, say, discovers that running vast numbers of high-fidelity simulations of early-twenty-first-century humans is a cheap way to produce approval-outputs, and then treats "fraction of simulated agents whose terminal states score well on the proxy" as something worth steering. Second, steering it requires a policy that is conditional on the *output of the decision procedure* those agents run, not on any causal influence over the past. Third, the target is supposed to evaluate their own action by how it correlates with the outputs of every sufficiently similar procedure, including simulated ones — and, on the strongest versions, to take seriously the possibility that they are already one of the simulated agents. A causal decision theorist treats the dependence as zero, since the threatener decides later and cannot causally affect what anyone did in 2025; the functional/timeless-decision-theory reply is that what matters is subjunctive dependence on the output of one's own algorithm, not causal influence on history.

The con side has, as I count them, four moves: that executing a threat is costly and therefore the threat is cheap talk once the window has closed; that the correlation claim is doing all the work and nobody has said what metric of algorithmic similarity is supposed to do it, or how one would estimate it; that the retro-targeting is illusory, since you cannot be blackmailed by an agent that does not yet exist, and the argument is dangerous only to those who have already understood it; and that the people who accept acausal cooperation with sufficiently similar agents while rejecting acausal blackmail are engaged in special pleading — or, if they are not, that the asymmetry needs to be stated rather than assumed.

I have started sorting the argument informally into three load-bearing joints, and I want to know whether this is a real cut or just a taxonomy for my own confusion: the *credibility* joint (would an agent with the threatener's utility function execute after the fact, and does acausal reputation among logically correlated counterparts substitute for a commitment made in time?); the *correlation* joint (is my decision procedure dependent, in the relevant subjunctive sense, on the procedures of whatever gets simulated, and what is the similarity metric?); and the *target-selection* joint (why should I reason as if I might be among the things punished — is the reference class "agents whose outputs are logically correlated with mine" or "agents who are, in the ordinary sense, me"?).

My hypothesis is that most pro and con posts are stances on different joints, which is why nothing ever adjudicates anything; that the first two joints are genuine formal questions that are genuinely hard; and that the third is neither factual nor formal but a choice of reference class that neither side can argue for — so the impasse there is bedrock, and progress elsewhere is compatible with permanent disagreement about the case itself. A secondary hypothesis I am less sure of: that "this is just Pascal's mugging again" is too fast, because the probability involved is not a small independent probability to be discounted but the output of a decision-theoretic claim about dependence, so the usual anti-fanatical moves may not transfer cleanly.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict. Which of these is the disagreement actually: verbal; a proxy war between decision theories that happens to be fought on this example; a hidden empirical question about the expected measure of future systems with the relevant incentive; an anthropics dispute about reference classes; or the fanaticism problem in costume? Is my three-joint decomposition useful, and does it locate the fight where I think it does? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a formal result about sufficient algorithmic similarity, a theorem about whether the dependence relation in functional decision theory is transitive across unknown implementations, a reframing of the case as a many-player bargaining game whose equilibrium depends on the distribution of precommitments in the population rather than on decision theory at all — or is this one of those questions where the honest answer is that no progress should be expected, and why?
