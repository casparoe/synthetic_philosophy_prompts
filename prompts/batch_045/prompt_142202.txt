I'm in the final year of an undergraduate philosophy programme, and this term's seminar is on scepticism and the replies that have been made to it. My term paper argues that the transcendental replies to scepticism share a single structure, and that once that structure is made explicit it becomes clear how much work it is doing and how little of it is evidential. I stated the structure as a schema so that I could compare the cases in which it seems to me to work with the cases in which it does not, and the comparison has gone badly for me. I would be grateful for a diagnosis of the argument below rather than a survey of what has been said about this style of argument.

Call a practice indisputable when the act of denying that the practice is engaged in, or that it is ever in order, is itself an exercise of it, so that the denial cannot be performed without cancelling itself.

(1) Some practices are indisputable. The two I need are these: the practice of making perceptual judgements about how things objectively stand — ascribing properties to persisting things, checking one judgement against another, correcting them by looking again, treating disagreement as something to be settled by how things are; and the practice of giving and asking for reasons — offering considerations for what one says, criticising other people's inferences, accepting correction from them.

(2) Every practice has enabling conditions: things that must obtain for the practice to be possible at all.

(3) Where a practice P is indisputable and Q is an enabling condition of P, a denial of Q is self-undermining in the same way a denial of P is: to deny Q one must exercise P, and in exercising P one is committed to Q. So we are entitled to Q.

(4) The entitlement in (3) is neither empirical nor inferential. It does not rest on evidence for Q and cannot be undermined by the absence of evidence for Q, since it derives entirely from the structure of a practice one cannot so much as deny without employing. This is what was supposed to separate a transcendental argument from an inference to the best explanation.

(5) Applied to the first practice: an enabling condition of perceptual judgement as described in (1) is that there be mind-independent persisting objects which our judgements are answerable to and can go wrong about. So there is such a world, and the external-world sceptic, who helps herself to the vocabulary of judgement, experience and error in order to state her doubt, has conceded in the stating of it what she denies.

(6) Applied to the second: an enabling condition of giving and asking for reasons is the existence of a plurality of creatures who can give and ask for reasons, since being corrected by another, and correcting oneself in the light of another, are internal to that practice. So there are other minds, and the argument from analogy — which I have never found convincing — turns out to be unnecessary.

(7) The schema also runs on cases where I take the conclusion not to be available, and nothing in (1)–(4) seems to distinguish them from (5) and (6):

(7a) Induction. An enabling condition of the practice of forming expectations about the unobserved on the basis of the observed is that the unobserved resemble the observed. So the uniformity of nature is established from the armchair, and the problem of induction is not answered but dissolved.

(7b) Temporality. An enabling condition of the practice of deliberating and acting for reasons — of deciding what to do now given what is past and what is expected — is that the division of events into past, present and future be a feature of reality itself and not merely of the deliberator's standpoint. So a dynamical conception of time is established from the armchair, in a department where most people take that to be either false or at any rate a substantive metaphysical thesis that no armchair argument could settle.

(7c) Colour. An enabling condition of perceptual judgement as described in (1) is that the objects judged about possess at least some of the sensible qualities they must be represented as possessing, since a practice of ascribing colours to things is internal to the practice I described. So the metaphysics of colour is settled from the armchair, and empirical inquiry into how colour properties are realised gets no say about whether there are any.

(8) As far as I can tell, the difference between the applications I accept and the ones I reject is simply whether I antecedently accept the conclusion. Any claim woven tightly enough into a practice I cannot disavow can be converted by (3) and (4) into an a priori entitlement. A schema that vindicates whatever framework commitments I happen to be unable to stop using is not a reply to scepticism; it is a description of my captivity. So the schema proves too much, and I cannot see which step to give up.

What I have tried, and why I am still stuck:

One response I got was that (4) is the problem: what such arguments yield is not knowledge but something weaker, a non-evidential warrant, and that this is enough to answer the sceptic because the sceptic was only ever owed a warrant and not a proof. I do not see how this helps. It relabels the conclusion in (7b) as a framework commitment rather than an item of knowledge, but I am still landed with the dynamical theory of time as something I am warranted in holding independently of any evidence, which is the result I was trying to avoid.

My own attempt was to read (2) as a claim about what practitioners must represent rather than about what must obtain: Q is an enabling condition of P when engaging in P requires representing things as Q. But on that reading (3) delivers only that we must represent Q, which the sceptic in (5) will concede while denying Q; and the same reading turns (6) into the claim that we must represent there as being other reason-givers, which is not what I wanted either. If I retreat to the strong reading of (2), I am back with (7).

What I want to know is which step to reject and what rejecting it costs. Two constraints, so that you can see what I am trying to preserve. First, I would most like to keep (6): the argument against solipsism seems to me genuinely stronger than the argument against external-world scepticism, and I would like to be told that this is a real asymmetry rather than a prejudice — that something about the practice of giving and asking for reasons distinguishes it from the practice of perceptual judgement in a way that shows up in the schema. Second, I would rather give up (3) or (4) in full generality than accept (7b), and I would rather accept that (5) fails than accept that (5) and (7b) stand or fall together. If the honest answer is that no reading of (2) discriminates (5) and (6) from (7a)–(7c), say so, and tell me which of (5) and (6) has the better claim to survive and what the surviving argument would then be worth.

There is one further thing I noticed and cannot place. The indisputability in (1) seems at first to belong to the practice as a whole, but what is really true is only that no particular denial can be made without using the practice. It does not obviously follow that the practice cannot be described from outside in a vocabulary that is not the practitioner's — my own paper does this when it describes the practices in (1) as dispositions that creatures like us happen to have. If the practice can be surveyed from outside in that way, then the necessity claimed in (2) looks conditional on a description I can withhold, and the argument looks as though it only works for as long as I agree to stay inside. I do not know whether that observation is a refutation of the schema, a restatement of the problem in (8), or an independent mistake of my own.
