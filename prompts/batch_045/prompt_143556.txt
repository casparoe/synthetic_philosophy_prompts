My friend and I got into a four hour argument about decision theory last night and I wrote down everything I actually believe afterwards to see whether it holds together. His big move was: you pay the mugger but you stiff the blackmailer, that's just inconsistent. I couldn't tell if that was a real point or just something that sounded good at 1am. So here's the list, roughly in the order I said it:

(1) I pay in the counterfactual mugging. Perfect predictor, fair coin, coin came up tails, it tells you so and asks for $100, and it would have handed you $10,000 in the heads branch if it had predicted you'd pay. I pay.

(2) I don't pay the XOR blackmailer. You get a letter saying it was sent iff exactly one of these is true: you have termites, or you'll pay the sender $1000. Termites cost vastly more than $1000 to deal with. I don't pay.

(3) I pay the driver in the hitchhiker case. You're stranded in the desert, someone drives you into town iff they predict you'll pay them once you're safe, and then you're safe. I pay.

(4) I smoke in the smoking lesion case. There's a genetic lesion that causes both a taste for smoking and cancer, smoking itself does you no real harm. A correlation you have zero causal influence over gives you no reason to do anything. So I smoke.

(5) The reason I'd give for (1) is the same reason I one-box in Newcomb's problem: my choice right now is evidence about which world I'm in, and I want to be the sort of person whose choice makes the good news likely, the sort a predictor rewards.

(6) I don't think there's any seperate currency of "rationality". If you ask me why one decision rule beats another, my answer is always going to cash out in the money the agent actually walks away with, not in which boxes end up filled or which policies look elegant.
