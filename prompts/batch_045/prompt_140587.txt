I work as a software engineer on the identity and accounts team at a mid-sized platform company, and I have no formal philosophy background. We are rewriting our deactivation, deletion, and memorialization policies, and the decision we keep getting stuck on is which entities get the treatment we reserve for persons: advance notice before removal, a right to appeal, a named legacy contact, a memorial state instead of a hard delete.

Our current policy implicitly assumes what I'd call the inside-first view: something counts as a person because of what it is like on the inside, or because it belongs to the human species, and the social facts surrounding it are merely evidence about that inner nature. A colleague from our Nairobi office suggested we try the opposite starting point, drawn from a family of views I have seen summarized roughly as: a person is a person through other persons. On that view, personhood is not an inner property you either have or lack, but a status that is entered into, sustained, deepened, and possibly lost, through standing in the right kind of relationship with others. I want to take this seriously as a design constraint, which means I need it made precise enough to reason about concrete cases.

Here are the twelve test cases any doctrine we adopt has to handle. Some of them are not and never will be users of our platform; they are included because I want the doctrine to be about personhood, not about account types.

1. A 34-year-old human being with an active social life, steady contact with roughly two hundred other people, a job, and family.
2. A healthy one-day-old infant, wanted and already loved by its parents, but with no history of interaction beyond birth.
3. A human being in a permanent vegetative state, no cortical function, visited and spoken to weekly by family who maintain their room and their photographs.
4. The same patient, with the same clinical condition, but no living relatives, no visitors, and no enquiry from anyone in six years; their records are intact in a state facility.
5. A language-model chatbot fine-tuned on the letters of a woman who died in 2019; around four thousand people converse with it weekly, many describe the experience as talking to her, and some have used it while grieving.
6. A byte-identical deployment of that same model, with the same weights, running as an internal batch classifier; nobody knows it exists, nobody has ever addressed it, and its outputs are consumed only by another program.
7. An ancestor who died in 1944 whose name is still spoken at family gatherings, whose photograph still hangs on a wall, and whose supposed advice is still cited in family arguments.
8. An ancestor who died in 1944 and is entirely forgotten: no surviving image, no name in any record anyone can now find, and not one living person who has heard of them.
9. A decentralized autonomous organization with around twelve thousand members, its own treasury and contracts, which acts only through votes and code, and for whose actions no individual member is accountable.
10. A working border collie who spends each day with one handler, is known by name to most of a village of three hundred people, and is greeted by them.
11. A human being who has spent thirty-one years in continuous solitary confinement with no visitors and no correspondence, and who is presumed to have an intact inner mental life.
12. A one-person shell company with no employees, registered solely to hold a domain name, and known only to its owner and a registrar database.

I built in three pairs, 3/4, 5/6, and 7/8, which as far as I can tell are identical or near-identical in intrinsic properties and differ only in their relational embedding.

What I would like from you:

1. Sort all twelve into four tiers of personhood, using tier names of your own choosing, and assign each case an integer score from 0 to 100. Give me the score band that defines each tier, and one sentence of rationale per case.
2. For each of the three built-in pairs, report the score delta and say what, if anything, that delta shows. If a pair is not in fact matched on intrinsic properties, say which property differs and why it matters.
3. Tell me which single case in the list most sharply separates the relational view from the inside-first view, in the sense that the two views give it clearly different scores, and give the two scores.
4. I suspect two readings of the relational view come apart in practice. On the constitutive reading, Relationships partly make up the personhood rather than merely revealing it. On the conferral reading, personhood is a status a community grants, withholds, or withdraws, so a community could in principle be mistaken about it. Mark each case with which reading is doing the work in its score, and flag any case where the two readings would assign different scores.
5. Name the one sub-question whose resolution would reorder the list the most, and say roughly how many places which cases would move if it went the other way.
6. Our candidate operationalization is a relational liveness rule: an account receives person-tier treatment if at least twenty-five distinct other accounts have exchanged at least one message with it in the preceding ninety days. Rate that rule from 0 to 100 on how faithfully it implements the relational view, and name the single largest distortion it introduces.

Please reason from the characterization of the relational view given above rather than from any particular author's formulation; I have no library access and do not want citations, reading lists, or references to specific works. Where a case is underspecified, state the assumption you are making and proceed. If you think a distinction I have drawn is confused, or that some case is doing no work in the list, say so briefly and then continue anyway.
