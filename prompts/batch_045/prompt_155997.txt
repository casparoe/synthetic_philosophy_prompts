Hey — I'm putting together the handout for my grad seminar in formal epistemology, the week on updating on evidence that doesn't arrive with certainty. I want to give them a decision tree rather than the usual reading response: they all know plain conditionalization cold, almost none of them have read anything on uncertain evidence, and I want the thing to sort them by what they say about cases rather than by which rule name they've half-overheard. So build me the instrument.

Every branch node should be one question, phrased so that someone who has never heard of Jeffrey conditioning could answer it honestly, and every leaf should be a named position about what to do when experience shifts your credences without making anything certain. Six to eight internal questions, eight to ten leaves. Build it from the logical space — the questions and the answer combinations they allow — and then locate the actual positions in that space, and note the combinations nobody occupies with a one-line diagnosis of why (incoherent? unattractive? just unexplored?).

Please use these cases rather than the usual ones, apart from the cloth as a warm-up:

1. The cloth by candlelight, partition {green, blue, violet}, your credences go to 0.7 / 0.25 / 0.05, and the hypothesis of interest is "this is the new synthetic fiber," which is correlated with color. Then the same experience described over the coarse partition {green, not-green}: 0.7 / 0.3. The rigidity formula gives different answers for the two. Which partition, if either, is the one the experience "sets"?
2. The half-heard message. A source transmits one of two words, "green" or "violet"; you're unsure which you heard and end at 0.7 that the word was "green." But the transmission is itself more likely in some states than others — the sender only transmits when the cloth is defective, and defective cloths are more often green. So the shift in your credence about the word also tells you something about defectiveness, conditional on color. Does the shift carry more information than the color credences alone?
3. Two shifts back to back, one over {green, blue, violet} and one over {silk, polyester, wool}, and doing them in the two orders lands you in different places. Is that path-dependence acceptable, or is it a reductio of the rule?
4. The calibration readout. A neurologist you trust tells you that in an hour your credence that the cloth is green will be 0.7 — evidence about your own future credal state, not, apparently, about cloths. There's no partition of cloth-properties whose credence got exogenously moved, and rigidity can't even be stated.
5. The implant case. A subject with a neural implant sees a color she has no antecedent concept for, so there is no cell of any partition she antecedently had opinions about for the experience to move. Is this a case for the rule at all, or does it mark a boundary of the whole framework?
6. Three things not to conflate: "suppose for the sake of argument that the cloth is green," "if the cloth had been green," and "I looked at it and it seemed greenish." Should one rule cover all three?

Leaves I want present: Jeffrey conditioning with rigidity as usually stated; the view that there is always some proposition about the experience you become certain of, so plain conditionalization suffices; a minimum-information / closest-function rule; a likelihood-based rule where the input is how probable the evidence-event is across states rather than a new credence over a partition; something that handles case 4; something that handles case 6; and a skeptical leaf saying the input underdetermines the output. Plus at least one empty leaf.
