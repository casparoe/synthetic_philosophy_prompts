I work in philosophy of cognitive science, and the fight over heterophenomenology has been in exactly the same posture for as long as I have been in the field: the parties know each other's moves, each new exchange restates an old one, and nobody's position has shifted. I want a diagnosis of the stall, not a verdict on the method and not a survey of the literature. I don't need citations or the attribution history; I know who is supposed to have said what. What I want is a structural account of why this argument cannot get traction, grounded in what each side is actually committed to.

The two positions, as tightly as I can state them:

H (the heterophenomenologist, in the form defended by Dennett): a science of consciousness must proceed third-personally. You record subjects' utterances as texts, suspend judgment about whether the things they appear to be describing really exist, construct the heterophenomenological world they seem to be living in the way you would reconstruct the world of a novel from its sentences, and then explain their discriminative, mnemonic, and report-producing capacities functionally and neuroscientifically. There is nothing a first-person method can add that this procedure cannot in principle recover, and the properties critics invoke — intrinsic, ineffable, directly apprehensible only from the inside — are defined so as to be unavailable to any method, so they are not data and never were.

C (the critic): this changes the subject. What needs explaining is why there is something it is like to undergo the states in question at all, and a method that refuses to take a subject's report as presumptively about that has already settled that the target is report-production and discrimination instead. On the critic's own conceivability cases — a difference in what it is like with no third-person trace, or a subject with the discriminations but without the what-it-is-like — the method could not detect the difference even if it obtained, so it cannot be the whole method for the target. Critics grant that reports are evidence; they deny that the class of report-and-behavior facts exhausts what a theory has to account for.

Each side's decisive move is a charge that the other has begged the question about what the data are, which is part of why I think this is going nowhere. My own hypothesis is that the dispute isn't about consciousness at all but about what an empirical science of X has to deliver: whether it must be able to adjudicate between what its subjects say about X, or only predict and causally explain their saying it. If that's right, the two sides are answering different questions under one name. But I also have a second suspicion, that a hidden empirical question is doing the work — whether reports of this kind are coordinated with a single underlying structure tightly enough to serve as measurements, or are as noisy and protocol-dependent as the second probe below suggests. And a third, that it is simply verbal about the word experience.

Three cases I've been using to try to force the issue. I'd like your diagnosis to predict and evaluate what each side should say about them, rather than just redescribing them:

1. The swap pair. A and B are indistinguishable by every third-person measure we have: same psychometric curves across a battery of tasks, same reaction times, same calibration of confidence, no differences in neural recording at our resolution, same learning curves, never differing on any forced-choice or discrimination task. Under repeated careful interviewing, though, A consistently describes a stimulus as holding the same orange throughout a ten-second presentation, while B describes it as fading to grey around the halfway point and then returning. Nothing distinguishes them except what they say.

2. Protocol sensitivity. Whether either of them reports a fading turns out to depend on how the question is put: did the color change gets no from both; did the look of it change gets yes from B only; did anything about your experience change gets yes from both. The text is partly an artifact of the interview protocol, and there appears to be no protocol-independent fact of the matter about the subject's own view.

3. The trained reporter. Subjects given a few hundred hours of disciplined descriptive training — fixed vocabulary, repeated trials, cross-checking between subjects, inferential and theoretical terms excluded — produce reports that are stable across sessions in a way naive reports are not, and that predict subsequent discrimination performance and neural measures better than the subjects' own confidence ratings do. If H accepts these as better data, first-person method seems to earn its keep; if H refuses them, the refusal looks like a metaphysical commitment wearing a methodological badge.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, and why has it been immune to argument? Is it verbal, is it a clash of standards of evidence or of what counts as data, is it two questions under one heading, is a concealed empirical question doing the work, or is the question ill-posed? Tell me which, tell me why the alternatives fail, and tell me what would count as progress given your answer — a distinction, a result, a change of question — or argue that no progress should be expected and say why. Please keep the prose tight; no literature review, no citations, and don't spend words on who said what.
