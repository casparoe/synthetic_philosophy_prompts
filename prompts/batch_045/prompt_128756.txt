I'm finishing a novel whose protagonist, Miriam Hale, is a sixty-two-year-old poet — mostly free verse, one well-received book in the nineties, a long quiet stretch — who discovers halfway through that her collected poems were folded, without permission or payment, into the material from which a commercial image-and-text generator learned to work in something like her register. Against her agent's advice she agrees to appear at a small arts festival where one of the system's builders is also on the panel, and the chapter that closes the book's first half is meant to be her reading: a new poem, printed in the manuscript whole and without gloss, which has to do its work on the page for a reader who has never thought about any of this and for a philosopher of art who has thought about nothing else for a decade. What I need from you is that poem.

I've fixed on a sestina rather than free verse, and the choice is itself part of what Miriam is arguing. The sestina is the most machine-like of the fixed forms — six unrhymed six-line stanzas in which the six end-words of the first stanza are repeated in a set rotation (last, first, penultimate, second, antepenultimate, third, and so on) until each has closed a stanza, then gathered two to a line into a three-line tornada — so that anyone can run the procedure on a fixed input and get a poem-shaped object out. Miriam wants to know whether running it yields something only she could have written, or whether the form will quietly expose her own practice as the same kind of procedure. Please use these six end-words, and let each carry its weight: hand, copy, voice, model, inheritance, remainder. I'd like them to stay legible in a pre-digital register — the painter's model, the apprentice's copy after a master, an inheritance received and passed on, what is left over when the sources are subtracted — so that a reader in 1900 would take the poem for a meditation on making, while a reader now hears the second meaning underneath, especially in model, which should mean both the thing you work from and the thing that works. Lines of roughly ten syllables, flexible rather than metronomic; plain and concrete in diction, domestic rather than oracular, closer in register to a mid-century American sestina than to the troubadour invention; and no jargon at all — no training data, no pixels, no mention of machines by name.

The philosophical problem I want the poem to think with is one a minor character in the novel, an art theorist named Levon Ashjian, announces in an earlier scene and calls the inheritance test: a made thing is genuinely creative, he claims, only if a competent later maker could learn from it some move they could not have learned from its sources alone — novelty, that is, that is transmissible rather than merely new. Miriam is half convinced and half repelled, because the test seems to acquit the system that absorbed her work (people plainly do pick up moves from its output) and yet to miss whatever it is she still wants to withhold from it. Let the poem put pressure on that test rather than answer it: the first three stanzas may reasonably sound like the case for the machine, since the form is a procedure and her skill is a procedure too; something should shift in the fourth or fifth, in the way the same six words come back changed by the company they've been keeping — which is, I take it, the real philosophical discovery available here, that the new arrives from placement and accumulated context and not from a private act of intending. The tornada should deliver whatever judgment the poem has earned without stating it as a thesis, and should leave the question open without going vague: I don't want a grievance lyric, and I don't want a shrug, and I especially don't want either of the two cheap positions — that the system feels nothing and so makes nothing, or that recombination was always all that human creativity was. After the poem, give me a short prose note, no more than two hundred words, on what the finished poem actually commits Miriam to and where someone who works on aesthetics and AI would want to object; I'd rather find that out before the copyedit than in a review.
