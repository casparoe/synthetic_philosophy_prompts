I have to teach the gradual-replacement argument — the case in which neurons are substituted one at a time for functionally equivalent prostheses, and the trichotomy it is meant to force among experience staying fixed, fading with the proportion of biological tissue replaced, and changing in some other way while functional organization is held constant — in a single seventy-minute seminar. The class is roughly half advanced philosophy undergraduates and half cognitive science undergraduates. They have met functionalism and the knowledge argument; none of them has met this argument. The room will split, predictably, between those who think the case is settled in advance by the observation that the computation is unchanged and those who think a question no possible experiment could settle is not a question at all.

Do not explain the argument to me; I know it. Give me a plan for teaching it. I want, in this order:

1. The dependency structure. Which distinctions have to be in place before the substitution scenario can even be stated without begging a question or inviting a conflation, and what collapses if one of them arrives late. I take it that the target is a dependence claim about the character of experience and not a claim about what happens to a person, and that functional organization has to be read counterfactually rather than as a match on the actual sequence of inputs and outputs; tell me if either of those belongs somewhere other than first, and what else is load-bearing.

2. The carrying example. My candidate is a two-channel version: the subject has two visual channels that are, or are stipulated to be, functionally duplicate; the substitution is completed in one channel while the other is left biological, and at the midpoint the same stimulus is presented separately to each. I want the class to ask whether anything is different on the prosthetic side, because I think that question is a trap that will drag the premise the argument actually needs — that a change in experience would normally show up in what the subject can notice and report — into the open, where I can then make them own it. Tell me what this example must have and what it must lack for that to work, whether the duplication should be stipulated or motivated, and whether the trap is where I think it is. I also toyed with performing the substitutions during dreamless sleep, and with running the second half of the sequence in reverse; tell me whether either earns its place or is noise.

3. The misconception map. Here is what I expect: the Ship of Theseus framing (identity and survival rather than preservation of experience); pinning the whole issue on carbon versus silicon when the argument is meant to be neutral about which non-functional property is supposed to do the work; the charge that the argument assumes functionalism instead of arguing for it; the objection that a genuinely perfect prosthesis is impossible and so the case is moot; and the retreat to vagueness, the claim that there is no fact of the matter about where experience gives out. For each, the point in the sequence at which it should be anticipated or defused, and the one or two sentences that do the defusing. Add the ones I have missed, including any that come specifically from the cognitive science half of the room.

4. Where the gradualness is doing the real work. I want them able to say what breaks if the substitution is done in a single step, and I do not want them leaving with the impression that the argument shows uploading to be safe.

5. One thing I should openly refuse to explain at this level, and what I should say when I refuse it.

6. A closing check: one task or question whose answer would show me they have the structure of the argument rather than its conclusion.
