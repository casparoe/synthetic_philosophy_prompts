hey — hope this is the right place for this. I'm a TA for a first-year seminar and we're closing out our two weeks on infinity (Hilbert's hotel, Cantor's diagonal, the finitist complaint that only potential infinities are kosher). I want to end the last session with a poem instead of a bullet-point recap, and everything I've drafted so far is either cute-but-empty or secretly just the wikipedia article with line breaks. So: can you write it?

Rough shape I have in mind, change it if you've got something better: 30-40 lines, four or five numbered sections (night one, night two, ...) in the voice of the hotel's night clerk, plain working register, no whimsy for its own sake. Each night is a different maneuver, and there's a refrain that's the clerk's instruction to the guests — recognizably the same line every time, but with the numbers in it rewriten for that night's trick:

night one: the hotel is full, rooms numbered 1, 2, 3, ... with no last room, and one more person walks in. Everyone moves from n to n+1, room 1 comes open. I want the poem to make it clear why this fails in a finite hotel — the guest in the last room has nowhere to go — and that what's doing the work is that there is no last room, not some spooky property of "infinity."

night two: a bus with countably infinitely many people. Everyone to 2n, all the odds come open.

night three, the one I most want to land: infinitely many guests check out, and how many are left depends entirely on which ones left. If the odd-numbered rooms empty, infinitely many remain; if everyone above room 1 leaves, one remains; if everyone above room ten thousand leaves, ten thousand remain. Same operation, any answer you like — subtraction just isn't defined here.

night four, only if you can make it sing: the turn to Cantor. The guests can be numbered off, but the ways the hotel could possibly be occupied can't be — there are more arrangements than there are rooms.

last section: the finitist pushback, given fairly and left standing, that the whole setup is best read as a reductio of treating a completed infinite as something real. "You can always add one more room" is fine; a hotel with no last room isn't. Please don't resolve it. I want a finitist in the room and a set-theory person in the room to both feel they got a fair shake.

The other thing I keep getting wrong: my drafts all sound like they're pointing at a contradiction — "full, and yet there's room!" Thats the wrong note. What actually happens is that nothing contradictory occurs; what gives way is the old principle that a whole is bigger than any of its parts. I'd like someone who knows the literature to hear that distinction in the poem rather than be told it. No lecturing lines, no footnotes.

Two small asks: don't end on the word "infinity," and no ellipsis joke about the poem going on forever. And if you can make the last word of each section a number word, with the run of those numbers enacting that night's rearrangement, that'd be great — but don't warp the poem to get it.
