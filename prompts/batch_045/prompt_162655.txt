Hey — I need a prop for a public-engagement thing our consciousness reading group is doing (I'm doing my PhD on the illusion problem / phenomenal-realism side of things, and I volunteered to make the handout). Could you write me a parody document?

The ask: the "Information for Participants" booklet for a clinical trial, set in a world where illusionism about phenomenal consciousness is completely settled science — textbook settled, the way anaesthesia is settled. Quick definition so we're on the same page: illusionism is the view that nothing actually has phenomenal properties, there is no what-it's-like; what really happens is that our introspective systems misrepresent certain sensory and attentional states as having phenomenal character. The illusion is a real cognitive/representational state, it's just not itself a phenomenal one, and everything on the functional / access side is untouched. Don't cartoon it into " consciousness doesn't exist and also robots are fine" — the whole joke depends on the view being stated the way someone who actually holds it would state it.

The fictional bit I want you to work with: there's now a compound (call it ISD-3) that selectively downregulates the introspective misrepresentation. Discrimination, categorisation, verbal report, aversion learning and nociceptive withdrawal all stay intact. Before dosing, participants say things like "red looks like something to me." On the drug they say things like "my visual system is in red-detecting state V7," and when asked whether anything is missing they say no. But — and this is the detail I really want exploited — a good number of them also insist, sometimes quite upset, that they remember perfectly well that red used to look like something, and they can't square that with what they now say. Oh, and pain seems to be spared, nobody knows why.

Form: a participant information booklet, 700–800 words, deadpan NHS / clinical-trial boilerplate voice, numbered sections plus an FAQ. Sections I definitely want: what the study involves, side effects, what happens if you change your mind and withdraw, and compensation in the unlikely event of injury. Those last two should be doing a lot of the work.

Rules: nobody in the document winks. Never let anyone state the philosophical objections — the "illusion of the illusion" regress, why we'd have the illusion in the first place, whether participants' own reports are evidence, whether anything of value is lost, whether the memory of what-it-was-like is a memory of a fact or of an illusion. All of that should show up as bureaucratic awkwardness instead: a clause that can't quite be drafted, a side effect that gets classified as an expected pharmacological effect rather than an adverse event, a withdrawal procedure whose own preconditions the trial has removed. Include one short verbatim quote from a member of the trial's Patient Advisory Group, and no real names anywhere — invented trial staff are fine. It should be readable and funny to a smart non-philosopher, but I want the people in my reading group to read it and say "ok, that's fair," not "that's a strawman."
