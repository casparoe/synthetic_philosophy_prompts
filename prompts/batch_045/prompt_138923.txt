Also: no reading lists, no citations, no "so-and-so argued in 2003" — I've got no way to chase any of that up and I don't care who said it first, I want the argument.

Context: I'm helping draft an options paper for a small constitutional-reform working group, and the package they keep circling back to looks roughly like this. It's deliberately a composite — please don't fact-check it against any real country's law or tell me which bits exist where, just take the practice as I've described it.

THE PACKAGE

1. Citizens 18–69 are automatically enrolled and legally required to turn up on the day (or post a ballot, or get an excuse accepted in advance). Failure means a fine of about the price of a tank of gas, which is barely enforced.
2. What's required is attendance, not choice. A ballot left completely blank counts as participating. A ballot you've written or drawn anything on is void *and* counts as failure to vote — so writing "none of the above, you're all crooks" is a worse legal act than handing in pristine silence.
3. Over-70s are exempt from the duty entirely, as are people abroad on the day. 16- and 17-year-olds may vote if they register affirmatively, but nobody compels them.
4. Resident non-citizens — anyone legally resident two years or more — vote in local elections but not national ones. Once they've voted in three consecutive local elections they become eligible for the national franchise, even if they never naturalise.
5. Prisoners serving under two years keep the vote; two years or more lose it. The official justification given is that a serious offence breaks the "bond of civic trust" with the community.
6. Each parent gets an extra half-vote per child under 16, cast as the parent sees fit (the proxy-votes-for-kids idea that gets called Demeny voting; the half-weight version has literally been proposed for Austria by Vanhuysse). If the two parents disagree the half-vote splits between them.
7. Referendums have a participation quorum: half plus one of enrolled voters must attend or the result is void no matter how lopsided. So a campaign that expects to lose does better telling its people to stay home — and a blank ballot helps the other side clear the quorum.

What I want:

(a) What is the thing being compelled in 1–2 supposed to *be*? Physical presence, a genuine act of choosing, deliberation, or public acquiescence? And what does the inverted treatment of the written-on ballot presuppose about what the state wants from a voter — is blank-is-fine-but-protest-is-an-offence coherent with calling the activity voting at all?

(b) What view of *who should have a say* are items 3–5 working with — all-subjected-to-the-law, all-affected-interests, membership as something earned or forfeited by conduct? Note the joints that bug me: a resident non-citizen who's never been convicted gets a local say while a convicted citizen loses theirs; over-70s are released from the duty but keep the right. Does that asymmetry tell us the duty is grounded in contribution, in reciprocity over a whole life, or in something paternalistic?

(c) Item 6 versus one person one vote: is the half-vote a violation of political equality or a correction for it? What would a vote have to *be* — an expression of will, a proxy for interests, a stake in an ongoing concern — for the half-weight parent proxy to be the right shape? And does it matter that the child never authorised the delegation, or that the parent also keeps their own full vote?

(d) Item 7: what does making absenteeism a veto presuppose about legitimacy and about what silence means? Is there any coherent reading on which staying home is itself a political act — and if so, how can item 1's fine for staying home be justified? That's the bit I keep going in circles on.

(e) Finally: separate what the package strictly *presupposes* from what it merely fits well with, and tell me where it's underdetermined so that several quite different theories would license the same design. Then say which items would have to go or flip if we swapped in (i) a pure all-affected-interests view, (ii) the view that the vote is a right the holder may waive and the state has no business compelling, or (iii) an epistemic view on which the franchise is justified by the quality of the aggregate judgement it produces.

Close by telling me which single item you think is the hardest to justify under *any* of the theories you've reconstructed, and why. Take your time with it, a long answer is fine.
