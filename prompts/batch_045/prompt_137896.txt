(1) Nadia: The claim that violence has declined is just an empirical claim and it's reasonably well supported — homicide rates in Western Europe look like they've fallen by something like an order of magnitude or more over the last several centuries. And per capita is the only sensible way to state it. Absolute numbers are hopeless: by that measure a city of ten million with a low murder rate counts as "more violent" than a village of five hundred where a couple of men a year die in feuds, which is absurd.

(2) Wes: Per capita is better than absolute, fine. But you've picked the normalization that makes your case and then called it neutral. What you divide by isn't a technical detail, it's a view about whose risk counts. And here's the thing: take two worlds. World A has eight billion people and your annual chance of being killed by another person is one in a million. World B has eight million people and the annual chance is one in a thousand. Both worlds have about eight thousand murders a year. But world B is a thousand times more dangerous to live in. If you say B is more violent you've used a rate; if you say they're equally violent you've used a total; and nothing in the world settles which one the word "violence" tracks.

(3) Nadia: Your hypothetical shows rates are informative, not that totals are. If someone told you B has the same number of murders as A, the first thing you'd ask is how many people live in each — nobody treats the total as the answer on its own. And it isn't only homicide. Deaths in warfare per capita also look lower now than in small-scale societies, where a big share of adult men died violently. Even the twentieth century, for all its wars, isn't obviously worse per capita than that.

(4) Wes: Two problems. First, an annual per-capita rate and a lifetime risk can come apart, and lifetime risk is what an actual person faces. People live a lot longer now, so if the annual rate halves while lifespans double, the chance you eventually die at someone else's hand may barely move — the headline decline overstates how much safer an individual life got. Second, and this one worries me more: as killing gets rare, the measured rate becomes a creature of bookkeeping. Whether a given death gets recorded as a homicide, an accident, a death in state custody, or something else can start moving the trend as much as anything people actually do. So the trend is least reliable exactly where it's most dramatic.

(5) Nadia: Both of those are fair, and neither one shows violence is going up. The lifetime point changes the size of the decline, not its direction. And the bookkeeping worry is a worry you could raise about any long-run series; it isn't a reason to think the direction is invented. Really we should just be explicit about what we're counting. I mean realized violence — actual killings, actual battle deaths. You seem to want to count the violence that didn't happen.

(6) Wes: Partly, yes. Look at the peace among great powers since 1945. Enormous numbers of people do not die, and a large part of the reason they don't die is that a lot of people are credibly prepared to kill enormous numbers of people. Call that a decline in violence if you like, but a world with low realized violence and a standing capacity to kill hundreds of millions isn't obviously less violent than a world where small raids actually happen and nobody could organise anything bigger. So what's worth measuring is ex ante — what would happen in the branches that didn't occur, not just the corpses that did. And the moment you go ex ante you need probabilities for branches that never happened, and there's no canonical way to assign them. Which is why I think there may be no fact of the matter about whether the world is getting less violent.

(7) Nadia: But on your account every gun in a locked cabinet makes a society more violent, and the question becomes unanswerable — you're weighting events that never happened by probabilities nobody can get. And deterrence isn't violence, it's a threat. A threat might be coercion, or domination, or just plain bad, but calling it violence collapses distinctions we need. Better to say two separate things: realized violence is down, and separately, some of that peace may be fragile. Both are assertible, and neither needs a probability distribution over counterfactuals.
