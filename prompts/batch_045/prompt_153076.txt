I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy (sort of adjacent to population ethics) and I've been asked to take a one-off ninety-minute seminar for second-year undergrads on the scale argument about insect sentience. They've had a term of utilitarianism and one week on expected utility in decision theory, but basically no philosophy of mind, and none of them will have read anything on animal sentience — so don't give me readings, they won't do them. The room will be split: two or three have clearly been reading effective-altruism-adjacent stuff online and already arrive thinking insects are obviously the top priority, and the rest think the whole idea is a joke. I've got a whiteboard and no slides. I don't want you to explain the argument to me — I know it. I want a plan for how to teach it, and my current one is definately broken.

The bit I keep tripping over is the order. My supervisor says open with the question "what would have to be true for the number of individuals to stop mattering?" before I even say the word sentience, on the grounds that if you lead with the headcount you've lost the sceptics in the first five minutes. I'm not convinced: it seems to me you can't ask that until they have some grip on what the currency even is — whether we're aggregating over creature-lives, over seconds of experience, or over some "a whole life's worth of welfare" unit — because every time I try it the unit quietly shifts halfway through the argument, and I've started calling that the denominator problem. On top of that, a mate who works on insect welfare suggested I separate a credence in sentience from a credence about intensity: you might be reasonably confident an aphid has some kind of experience and yet be completely clueless about how bad a second of it is, and the scale argument multiplies both, so it inherits uncertainty from both sources and students collapse them into one number. And then there's the point I most want to land and have no idea where to put: people treat the tiny per-creature probability as if it gets averaged away over a trillion individuals, but the evidence isn't independent across species — if bees are sentient that's evidence about ants — so the "trillion coin flips" intuition is doing work it shouldn't. Say what has to come before what and why, and what breaks if I get the sequence wrong.

On the example that carries the whole thing: I've been going back and forth between an ant nest under someone's patio (enormous numbers, one individual they can picture, an intervention anyone could actually perform — but the numbers are made up and it invites "so just don't pour boiling water on ants" as the takeaway) and a wheat field sprayed with an aphicide (better-behaved numbers, more clearly policy-shaped — but no single creature to picture and it drags in rows about food prices). Tell me which to build around and why, and what the example has to have and has to lack to do the job. Then the misconceptions, in the order they'll actually arise rather than a tidy list: I'm expecting nociception-is-pain, "0.1% means we can ignore it", "tiny brain so the pain is proportionally shallow", "so you're saying we should all stop eating X", and the collapse into either "obviously insects win" or "obviously we can't do anything either way" — and for each, where in the sequence I head it off instead of rebutting it at the end. Give me one question for the last ten minutes that tells me they've got the structure rather than the conclusion, ideally something where changing one variable — duration instead of headcount, or making the credences correlated, or holding the population at carrying capacity — flips the answer. And tell me what I should openly refuse to get into in ninety minutes and how to say so without sounding like I'm hiding something; I'm guessing the candidate neuroscience theories of consciousness and anything about whether prevented deaths get replaced by later births, but you tell me.
