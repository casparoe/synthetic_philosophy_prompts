I work as one of three legal officers at the Office of the Claims Commissioner, a body created by statute two years ago to administer a reparations fund for a set of nineteenth- and twentieth-century wrongs — dispossession of land, forced labour on estates, and the seizure of cattle — committed under a colonial administration that no longer exists. The Act gives us almost nothing: a compensable claim is defined as loss traceable to a qualifying wrong, qualifying wrongs are listed by date and category, and everything else is ours to work out. In practice we read about forty claim files a week, each decided on written submissions with no oral hearing, each requiring a reasoned decision within thirty days and reviewable only for legal error. Most claimants are unrepresented, and most of the archives are silent or one-sided, so whatever framework we adopt has to be runnable by a caseworker with two days of training in half a day per file, with a legal officer reviewing only the files it flags — and it has to degrade honestly when the evidence is missing rather than quietly returning a no.

What I want from you is a decision procedure, and I want it committed rather than survey-style: one ordering of questions, one set of cut-offs, and your reasons for preferring them to the alternatives you can see. My own tentative principle, which I would like you to test, repair, or replace, is that there are two different kinds of claim that we keep running together. A harm claim says: a wrong was done to our predecessors, we are worse off now than we would have been, and that gap is what is owed. An enrichment claim says: someone took something, whoever holds it now is not entitled to hold it, and the thing or its value is what is owed. On my view harm claims have to be traced through time and get weaker as the chain lengthens and other causes pile up, and they run into the difficulty that in most of our docket the claimants would not exist at all had the wrong not been done — so the comparison with how they would have fared has to be made at the level of the group rather than of any individual, and I am not sure what licenses that switch or what it costs. Enrichment claims, I think, do not fade with time, but they stop at the value of what is still held and they can be defeated by a good-faith transfer, by the holder having spent the proceeds on something else, or by the holder now being a different kind of institution from the one that took it. Please give me the questions in the order I should ask them; what counts as a sufficient answer to each, stated as an evidence threshold a caseworker can actually check; the points at which the procedure returns a clear no; the points at which it must hand the file to the Commissioner because the question is one of values rather than fact, with a note of what the Commissioner is being asked to decide; and instructions for the common case in which the archive neither supports nor refutes the claim. Two defences I want you to rule on explicitly because they arise constantly: the anyway argument, that the land or the labour would have been taken later under a different and lawful authority, so the wrong made no difference to where we are now; and the mixed-claimant case, where the claimant group also inherited part of the benefit, or where the present holder's assets are now largely managed for the benefit of that same group.

Then run the procedure on three files. First: a community claims land taken in 1891 that is now a national park; its present members descend both from the dispossessed households and from labourers settled on the land afterwards; the state defends on the anyway ground, pointing to a 1912 statute under which it says the land would in any event have been compulsorily acquired, and also on the ground that the successor state inherits the position of everyone, perpetrators included. Second: a claim against a bank that is the corporate successor of a financing house which held mortgages over estates worked by forced labour and was repaid out of their profits; the house was dissolved in a 1971 merger that was at arm's length and for value, and a substantial share of the bank's present shareholders are pension funds whose members are drawn from the claimant community. Third, and this is the file I expect the procedure to handle badly and the reason I am writing: a religious order ran a mission station worked by forced labour until 1934; the order still exists but is insolvent, and what remains of it is a hospital and a school that serve the descendants of the very people it forced to work; tracing to the present holding succeeds cleanly, while the harm claim fails on the point about who would have existed. If your procedure says the right answer is to charge the hospital, say so plainly and say what it recommends instead; if it says something else, say why. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do. Our fund is fixed and the eligible claims already exceed it, so there is a rationing step at the end that I do not think any of this gives me, and I want to know whether that is a genuinely separate question or whether the framework has been quietly answering it all along. I would like the procedure you would actually defend, including the step you are least confident about and the case in which you would personally overrule it.
