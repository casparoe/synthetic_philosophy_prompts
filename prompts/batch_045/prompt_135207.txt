I'm a principal engineer at a mid-size consumer lender, and last week I lost a design-review argument that I think was settled with a sentence rather than a reason.

The change on the table: our loan-origination system routes an application to manual review if the applicant's address changed in the last thirty days. The rule predates everyone on the team, the commit history dead-ends in a vendor migration, and nobody can produce a document, an incident report, or a regulation that explains it. It currently sends about one in twenty-five applications to a human, which costs us money and adds a day of waiting for those applicants. I proposed deleting it and watching fraud and early-default rates for a quarter. A colleague blocked it with this line:

    Don't tear down a fence until you know why it was put up.

I have since gathered that the maxim has a name, a canonical passage, and a substantial afterlife in a conservative tradition of argument about inherited institutions, none of which I have read and none of which I want summarized. What I want to know is what work that sentence can actually do. It presents itself as one thought, and I don't think it is: I think it is several claims of very different strength, and that which one my colleague meant decides whether he was right.

Please write five sections, in this order.

1. The readings (~700 words). A numbered menu of the distinct precise theses the maxim could be expressing, ordered from the near-trivial to the strong. Include at least: readings about what the bare existence of a long-standing practice is evidence of; readings about individual versus distributed or tacit knowledge; readings that are really about burden of proof, option value, or irreversibility rather than about the practice itself; readings about standing and about who is entitled to alter arrangements other people rely on; and any reading on which the maxim yields the right result for reasons that have nothing to do with anyone's knowing why. State each as a claim a person could coherently deny, and note what each presupposes about the process that produced and preserved the practice.

2. Verdicts (~300 words). For each reading: a platitude; defensible but contingent on facts someone could in principle check; substantive and genuinely contested; or false or self-undermining. Flag readings that collapse into one another, and say which single reading is the strongest one actually available to my colleague. A table is fine.

3. Two cases (~400 words). First, our address rule: for each reading, what it would require us to do next, and what would count as having discharged the requirement. Second, a case where everybody already knows why the fence is there and the maxim seems to me to misfire — pick a convention that is arbitrary but that people coordinate around, such as which side of an escalator you stand on. Which readings get that case right, and do any of them get it right for the reason the maxim gives?

4. My distinctions (~200 words). Two things I came up with and cannot evaluate. (a) The three whys: the reason the rule was written, the reason it has been kept alive since, and the reason it would matter if we deleted it. These can come apart, and the maxim does not say which is meant. (b) The retention condition: the rule's existence is evidence of its usefulness only if it arose and survived in an environment where useless rules actually get deleted. Ours has no sunset mechanism, no periodic rule audit, and a known history of rules being added after incidents and never revisited. Are (a) and (b) constraints the maxim needs in order to be usable, or are they already implied by readings you listed?

5. What the maxim leaves open (~250 words). It supplies no stopping rule for the investigation, no standard of evidence for having found the why, no account of who bears the cost of inquiry, and no limit on its own scope. Spell out what has to be added for it to function as a decision rule rather than a mood, and tell me whether it applies to itself — the practice of deferring to inherited practice is itself an inherited practice.

Assume I have read the standard popular version of this idea and skip it. No citations, sources, or suggested reading: I want the arguments, not the literature.
