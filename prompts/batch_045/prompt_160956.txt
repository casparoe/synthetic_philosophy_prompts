I'm on the three-person committee designing a five-year research program on the direction of time for a foundation that will fund about six postdoctoral fellows at a time — two with graduate training in physics, four in philosophy — plus a modest budget for workshops and for commissioning one or two modeling projects (simulations, reanalysis of existing data). The foundation has said it will judge the program as a whole at the end rather than by output volume, and it wants us to name in advance three flagship questions, in order, with a defense of why those and not the others. So what I need from you is an agenda rather than a survey: which open questions about the direction of time and its relation to the thermodynamic gradient should a group with this composition put most of its people on, in what order, and judged by what criteria — importance, how much other work depends on the answer, tractability for six people over five years who can read physics but cannot do original cosmology, and how neglected each question is relative to how load-bearing it is.

Two complications. The first is a rule our board has adopted and asked us to apply, which I'll call No Borrowed Arrow: any account the program endorses must not, anywhere in the argument, presuppose some aspect of the very asymmetry it is trying to explain — not the reliability of records, not the fact that we remember the past and not the future, not the claim that what we do affects later rather than earlier events, and not our cosmological evidence about the early universe if that evidence is itself supposed to be a case of the record asymmetry. We cannot agree on whether this is a genuine filter or a device for pre-deciding the outcome: read strictly, it seems to rule out the standard answer — posit an extremely low-entropy initial macrocondition, derive an entropy gradient from it, derive records and memory from the gradient, then appeal to records as our evidence for the initial condition — which is circular, and it isn't obvious that any alternative survives either. So treat the rule itself as an agenda question: is it a good criterion for deciding what to work on, does it need weakening (a ban on presupposing the asymmetry in the explanation while permitting it in the evidence, say), or should we drop it? The second complication is that the board wants the ordering stated conditionally: suppose that within two or three years the cosmology side more or less settles why the early universe had the low-entropy condition it had, either because it falls out of inflationary dynamics under an agreed measure or because it is shown to be underivable and must simply be posited. Which of our flagship questions would that settle, which would it leave entirely untouched, and which would it make more urgent than they are now?

The candidates on our whiteboard, for whatever they are worth: whether the low-entropy initial condition is really a claim about entropy at all, given that in the early universe the deficit seems to sit almost entirely in the gravitational degrees of freedom — so that what needs explaining is the smoothness of the early universe rather than a low number on a thermodynamic ledger; whether that posit has to be supplemented by a probability distribution over the microstates compatible with it, and whether the supplement is doing hidden work; whether a final boundary condition can share or take over the explanatory load, as some time-symmetric and retrocausal reformulations of quantum mechanics would allow; whether the asymmetry of agency and deliberation is upstream of the thermodynamic gradient rather than downstream of it; whether Boltzmann-brain and cognitive-stability arguments are a legitimate way to break ties between cosmologies or a methodological blind alley; and whether the arrow of time is one phenomenon or several that the literature has quietly run together. I suspect at least two of these are overrated — the kind of question that generates papers while its answer changes nothing downstream — and part of what I want is for you to say which, and on what grounds. For each question you keep, tell me what kind of result in the next two or three years — a simulation, an observational constraint, a technical result, a conceptual one — would move it up or down the list, and what a single postdoc could realistically finish in five years. My own instinct, which I don't trust, is that the gravitational-entropy rephrasing is the most underrated item on that list and that the cognitive-stability literature is the most overrated; argue me out of it or into it.
