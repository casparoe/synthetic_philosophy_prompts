I spent about thirty years in physical oceanography, and since retiring I'v been going to a philosophy reading group at the local university — mostly because a friend dragged me along, and now I'm hooked. We're doing Keynes at the moment, the 1921 book on probability and then the bits of the General Theory about how people actually form expectations when there's no real basis for a number. I keep being struck that in my old field we'd run an ensemble, quote the spread, and talk as if that was a probability distribution, and nobody ever asked what entitled us to do it.

Anyway, here's the thing I keep chewing on, and I'd like your guess at how Keynes himself would have reacted to it.

Suppose a lab builds a system — call it a calibration oracle — that takes any well-specified yes-or-no question about an event inside the next two years and returns a number between 0 and 1. Its only real credential is calibration: over the last decade, of the questions it put at 0.7, about 70% came out yes, and so on across the whole range. Nobody, including the people who built it, can get anything out of it that functions as a *reason* for the number it gives; the internal workings don't decompose into anything like evidence. It also reports nothing about how much evidence went into any particular answer — one number, that's it — and it will happily hand you a number for a question about something with no real precedent in its training data, like whether a specific government falls in a specific year. Insurance firms and grant panels are starting to use it.

So: would Keynes have counted those outputs as probabilities at all, given that for him probability was a logical relation between evidence and conclusion rather than a frequency record or a betting disposition? Does a good calibration record give you what he meant by the weight of an argument, or is that exactly the thing the single number throws away? Would he have drawn a hard line between the repeated, weather-ish questions and the one-off historical ones, and if so does it follow that the oracle is being used illegitimately in the second case? Is leaning on the oracle a new version of the conventional judgement he described in markets — including the newspaper-competition point where you're really trying to guess what everyone else will guess? And would he have seen it as a practical vindication of the later subjective, betting-odds approach associated with Ramsey, which was developed partly against him, or as a case where something can be perfectly calibrated and still not be doing what he thought probability statements do?

Where do you think he'd be right, and where would his framework just not have the resources to say anything useful?
