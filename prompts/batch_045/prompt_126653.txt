The question, which had a 600 word limit and told them both parts had to be answered:

(a) Pascal claims that, being unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just. Explain this claim and say whether it commits him to the view that justice is simply superior force.

(b) Since 1954 the region of Verray has allocated social housing by a points system in which applicants receive extra points for having had a parent who worked in the region's mining industry. The rule was introduced at a time when ministers believed, wrongly as we now know, that mining families had distinctive long-term housing needs of their own. That belief was one of several grounds given in a 1954 white paper, and the rule was quietly kept on when the rest of the paper's recommendations were repealed in 1979. Compliance is near universal and nobody has challenged the rule in court. In 2019 an archivist publishes the internal memos showing that the 1954 justification rested on a mistake. Civil servants who knew about the memos earlier chose not to release them. I made the case up. On Pascal's account: (i) does the rule lose its authority once the memos are public? (ii) is the civil servants' silence something Pascal's view would recommend, and if so is that a point in favour of the view or against it? (iii) does Pascal leave any way of saying that the rule is unjust, as opposed to merely in force?

The answer:

Pascal's claim is best read against the background of the Pensées discussion of justice and force. His starting point is that both are needed for a stable political order: justice without force is powerless, since there will always be people who break the law, while force without justice is tyrannical and is accused of being so. The two therefore have to be combined. The difficulty is that people dispute about what is just and nobody disputes about who is stronger, so the only combination available to us is to declare that whatever is stronger is also just. In that sense we have made the strong just rather than making the just strong.

This does not commit him to thinking that justice is nothing but superior force. The claim is about what we do, not about what justice is: force has usurped the title of justice, which is not the same as its being all that justice consists in. Pascal thinks there is a genuine justice; the problem is our own corruption and self-love, which make us unable to agree on what it requires, so we fill the gap with force and with custom. His remarks about custom support this, since he says that custom is the whole of equity simply because it is received, and that this is the mystical basis of its authority. He also warns that it is dangerous to tell people that the laws are not just, because they obey only because they believe them just.

Applied to Verray, on (i) the rule does lose its authority once the memos are public. On this account the authority of a law consists in its being established and accepted, and the 1954 rationale is what established it; once that rationale is shown to be a mistake the rule no longer has the acceptance that made it authoritative, and it is exposed as resting on nothing. This is just what Pascal means when he says that whoever traces a custom back to its first principles destroys it. So the archivist's publication is, in Pascal's own terms, destructive of the rule's authority, which is why Pascal would think it right for officials to keep such memos hidden. On (ii), the silence is therefore recommended by the view, and this counts in its favour: a theory of authority that can explain why the civil servants were right to sit on the memos is getting something right about how authority actually works. On (iii), Pascal does leave a way of calling the rule unjust, but only a thin one. Since the test of whether something is just is whether it is established and obeyed, the only thing that could make the rule unjust is its actually being successfully challenged. Anything stronger than that, such as saying it is unjust because the 1954 belief was false, is a claim he has ruled out. In effect, for Pascal any long-standing custom is as defensible as any other.

It is worth noticing how close this leaves him to Montaigne, who had made much the same point about custom a generation earlier. For both of them the authority of a law rests on nothing beyond the fact of its being accepted.
