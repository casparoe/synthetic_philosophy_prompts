Could you act as a professional epistemologist — someone who has actually worked on self-knowledge, margins for error and the KK principle — and think about this slowly before answering: sketch two or three different diagnoses first, then tell me which one you'd commit to and why. And please, no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables in your reply; just continuous prose, like an email to a colleague.

Some background. I help run a small reading group in Bristol — two philosphers, a post-doc in psychophysics, and me (retired maths teacher, mostly there for the biscuits) — and for the last few weeks we've been chewing over the anti-luminosity argument, Williamson's, as it's usually presented: no condition is such that, whenever it obtains, the subject is in a position to know that it obtains. We then spent a session on the KK principle in its modest form, the one that says only that if you know p then you're in a position to know that you know p. Last Thursday Rowan, the post-doc, came in with a version of the argument that seems to show something far stronger than either of those conclusions, and none of us can find where it cheats. He sets it up with a panel rather than one person over time, specifically to block the complaint that the later subject remembers the earlier ones: two hundred students in adjacent booths, each booth's lighting a hair dimmer than the one before (in his other version each screen shows a patch of colour with a fraction more visual noise mixed in), every booth differing from its neighbours by less than anybody sitting in it could possibly detect, each student asked just once, "do you know the patch is red?", answering yes or no on a button. Take p to be "the patch is red".

His argument, as near as I can type it up: (1) Luminosity for knowledge, i.e. the KK principle — whenever a subject knows p, she is in a position to know that she knows p, and nothing beyond reflection is needed for that. (2) Margin for error: if a subject knows q in a case, then q holds in every case differing from that one by less than a detectable difference; knowledge isn't supposed to be a matter of luck, and if things could so easily have been otherwise in just the way they'd have to be, then you didn't know. (3) Rowan's panel is a slow-drift series: a string of cases, neighbouring ones subjectively indistinguishable, running from one where the subject knows p to one where she doesn't. (4) Now take the brightest booth, α0, and suppose the student in it knows p. By (1) she is in a position to know that she knows p, and since she is attending to the question and has just been asked, she does know that she knows p. Apply (2) with q = "she knows p": she knows p in the next booth along, α1. Run (1) again in α1, then (2) again, and so on down the row — and we arrive at her knowing p in the pitch-dark booth αn, where ex hypothesi she does not. Contradiction. (5) And α0 wasn't special: start the row wherever you like, at whatever case you'd be happiest calling a case of knowledge, and the same walk takes you to a case where the subject hasn't a clue. So nobody ever knows anything about any gradual, perceptible matter of fact — which is absurd, and Rowan cheerfully admits he doesn't believe it, though he maintains he does know when his own feet are cold.

What makes me more suspicious rather than less is that the argument seems to survive swapping "knows" for "is justified in believing", "is reasonably confident to degree 0.9", or "has adequate evidence", so long as you grant the matching luminosity-ish premise for each — which suggests the work is being done by something structural in the set-up rather than by anything peculiar to knowledge. We tried weakening (2) so that it only speaks of nearby cases in which the subject forms her belief by the same method, but the booths seem engineered to guarantee exactly that, and it didn't appear to help. So: which step do you reject? I'd like to keep (1) if I can, since I've a soft spot for KK; (3) looks like a plain fact about the world; and (2) looks like it more or less unpacks "knowledge isn't luck". If the thing is valid and I simply have to surrender a premise, tell me which is cheapest to give up and what precisely I lose by giving it up; if there's a slide, a scope shift or a modal sleight of hand hidden between the steps of (4), point at the exact line and explain why it looked innocent. One more thing, if you've room: does it matter that the series is spread across two hundred students rather than run as one student over an afternoon, or is that a red herring Rowan put in to look clever?
