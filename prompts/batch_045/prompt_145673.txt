I'm teaching a seminar on Tolstoy's *What Is Art?* and for the last session I want to close with a case instead of a paper. The case is the Banksy self-shredding at Sotheby's London in October 2018. You know the one: *Girl with Balloon*, the 2006 canvas version, went under the hammer for a bit over £1m; seconds after the gavel the canvas started sliding out of the bottom of the frame and shredding itself while a siren went off; it stopped about halfway, because apparently the mechanism jammed and Banksy had meant it to go all the way; the buyer kept it, the sale went through at the full price, the artist's authentication body renamed it *Love Is in the Bin*, Sotheby's put out a statement boasting that it was the first artwork in history to have been created live during an auction, and three years later it resold for around £18.6m against a £4–6m estimate, a record for the artist. Take all that as given. You dont need to look anything up, and I don't want a recital of the news coverage or any citations — I want your own mapping.

What I'm after is a Tolstoyan analysis that runs in both directions: what his theory says the people in this episode were doing and what it says about them, and what the episode in turn shows about the theory. Assume I've read the book; don't spend a third of your answer summarising it.

Start by setting the mapping up carefully, because I think most bad treatments of this case cheat here. Who or what is the artist — Banksy, whoever built the shredder into the frame years earlier, the authentication body that retitled it? What is the work — the painting, the half-shredded object, the event, the video? Who is the audience that has to be infected — the people in the room, or the people who saw it online afterwards? And what exactly is the feeling supposedly transmitted? If Tolstoy's apparatus can't settle one of those, say so instead of picking whichever answer makes the rest go smoothly.

Then the infection test proper. On the face of it this is one of the most infectious things in recent art: audible gasps in the auction room, and a joke that landed with millions of people. But I want two things worked through rather than gestured at. First, the feeling seems to depend on the audience already knowing how auctions work, what the numbers on the board mean, and who Banksy is and what he's supposed to stand for — you can't get the joke from a stencil of a girl and a balloon alone. Call it frame-dependent infection if you like; I've been using that label in my notes and I can't find a slot for it in Tolstoy's account, which seems to assume the feeling arrives with the work. Is that a real gap or just a case his transmission model handles fine? Second, Tolstoy lists surprise-and-strikingness and amusement among the marks of the counterfeit of art, not of art — so does the stunt come out as art-because-infectious or counterfeit-because-it's-a-gimmick, and can his theory say which verdict wins, or does it just issue both and stop?

Then sincerity and the market. He wants the artist to have actually felt the feeling and to work from an inner need, and he pins the production of counterfeit art on professionalisation, payment, criticism and art schools. Here the shredder was built into the frame well in advance, specifically in case the painting ever came to auction; the object was then sold, authenticated and re-titled by the artist's own body; and it made more money *because* of the stunt. So: does premeditation, or anonymity, or working through an authentication body break sincerity, or is sincerity for him purely about whether the feeling was really had? And does the reflexivity — the piece being worth more in virtue of what it did to its own price — give him an independent reason to call it counterfeit, or is market reflexivity simply off his map, something his theory has no vocabulary for at all?

After that, tell me which way the case cuts. Is it a confirmation of his diagnosis (the art world really is a market plus experts plus professionals, and this really is spectacle), an anomaly (maximal infectiousness sitting next to every symptom he lists for the counterfeit), or a kind of case he never anticipated — art whose subject matter and whose effect both essentially involve the market it's mocking, so that any success it has is also a co-optation? If you think it's a genuine problem for him, propose the *smallest* revision that fixes it and then say what that revision costs him somewhere else: his own novels, say, or his verdicts on the moderns he condemns. If you think he's fine as he stands, say which part of the theory does the work and why my worry is misplaced.

Finally, be explicit about his silences. Art about art. Authorship by an anonymous brand or hidden collective. Destruction as a mode of production. Certificates of authenticity as the thing that makes a work a work. Don't invent Tolstoyan views on these to fill space — I'd rather have a short list of what falls off the map.

If it can't, that's itself a finding, and I want it stated as one.

Keep it under about 1200 words, no footnotes, no citations, no "scholars disagree" hedging — just the analysis.
