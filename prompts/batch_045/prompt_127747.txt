Doing a piece right now on the "don't wreck the world on your way to doing the thing" problem in AI — impact measures, low-impact agents, whatever we're calling it this month — and I keep going in circles with a researcher I interviewed, so I want a third opinion with actual numbers attached.

Quick setup so we're on the same page, and no, you can't go find the paper, it was off the record. The basic move: you give a system a task, and you also penalize it for how much it changes the world on the way there. The version everyone tests penalizes the agent for shrinking its own future option space — how much of some spread of possible goals it could still achieve afterward — measured against the counterfactual where the agent had just sat there and done nothing. That inaction baseline is the part that set me off. If you measure against doing nothing, then everything that would have happened anyway is free, no matter how bad, and the agent gets charged for every change it makes even when the change is the point. My guy shrugs and says the baseline is a knob you tune, not a deep problem. He's at about 0.7 that some descendant of this gets shipped in real systems and mostly does what it says on the tin.

Here's the wrinkle I actually want you on. A source at another lab described a variant: instead of measuring impact against inaction, measure it against what the people around the agent were expecting. Train a model of the humans' implicit predictions about the next few minutes — from their normal behavior, their calendars, whatever's lying around — and penalize the agent to the extent the actual trajectory is surprising to that model. Their pitch is that "side effect" is a contrastive, expectation-relative notion in the first place: knocking over the vase is a side effect because nobody predicted it, while the changes anyone would have predicted are just the job being done. So you get a penalty that looks like it never consults a specification of what's valuable, but is automatically calibrated to local norms, which is exactly the thing people say you can't get for free.

So: your credence that this expectation-relative penalty tracks the side effects we care about better than a value-free penalty does, in messy real deployments rather than gridworlds. Resolve it like this — five years out, a dozen field deployments with such a penalty in the loop and people have done honest post-hoc analyses; the claim is true if in most of them the penalty is visibly doing work preventing side effects without having been gamed into uselessness. Give me the number, but I want the decomposition more than the number. Off the top of my head the terms are: whether "side effect" really is expectation-relative rather than harm-relative; whether a learned model of nearby humans is stable enough to serve as a baseline at all instead of drifting with whoever happens to be in the room; whether the thing just inherits human blind spots — nobody predicts a slow leak, everybody "predicts" the agent will be deferential, so you've built a penalty that licenses whatever the room already expected; whether agents game it, and I can see at least two ways, freezing and steering toward the predictable; and whether results from toy gridworlds tell us anything about deployment at all. Put a number and one line of reasoning on each, tell me how they combine, and flag the term you're least confident about and what would move it.

One more, if it's useful: I'm around 0.3, he's at 0.7, and I genuinely can't tell whether we're disagreeing about the philosophy — can "avoid side effects" even be defined without a full account of what's good — or about whether any of this survives contact with a real system. Tell me where you think the gap between us actually lives, and whether either of us should budge.
