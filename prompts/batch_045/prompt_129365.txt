I'm reading a chapter in a popular book about perception that tries to explain the debate over whether cognition penetrates perception, and I'd like to know how much of it to trust. Lightly condensed, the passage runs:

Perception is cognitively penetrable when what you believe, expect, or want reaches down and changes the experience itself — not merely where you look, what you attend to, or how you describe things afterward. The interesting cases are easy to list: a radiologist sees a tumour where a novice sees only noise, a chess master sees a board rather than a scatter of pieces, and a subject told that an ambiguous figure is a duck sees a duck. Everyone agrees these effects are real; the fight is only about how to describe them. But notice that in none of them does cognition add anything new to the scene: it only alters the strength or salience of features the stimulus already supplies. Call that weak penetration, and call genuine penetration — a case where a belief introduces content that isn't there — strong penetration. No experiment has ever demonstrated strong penetration, because every putative case can be explained by attention, by eye movements, or by what the subject was willing to say out loud. So we should accept the modularist verdict that the perceptual systems are informationally encapsulated and impenetrable. This matters because perception is supposed to be the neutral tribunal that checks our beliefs. If a belief can shape experience, experience cannot be independent evidence for it: a penetrated experience is evidentially inert with respect to the proposition that did the penetrating, so the belief simply launders itself through perception.

Something makes me suspicious — the chapter both leans on the expertise cases and then seems to define them out of the debate — but I can't locate the mistake. Please go through the summary claim by claim: which parts a philosopher who defends cognitive penetration would accept, which are reasonable simplifications, and which are misrepresentations. In particular, is the weak/strong distinction one the literature actually draws, and does the chapter's use of it settle the question by stipulation? And if I want a concrete test: suppose a subject's belief that a face is hostile makes the face look hostile purely by amplifying the apparent severity of a scowl that is already visibly there — does that count as weak or strong on the chapter's taxonomy, and does the chapter need it to be weak for its conclusion to stand? Finally, does the epistemological moral at the end follow from the parts of the summary that are right, or is it an overstatement?
