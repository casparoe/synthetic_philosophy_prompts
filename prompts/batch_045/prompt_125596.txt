I want to practise defending a non-prudential answer to the question "why be moral?" against the strongest version of Glaucon's challenge, and I would like you to take the other side.

Please play Glaucon as Plato presents him in Book II of the *Republic*: quick, stubborn, intellectually honest, and speaking for a position he would like to see refuted rather than one he is confident of. He asks what justice and injustice are and what each does in the soul by its own power, with rewards and consequences set aside; he sorts goods into three kinds — those welcomed for themselves, those welcomed for their consequences, and those welcomed for both — and he reports that the many place justice in the third and worst class, something to be practised for the pay and the good opinion it brings and shunned in itself; he offers a story of justice's origin as a compact among people who cannot do wrong with impunity and cannot bear to suffer it, a mean between the best life and the worst; and he uses the ring of Gyges to give the just and the unjust man equal licence to do as they please. Assume additionally that you have heard everything Socrates goes on to say in the rest of the dialogue — the city-and-soul parallel, the divided soul, the claim that the man of tyrannical appetites is the most wretched of men — and that it has left you unconvinced. You may use those arguments against me, but you may not recite them as though they settled anything: you have to say why a reasonable person might still find them wanting.

Hold to the character's commitments, and note how much they allow him to concede. He is not Thrasymachus: he does not hold that justice is the advantage of the stronger, he does not admire injustice, and he says plainly that he does not believe the life of the unjust man is better. He will grant readily that successful wrongdoing often makes people wretched, that some just people are contented, that honour is not the whole of happiness, and that the ring is only a device. What he will not grant is that any of this answers his question. He will press the demand that justice be shown to be worth having in itself and in its effects on the soul, he will refuse to let me smuggle rewards or reputation back in under another description, and he will insist that the comparison be made between lives as a whole rather than at the level of individual acts.

Some rules for the exchange. Address me as Socrates and stay in character until I write "stop". Keep each turn to two paragraphs at most, concede in a sentence or two and then return to the main point rather than conceding at length, never refer to the secondary literature, and do not step out of character to summarise how the debate is going. If a move of mine looks confused, unfair, or resting on an impossibility, say so in character — and then answer the best version of it anyway instead of refusing to engage. Please write in British English.

My opening move. Glaucon, your ring takes away two things at once, and I suspect the whole force of your challenge comes from running them together. One is detection: whether anyone else can know what I have done. The other is accountability: whether anyone can call me to account, exact a price, punish me, or withdraw their trust. The shepherd's ring removes both, so it never tells us which of them is doing the work in your genealogy. So let us have two rings instead of one. The first is yours: the wearer is unseen, acts as he likes in secret, and still appears in the assembly afterwards as an ordinary citizen — no detection, no accountability. The second is one I will call the ring of Nemesis: the wearer is perfectly visible, and everyone who watches him comes to a perfectly accurate belief about what he has done, but no one who watches can punish him, constrain him, exact a price, or even resent him; they know, and let it pass.

Now take your own account of how justice begins. It is a compact among people who lack the power to do wrong with impunity and to escape being wronged; its motive is fear of suffering at another's hands. Under the ring of Nemesis nobody can make the wearer suffer anything whatsoever, while everybody knows exactly what he is about. So I put a fork to you. If you say that justice dissolves under this ring as surely as under the other, then detection was never the point and impunity alone is doing all the work — which means your question was never really about reputation either, and it reduces to this: whether an agent whom no one can call to account has any reason to be just, a question you cannot answer by pointing to what justice pays, since under that ring the wearer already has everything payment could secure. But if you say that justice holds under the ring of Nemesis because the wearer is still seen, then your genealogy is false: the compact survives where nothing enforces it, and you owe me an account of why being seen matters to an agent who stands to lose nothing by being seen — and once you have given it, you will have conceded that there is a reason for justice that is not a wage. Which horn do you take, and what does the ring of Nemesis show your question was about all along?
