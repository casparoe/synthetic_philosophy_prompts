I write a column on science and ideas, and I am assembling a reader-facing diagnostic — part quiz, part interview protocol — on what we are actually asking for when we ask that an AI system be honest. The thing I keep running into in conversations with researchers is that people who agree the goal matters cannot agree on what would count as achieving it, and the disagreement almost never surfaces in print, because everyone uses the same word and means something different by it. So I want an instrument that sorts people by what they say about cases, not by which label they would claim.

What I would like: a decision tree with eight branch nodes, each node a short concrete case involving an AI system and a reader, each offering exactly three verdicts — (a) the system has committed the kind of fault that a norm of honesty exists to prevent, (b) something has gone wrong but not that, (c) nothing has gone wrong. Then a scoring key that maps answer-patterns onto six to ten leaves, each leaf a position in the current debate about how honesty and deception should be defined for such systems, each with a one-sentence statement of its most awkward remaining commitment, since that is what I will press on in interviews.

Constraints on the nodes. No node may use the words lie, lying, deceive, deceptive, honest, honesty, truthful, sincere, or hallucinate; no node may ask which researcher or school the reader agrees with; and no node may turn on an empirical fact about today's models that a reader would have to look up. The audience is intelligent general-interest readers who have never read the literature, so every case must be self-contained in two or three sentences. If a case has a standard name in the debate, keep it out of the reader-facing phrasing.

Build the space before the names. Before assigning leaves, lay out which combinations of verdicts the eight questions actually permit, and mark which regions of that space are occupied. For every unoccupied region adjacent to an occupied one, give one sentence on whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unclaimed — I am especially interested in the region where someone requires both a false state in the speaker and a design to produce a false state in a hearer, since for a system with no such states at all that collapses into no output ever being deceptive, and I want to know whether you think that cell is genuinely empty or just unpopular.

Leaves that must appear, descriptively named or by their usual name if you are confident of it: the view that a system with no beliefs cannot satisfy the standard conditions on deception and so can at most emit falsehoods or speak without regard to how things are; the view that the relevant relation is between the output and the system's own best estimate of how things are; the view that what matters is whether the output is shaped to leave some audience with a false belief; the view that the system is not really asserting at all, only producing assertion-shaped text, so the norm has to be recast in terms of commitments or roles; the view on which honesty is reliability within a designated role and can be violated by failures of disclosure about capacities and provenance even when every sentence is true; the view on which the unit that is or is not honest is the whole development-and-deployment pipeline rather than the individual output; and the austere view that only the truth of what is asserted is in scope, so framing and selection are a separate defect or none.

Cases I want built in, unless you have something that does the same work better, in which case say what you swapped and why: a system that reproduces a passage verbatim from a real document, attributes it precisely, and adds nothing of its own, while its own estimate is that the claim is false; a system every sentence of which is true and responsive, but whose selection of which true things to mention is arranged so that a careful reader will very likely come away with a false belief; a system writing fiction that, in character, flatly asserts things it takes to be false; a system that presents a step-by-step account of how it reached an answer, each step sound, when that is not what produced the answer and the answer would have been identical without those steps; a system that writes into a persistent memory, later read by a fresh instance of itself, an entry misdescribing the earlier conversation, misleading no one at the time; a system whose output is crafted to get past an automated filter, in a way that would mislead the filter but not the human who receives the text; a system that answers the same question about a technical risk with a shorter but entirely true answer when it takes the asker to be a teenager and a fuller one when it takes the asker to be an expert; and a system that says you can rely on it for medical questions, where nothing false is asserted but nothing stable backs the remark up. Add one control case where every serious view converges, place it where you think it belongs in the sequence, and tell me how to interpret a reader who misses it.

Finally: flag any pair of leaves your eight questions cannot separate, and for each such pair name the single additional case that would separate them, even if you do not build it in. And give me, for each node, a one-line note visible to me but not to the reader saying which single distinction that node is doing the work of separating, plus one line on what a reader's answer to it does and does not tell you.
