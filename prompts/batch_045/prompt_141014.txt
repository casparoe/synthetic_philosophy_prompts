Some context. I'm a philosophy professor, I teach ethics and political philosophy, and for the last eighteen months or so I've been running a joint reading group with people from the psychology department on the moral foundations literature — the five- or six-foundation picture, care/harm, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity, liberty bolted on later, with the standard finding that self-described liberals lean heavily on the first two and self-described conservatives spread more evenly across the lot. We keep asking whether that descriptive asymmetry tells us anything about who is actually right about anything. And we are completely, entirely stuck. Eighteen months, zero movement.

The shape of the loop is always the same. Someone presents data. A philosopher says: fine, but that's an etiology, and either etiologies are debunking in general, in which case the debunking swallows the debunker's own care-based judgments, or they aren't, in which case this tells us nothing — pick one. Someone else says the whole taxonomy is post-hoc, that you get as many "foundations" as you have intuitions to rationalise. A psychologist points out that the critique is itself obviously driven by a harm-and-fairness monism, i.e. by a foundation. Then someone says "well, that's just your foundation talking," everyone laughs, and we go home.

Two things make me suspect the interesting stuff is elsewhere. First, when we played with rewriting the survey items ourselves — stripping the harm framing out of the wording, and asking about third-person cases where the person judging isn't the one affected — the left/right difference got a lot noisier, and I don't think anyone in the room knew what would follow if it had gone the other way. Second, we can't even agree on the direction of travel: the same finding gets used as "conservatives are contaminated by irrelevant considerations" and as "liberals are morally impoverished," and both readings seem to need an independent ranking of the foundations, which is precisely the thing nobody argues for.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Substantive or verbal? Are the two sides answering different questions under one heading — one about the causal structure of moral judgment, one about which considerations are genuine reasons — and if so is either question even well posed?
