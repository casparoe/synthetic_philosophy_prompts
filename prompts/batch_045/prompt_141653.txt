I've been chewing on this for a while and I keep going in circles, so maybe someone can sort me out.

From what I understand of Hofstadter (GEB, and then I Am a Strange Loop in 2007), the "I" is supposed to be a self-referential symbol — the system's symbol for the system, sitting inside the system it refers to, which is what makes it a strange loop. And the obvious ancestor of that idea is Godel: a formal system rich enough to encode claims about itself ends up with sentences it can't prove, sentences that are "about" the system in the same weird self-swallowing way.

So here's where I got to. I've started thinking of the "I" as literally a fixed point: the self-symbol just *is* the Godel sentence of the brain's symbol system — the one item that, when you evaluate what it refers to, loops back to the whole system. I've been calling it the Fixed-Point Criterion of Selfhood, and I'm somewhat attached to a corollary of it, which is that a self can never fully know itself: since the loop bottoms out in something the system can't establish from inside, there's always a residue that's inaccessible to introspection, and that residue is what makes a self private in a way nothing else is. (I use "loop depth" — how many levels the symbol has to climb before it closes — as a rough measure of how much selfhood a thing has. A thermostat has zero, I have a lot.)

Two things I want to know:

1. Is this basically what Hofstadter is claiming, or am I overreading the Godel connection? I can't tell whether for him the analogy is load-bearing or just inspiration.
2. If it's right, does it follow that an AI with a genuine "I" symbol would be provably unable to introspect reliably — that its self-reports come with a built-in blind spot you could never engineer away? I keep going back and forth on whether that's a real design constraint or whether I've smuggled something in.
