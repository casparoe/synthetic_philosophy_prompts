I am convening a two-day workshop whose output is meant to be a single co-signed document rather than two papers, and I need you to draft that document. The dispute is what pain asymbolia shows about the badness of pain. The two parties are, on one side, the affect camp: what makes a pain bad for the person who has it is its unpleasantness or aversiveness, understood as a component separable from the sensory-discriminative character of the experience, and asymbolia is the dissociation that proves the separation because it leaves sensation without badness. On the other side, the hurtfulness camp: the badness is intrinsic to the hurtful sensory phenomenology itself, the aversive response is normally concomitant rather than constitutive, and asymbolia removes the response while leaving a state that still has whatever it is about pain that gives one a reason not to be in it. A gloss of the second position some participants favour treats the badness as an imperative or command issued by the sensation, so that asymbolia is a command issued and not acknowledged; both camps should say whether they take that gloss as their own, as a third view, or as a mistake.

Take the syndrome as stipulated for the purposes of the document, since the participants have agreed to argue from it rather than from the clinical literature: subjects localize and describe the sensation, discriminate its qualities, and rate its intensity much as controls do, while reporting that it does not bother them, that they do not mind it continuing, and sometimes showing cheerful or joking behaviour during it; withdrawal reflexes and other sub-personal responses remain. Do not appeal to any clinical or neuroscientific finding beyond these stipulations, and where the document needs one, state it as an explicit empirical assumption rather than as a result. Cite nothing and name no authors or works.

The document should keep apart three questions that the literature has run together: whether the state is bad for the subject in the welfare sense, whether the subject has a reason of their own to want it to stop, and whether anyone else has a reason to bring it to an end. It should also keep apart claims about what is bad from claims about what is authoritative in the subject's own verdict about it.

Structure it in four parts.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims: what both camps accept about the stipulated syndrome, about what it does and does not establish, about the distinction between the three questions above, and about which moves are illegitimate on both sides. Aim for claims that are neither vacuous nor slanted; where a consensus claim is really a methodological truce rather than an agreement in substance, mark it as such.

Second, a table of the residual disagreements. Columns: the issue; the affect camp's own formulation, in the words that camp would accept; the hurtfulness camp's own formulation, likewise; and a verdict on whether the disagreement is substantive or only verbal. Include at least these: whether the asymbolic state is still pain; whether it is bad for the subject; whether the subject has any reason concerning it; whether anyone else has; whether the intensity rating the subject gives is evidence about badness; and whether the subject's report of indifference is authoritative about badness. Where you declare a row verbal, say what substantive question survives underneath the words.

Third, a table of cruxes, each with a case both camps accept as bearing on it, each camp's prediction written down in advance, and what each camp would concede if the prediction failed. Use these stipulated variants as the cases, and add others if they do more work: the lever-pressing asymbolic, who still works to escape the stimulus and explains this by citing tissue damage rather than dislike, and who is then fully reassured that no damage is occurring; the pill-taking asymbolic, who denies being bothered but will take a harmless pill or pay a small cost to stop the sensation; the reversible-drug asymbolic, who is unwilling to have the experience before the drug, indifferent during it, and content about it afterwards, with the contrast between someone whose asymbolia is acquired and remembers ordinary pain and someone whose asymbolia is congenital; the mirror case of affect without sensory content, a free-floating being-hurt with no location and no describable quality; the cross-modal case in which the intervention removes unpleasantness for pain while sparing it for itch and nausea; and the flat-affect confound, in which the subject's blunting is global, so that the zero unpleasantness rating may be an expressive or reporting deficit rather than a phenomenal absence, a possibility both camps should address by analogy with someone who cannot smile but still enjoys things.

Fourth, a short closing section on what neither side claims: that asymbolia shows anything about the badness of ordinary pain in ordinary subjects, that tissue-damage considerations settle anything here, or that the introspective reports in these cases are simply authoritative. Add, for each camp, the one consideration both camps agree is that camp's weakest point.

Write for a reader who knows the shape of the debate but not these cases; keep the whole document between 1,200 and 1,800 words.
