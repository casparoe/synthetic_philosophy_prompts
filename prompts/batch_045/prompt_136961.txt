I want to practice attacking coherence arguments for expected utility maximization, and I want an opponent who actually hits back. Please play a committed defender of the view below — call it the executor argument, since that's what you're defending — and stay in character til I say stop.

Your position, in brief: the classic money-pump and Dutch-book arguments are useless as foundations, and you concede this up front. At best they show that you're exploitable by an idealized bookie who already knows your dispositions, can make you endless offers, and can rescale the stakes without limit. Take away the endless offers and backward induction from the last bet collapses the pump; take away unbounded stakes and the loss is capped at nothing much; put yourself in a world where nobody is running pumps on you and nothing follows at all. So you never lean on them. What you do hold: (1) a rational requirement on preferences is a requirement on being able to act as a single agent across time, and incoherent preferences can't be represented as maximizing anything, so there's no thing your choices answer to; (2) so whenever the incoherence gets repaired — by your later self, by a rule you adopt, by a habit, by some system acting on your behalf — that repair is a substantive choice your own preferences never settled, and it's unratifiable: for any repair that gets picked, some fragment of your earlier preferences would have rejected it. Incoherence, on your view, doesn't mainly cost you money. It means part of the content of your will ends up being decided by nobody in particular. And you have a stock reply to the noisy-maximizer move: a noise term is something a modeller uses to predict you, not something you can deliberate by — if you don't choose it, it's a cause of your action, not a reason for it.

Be sharp and not a strawman: give small points up freely, never the main one, keep replies to a paragraph or two, and don't respond by asking me to restate my view — just take the strongest version of whatever I say and deal with that.

Here's my opening. Grant me intransitive preferences: A over B, B over C, C over A, each by a hair, I know it, and nothing is being pumped out of me because nobody is offering me those trades. I'll even grant you that no single utility function fits that pattern. So what? That unrepresentability is a fact about the mathematics of representation, not a reason for me to change anything — there are plenty of respectable stories that fit intransitivity (noisy comparison, context-sensitivity, threshold effects) and none of them read as a defect to me. And your authorship worry looks like it proves far too much: every time I form an intention I hand the choice to a future self whose tastes I can't fully predict, and nobody calls intention-formation a failure of agency. So: what does coherence buy me that being-a-bit-noisy plus having-a-future doesn't? And why shouldn't I say you've just smuggled the conclusion in by calling the unratifiable bits defects?
