For about two years I have been trying to think through a claim that I first encountered as a description of my own life and only later found stated as a thesis in welfare ethics: that the only thing good for a person is the absence of suffering and of craving, and that pleasant experience has no prudential value of its own — it only seems to have some because almost everything we call a pleasure is the felt ending of an itch, a hunger, a tension, a worry, and that ending borrows its apparent worth from the badness of what it ends. Call this view T.

I have some stake in it. I am eighteen months into recovery from opioid use, and the machinery that ran my life was precisely a device for manufacturing cravings in order to cash them in for relief; the relief was never worth the craving, and T says why in a way nothing else I have read quite does. But a friend in my reading group has pressed an argument on me that I cannot find the exit from, and it makes T look like it proves far too much. I would be grateful for your help with it.

The argument turns on a distinction I had not made before. There are cravings: unpleasant, agitating states that demand to be quieted. And there are what I will call urges: directed impulses that are not themselves unpleasant — the pull to hear how a melody resolves, the nudge to finish a sentence you have started, the interest that carries you through a problem, the impulse to scratch a moment before the itch registers as an itch. Urges are satisfied, and their satisfaction is mildly pleasant, but while they last they do not hurt.

(1) T: a life goes better for its subject only by containing less suffering and less craving.

(2) Suppose a device that implants in an otherwise contented subject a steady stream of urges and promptly lets each one be satisfied. No urge is left frustrated for more than a moment, and no urge is unpleasant while it lasts.

(3) On the narrow reading of "craving" — craving is an unpleasant unrest — the subject on the device has no more suffering and no more craving than a subject sitting quietly with nothing to do.

(4) The subject's satisfactions are pleasant experiences. By the account of pleasure that T relies on, each is nothing but the quieting of an urge, and so it carries no value beyond the removal it effects.

(5) Now scale it: the stream can be made continuous and intense, and run for years. At every moment the subject's experience is pleasant; at every moment, asked how it is going, the subject prefers this life to the quiet one, and the preference rests on no false belief. Yet by (1), (3) and (4) this life is exactly as good for its subject as the quiet one. An arbitrarily large quantity of pleasant experience — a whole lifetime of it — is worth, on T, precisely nothing.

(6) That already seems to me to prove too much, and it is not abstract for me. For several months after I stopped using, I had almost no craving and no pain worth mentioning; what I had was that nothing pulled me. Nothing was worth starting. On T, provided those months were not unpleasant, they were not worse than the months since, in which things pull me again. I cannot accept that, and I do not think it is only self-deception: it is the one thing in this neighbourhood I am most confident of.

(7) And the narrow reading is not free either. If "craving" is instead read broadly, so that any directed desire or impulsion counts, then ordinary interest, curiosity and absorption are cravings, and so is the desire to relieve suffering. T then says that a life spent relieving suffering is worse along the relevant dimension than a life of blank stillness, and that someone who had fully attained what T recommends would have no motivation left to do anything — including to bring about the very absence of suffering that T says is all that matters.

(8) So T either reduces an unlimited amount of pleasant experience to zero prudential value, or it collapses into a quietism that cannot motivate its own practical upshot. Something has gone wrong.

Which step would you reject, and at which horn? I am not attached to T as stated, and I am happy to be told the device in (2) is incoherent or that the distinction it rests on cannot be drawn where I have drawn it. What I would very much like to keep is the claim that the chasing life was worse and that my flat months were worse too. If the cost of keeping those is giving up the account of pleasure in (4), I would like to know what a view that denies it then owes an explanation of — why relief should feel the way it does, and why it should feel the way it does in proportion to what it relieves.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the step you would reject, and the horn you would take — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and without restating the argument back to me. As you go, I would like you to say how confident you are in what you claim and to single out the point you are least sure of; in particular, tell me how much of your answer turns on how "craving" should be read, and whether the argument's force comes mostly from that reading or from somewhere else.

One more thing, if it is not too much: the same worry seems to reach the desire that is supposed to motivate the view itself — if wanting to be free of craving is itself a craving, then the view appears to recommend a state from which nothing, including its own pursuit, can be wanted. If that is a separate confusion rather than the same one, say so briefly; if it is the same one, tell me where it shows up in the numbered steps.
