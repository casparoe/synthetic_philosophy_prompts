I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on probability and conditionals — 16 students, one 75-minute session. They have had conditional probability, the axioms, and the betting/Dutch-book reading of credences, but nothing on the triviality literature; the two weeks after this one cover the suppositional treatment of conditionals and then possible-worlds semantics, so this session should leave a problem sitting on the table that those views have to answer.

I want a classroom activity that makes them feel the force of Lewis's triviality results before anyone names them: not a proof on the board, not an illustration, but a game in which they run into the collapse themselves and only afterwards learn what they ran into. Constraints: no real money and no stakes beyond play money or points; no lecture up front beyond five minutes on how a called-off bet is settled; the label and Lewis's name should not appear until after the collapse.

Here is the argument I want them to hit. Please check it and tell me plainly if any step needs a premise I have not named, or if I have it wrong. Setup: a fair die, A = the roll is 1, 2, 3 or 4, C = the roll is 1, D = if A then C. Premises: (i) the fair price of a conditional bet — stake placed only if A, called off and refunded if A fails — is P(C|A) = 1/4 of the stake, and nobody disputes this; (ii) the equation: the conditional sentence D expresses a proposition of its own with a price, and that price equals the conditional-bet price, so P(D) = P(C|A) = 1/4; (iii) D is an ordinary proposition in the algebra — buyable, sellable, negatable, and available as something to condition on; (iv) stability: (ii) holds not just in the opening market but after any public announcement E, so in the market conditional on C the price of D is P(C|A∧C) = 1, and in the market conditional on ¬C it is P(C|A∧¬C) = 0; (v) the case is non-degenerate: P(A∧C) > 0 and P(A∧¬C) > 0. From (iv), D is worth a dollar whenever C holds and nothing whenever C fails, so D and C are the same ticket and P(D) = P(C) = 1/6 — which contradicts (ii). And the general shape is that the equation plus stability forces P(C|A) = P(C) for every non-degenerate pair, i.e. learning A never moves your price on C.

My rough idea for the arc, which you should keep only if it is the best thing you can build: open by eliciting prices on A and on C and getting conditional bets priced with no resistance; then ask for a price on a ticket that pays on D and let them notice that 1/4 is not available in a six-cell space; let them escape by enriching the space with an extra randomizer, which forces a gerrymandered truth table; then close the trap with the rule that encodes (iv), which is exactly what forbids that truth table. Replace it with something better if you can, but commit to one design — a single runnable activity, not a menu of options.

Please give me: the rules as participants will encounter them (what a team must post, what it may refuse, and what happens when it cannot comply); roles, including who plays the house or auditor and whether that should be a student; a materials list; a scoring or payoff scheme that needs no real money and that rewards holding a coherent price sheet under pressure; a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes; and a debrief that maps the moments where things went wrong onto the premises above and onto the steps of the derivation. The debrief should also say which premise is doing the real damage and where a student who refuses it thinks they have gotten off cheaply.

Then be honest about two things. First, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I expect the conditional-bet/bet-on-a-conditional conflation, the granularity of the die being mistaken for the point, degenerate cases with zero-probability antecedents, uneven finishing times, and at least one student insisting D has no truth value when A is false — and how I should handle each without turning the session into a lecture. Second, what the activity cannot do: my sense is that the collapse is manufactured by house rules that a philosopher could simply decline, that a single pair in a finite space cannot display the generality of the result over a language closed under the conditional, and that the game cannot tell the difference between the available escape routes, so the facilitator must supply those and their costs. Tell me if there is more that belongs on that list.

Finally, commit. Give me one activity with definite choices and your reasons for them; take a definite position on which premise the debrief should nudge students toward surrendering and what that position costs them; flag any step of my reconstruction that is mistaken or under-supported; and add a short note on where your design depends on one particular reading of the triviality results rather than on another.
