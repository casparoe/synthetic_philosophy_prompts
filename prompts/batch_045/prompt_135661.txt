I TA'd the trolley section of our intro ethics course this spring and it went badly, not because the students were obtuse but because every time they pushed on a variant I found myself reaching for a different principle, and by the end I genuinely couldnt tell whether I had a view or just a pile of case verdicts. I'm trying to write down where I've landed before I run the section again in the fall, and I want you to audit the package as a whole rather than explain the doctrine of double effect to me — I know the textbook version, the intending/foreseeing bit, the proportionality bit, all of that. Here's the set, roughly in the order I noticed myself committed to it:

(1) The standard pair: flipping the switch so the trolley kills one on the side track instead of five on the main line is permissible; shoving the fat man off the footbridge so his body stops the trolley is not. I think that's a real moral difference, not just a quirk of our psychology that we should debunk and move on from.
(2) What explains it is the clause about means — the death must not be what's doing the work in producing the good outcome. I want that to be a claim about the causal structure of the action, and I'm suspicious of leaning on what the agent "really" has in mind; two agents could have identical mental states and I'd still want to say the cases differ.
(3) I think the doing/allowing distinction is junk — killing and letting die differ in no moral way as such, and I don't think that distinction is secretly what's separating my verdicts in (1).
(4) The loop variant, where the side track curves back around so that switching only helps if something stops the trolley, and the only thing that will stop it is the body of a fat man stuck on that track. I'm fairly confident it's still permissible to switch, and I want to keep that even if we stipulate that a merely unconscious body sprawled on the rails wouldn't do the job — it's the business of his being crushed under it that stops it.
(5) A case I invented specifically to annoy myself and now can't let go of: a precision strike on a munitions factory; children living nearby will die; and part of how the strike ends the war is that the grief over those deaths breaks the enemy's will to fight. So the deaths are genuinely on the causal route to the good outcome, they aren't just foreseen alongside it. My gut says this is no different from ordinary strategic bombing where the deaths are pure side effects, i.e. permissible. But on the clause in (2) their deaths look like a means, which should sink it — and every way I've tried of softening "means" enough to rescue (5) seems to also rescue shoving the fat man in (1), which I don't want.
(6) Numbers matter to me: other things equal I ought to minimize deaths, and five-versus-one is a big part of why I flip the switch at all.
(7) The transplant case — surgeon kills the one healthy patient for his organs to save five — is impermissible, but I think that's not really a double-effect thing at all; it's about her role. She has a job and that isn't it.

So: which of these are actually in tension, and how badly? Please rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally awful, and for each one say what exactly has to give and what that costs me somewhere else in the set — in particular, if the cheapest repair for (5) breaks (1) or (4), say that explicitly. Also flag the pairs that only look like they conflict and say why they don't, because I've started to suspect I'm flagging tensions that aren't there and walking straight past the one that is. If there's a single revision that cleans up more than one of these at once, tell me which.
