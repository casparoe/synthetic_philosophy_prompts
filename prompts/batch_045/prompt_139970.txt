I'm a data scientist — ranking and recsys, A/B tests, survey sampling, some causal inference — and I've fallen into a rabbit hole about lottocracy, the replace-elections-with-random-selection stuff. I half feel like I get it and half feel like I'm being sold a metaphor, so I want to force the translation.

Restate the case for sortition in the vocabulary I actually use. Not "a random sample is representative," which I allready get — I mean the substantive arguments: that elections select for the wrong traits, that descriptive representation matters and not just as a proxy for good outcomes, that random selection is immune to faction and rent-seeking in a way voting isn't, that a small deliberating body can produce something better than the population's raw opinion. Put those in terms of: sampling frames and who gets to define them, estimators and their bias and variance, covariate balance, stratification, adversarial optimization and Goodhart's law, distribution shift, averaging over an ensemble versus picking the single best predictor, and the gap between a population parameter and a causal estimand defined under an intervention. Be faithful, and flag every place where you're smuggling something in rather than translating.

Two bits I care about most, because they're the ones I haven't seen handled:

First, there's a design that gets floated where you don't sample one-person-one-chance, you sample in proportion to how much each person stands to be affected by the decisions that body will make while they're sitting in it. In my terms that looks like importance weights under covariate shift, or oversampling a rare high-cost subgroup. Translate it both directions and then tell me if it even hangs together: what would the estimand have to be for stake-weighting to be the unbiased estimator of it, and if there isn't one, what's the actual argument for it?

Second, mini-publics deliberate, and afterwards the members' views are usually not what a fresh sample off the street would say. I can't tell whether to call that drift. If the target is what the public would think if it were properly informed, that's an intervention-dependent quantity — do(everyone gets briefed and talks it through for three days) — and the mini-public is an attempt to estimate it with a tiny n and no control group, where the briefing materials and the facilitators are themselves part of the treatment. But if the target is descriptive representation, then the same drift just is the sample ceasing to be a sample. Which is the real target? Can one position want both without equivocating, and what would I call the failure mode where the treatment is doing all the work?

Then the residue report: what in the sortition literature has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? My guesses, tell me if I'm wrong — legitimacy and consent, since my sampled units don't consent, don't owe me anything, and coercing someone into office is just a cost I have no line for; and authority that isn't accuracy, and the "will of the people" thing, which smells like being asked for a population parameter when nobody has said what the variable is. And the other direction: is there anywhere my vocabulary is richer in a way that hands the sortition people a problem they've underrated? I keep thinking whoever controls the sampling frame, the stratification and the briefing is the real sovereign, and in my world that's just the boring obvious point that whoever collects and labels the data determines everything downstream — but maybe it's less boring when the output is law.
