Hi there. I'm putting together the take-home final for my final-year philosophy of mind seminar, and I want one essay question on pain asymbolia and whether pain is essentially bad. Problem is, everyone in the room has already absorbed the standard two-component story (sensory-discriminative versus affective-motivational) and can recite it half-asleep, so a straight "does asymbolia show that pain's unpleasantness is detachable?" question will just get me twelve identical papers. I want something that forces them to work out *which* essentialist claim a case actually threatens, and which one it leaves untouched.

Rough plan: hand them two stipulated cases that dissociate in different directions, and make them say what follows from each.

Case one, Dana: after a lesion she keeps the full sensory profile — she locates pinpricks to within a millimetre, tells burning from stabbing from aching, and ranks stimuli by intensity more accurately than unlesioned controls — and she keeps the withdrawal reflex and learns to avoid a paired stimulus. But she takes no analgesics, never asks for the stimulation to stop, and says consistently that it doesn't bother her and that she'd be perfectly happy for it to go on indefinitely. When pressed, she says something along these lines: it's not that it feels fine, it feels the way it must feel for you when it hurts, it's just that it doesn't matter to her. Now the bit I've made up: the dissociation is temporal too. Ten minutes before a trial she knows is coming she is measurably anxious, will pay to have it cancelled, and says she is dreading it; immediately afterwards she says that was horrible and she is glad it's over — while insisting that during it, it wasn't unpleasant and didn't bother her. Please stipulate away generalised anxiety and any memory impairment.

Case two, Theo: same lesion, same preserved sensory profile, but the dissociation runs the other way. Theo says the pain is exactly as unpleasant as it always was, rates it nine out of ten, calls it awful — but he has lost every inclination to do anything about it. No withdrawal, no reaching for analgesics, he will sit there as long as you like, and he reports no desire whatsoever for it to stop.

Then the question: is pain essentially bad? And, more usefully, which of these two cases does any real work against which version of that claim.

What I'd like from you:

1. The question exactly as I'd put it on the sheet — vignettes and all, 90 minutes, 1200 word limit — written so that simply reciting the two-component distinction scores almost nothing.
2. A model answer of about 700 words.
3. A marking scheme out of 25, built around five must-hit points, with the marks for each one and, for each, a single line on how people normally get it wrong. Say explicitly what an A answer does that a B answer doesn't.

Two housekeeping things. No citations, bibliographies or reading lists anywhere, please — I mark these blind and I definately don't want them name-dropping papers I haven't read. And the model answer itself has to be written in American English (our external examiner is American and the department made a ruling about it), while the question sheet and the marking notes can stay British.

I'm not at all sure myself where the right answer lies, so don't hedge the model answer into mush: commit to a line, show where the marks sit, and let me worry about whether I agree.
