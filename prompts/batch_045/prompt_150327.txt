I run a weekly reading group on decision theory for graduate students in economics and philosophy, and I've been building a set of teaching dialogues in which two competent interlocutors argue out a live disagreement rather than lecture at each other. The next topic is the Kelly criterion and the "geometric rationality" case for maximizing long-run growth rate, and I'd like you to write that dialogue. The setup I want them to fight over is a deliberately nonstandard version of the usual repeated favorable bet, so that neither side can win by reciting the textbook case. There is a syndicate of N = 50 gamblers facing an indefinite sequence of rounds. In each round every gambler stakes a fraction f of their current wealth on an even-money coin that comes up heads with probability 0.6: heads multiplies the stake by 2 (so wealth goes to W(1+f)), tails forfeits the stake (wealth goes to W(1−f)), and each gambler's coin is independent of everyone else's. The syndicate sets one policy f for all its members. At the end of each round a redistribution rule runs: every member's wealth is moved a fraction r of the way toward the cohort's mean wealth, where r is fixed in advance (r = 0 means each gambler is effectively playing alone and keeping their own results; r = 1 means the whole cohort pools and re-splits its wealth every round). Finally, the game does not run forever: at the end of each round there is a constant probability h = 0.02 that the game is shut down and everyone keeps what they have, so the number of rounds is geometrically distributed with mean 50.

I want the argument to turn on what the optimal f is, and on what follows from that for how a single isolated gambler ought to reason. Please have the characters work through, out loud and in arithmetic the reader can check: why the isolated case (N = 1, or r = 0) favors f* = 2p − 1 = 0.2 as the maximizer of p·log(1+f) + (1−p)·log(1−f); why maximizing expected wealth, or maximizing the expected value of a power/CRRA utility of terminal wealth, either disagrees with that or agrees with it only because of a scale-invariance coincidence, so that "Kelly" is not automatically a constraint on rationality as such; why the fully pooled case pulls the optimum toward f = 1, since the cohort's wealth then grows at essentially the arithmetic average of the individual returns and no individual can be ruined while the pool survives; and how the optimum moves as r and N vary, with at least one explicit intermediate calculation rather than hand-waving. The deeper fight I want surfaced is whether the isolated gambler is the right unit of analysis at all: one character should press the time-average-versus-ensemble-average point (an individual lives one realized path, and the path's growth rate is what compounds), and the other should press the rejoinder that a collection of independent bets that is periodically merged is, in some sense, the same decision problem viewed at a different scale — so what exactly licenses caring about the geometric mean of one's own wealth rather than the arithmetic mean of the pool one could have joined? Both characters should also be forced to be honest about the stopping rule: if one of them invokes the 0.02 shutdown hazard to blunt the usual claim that Kelly dominates any essentially different strategy with probability one as the horizon goes to infinity, the other should be allowed to point out precisely what the geometric horizon does and does not deliver — in particular, that a shutdown time independent of wealth multiplies the expected number of rounds of log growth rather than changing which f maximizes expected log terminal wealth, and that the hazard therefore only helps the anti-Kelly side given some further assumption about bounded or non-multiplicative utility.

Format: a dialogue of roughly 1,100 to 1,400 words with speaker labels, between two characters of your invention who are equally competent — neither should be a slow learner or a caricature, and each should concede at least one genuinely damaging point made by the other rather than the two of them talking past each other. Keep it fully self-contained: no citations, no bibliographic pointers, no appeal to what some author or paper argued, and no jargon left undefined; every contested claim should be settled by an explicit computation or a small explicit example in the text. Write the arithmetic in plain English as well as in symbols where possible, since some of my students are philosophers who get nervous around expectations. Close the dialogue with each character stating, in one or two sentences, exactly what they now take the remaining disagreement to be and what kind of consideration would settle it — not a vague agreement to disagree.
