I'm retired — 30-odd years as an atmosperic chemist, which means I spent a whole career putting error bars on things nobody could measure directly, and now I've picked up a philosophy bug and I'm reading my way through strategic theory. So: give me a number, with the working shown.

Here's the setup, and part of it is invented on purpose. Call it the clockwork retaliator. A nuclear-armed state has the standard problem: no adversary quite believes its leader's threat to retaliate, because by the time retaliation is called for, carrying it out is pure self-destruction and everyone can see that. So the state solves the credibility problem the way an engineer would — it takes the human out of the loop. Pre-delegated launch authority, automated triggers (loss of contact with national command, confirmed inbound tracks, a timer that fires if nobody authenticates in time), and machine-assisted sensor fusion that compresses detection-to-decision from tens of minutes down to single digits. The appeal is obvious: you get the deterrence benefits of seeming irrational without anyone having to actually be irrational. Call it mechanical commitment, as against the older trick of cultivating a reputation for recklessness and leaving part of the risk to chance. I gather real systems of roughly this shape have been built, but don't spend your answer recapping that — I want your number.

The proposition, as precisely as I can state it: take a severe crisis between two nuclear-armed states, both with survivable second-strike forces, forces and targeting doctrine held fixed, so that the only thing that varies is the decision architecture. Give me your credence that a clockwork-retaliator posture on one or both sides makes nuclear use in that crisis *more* likely than a human-in-the-loop posture would, rather than less. I've been sitting around 0.6 on "more likely" and I can't really say why.

The terms I care about, though add any I've missed: (a) whether mechanical commitment actually buys credibility at all — does the adversary believe the trigger works and can't be quietly switched off, and does removing human judgment also remove the bargaining room that brinkmanship needed? (b) the false-positive rate — what a real early-warning and command system's per-crisis error rate plausibly is, given that well-documented false alarms have happened and the humans in those loops overrode them; (c) whether a triggered mechanism is something an adversary can trip on purpose — a conventional strike on the sensors, a spoofed track, a cyber intrusion — which gives you the catalytic-war scenario and a new reason to preempt; (d) selection effects: which states would actually adopt this, presumably the ones least confident in their own command-and-control, i.e. the ones whose hardware is worst; (e) whether the metric is even the right one, since a posture can make each individual crisis less likely to blow up and still raise the chance that one blows up over fifty years, and I want to know which of those you're scoring; (f) whether the escalation path you're pricing is deliberate (someone rationally pushing and miscalculating) or inadvertent (the system doing exactly what it was built to do).
