Two people on my team got into an argument over lunch that ran way past lunch, and they've asked me to referee it, which I am not qualified to do.

We run a novelty detector on sensor streams (vibration, current draw, flow) off a few hundred municipal water pumps; when it fires, an engineer drives out and looks at the pump. The fight started with one particular fire: the engineer found early cavitation — pitting on the impeller.

Sam: good fire. That's a genuinely different mechanism producing those readings, so the detector was right to call it novel.

Priya: no it isn't. Cavitation is just the pump running near the edge of its normal envelope. Same parts, same equations, same physics that generated the training data — we simply never sampled that corner of it.

Sam: then "novel" has no content. If every mechanism change is just a reparamaterisation of the same equations, then nothing is ever novel and the detector can't be wrong about novelty, only about density.

Priya: fine, give me a criterion instead of a gut feeling. Mine is intervention-invariance: two setups involve the same mechanism iff they respond the same way to the interventions you can actually perform. Cavitation does — raise the suction pressure and it goes away, exactly like the manual says.

Sam: that gives the wrong answer on the case the whole system was built for. A failing bearing and a healthy bearing respond identically to every intervention an operator has (throttle it down, it gets quieter), so your criterion calls that the same mechanism too — and that's the fire we most wanted.

Priya: ok, then mechanism identity isn't absolute, it's scale-relative. Same mechanism at the level of the pump, different at the level of the impeller surface, different again at the level of fatigue in the metal.

Sam: that just relocates the fight. Now "is this anomalous?" means "at what scale should I have described the system?", and nothing in the data answers that.

Priya: right, and that's my actual point — "same mechanism" is a modelling decision, not a fact about the pump, so a system that announces "new mechanism detected" is smuggling in a choice nobody ever made explicitly.

Sam: whatever it is, it has to earn its keep by licensing extrapolation. We care whether it's the same mechanism because we want to know whether what we learned on the healthy fleet still applies here. So: same mechanism iff a rule fit on the training data still ranks cases correctly on the new ones. Call it portability.

Priya: that's circular. The mechanism talk was supposed to justify extrapolation, and now it's only a summary of whether extrapolation happened to work.

Then a second thread: a pump that fired during a heatwave because demand was unusually high. Sam: false positive, same mechanism, unusual input. Priya: no — and note that if your detector can't tell "unusual input to the same mechanism" from "different mechanism", it shouldn't be described as doing mechanism detection at all. Also, that's the fire where you most want a human to look, and the pump was fine but the system wasn't.

Please go through it move by move and tell me who's right about which specific claim — not just who wins overall. And: can "same mechanism" be cashed out as anything more than a modelling choice, or is Priya right that the notion is ours rather then the pump's? What, if anything, are both of them missing?
