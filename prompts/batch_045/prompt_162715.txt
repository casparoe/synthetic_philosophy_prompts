Question 3. In The Querist Berkeley argues (1) that the wealth of a nation consists in the industry of its inhabitants, not in its stock of gold and silver; (2) that money is to be understood functionally, as a token or tally that conveys to its holder a claim on the labour and goods of other people and thereby sets industry to work, rather than as a commodity with a value of its own; and (3) that Ireland's misery is due in part to a scarcity of the circulating medium and of credit, which he proposed to cure with a national bank that would lend on real security (land, stock, goods) and issue paper notes.

And here is the anonymised answer I'm supposed to grade (I've pasted it as written, typos and all):

Berkeley's target throughout The Querist is the mercantilist identification of wealth with treasure. His own view is a functional one: money is not a good to be hoarded but a ticket that transfers a claim on other people's labour and, in doing so, puts hands and land to work. That is how he can hold both that money is not wealth and that Ireland is miserable for want of it — money is the instrument by which idle resources are mobilised, and a country with plenty of unused labour and land can be poor precisely because it lacks the medium that would set them going. His remedy follows directly: a national bank lending on real security and issuing notes, since the notes are claims on real goods and their issue is limited by the security behind them.

(a) This monetary nominalism is an application of Berkeley's metaphysics. In the Principles and the Dialogues he argues that matter does not exist and that the objects of sense are ideas; if that is right, then gold and silver cannot have any value in themselves, and so nothing but a sign can serve as money. His theory of signs and his theory of money are thus the same doctrine in two registers: just as words mean what they mean by convention rather than by resembling what they stand for, so money is valuable by convention rather than by being made of something valuable. The New Theory of Vision supplies the model — visible ideas stand for tangible ones by arbitrary appointment, exactly as a note stands for goods.

(b) The chartalist reading is exactly right, and it is the most modern thing in Berkeley. Money is valuable because authority declares it money and takes it back in payment of taxes; that is what would make his bank's notes circulate — the declaration, not the security behind them. Berkeley is therefore committed to something stronger than he ever quite admits: any token the state names would serve equally well as money, so long as people believe the state stands behind it. That is the real content of his nominalism, and it is why he spends so little time worrying about what the notes are backed by.

(c) Berkeley would choose (A), the fixed-supply asset. His complaint in The Querist is that rulers and bankers debase the currency by over-issuing paper, and the remedy for that is a measure that no one can expand: honest, hard money whose supply is outside anyone's discretion. Arrangement (B) hands an issuing authority exactly the discretion he distrusted, and an index of idle capacity is a bureaucrat's fantasy with no place in his thought; besides, he puts Ireland's idleness down chiefly to the character and habits of its people, a moral failing rather than a monetary one. What Berkeley wanted was not more money but better money.
