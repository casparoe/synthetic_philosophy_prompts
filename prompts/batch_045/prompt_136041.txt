I've just spent a long and unsatisfying evening arguing with an old friend — he's a sociologist, I'm a second-year internal medicine resident — about Foucault, and I don't want another round of opinion; I want to know which parts of what he told me I should actually believe. He was expounding, with real confidence, what he called "the standard picture," and he said Foucault's move is to stop thinking of power as a possession. I'm reconstructing from memory, so the phrasing is mine rather than his, but his version went roughly like this:

"Foucault's basic move is to stop thinking of power as something a sovereign or a state has and uses to say no, backed by the threat of force, and to think of it instead as a field of relations running through the whole social body, so that there is no position outside power from which one could neutrally judge it. In the modern period it changes in kind, not just in degree: it stops operating mainly by prohibiting and punishing and starts operating by producing. It produces the kinds of person it then administers and studies — the delinquent, the hysteric, the homosexual understood as a species of person rather than as someone who performs a certain act — and it produces the vocabularies in which those people come to describe themselves. That's what 'power/knowledge' means: the human sciences did not discover these kinds of person; the clinics, the courts and the schools needed to classify and manage them first, and the sciences grew up around that administrative need. So there is no neutral, pre-political truth about the human subject waiting to be uncovered — what the human sciences offer is knowledge that serves whatever arrangement of power produced it, and calling it knowledge is just the way that arrangement licenses itself. Discipline is the name for the techniques by which this productive power works on individual bodies: timetables, the serial breakdown of tasks, ranking, continuous examination, and the normalizing judgment that locates you on a distribution rather than declaring you innocent or guilty. He even says something like the 'soul' we think we have is really the instrument by which the body is held in place. And the Panopticon is the diagram of the whole thing: because the inmate can never tell whether he is being watched at this particular moment, he ends up watching himself, which is what makes the scheme so cheap — and, of course, why Bentham's tower turned out to be a prophecy of the security camera."

The reason I couldn't let it drop is that I've just joined our hospital's graduate medical education committee, and we're about to vote on a "Clinical Practice Dashboard" for residents. It composites documentation latency, adherence to order sets, patient-satisfaction free-text, and peer-nominated professionalism flags into a single percentile that is shown to each resident alongside the cohort distribution; there are no absolute thresholds and no pass/fail, only where you sit relative to your peers. My friend thinks this settles it: it is continuous examination, a normalizing judgment with no standard behind it, and self-policing, so within a few months, he said, I won't be able to tell whether I'm a good doctor or just a well-scoring one — and, worse, there's no point opposing it in committee, because on Foucault's own view resistance is only ever a move the system has already made room for: power produces the very subject who objects, so all anyone can manage is a private, local opt-out that changes nothing. That last bit is the part I most want checked. I'm inclined to reject the relativist-sounding claim that the human sciences can do no better than serve whatever arrangement produced them — it seems to me that a metric can distort a practice without there being no fact of the matter about the practice — but I can't tell where that claim stops being Foucault and starts being my friend, and I worry that the "no outside of power" line means my objection in committee is itself just my professional formation talking.

So please give me a claim-by-claim audit rather than a general verdict. For each substantial claim in the reconstruction, tell me whether a scholar of Foucault would accept it as stated, treat it as a fair compression of something he does say, or regard it as a misreading that a defender would object to — and in the last case say in a sentence or two what the defender would say instead, including the cases where the misreading is a genuinely contested interpretation rather than a plain error. I'm especially interested in three things: whether the "power/knowledge" claim really commits him to there being no neutral knowledge of the human subject; whether the fatalism about resistance is his conclusion or a standard objection against him that he would have repudiated; and whether the panoptic scheme is really just a prophecy of cameras and data collection, or whether something is lost in that translation. Then tell me whether the conclusion my friend draws about the dashboard survives once the summary is corrected: does a Foucaultian framing give me any argument I could honestly make to the committee, does it undercut my standing to make one, or is it simply the wrong tool for a decision about whether to adopt a metric? For each of your verdicts, mark how confident you are — confident, reasonably confident, or a live dispute in the literature that you're taking a side on — and then single out the one claim in your reply you are least sure of, and say what would change your mind about it.
