My friend Mara reads my drafts before anyone else does, she's a philosopher of mind, and for three books now I've been asking her the same question about a scene — is this character conscious at this moment, is there anything it's like for them — and she always answers flat yes or no, never hedges. Her verdicts so far, more or less in order:

Book one: Elias sleepwalks, makes two cups of tea, steps around the cat, says mm when his wife asks if he's up, remembers nothing. Mara: not conscious, the walking is smart but nobody's home.
Book two: Nadia, who lost V1 years earlier, walks a bombed-out corridor at night, never touches a loose brick, insists the whole way that she sees nothing and is only guessing. Mara: not conscious of the rubble, not conscious of the look of the corridor, she's being steered by information she has no experience of.
Book two, twenty pages on: Nadia awake, watching her daughter asleep in the next bed. Mara: yes, obviously.
Book three: Ines, long-haul trucker, comes to forty miles down the highway with no memory of driving it, but she tracked the road the whole way and would have braked for anything. Mara: conscious the whole time, thinly — conscious of the road and of almost nothing else, she just wasn't laying anything down.
Book three: Ines under general anaesthetic while the surgeons argue over her, monitor showing her brain tracking the words. Mara: not conscious at the time, not a bit.
Book three: a boy in an absence seizure for eight seconds mid-sentence, hand still moving. Mara: not conscious.
Book three, last chapter: a pianist playing a sonata she's played a thousand times, completely absorbed, no inner words happening at all, not thinking about herself, not aware of the room. Mara: conscious, and conscious of the music.

Book four is the first one with any tech in it, and here's the thing I need from her. My protagonist has an implant, and she can turn it on and run dark: perception and action keep going beautifully — she flies a route she knows, fences, plays a memorized sonata, holds a routine conversation — but nothing reaches the global workspace, nothing goes into memory, and if you ask her mid-run what she sees there is no answer and no sense that a question was even asked. Her eyes track perfectly. Then there's a second function, the hook: seconds later the implant can feed the run's contents back into the workspace, and she can then describe what it was like in vivid specific detail, and the descriptions match the camera logs. She insists afterwards that she was there.

So: will Mara say she was conscious during the dark run, or not? I care less about the answer than about the rule she's actually running — is it that a conscious state is one that's broadcast to everything else in the head, or one that would be available for report if you asked, or one with the right kind of rich recurrent processing whether or not anything downstream can use it — and which of those rules fit her seven verdicts equally well and split on this one. Two variants while you're at it: in one scene the dark run improvises a brand new melody and gives a correct answer to a question nobody scripted, and in another the run is never hooked and the logs are wiped that night, so nobody ever knows. Does the first one change her verdict, and does it make her own rule look strange, given that flexible availability is supposed to be what the workspace is for? And does the second one mean there was experience that no one could ever have evidence of?

I need to know because I have to decide whether to write these chapters from the inside or not, and whether her claim afterwards that she was there is worth anything. Please tell me plainly what you think she'll say and why that rule over the others, and name the one case I could put to her that would tell the rules apart. And I'm not going to read papers, so please don't point me at any — just the reasoning, in continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, nothing in bold, no tables, the way you'd write it in an email, because I'm going to read it out loud to myself on a walk.
