Had about three hours of arguing with my friend Rowan last night over pints about that bit in GEB where Hofstadter splits a thing into a frame message, an outer message and an inner message — the where-actually-is-the-meaning chapter. Rowan's line, which I couldn't knock down then and still can't, was that the frame message isn't a separate layer at all: any receiver worth the name, he says, can work out that something is a message and how to read it straight from the signal, the way recovery software identifies a file's format from the raw bytes with no filename and no metadata. Then he pushed further and said if the inner message depends on the receiver, any reading is as good as any other, which I'm certain is wrong but couldn't say why. Anyway, I've somehow ended up with the job of doing this in forty-five minutes next month for a reading group of about twelve adults who are working through GEB very slowly — no philosophy, no computer science, one retired engineer who will want to talk about encoding, and someone who'll get annoyed if the session turns into opinions. Don't explain the distinction to me, Rowan has already explained it at me plenty; plan how I should explain it to them. What has to come first and what depends on what — does the this-is-a-message-decode-me bit genuinely have to precede the rest, or does the receiver's machinery have to be on the table before that even makes sense? Which example should carry the whole session and why: I've been going between Hofstadter's own record-player thing, a USB stick with a single unnamed file on it, and a postcard in a language nobody in the room reads, and I want to know why the one you pick works and the others don't. I'm guessing the example has to include a case where the frame never gets recognised so nothing happens at all, and a case where the outer message is recovered perfectly and the meaning still doesn't land, and that it has to block them from settling everything by asking what the sender meant — tell me if I'm wrong about any of that and what else it must have or lack. Then the misconceptions in the order they'll actually form, and where in the sequence to head each off: I'm expecting meaning-is-a-stuff-inside-the-object, if-you-can-decode-it-you've-got-the-meaning, an-undeciphered-message-has-no-meaning-at-all, and the frame-message-is-just-the-medium. Tell me where Rowan's two objections belong in that list and what I should say when the engineer raises the first one, because if I fumble it he'll take over the session. Give me one question to ask at the end that shows whether the layers have landed rather than whether they can recite the three labels, and tell me what to openly refuse to get into at this level and say out loud that I'm skipping — I do not want to be dragged into incompleteness, and I really don't want to end up arguing about whether language models understand things, though somebody will try. Write it as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just a letter-ish thing I can read on the bus, and in British English.
