I retired from a condensed-matter group a few years ago — thirty-odd years of diffraction work and Monte Carlo, so I'm the one in my philosophy reading group who gives up arguing and just simulates things — and I've got myself stuck on Bertrand's chord problem. You know the setup, spare me the tour: equilateral triangle inscribed in a circle, chord drawn "at random", is it longer than the side, and three textbook procedures give 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4. My question isn't which is right; it's why this has been chewed on since the 1889 book and still hasn't moved. Here's my own wrinkle, which is what started the fight: I said, stop arguing about words, let's specify a machine — pick a point uniformly in the area of the disc, pick an orientation uniformly in [0, 180°), draw the line through that point at that orientation. Perfectly well defined, perfectly symmetric under rotation, and when I ran ten million draws the probability came out near 0.609, which is none of Bertrand's three numbers. (Please check that value analytically if you can — I get 1/3 + √3/2π and I want to know whether I've blundered.) So a fourth machine gives a fourth answer, and as far as I can tell you can cook up as many as you like.

My hypothesis, offered as one, is that there's nothing left to dispute: "a random chord" doesn't denote anything until you've fixed a measure or a physical gadget, so the camps are each reporting on a different experiment under one name and the whole paradox is verbal. Two things stop me resting there. First, the statistician in the group insists the invariance style of argument — I think it's Jaynes, 1973, something about well-posed problems, requiring indifference to the position and size of the circle — singles out 1/2 uniquely, and then goes quiet when I point out that my machine is exactly as rotation-symmetric as any of theirs and gives 0.609. I really don't see why translation invariance should constrain a circle bolted to my kitchen table, and he can't say why either, he just says it obviously should. Second, I've come across a claim that a later paper pushed that same style of reasoning and got the other numbers back out, depending on what you hold fixed.

So: what kind of stalemate is this, actually? Verbal, with no shared question; a hidden formal question about which transformation group or which limiting/conditioning step is the right one; a real substantive fight about what the principle of indifference is even supposed to be — a constraint on an information state (the maximum-entropy sort of thing) versus a symmetry requirement on an actual randomising device; or just people defending bedrock intuitions about symmetry that neither side can argue for? Don't walk me through the three methods again, I've read them till they're coming out of my ears. I want a diagnosis with reasons, and then an honest answer about what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or about why I should expect none and go back to my simulations.
