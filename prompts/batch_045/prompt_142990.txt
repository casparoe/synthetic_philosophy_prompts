I'm a retired experimental physicist — thirty-odd years of data analysis, most of it Bayesian by the end — and in the last coupe of years I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the local university, mostly for something to do. Last week they did anthropics: the self-sampling assumption, the self-indication assumption, the Doomsday argument, the presumptuous philosopher case. I came away with the feeling that I'd been watching two people argue about a prior while using a vocabulary that doesn't have a word for "prior." But I couldn't quite make that work, and I'd like you to try.

Here's what I want. Restate the SSA vs SIA dispute — and the two big applications, the Doomsday shift and the presumptuous philosopher — in the vocabulary I actually have: hypothesis spaces, priors, likelihoods, Bayes factors, evidence, nuisance parameters you marginalize over, and the measure problem, by which I mean the cosmology-seminar business of picking a measure over the space of observers or observer-moments so that ratios of numbers of observers come out finite and the answer sometimes depends on which measure you picked. I've used all of that and don't need any of it explained; I know maybe a dozen words of philosophy jargon.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about:

1. Is SSA vs SIA, at bottom, a disagreement about the prior/measure over observer-moments — or is there something left over once you say that? If something is left over, say what it is, in my words if you can.
2. The reference class problem. Is that the same shape as measure ambiguity in cosmology (an unforced choice that changes the answer, which everyone agrees is a bad situation), or is it more like a disagreement about what counts as the data?
3. The likelihood. Under every hypothesis in these cases the probability that somebody or other exists looks like 1, so nothing discriminates there — and yet people write down a Bayes factor and get an answer. So where is the discrimination actually coming from: a real likelihood, something smuggled into the prior, or the treatment of an indexical fact ("I am this one, here, now") as a datum? Tell me which, and tell me what breaks if you refuse to let indexical facts be data. I have a suspicion that a lot of the argument is really about whether a fact like "I am the sixtieth billionth human" is the sort of thing you can conditionalize on, and I'd like that said in a form I can check.
4. Sleeping Beauty. Thirders vs halfers: is that a fight about the measure over observer-moments, or about the evidence, or about something my vocabulary has no grip on at all?

And my own toy, which you should feel free to demolish. In the simplest Doomsday-style setup, where the only indexical datum is my birth rank, it looked to me as if the entire difference between the two assumptions is one extra factor of N in the weight on each hypothesis — N being the total number of observers in the reference class — so I wrote down a one-parameter family: weight proportional to (SSA weight) times N^alpha, with alpha = 0 landing on SSA and alpha = 1 on SIA. Is that a fair way to restate the dispute? And is alpha sitting in the prior or in the likelihood, or is whether its the prior or the likelihood itself part of what's being argued about? Is any value of alpha actually inconsistent rather than merely unpopular? Does the presumptuous philosopher case pin alpha down, or does it only show that alpha = 1 has a consequence people dislike — which, in my old field, is not the same thing as a refutation? And if I go further and say alpha is a gauge choice, a bookkeeping convention that has to cancel out of anything you can actually measure, does anything of the philosophical dispute survive, or does the whole thing evaporate?

Please spend most of your words on where the translation fails rather than where it succeeds, and don't be polite about the toy — if the N^alpha business is confused I'd rather find out now than take it to the reading group.
