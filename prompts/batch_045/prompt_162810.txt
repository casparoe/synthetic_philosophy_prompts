The chapter is about the procreation asymmetry — the move people associate with Benatar's work: we have a reason not to bring into existence someone who would have a life not worth living, but no symmetric reason to bring into existence someone who would have a life worth living, even a very good one. I'm not using it for antinatalism; the first third of the chapter argues that it only gets you there if you add a strong premise about risk and uncertainty, and I set that aside. My actual thesis is sideways: I claim the asymmetry is best read as a claim about authorization rather than about harm or about impersonal value. The reason against creating the suffering person is the same shape as the reason against operating on someone who can't consent to it — you're imposing on someone who neither authorizes the imposition nor gains from it. And authorization-based reasons are waivable, so if the person you create would later, with full information, endorse their own creation, the anti-creation reason should lose much of its force. This produces what I call the "borrowed life" case, which is where I expect to get killed: someone whose life is good overall but contains one large unconsented-to early harm (a painful congenital condition corrected in infancy, say), who grows up and wholeheartedly endorses having been created. Read the usual way, the asymmetry says there was a serious moral reason not to create this person. On my reading, given the endorsement, there wasn't. I bite the bullet — the reason is defeated or at least much weaker — and I argue this is a feature, not a bug, because it's the only way the asymmetry can handle the fact that most actual parents create people whose lives contain serious suffering and we don't want to say those parents thereby wronged their children.
