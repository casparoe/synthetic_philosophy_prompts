Utilitarianism adds everyone's welfare up into one grand total and then maximizes that total. Rawls's separateness of persons objection is that this treats all of humanity as one giant person: it takes the perfectly sensible principle of rational choice for a single individual — that you can accept a small loss now for a bigger gain later — and applies it across people, so it lets you sacrifice one person for a small gain spread thinly over many. Once you allow any trade-off of that kind at all, you're committed to Nozick's utility monster: if some being converts resources into happiness efficiently enough, the sum says everything should go to it, and you have no principled way to say no. So the objection shows that the only way to really take the distinctness of persons seriously is to give each person an inviolable side constraint, or at least lexical priority, so that no amount of benefit to other people can ever justify making someone worse off. That's why it's really an argument for rights, and why the contractualists picked it up. And because it attacks aggregation as such, it hits prioritarianism and every other consequentialist view that weighs people's interests against each other just as hard — numbers counting at all is the problem — so if you accept the objection you're a rights theorist or a contractualist, and that's it.
