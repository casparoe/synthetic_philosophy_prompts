I'm a staff engineer on the platform team at a consumer finance app with roughly four million monthly users. I have no philosophy background, and I'm not looking for one: I need something I can use in code and design review.

Here is the recurring situation. Several times a month a pull request or design doc crosses my desk containing a change that steers users: a default that's been flipped, a step made easier or harder, an option pre-selected, a notification reshaped, an off-switch moved somewhere quieter, a flow that only some users see because it only "worked" on them in an experiment. Our review template has a single line about this — essentially "is this a dark pattern?" — and it is useless: nobody has ever answered yes, and the discussions that do happen collapse into competing intuitions and whoever is seniorest in the room. Meanwhile these changes ship to millions of people, are cheap to reverse in a later release but not within the sprint, and the people affected never get asked. I can block a change in review; a product manager can escalate past me to a director; legal and accessibility sign off on some of them but not on these questions.

What I want from you is a decision procedure I can actually run, in twenty to thirty minutes, on a real proposal, with the information a platform engineer has in front of them: the diff, the experiment readout if there is one, the metrics the team is being measured on, and a rough sense of who is affected. I need it as an ordered set of questions with a stopping rule: what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, at which points the procedure returns "no" and the change doesn't ship, where it returns "ship with this modification," and where it has to stop and hand the decision to a person or a different function because the remaining question is one of values, or because it's an empirical question we cannot answer inside a sprint. I want the hand-off points named explicitly, including which of them are my organization's job to staff and which ones we're currently pretending are engineering decisions.

Some things I've picked up second-hand, which I don't trust my own version of: that steering which leaves every option available is different in kind from removing options; that steering people toward what they themselves already want is different from steering them toward what the designer wants; that some interventions leave people better able to choose next time while others work precisely by bypassing the part of them that chooses; and that how visible the steering is matters. I don't need to know who argued what or in what year. I need the distinctions that change what I should ship, kept only if they survive being turned into a question an engineer can answer in a few minutes without a philosopher in the room — and I'd like you to tell me honestly which of the distinctions above you had to drop or mangle to make them operational, and whether there's a distinction I haven't mentioned that has to be in there.

There are six situations my team hits repeatedly, and the procedure has to give an answer on each rather than gesture at them:

The first is the one I distrust most: we very often claim to be serving the user's own goals while the only evidence we have is a movement in a metric we chose, which is usually correlated with revenue. I need a test, runnable with the artifacts we have, for whether we are serving the user's ends or substituting our own — and I need to know what to do when the honest answer is that we can't tell.

The second is that many of these mechanisms only work if the user doesn't notice them, while our policy requires disclosure in release notes and the help center. This produces steering that is technically disclosed and practically undiscoverable. I want to know whether that disclosure is doing any real work on your account, or whether it's a compliance ritual — and if the latter, what the procedure demands instead.

The third is the one I think about most and have never seen discussed. We A/B test steering changes and ship them only to the segments where they moved behavior. That means the population that actually experiences the steering is, by construction, the population susceptible to it: we are selecting for persuadability. Is that morally different from shipping the same change to everyone, and if so in which direction? Does it matter that the excluded users — the ones who weren't moved — are arguably the ones whose ends we have best evidence about?

The fourth is asymmetry of friction. We routinely make the path we approve of smoother and the path we disapprove of rougher: cancelling a subscription, turning off a feature we believe is good for people, reaching a setting that costs us money. Sometimes added friction is a speed bump that protects someone from their own haste; sometimes it's sabotage of a choice we've technically left available. I need a rule that separates these, and I need it to be a rule and not a vibe.

The fifth is third parties. Some of these changes help the person using the app while pushing a cost onto someone else — a slower shipping option that helps our logistics and raises costs for a driver, a snooze that moves a task onto a support agent, a default that improves one household member's outcome at another's expense. I don't know whether the procedure is allowed to count those people, and if it is, how much weight they get relative to the user.

The sixth is the easy-looking one: cases where the user has explicitly asked us to steer them — screen-time limits, savings commitments, a self-block list that's deliberately hard to edit. I assume that's a free pass, but I've been wrong about this kind of thing before, and there are edge cases where the request was made under conditions we engineered.

I also want to flag a structural fact about my role, because I think it should change the procedure rather than just be noted: much of what I ship is a default in a shared component or a design-system primitive used by forty-odd teams I'll never meet. So I'm not deciding one intervention; I'm deciding the path of least resistance for a whole class of future interventions, most of which I won't see, and the teams building them have less latitude to think about this than I do. If that means the procedure needs a different or stricter branch for platform-level defaults than for a single screen, say so and write that branch.

Then I want it run on three real cases, with the verdict and the reasoning shown, no hedging into a summary of considerations:

One: round-up savings. Spare change on each purchase is swept into a savings pot, default on at enrollment, off switch two taps inside settings, and the company earns interest on the float. It measurably increases savings balances. The team that proposed it is measured on deposits.

Two: quiet hours in our shared notification library. Notifications are batched and badge counts suppressed, on by default for every app that adopts the library; users can disable it, but the toggle sits in a submenu. We have no evidence users want this. My own team believes it's good for them and wrote it partly for that reason.

Three: carbon-aware shipping. The slower, cheaper delivery option is pre-selected at checkout. The experiment showed no effect overall, but a large and significant effect among users checking out on mobile late at night; the proposal is to ship the pre-selection only to that segment. It saves the company money, it genuinely reduces emissions, and it saves those users money too. I personally want to ship it. Give me the honest answer even if it is "no," and even if it is "either everyone or no one."

Finally, on confidence: for each of the three verdicts, tell me the single argument or consideration that would most plausibly make you change it, and where in the reasoning you think the failure is most likely to be. Label which steps of the procedure rest on contested empirical claims about how people actually behave or about what our users want, as opposed to steps that follow from the conceptual distinctions alone — I want to know which parts of this I'd have to re-litigate if someone sent me a new study. Then name one class of case where you think the procedure as you've written it would give the wrong answer, and say what it is about the procedure that makes it misfire there.
