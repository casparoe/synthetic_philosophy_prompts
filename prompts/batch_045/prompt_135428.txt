I'm putting together the handout for a graduate reading group on relativism, contextualism and truth pluralism, and this term I want to devote one session to the Jain material — anekāntavāda, the conditional predication usually called syādvāda, and the sevenfold schema built out of "is", "is not" and "indescribable" — because my sense is that it gets filed next to relativism in people's heads and doesn't belong there. My first attempt at an exercise was useless: I asked people directly whether they were relativists about truth and they either said no or asked what I meant, so now I want an instrument that sorts them by what they say about cases. The cases I want to use come from my own field, disputes about algorithmic systems, where the same flat-footed question gets opposite answers from competent people: "the loan model is fair" (given that the standard fairness criteria can't all be satisfied at once), "the model decided to deny the application", "the model understands the text it was given", "the training data is representative of the population", "it is still the same model after fine-tuning", "the company knew the product was unsafe", "the output was the system's assertion that P", "the harm was caused by the algorithm rather than by the people who deployed it", "the data subject consented". Please give me nine or ten such items, each a concrete claim with two or three answer options phrased so that someone with no Jain background and no interest in the labels can pick one, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto leaves rather than asking anyone to self-identify.

The structure I care about most is the order of construction. I don't want a list of named views with cases bolted on; I want the logical space first — the yes/no questions whose combinations generate the cells — and only then the actual positions located in it. As I see it the spine is three questions: does the claim hold, does its denial hold, and is it the sort of thing one should refuse to describe either way; that gives eight cells, seven of which the tradition is supposed to occupy, and I'd like you to say what is in each, why the eighth (denying all three) is treated as empty, and whether some position I haven't thought of — a Madhyamaka-style refusal to take a stand, or a plain quietism about the question — really does sit in it, and how a Jain account would reply. Among the leaves I want, besides the seven predications themselves: a dialetheist or glut-theoretic position, an ordinary contextualist or subject-sensitive treatment that puts the disagreement down to unstated parameters, a genuinely relativistic one on which the truth of the claim varies with a standpoint, a purely epistemic "we aren't in a position to say" reading of the third option, and the deflationary diagnosis that the original sentence was simply equivocal and needs to be disambiguated rather than hedged. I'd also like the standpoint theory (nayavāda) handled as a possible second axis rather than as a synonym for the sevenfold schema — whether every predication is implicitly indexed to a standpoint, and what the difference is supposed to be between that and saying the sentence was context-sensitive all along — and one item somewhere that separates reading the conditional operator as an epistemic hedge ("perhaps") from reading it as a qualifier of respect ("in some respect, of a certain sort").

Two last things. First, flag honestly any pair of leaves your instrument can't separate — I strongly suspect the "indescribable" mode and ordinary quietism come apart only in theory, and that the standpoint-based view collapses into contextualism unless something extra is added, and I'd rather see that admitted than papered over with an extra question. Where a cell in the space is unoccupied, give me a one-line diagnosis of whether nobody is there because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored. Second, on the practical side: the readers are analytic philosophers with no Sanskrit and no Jain background, so write the leaves and the questions in plain English and give Sanskrit or Prakrit names only where you're confident of them — an English label plus a description is worth more to me than a name someone in the room might quietly doubt, and there's a fourfold "nikṣepa" distinction half-remembered from somewhere in this material that you should only use if you can state it without guessing. I don't need any bibliography, references or suggested reading; I have the syllabus, and I want the handout to stand on the cases and the distinctions alone.
