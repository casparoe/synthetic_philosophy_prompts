I retired last year after a career in ecosystem modelling — most of what I actually got paid for was arguing about causal attribution in systems dominated by feedbacks, where the honest answer to "what caused this" is usually "nothing, unless you first tell me the counterfactual baseline, the grain of description, and how big a difference has to be before it counts." So I joined a philosophy reading group, and we have now spent something like three sessions on the origins of the Cold War, and it feels exactly like those old departmental arguments except that nobody seems to notice the question is doing something odd.

The three standard camps — the one that puts it on the Soviets, the one that puts it on the Americans, and the later "structures, security dilemma, everybody's hands dirty" one — have all got their evidence on the table, everybody has heard everybody else's evidence, and nobody moves. I dont want a survey of the camps and I dont need names, dates or books, I can look those up myself. What I want is a diagnosis of what kind of stuck this is.

My hypothesis, which I'd rather you push on than flatter: "who started it" is ill-posed because it silently bundles at least five questions that have different answers —

- onset: whose action was the earliest one that was aggressive or wrongful
- difference-making: whose behaviour, had it been different within the range actually available to them, would have changed the outcome most
- lock-in: who turned a rivalry that might have stayed an ordinary rivalry into a stable, armed, bipolar standoff
- avoidability: was there a path not taken, and was it anyone's job to take it
- fault: given real constraints and real ignorance at the time, who deserves blame as opposed to mere causal responsibility

— and in a positive-feedback system difference-making tends to come out either near-symmetric or wildly sensitive to the baseline, so the "who" question has no stable answer at that grain.

So: is that decomposition right, is it complete, and here's the part I actually care about — is it a neutral clarification, or is it itself a move in the dispute, quietly settling things in favour of the structural camp by making the blame question look like the confused one? If it is a move, what is the best argument against it? And once you strip out whatever the decomposition handles, what is left: a factual disagreement about what was available to whom, a disagreement about what counts as a cause in history, one about whether collective agents like states can bear blame at all, or one about whether historians should be in the blame business in the first place?

Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal model, newly opened archives, changing the question, or nothing at all? I have a soft spot for the idea that tools from my old field (explicit baselines, sensitivity analysis on the counterfactuals) could do some work here, but I suspect a historian would say that is a category error, because history is about singular events and agents reasons rather than lawlike regularities. Tell me honestly if I am kidding myself, and tell me whether any of this matters outside the seminar room — does blame in historiography do any real work, or is it mostly grudge-holding with footnotes?
