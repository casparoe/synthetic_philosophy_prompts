im writing my final short paper for this undergrad seminar on animals and the environment, and we get to pick any real institutional practice and reconstruct the moral theory it has to be presupposing. i want to do mine on the food system at my own uni, because it's genuinely weird and i cant tell whether it's flat-out incoherent or whether i'm just missing the theory that ties it together. the prof said not to spend the paper sorting practices into utilitarian / deontological / virtue-ethics boxes and not to spend it summarizing famous positions, so please don't do that either, and no readings or citations, she wants the argument from us.

here's the practice as concretely as i can put it:

- dining services scores every menu item by carbon footprint per serving and prints a green / amber / red dot on the menu card. beef is basically always red, chicken is usually amber, lentils and tofu green, and the default hot option is plant based unless you ask.
- completely separately, the student animal ethics society runs a campaign along the lines of "if you care about animals, eat the cow": one steer is a huge animal and gives hundreds of servings while a chicken gives you like two or three, so per meal beef kills far fewer individuals than chicken. their poster also claims chickens are killed younger and have worse lives per day lived, idk if that bit is right.
- when the uni does serve beef it buys pasture-raised, grass-finished, "regenerative" beef from nearby farms, on the grounds that the animals had decent lives and that the grazing is good for soil and wildflowers. as far as i can tell that means way more land per kilo than feedlot beef, and the rancher also has to deal with coyotes and foxes going after calves.
- meanwhile the grounds department has a biodiversity plan: they turned some mown grass into meadow, put up nest boxes and bat boxes, and they pay contractors to trap rats and to shoot the geese and deer that cause problems on campus.
- and the sustainability office does count the field animals killed to grow the grain that goes into animal feed (they had a slide about mice and insects at harvest), but nothing counts the animals the uni itself kills or attracts on its own land, and no document anywhere mentions wild animals' suffering as such, only numbers of animals and species and habitat.

so what i'm stuck on: it looks like the same institution counts wild animal deaths when that pushes toward beef, and then ignores wild animal deaths, including ones it causes itself, when counting would push somewhere inconvenient. my seminar group came up with a test case that i kind of want to use: instead of deaths per serving, score food by expected days of pain caused per serving, including the pain of wild animals in the field and of whatever gets killed by predator control, and including the question whether the farmed animals' lives were worth living at all versus never existing. call it a pain-days label.

so my questions:

- what theory of animal harm does the current package actually presuppose, and specifically: about death versus suffering, about how many individuals versus how much welfare, about whether a life that would otherwise never have existed can be a benefit that offsets the harm of killing, about causing versus merely allowing or being complicit in a market, about farmed versus wild animals, and about whether naturalness or habitat/species-level value can just outweigh individual animals?
- which parts of the practice strictly presuppose which of those commitments, and where is it underdetermined, so that two or three quite different theories would license exactly the same menu and the same menu labels?
- are the eat-the-cow bit and the pasture-raised bit pulling in opposite directions once you count field animals and predator control, or is there one theory that holds them together?
- if the uni really swapped in pain-days accounting, what would have to change in practice? would it flip the beef versus chicken advice, would it flip the pasture-raised preference, would it say something different about the plant-based default, and what would it have to say about the grounds department's traps and culls?
