I think I broke the Fermi paradox on accident and I'm not happy about what I broke, because the thing I built also seems to dissolve every other anthropic argument I actually like. Long version, sorry, but I need the steps written out to see where it snaps.

(1) Start with the standard "they'd be here by now" argument, which I'm happy to grant for the sake of the rest. The galaxy has been habitable for something like ten billion years. A civilization that started expanding at even 1% of lightspeed would saturate the Milky Way in a few tens of millions of years, which is a rounding error against ten billion. So either interstellar expansion essentially never happens (impossible, or everyone self-destructs / decides to stay home / gets stopped by someone else), or else no civilization anywhere in the galaxy has ever reached the point of leaving its home system. Either the filter is ahead of us or we're first. Fine.

(2) Now consider what I'll call the synchronized-arrival world. Suppose the hard steps to intelligence need a long stretch of galactic history — metallicity, a galactic habitable zone, some sterilization rate decaying, whatever — so that technological civilizations can't appear until the galaxy is quite old, and when they do appear they appear in a narrow window, within a few tens of millions of years of each other, because they're all gated on the same clock. In that world there could be a million civilizations right now, every one of them pre-expansion, every one of them staring up at a silent sky.

(3) Here's the part I think is the killer. In the synchronized world, all million of those civilizations run step (1) and all million conclude "we're alone," and all million are wrong. But that means the likelihoods are: P(silent sky | alone) = 1, and P(silent sky | a million synchronized civs) = 1. Likelihood ratio of exactly one. The silent sky doesn't discriminate between "we're the only one" and "we're one of a million." What step (1) actually refutes is only the version where start times are smeared out across galactic history — unsynchronized many — not "many."

(4) And the reason it can't discriminate looks completely general, which is where I get nervous. The silent sky is uninformative because my being in a position to see a silent sky is guaranteed under both hypotheses. I'm reasoning from evidence whose occurence is entailed by the fact that I'm around to reason at all. That structure has nothing to do with aliens specifically.

(5) So apply it elsewhere and watch things burn. Take the anthropic-shadow style argument: we have no record of any civilization-ending catastrophe, and you might think that's evidence they're rare — but wait, any observer is by construction one who survived, so "no catastrophe in our past" has probability 1 under every hypothesis, likelihood ratio one, update cancelled. Yet I think the anthropic-shadow update is basically correct and I don't want to give it up. Same for the doomsday-flavored stuff; same, honestly, for anything where you notice you're in a special-seeming position.

(6) Worse: on this logic no future null result could ever be informative, in principle. Suppose we point a big survey at a million nearby stars looking for waste heat and find precisely nothing. Under the million-civs hypothesis, most observers in my position also find nothing, because their neighbors are also pre-expansion. So a completed null survey moves me by zero. That's insane — or if it isn't insane, I really want to know why, because it means SETI can only ever confirm and never disconfirm.

(7) So: either I've stumbled onto a genuine general solvent that dissolves every "no evidence of X, therefore X is rare" argument that has an observer-selection story behind it — and the Fermi paradox was never a paradox, just an artifact of ignoring step (3) — or one of these steps is smuggling something. I genuinely can't tell whether the problem is in (2), in the jump to (3), or in the generalization in (4)/(5), and the fact that I can't tell is what makes me think I've screwed up rather than discovered something.

A friend says the way out is that I've thrown away the datum of *when* we showed up: that arriving late in Earth's habitable window is itself evidence for hard steps and against synchronization, so (3) doesn't get off the ground. Maybe! But even if that's right it only patches this one case, and it doesn't tell me what went wrong in the general argument, which is the part I care about.

So: which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? If I have to pick I'd rather keep anthropic shadow than keep the Fermi argument in its usual form, but I'd like to know the exchange rate before I trade. And if the honest answer is that (3) is fine and the solvent is real and anthropic reasoning really does dissolve, I'd like that argued for rather than asserted, because I don't believe it.
