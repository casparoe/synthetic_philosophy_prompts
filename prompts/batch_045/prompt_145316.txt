Before I retired I spent something over thirty years in experimental physics, a good deal of it in metrology, and since then I have been sitting in on philosophy; partly as a consequence of that I have been co-opted onto the examining board for a master's-level module on decision theory and scientific inference, and the single question allocated to me is to be on Good's theorem, the result that for an agent who maximises expected utility, information that arrives free of charge cannot lower expected utility assessed before it arrives. I have to produce the question, the answer I would accept, and the scheme I will mark against, and I would like you to draft all three for me.

The problem I want to solve is this. Every candidate will know the statement and, quite possibly, the short proof by Jensen's inequality; I want a question that a candidate who can recite both but cannot set up a decision tree fails. So build the question around short, self-contained cases with the numbers supplied in the question, and require the candidate actually to compute something. The paper is closed-book with no calculators, so the arithmetic must be doable by hand. The computation should then be the basis for a claim about the scope of the theorem, not merely about the case.

I would like three cases, or four if you can manage it economically. One should be a fake counterexample of the kind that is second nature to anyone who has taken real measurements: the signal arrives only because the agent performs an operation that also perturbs the variable of interest, so that the operation can be worth less than nothing although the signal it yields is not. Here the candidate should have to decompose the value of the operation into an informational part and a causal part, and to say that the theorem is a claim about conditioning on a signal within a fixed process, not about intervening to produce one. If you have room, a related fake counterexample would be useful: being offered the measurement at all may be bad news the agent cannot decline, while the reading, conditional on the offer, is still worth having.

A second case should be a genuine violation, and I would like you to check my instinct on this: a structure in which the agent's own action is evidence about the payoff-relevant state without causing it, together with a free and perfectly reliable signal that screens the action off from the state, so that conditioning on the signal removes an evidential reason to abstain and the agent ends up doing what she would rather have been committed against doing. My thought is that the usual argument fails here because the maximisation is not taken over a single act-independent distribution, and I want the candidate to identify precisely which step of the proof breaks rather than to wave at the word causality.

A third should be a case in which the signal itself belongs in the utility function, where the agent would pay not to know, and I want the candidate to distinguish that from the theorem's being false: what has gone wrong is the specification of the problem, not the mathematics, and there is a reformulation under which the theorem holds again if one is careful. Alongside these I want the candidate to have to say why the guarantee is ex ante only, so that a particular realised reading can leave the agent worse off than she would have been had she not looked, and to say when the value of a signal is exactly zero, namely when no reading could change the action.

What I need from you, in this order: the question exactly as it should appear on the paper, suited to forty-five minutes; a model answer of seven hundred to nine hundred words; and a marking scheme out of twenty, everything in continuous prose, in which each allocation of marks is tied to an observable move a candidate either makes or does not make, with the characteristic blunder that loses each mark, and with an explicit statement of what an answer that states and proves the theorem but never engages the cases is to receive, which I take to be a bare pass or below. I will be marking these myself with two others and I would rather not have to make fine judgement calls, so the moves should be checkable rather than holistic. Please also make sure that nothing in the question rewards having read the literature or knowing the history of the result: no candidate should need a citation, a bibliography, or a name other than the theorem's to answer it.

One last thing about presentation: write the whole thing as continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text and no tables, in the manner of a letter to a colleague, because I will be copying it straight into the exam paper and then into a marking folder where formatting will only get in the way.
