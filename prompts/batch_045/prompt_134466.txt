Hi! I need someone to tear apart a draft. It's for a volume on first-personal knowledge and decision-making, 7000 words max, and I've got about 5200 words of it that I think hangs together, but I've been staring at it too long.

Quick set-up so you know where I'm coming from. The standard transformative experience picture — Paul's book, the usual cases (first child, vampires, tasting durian, the new colour) — is basically binary: either you can simulate the outcome well enough to hang a utility off it, or you can't, and if you can't, expected-utility reasoning is off the table and you're left choosing by what I'll call the revelation fallback, i.e. you decide whether you want to find out who you'll become. My paper argues that the binary is an artefact of the cases people pick, and that once you look at graded cases the whole thing looks different.

Here's the skeleton of what I've got, with the parts I actually want comments on flagged.

§1. The target. The binary, and the argument for it: first-personal knowledge of what an experience is like can only be got by having it, utility requires that knowledge, therefore EU is unavailable.

§2. Near-miss cases. Having a second child. Moving from quartets to full orchestra when you've only played chamber music. Learning Portuguese when you speak Spanish. These are epistemically new — the second child isn't a rerun of the first, the orchestra isn't a louder quartet — but the agent is plainly in a better epistemic position than the nulliparous agent deciding about a first child, or the monolingual English speaker deciding about Portuguese. The binary view has to call either both transformative or neither transformative, and both answers seem wrong.

§3. The positive bit. In near-miss cases the agent can't simulate the target experience, but she can simulate a space of candidate experiences that it belongs to, built out of her own prior experiences of structurally similar events, and she knows something about the spread within that space. Call it an anchor set. Crucially the anchor set isn't testimony: testimony gives you other people's utilities second-hand, whereas an anchor set is assembled from your own first-personal history, so it doesn't inherit the standard worries about whether someone else's report could tell you what it's like. Given an anchor set, you can reason about a range of preferences you might end up with instead of just punting to whether you want to discover yourself.

§4. The condition. This is the bit I most want attacked. An anchor set licenses extrapolated EU only if the agent's rank ordering of the dimensions along which the candidate experiences vary is invariant under the transformation. If the dimensions get reweighted — say becoming a parent changes how much the dimension of unstructured leisure time matters to you relative to everything else — then the extrapolation is self-undermining, because you're extrapolating along axes whose importance the experience itself changes. Call it the drift problem.

§5. First-order vs second-order. The distinction I'm selling: first-order transformative uncertainty is uncertainty about where in the space you'll land; second-order is uncertainty about which space you'll land in. My claim is that only second-order uncertainty forces the revelation fallback, and that the canonical cases are all second-order, which is why the framework looks globally sceptical when it's really making a local point.
