I have been reading about distributive justice on my own, and I keep running into a pattern that I do not have a word for, and the words I do have do not seem to fit it exactly.

The pattern is this. Two people are given genuinely equal bundles — the same income, the same health coverage, the same access to schooling — and one of them reliably gets less out of it: less health, less real freedom, less of whatever it is the theory is supposed to be equalizing. Sometimes the difference is a disability. But very often it is not; it is a skill, a habit, or a set of expectations that makes the same goods yield less. And the part I cannot place is that this deficit is frequently itself the residue of an earlier inequality. The person grew up in a house with no books, or spent a decade poor, or was raised never to expect anything, and as a result converts the same resources into a good life at a worse rate than someone who did not. Give two such people identical retraining vouchers and the one from the bookless household gets less out of hers. Offer a real menu of options to someone who has never been permitted to want anything and she reports being perfectly content with what she had.

The discussion of expensive tastes seems to be about something in this neighbourhood, but as I understand it that debate concerns preferences people are asked to take responsibility for, which is not what troubles me here: the deficiency is not a taste, it is a capacity, and it was not chosen, it was produced. The idea of conversion factors that I have encountered in writings on capabilities seems closer, but it appears to treat such differences as brute facts about bodies and environments, whereas what interests me is that the thing needing compensation is downstream of the very inequality one is trying to correct — so that taking it as given seems to lock the earlier injustice in, while compensating it fully seems to require giving the worse-off person more than the better-off one, perhaps indefinitely, and perhaps without ever closing the gap in outcomes.

Does this have a name in the literature — the endogenous, self-compounding conversion gap, or the objection that a currency of equality which takes people's abilities to convert goods into flourishing as fixed will reproduce the inequality it was meant to undo? If the nearest established notions are the ones I sketched, I would like to know precisely how my case differs from each and which fits best, since a bare label would not tell me what I want to know. If there is nothing established, I would like a name and a definition, together with its boundary: where does a genuine conversion handicap shade into a mere expensive taste, and does the argument commit you to saying the same thing, in reverse, about someone who converts unusually well — that her surplus is not hers either?
