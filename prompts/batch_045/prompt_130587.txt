So I've got two friends who've been going at each other about Athens for years, and I want to make them actually produce something rather than just winning the argument at dinner again. You're writing the document they'd co-sign.

Camp A thinks the three famous disasters — the Mytilene decree of 427 and its reversal the next day, the Sicilian expedition, and the trial and execution of Socrates — are the visible tip of a structural defect in radical direct democracy: a mass assembly with no filter, no permanent staff, no precedent binding it, deciding everything on the day under time pressure and emotional heat, in a rhetorical culture where the way to win the floor is to outdo the last speaker in severity. Camp B thinks the Assembly's record is mostly competent and that these three are famous precisely because they were aberrations, each with its own local explanation: a reprisal voted after a year of blockade and genuine panic about the empire, reversed within a day and only barely stopped in time; a risky but not crazy gamble in Sicily that failed mainly through execution and bad luck, and through the recall of its most able commander mid-campaign; and a prosecution rooted in the trauma of the Thirty and in specific accusers — which, as Camp B never stops pointing out, wasn't the Assembly at all but a lottery-drawn jury, before which Socrates seems to have gone out of his way to be unhelpful.

Here's the scaffolding I want them (i.e. you) to use, because I think it separates the real disagreement from the noise:

- A three-way taxonomy they have to apply to each episode: (1) decision error — wrong given the evidence and standards available to the decision-makers at the time; (2) procedure error — the procedure is systematically tilted toward bad outcomes even when it happens to come out fine; (3) bad outcome by luck — sound procedure, terrible result. A single episode can be more than one, and I want them to say which for each case rather than lumping.
- The same-voters counterfactual: for each episode, would the same Assembly, better informed or put through a different procedure, have decided otherwise? If yes, the complaint is informational or procedural; if no, the preferences themselves are the problem. That's a genuine crux, not a slogan.
- A comparator clause: both agree in advance to benchmark Athens against the best available alternative for a large Greek polis of that period — Sparta, Syracuse, the Boeotian oligarchies — and not against a modern epistocracy or a philosopher-king. Then say what happens to their disagreement if you change the comparator.
- A pre-registered threshold: Camp B owes an answer to "how many more Mytilenes before I concede a regime-level defect?" and Camp A owes an answer to "what pattern would make me concede that these were contingencies?" Write both numbers/conditions down before the evidence, not after.

Format: a numbered agreed-claims section they'd both sign (I want real claims, not mush — if the honest consensus is thin, keep it thin and say so), then a disagreements table with columns for the crux, what both accept as bearing on it, each camp's prediction phrased so that camp would sign it, and what would actually move each side. Then a short section saying which parts of this are disputes about words — "democracy" meaning the Assembly versus the whole system including sortition juries and the Council, "failure" meaning immorality versus imprudence versus self-harm, that kind of thing — and why.

If it helps as a fourth case, use the trial of the Arginusae generals, since that's the one where the Assembly did the worst thing and then immediately regretted it and went after the prosecutors, and where Socrates himself, on one account, was the presiding officer who refused to put the illegal vote.

Two last things: don't invent or lean on precise numbers where the evidence is contested — whether the second Mytilene vote was close, how the two votes in Socrates' trial compare — flag them as contested instead. And at the end tell me plainly which cruxes this collaboration cannot settle with the evidence anyone will ever have, and whether either camp should be embarrassed by that or not. Casual register is fine, this is for a reading group, not a journal.
