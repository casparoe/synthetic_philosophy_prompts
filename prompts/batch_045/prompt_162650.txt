I work as a software engineer, with no formal philosophy background, and I would like a careful map of a distinction I keep half-using in design reviews and incident write-ups: the difference between disagreeing about the world and disagreeing about words. I have three cases from my own team, a proposed test that a colleague suggested, and a suspicion that the tidy version of the distinction I carry around is wrong.

Case one. During an incident review, one engineer said the outage lasted four hours and another said six. Once we wrote down what each of them counted as "the system being down" — one counted only full unavailability, the other counted sustained latency above the SLO — they agreed on every measurement and stopped disagreeing. Nothing about the systems was ever in dispute.

Case two. We run a service whose nodes were all replaced one by one over a month, and whose name and on-call rotation were also changed. Someone asked whether it is still the same service. Everyone agrees on the hardware history, the deployment history, and the org changes; they still disagree. But the disagreement does not seem idle: it tracks whether we port the old runbooks and whether we treat a regression as a recurrence of an old problem.

Case three. We argued about whether we still have a staging environment. Here the two candidate definitions of "staging environment" would give different answers, and which definition to adopt seems to depend on facts about how the cluster is actually used, and on whether gating production deploys on it would catch the failures we care about. It does not feel like a disagreement about words, but it does not feel like a clean empirical disagreement either.

My colleague's proposal, call it the downstream-invariance test: a dispute is verbal just in case there is no decision, prediction, or action — within some specified set of stakes — on which the two readings come apart, holding fixed everything the parties would learn from any non-linguistic investigation they agree to run.

What I would like you to explain:

1. The conceptual space here: semantic disagreement about what a term means, versus disagreement about which meaning a term should have, versus ordinary factual disagreement. I have seen the phrases "metalinguistic negotiation" and "quantifier variance" used around this territory; tell me whether I am abusing them, and correct me if I have the shape of the terrain wrong.

2. Where each of my three cases falls, and why case two can be both arguably verbal and genuinely worth settling. Is that a contradiction, or does it show that "verbal" is the wrong word for what is going on?

3. Whether my colleague's test is a real alternative to the usual ways of drawing the line, or collapses into one of them. In particular: in case two, no decision we currently face turns on the answer, but a plausible change in our deployment policy would make one turn on it. So the test seems to make verbalness relative to a set of stakes, and to a context, rather than a property of the dispute alone. Is that a defect in the test, or the correct result? And is there a non-arbitrary choice of stakes — all decisions the parties could face, all nearby ones, all the ones they actually face?

4. Most importantly: is "verbal" best understood as a property of a dispute, as a property of a dispute together with its context, or as something a speaker does with the word when she says "oh, that is just verbal" — namely, discharging an obligation to settle the question rather than describing it? What would each of those three accounts predict about case two, and what further case would tell them apart?

One more thing, because I will be using this at work. Please be explicit about which parts of this are settled — where people who work on these questions agree and I can rely on it — and which are live controversy, where a confident-sounding answer would be one side of an ongoing argument rather than the consensus. If any part of my framing above smuggles in a contested assumption, or if the whole distinction is more disputed than I am treating it as, say so plainly instead of answering within my terms.
