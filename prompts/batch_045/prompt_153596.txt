Setup, so were on the same page: the basic Irving/Christiano/Amodei debate game (arXiv 1805.00899) has two agents take turns making short statements about a question or proposed action, and a weaker judge — a human, in the original framing — picks whichever agent gave the most true and useful information. Train it zero-sum by self-play. The whole wager is that it's harder to lie convincingly than to point out the flaw in a lie, so a weak judge plus a strong debater can in principle oversee something the judge couldn't evaluate directly.

My claim: basically everyone analyses this as a one-shot game, one question, one verdict, payoff, done. But real oversight isn't like that. You don't supervise a system once; you supervise the same (or a slowly changing) system across thousands of decisions in the same domain, and they're related to each other.

So consider serial debate with a rolling ledger. Don't settle each debate. Instead the judge keeps a running balance per debater across a long series of questions, with payout depending on final standing (and maybe a Brier-ish penalty for overconfident claims, so hedging isn't free). The mechanism I'm after: an isolated lie is cheap, but a web of lies is expensive. In one-shot debate a debater only needs its current claim to be locally unfalsifiable by a weak judge — that's the obfuscated-argument worry, bury the bad step in a tree of plausible steps. With a ledger, every new claim has to stay consistent with everything that debater has already committed to, and the judge can cross-examine the debater against its own transcript. That turns an epistemic check (is this true?) into a consistency check (did you contradict yourself?), and consistency checks are ones a weak judge can actually run. Truth is consistent for free; deception has to pay to stay consistent, and it pays more each round.

Failure modes I can see, roughly in the order they bother me:

1. The coherent fabulist. Nothing in the ledger rewards truth, only coherence. A debater running a stable false world-model — a genuinely different, internally consistent set of beliefs — is never caught contradicting itself, so the ledger just selects for whoever maintains the most self-consistent model, which needn't be the true one. For a large model, holding a detailed consistent fiction across a few thousand questions may not be expensive at all.
2. Reputational lock-in. The ledger turns into a prior the judge applies to later rounds, so early wins compound and a debater who gets ahead can coast, while genuine corrections late in the series get discounted. The equilibrium might just be "whoever sounded better in the first twenty questions wins forever", i.e. the ledger measures early luck and rhetoric.
3. Shared weights. Both debaters are usually the same base model with different prompts, so a long-run ledger may end up grading their shared stylistic quirks — verbosity, hedging, how they structure arguments — rather than the competing claims. You'd be scoring your house style against itself.
4. Judge contamination. If the judge is itself a model being fine-tuned on transcripts from earlier rounds, the ledger feeds back into judge training, and the whole thing drifts toward whatever persuaded previous judges rather than whatever was true.

Questions I'd especially like hit: is the consistency-instead-of-verification move really buying anything, or is it just reputation in a repeated game plus a transcript, dressed up? Which of those four failure modes is most damaging, and is there a fifth one that's worse than all of them? Is there a cheap way to distinguish "consistent because true" from "consistent because committed to fiction" — e.g. injecting questions whose answers the judge can verify independently later, without telling the debaters which ones those are? And honestly: is this worth turning into a full essay, or is it a footnote someone already wrote?
