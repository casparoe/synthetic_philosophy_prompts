Last week I was at one of those consciousness-and-machines meets, mostly reporting a piece on whether anyone should care about "AI welfare," and after the last talk I got stuck at dinner between two people who would not let this go. One's a simulation physicist who builds coarse-grained models of active matter (call her Nadia); the other's a philosopher of cognitive science (call him Frank). The subject was Hofstadter's careenium, which in case you don't know it: imagine a huge box of tiny magnetic marbles he calls simms. They jostle, and they clump into larger persisting structures — simmballs — and those simmballs shove each other around, merge, split, drift, in ways that have their own regularities. His question is who shoves whom: do the high-level patterns, the "symbols," push the substrate around, or is it always marbles pushing marbles?

The exchange, as close as I can reconstruct it (I think I've got the order right, I was on my second glass):

(1) Nadia: It's obviously all bottom-up. Every simmball motion is a lot of simms moving, and the simm-level story is causally complete, so the simmball level contributes nothing causally. It's compression. A useful shorthand. Patterns don't push, they're what pushing looks like from far away.

(2) Frank: That's the stock causal-exclusion move, and it sneaks in the premise that the micro-level laws are the ones doing the work. In the careenium the simmball-level generalisations look autonomous: you can't get them out of the simm dynamics without already knowing which clumps count as simmballs, and they support fine-grained counterfactuals — put this simmball there instead and a whole downstream cascade of simms goes differently. That's reliability under intervention, which is about as close to causation as we get an operational handle on.

(3) Nadia: Interventions settle nothing here. Any "simmball-level intervention" is carried out by physically rearranging a huge correlated batch of simms, so all you've shown is that some micro states have micro effects — the counterfactuals are true, but they're made true by the marbles. And look, your high-level laws break: every so often a simmball fizzles or merges in a way the simmball story doesn't predict. That's the signature of a handy description, not of a cause.

(4) Frank: Exceptions don't mark a fake level; the simm-level story runs on idealisations too. And so try the case I'll call the leaky careenium — my own variant, not Hofstadter's. Suppose the substrate is a hot, noisy thermal bath: local simm dynamics is genuinely random, the simm-level trajectories are unpredictable in principle, and the only stable, manipulable, counterfactual-supporting regularities in the box are at the simmball level. If causation tracks what can be reliably controlled and predicted, then there the simmballs are more causal than the simms, not less. If your account still drains all the causal work down to the simms in that case, it isn't physics, it's micro-chauvinism.

(5) Nadia: Then you've made causal facts depend on our epistemic situation. In the leaky careenium the simmballs get to be causes because we can't track the simms — that's causation collapsing into explanation. There's also a straight cost: on your view every simm's motion gets two causes, the micro one and the macro one, and you owe me an account of why that isn't systematic overdetermination. One is enough.

(6) Frank: It isn't overdetermination if the two aren't separate events — it's one event under two descriptions. But notice that if you take that route, I don't see how you still get to say the simms are the real causers and the simmballs are along for the ride.

I said nothing useful and went back to my hotel. Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the six, tell me whether it's clearly mistaken, partially right, or clearly right, and why, in a sentence or two — I care about which specific claim in each move survives, not just who wins. Then: who's more right overall, what (if anything) are they both missing, and is the leaky careenium doing real work in Frank's argument or is it a red herring?
