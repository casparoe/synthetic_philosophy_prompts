I am the permanent rapporteur — I keep the record, draft the text, and write the one-page note that goes out with each draft — of the standing drafting committee of a coalition of six membership organisations that issues joint position statements on health and social policy. Our members disagree not about the facts but about what matters: a public-health association that aggregates population welfare; a patient-choice advocacy organisation organised around individual autonomy; a professional association of clinicians whose commitments are role-based (non-abandonment, professional integrity); a disability-rights organisation concerned with equal standing; a network of faith-based hospitals and hospices bound by teaching on the sanctity of life; and a health-economics institute committed to cost-effectiveness. We publish eight to ten statements a year. They are quoted in regulatory consultations and in parliamentary evidence, and a member declining to sign is a public event; two withdrawals in three years have led our funders to ask what a joint voice is worth.

I have read a little of the philosophical discussion of compromise between value systems — the suggestion that parties who rank outcomes differently can each do better by their own lights under an agreed package than they would under no statement at all, in something like the way parties with different endowments each gain from trade. What I need is not more of that, but a procedure I can actually run.

The constraints are real. I get one ninety-minute session with the six representatives and one two-week written round. The representatives are senior policy professionals with no philosophical training, and none of them will read anything longer than a page. The chair rotates among the members, so I cannot rely on a neutral arbiter. Every member has a named representative who must personally sign, and I am the one who has to tell a member that the text it is being asked to sign is not one it can defend to its own constituency.

Please give me the following.

An ordered set of questions to run on a proposed package — the joint text as a whole, not clause by clause in isolation — with a statement, for each question, of what counts as a sufficient answer, which answers stop the procedure with a no, and which hand the decision to a person because it turns on values rather than on facts. The questions have to be answerable in the session by a non-specialist in a few minutes each.

Alongside it, a classification of the moves a concession can consist in, since I think the kind of concession matters more than its size. My own rough distinction is between conceding that one's principle does not reach this case, conceding that it carries less weight here than one claimed, conceding that someone else should decide, and leaving the words open so that each member can read them its own way. Correct, refine, or replace this: I want to know which of these distinctions has to survive into a one-page procedure and which can be dropped without loss.

A test that works across statements rather than within one. Members make small concessions repeatedly, and I suspect that a run of individually defensible concessions can carry the coalition somewhere none of the members would have endorsed at the start, without any single step being the one to blame. I keep a record of every concession by member and by kind; tell me what to do with it, and what to check when the same member has conceded more than once in the same way.

Two further complications the procedure has to survive. First, the same sentence is a different act for different members: for one it is a policy recommendation, for another it is an act of institutional witness that its own authority will judge, so identical words do not cost them the same. Second, some of our representatives have learned that declaring a red line early is rewarded; if the procedure simply asks each member where its limits are, it will be gamed, and I have no way to verify what a member tells me about its own thresholds.

Then apply the procedure to three cases I have in front of me, showing where it stops and why:

1. A statement on a national screening programme: automatic enrolment with an opt-out, which the public-health association wants and the patient-choice organisation resists; a duty-to-advise clause the clinicians want; a guarantee of accessible invitation formats the disability-rights organisation wants; and a cost-effectiveness threshold the health-economics institute wants, which would exclude one age band the public-health association cares about.

2. A statement on assisted dying for a parliamentary consultation, where the faith-based network cannot sign anything that implies permissibility and the patient-choice organisation wants an endorsement of access.

3. A statement on automated eligibility decisions in welfare, where the concession being asked of the patient-choice organisation is small and, taken alone, defensible — but it is the fourth concession of the same kind it has made in two years, and the regulator's consultation closes in three weeks.

I would expect the third to be the one where the procedure tells me something I do not want to hear; say plainly what that is and what I should do with it. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which of my problems it will leave exactly as it found them.
