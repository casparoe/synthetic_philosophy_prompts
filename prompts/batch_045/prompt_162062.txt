Hey — I'm running a one-off seminar session next week with a mixed room (my own grad students plus a handful of CS PhD students who are sick of the discourse), and I want to hand them something that forces them to locate themselves instead of grabbing a slogan. What I want from you is a decision tree, not an essay and not a reading list.

How I'd like it built:

- Nobody starts at a named position. Every node is a question about a concrete case or a claim someone could genuinely go either way on, phrased so that you can answer it without knowing which leaf it leads to. So not "does understanding require consciousness?" — give me the case, and let the requirement fall out of the answer.
- Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first the questions, then how many answer-combinations they allow, then drop the actual positions into the cells. If a cell is empty, say why — incoherent, unattractive, or just nobody's bothered — and flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate rather than pretending it can.
- Seven branch questions max, around eight leaves. Leaves I want included: the denial (fancy pattern matching, no understanding here), the behaviorist/functionalist yes, the yes-but-alien view (real competence, nothing it is like), grounding-required views, a mechanistic view that says we should be looking at the internals before verdicts, the view that the question is terminological and should be dissolved rather than answered, and one leaf for the competence being borrowed from the people who wrote the training data rather than belonging to the model at all.
- Cases I want worked in somewhere: a text-only model that answers counterfactual kitchen questions correctly without ever having seen a kitchen; a model that passes every behavioral probe you can design but whose internals turn out to be retrieval plus template filling; a model that can't tell you when it's guessing and can't use what it "knows" to steer anything; a model that gets a question right in one phrasing and collapses under an adversarial paraphrase; a model that can only revise after retraining, not from a single sentence in its context window. Swap in better ones if you have them.
- A distinction I've been toying with: understanding-as-answering vs understanding-as-being-answerable-to — whether what matters is emitting the right continuation or being the kind of thing that can be wrong in an informative way. Tell me honestly whether it earns a node; if it doesn't, drop it and say why.

Two things I definately don't want: the Chinese Room as a node, and any question that just asks whether they think it understands. My success condition is that someone who answers honestly and recieves the leaf with mild annoyance at least a third of the time.

Format: one page, under 600 words — I'm photocopying it as a single handout. Bullets and a rough ASCII tree are fine, no prose preamble.
