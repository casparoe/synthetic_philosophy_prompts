You are helping me draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration on the cognitive penetrability of perception. The two parties have argued past each other for years and have now agreed to produce one document they can both sign.

Party A, the Impenetrabilist, holds that the processes that deliver perceptual states are informationally encapsulated with respect to the subject's standing beliefs, desires, and expectations: cognitive states can change what a subject attends to, where she looks, what she infers, and what she says, and can slowly re-tune perceptual mechanisms through learning, but they cannot change the content of what she sees, hear, or feels on an occasion in virtue of what she believes. Party B, the Penetrationist, holds that at least some perceptual states have their content fixed partly by the subject's cognitive states, so that two subjects — or one subject at two times — can differ in what they perceive while the stimulation and the lower-level transductive and learned machinery are held fixed, and the difference is explained by the content of what they believe, want, or understand.

Write the document in four parts.

1. A numbered Agreed Claims section. Both parties have converged on the empirical facts, so do not spend words rehearsing who found what: state what they jointly take the phenomena to be. Include at least the following, where you can formulate them non-vacuously: the distinction between a change in perception and a change in judgment, inference, or report; a shared hierarchy of evidential markers for "genuine perceptual change" — sensitivity changes in forced choice, adaptation aftereffects, interocular or cross-modal transfer, persistence under instructions to disregard, and presence in a report-free dependent measure — and the shared admission that no one marker is individually decisive; the Peripheral Route Exclusion clause, on which effects that are fully mediated by eye movements, pupil diameter, or the shifting of spatial attention do not count as penetration even if they change performance; and the concession, on both sides, that perceptual mechanisms are plastic and that learning changes them, so that "the perceptual system was different afterwards" cannot by itself settle anything.

2. A Disagreements table: one row per residual disagreement, with columns for the issue, the formulation Party A would sign for its own view, the formulation Party B would sign for its own view, and a flag for whether you judge that disagreement to be genuinely substantive or merely verbal. Each formulation must be one its own side would actually accept, and must be strong enough to be worth disagreeing about.

3. A Cruxes table: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party, with columns for the crux, the case or measurement both parties accept as bearing on it, Party A's prediction in advance, and Party B's prediction in advance. Add, for each crux, what each party would count as a defeat for its own view. Both parties have agreed in advance to treat the following protocol as bearing on the cruxes, so build the cruxes around it rather than around generic appeals to future data. (Nothing here depends on anyone's having run the study; the point is that both accept it as diagnostic.)

The protocol: subjects train for a fixed number of hours, with identical trial counts and identical feedback, on forced-choice discrimination within a family of synthetic textures generated by a parametric three-parameter statistical model that no subject has encountered before. There are five arms. N is never told the generative rule. E is told the rule at hour zero. D is told the identical rule only after hour 120, with no additional training. F is told, at hour 120, a rule that is true of the training exemplars but inverted on the transfer set. M is told the correct rule but only in a formulation that makes no mention of the discriminative dimension, so that the belief is true of the stimuli without being about the dimension along which discrimination is measured. Dependent measures are taken at hours 120, 130, and 200: discrimination threshold, magnitude of a texture-contingent aftereffect, transfer to the untrained eye, and a report-free measure collected under fixation control.

Among the cruxes I expect you to isolate: whether an aftereffect whose magnitude tracks a newly acquired belief counts as penetration or as the signature of a re-tuned but still encapsulated mechanism; whether the semantic relation between the content of the cognitive state and the perceptual dimension matters, or whether any belief-driven change counts; whether an effect that tracks the believed rule on the transfer set rather than the statistics actually instantiated discriminates the parties, and what each predicts about arm F; and how the perceptual system is to be individuated over time, so that "the same mechanism, differently tuned" and "a different mechanism" can be told apart.

4. A short closing section: which components of the dispute you take to be verbal, and why, with the argument that would show they are not. Do not settle the dispute by declaring it wholly verbal, and do not adjudicate it; if a component resists both treatments, say so and leave it standing.

Constraints: no citations, bibliographies, or appeals to what some further study would show; the predictions must be concrete enough to be wrong. Write the whole thing in formal, precise prose that a third party could use as the terms of reference for running the study.
