I've been arguing with a friend about Benatar's asymmetry and I've managed to build an argument that seems to push it somewhere no defender of it wants to go. I've tried to multiply it out honestly and it keeps landing on the same place, so I'd like a diagnosis rather than a reassurance.

Set-up. Take the asymmetry in its usual four-part form: the presence of suffering is bad; the presence of happiness is good; the absence of suffering is good even when there is nobody who enjoys that absence; the absence of happiness is bad only when there is somebody whom it deprives. Grant its standard consequence: bringing a being into existence is never better for that being than not bringing it into existence, and is sometimes worse. Call this Asymmetry. Call the reason usually offered for it the No-Subject Reason: a benefit has to land on somebody, and in the outcome where the possible person never exists there is nobody for it to land on, whereas avoiding suffering needs no recipient.

The argument:

(1) Asymmetry is true.

(2) Temporal neutrality about nonexistence: whether a stretch of a being's nonexistence is bad for that being cannot depend on whether the stretch lies before or after the being's existence. The being's condition during the stretch is the same either way — there isn't one — so the only remaining difference is the direction of time, and direction of time is not the sort of thing that can make a state of affairs worse for someone. This is the same thought that powers the old symmetry argument about the time before you were born: if that stretch did you no harm, its mirror image does you none either.

(3) So, by (1) and (2): ceasing to exist is never worse for the one who ceases than continuing to exist. Death is not a harm to the person who dies; it only ever spares them. Call this the No-Harm-of-Exit lemma.

(4) Fix any person at any time. By (3), continuing to exist carries zero expected benefit-credit, and it carries some nonzero probability of net suffering. So exiting at that time weakly dominates continuing, in expectation, for that person. Call this Exit Dominance.

(5) Nor does exit make things worse for anyone else in the way that would matter here. It deprives the person of no happiness, by (3); and by the fourth part of the asymmetry, forgone happiness is not bad unless it deprives somebody. Survivors' distress is a real cost but a contingent one, so hold it fixed at zero by stipulating painless exits nobody notices. A clean version of the argument shouldn't depend on how noisy the exits are.

(6) So for every existing sentient being, painlessly ceasing to exist is personally weakly dominant and impersonally strictly better: avoided suffering counts positively, forgone happiness carries no offsetting debit.

(7) If an available act is weakly dominant for each person it affects and strictly better impersonally, then, other things equal, it is not merely permitted — it is what a benevolent planner should arrange, and on any maximizing view required.

(8) Therefore the asymmetry, if true, does not recommend slowing the conveyor belt. It recommends winding the thing down: a coordinated, painless, permanent exit for everyone alive, and the sooner the better, since each day of continued existence adds expected suffering against no personal benefit-credit.

(9) Nobody who defends the asymmetry accepts (8), and neither do I. The view is supposed to be about adding people, not about removing them; it is supposed to leave everyone already on the bus with a reason to stay on the bus. So something in (1)–(8) is false.

What I want is the location of the break and the price list. Concretely:

- If the break is at (3): is the No-Subject Reason being stretched past its own rationale? At the creation decision there is no subject, and no desires, to be benefited; at the exit decision there is a subject with forward-looking desires that continuing would satisfy and exiting would frustrate. If that is the fix, what does the asymmetry become — a claim about merely possible people only, quiet about existing ones — and can it still generate the anti-natalist conclusion it was built for, given that this conclusion is supposed to be about what we should do here and now?

- If the break is at (4): does Exit Dominance misdescribe the choice? Continuing is not a fixed gamble; it is an option that contains exiting later as a sub-case, so the value of continuing is at least the value of exiting now, and it is the asymmetry's refusal to credit future happiness, not anything about time, doing all the work in making the comparison look one-sided. If so, is that zero-credit clause the load-bearing and objectionable part, rather than the step (3) I thought was weakest?

- If the break is at (5) or (7): is the argument smuggling in an impersonal maximizing standard that the asymmetry's own defenders reject, since they typically frame the view as a duty not to create sufferers rather than a duty to minimize suffering? And if they reject that standard here, does the asymmetry still yield any action-guiding conclusion at all, or does it collapse into the inert claim that existence is not a benefit to the person who exists, which by itself is a reason to do nothing?

Tell me which step you reject, why that step looked fine, and what the rival rejections would cost — in particular whether any of them leaves the asymmetry intact as a reason against procreation while blocking (8). I'd most like to keep (1); I have no attachment to anything downstream of it. If the break has a standard name in the literature, say what it is, but I want the reasoning, not a pointer to where to read it.
