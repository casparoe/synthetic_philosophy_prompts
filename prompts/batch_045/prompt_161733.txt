The PhD student running our HPS reading group has one image he keeps coming back to against scientific realism: the history of science is a graveyard. Theories get buried — phlogiston, the humours, caloric, the luminiferous ether — each was well confirmed in its day, so by induction ours are going the same way; and since the central terms of the dead ones (phlogiston, caloric) turned out not to refer to anything, ours probably dont either. His punchline is that the realist is stuck claiming our era is special, which is exactly what the dead guys would of said.

Can you audit this for me? I want the correspondence table — what plays the organism, what plays death and the gravedigger, what burial maps onto — and then which features of an actual graveyard the history of theories doesnt have. Two things I cant get straight on my own. First, things get exhumed: atomism, continental drift. Does resurrection help the realist, or does the anti-realist just say they were never properly dead? Second, I want to split burials into two kinds — a theory that dies because a successor explains its successes, versus one that just gets abandoned as a dead research program — and my sense is the induction only has teeth for the first kind, but I cant tell whether that distinction is doing real work or just gerrymandering the sample.

And is the conclusion actually carried by the image? The step from 'some central terms of past theories failed to refer' to 'ours probably do too' feels like something he adds on rather than something the analogy hands him. My supervisor says the better image is Neurath's boat — planks swapped out one by one, none of them original, same ship, still afloat — but I worry that just builds in the continuity the realist needs. Does the boat do better work than the graveyard, and is there some third comparison that does better than either corpses or planks?
