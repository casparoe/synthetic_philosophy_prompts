For the philosophy-club zine at my school I'm putting together a one-page flowchart called Quit or Keep Going?, and I'd like help building it properly. Every summary I've found says the same thing: the past is gone, only future costs and benefits matter, so staying in is always a mistake. That feels too fast. Grinding on with a book you stopped enjoying, staying in a band you've lost interest in, and finishing a group project after your partner dropped out don't seem to me like the same mistake — or, in some of those cases, like a mistake at all. I suspect there are several different reasons why carrying on can still be reasonable, and that the chart should sort cases by which reason is actually in play rather than just labelling them all as errors.

Please build me a decision tree with about seven leaves. Every branch should be a question about a concrete case someone my age would face — a half-finished coding project, a friendship that has drifted, a sport you no longer enjoy, a promise to help someone move, a language course you have nearly paid off — and no question should merely ask whether continuing is worth it, or use jargon that gives away which leaf it leads to. Build the tree from the distinctions rather than from a list of names: I want branches for whether anything is really recoverable or whether continuing keeps an option open or buys information, whether the person has tied the project to who they are or taken on a commitment that gives them a reason to go on, whether other people are relying on them, and whether what is at stake is the sort of thing that can be swapped for an equivalent later (money versus someone's trust, or a last chance to do something). Then please mark which combinations of answers the tree allows but that nobody sensible holds — and say whether they are empty because incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored — and flag any two leaves your tree cannot really separate.
