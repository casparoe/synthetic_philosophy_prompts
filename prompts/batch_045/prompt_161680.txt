For the past year I have had two occupations that keep collapsing into each other. By day I am a research engineer on a team that trains language models with reinforcement learning from human preference data, where much of my job consists of noticing that a policy has found a way to score highly on the learned reward model without doing anything the reward model was meant to point at. By evening I am one of my mother's two carers. She has Parkinson's disease; since her neurologist raised her dopamine agonist she has developed compulsive gambling and spending, has let most of her earlier interests lapse, and says, with complete sincerity and some impatience with us, that this is the happiest she has been since the diagnosis. My brother and I disagree about whether anything has gone badly for her, and we disagree in a way I cannot locate: every argument I make to him about her is an argument I have made at work about a policy gaming its reward signal, and I want to know whether that is a structural identity or a pun.

What I would like from you is a decision tree — a classification instrument, not an essay — on wireheading and reward tampering: something that moves a reader from a single starting commitment they can grant without knowing anything about the debate, through a series of branch questions, to a named leaf position.

Requirements on how it is built:

Start from the thin claim both sides of my family argument accept: that the intervention raises the subject's reported reward or pleasure and lowers nothing the subject reports as suffering. Do not let any node presuppose a theory of welfare.

Generate the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Give me the branching questions first, state which combinations of answers they permit, and only then place the actual positions in the cells — and mark the unoccupied cells, with a one-line diagnosis of why nobody is there (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored). The leaves should include, at minimum: the view that nothing bad has happened at all; the depletion or opportunity-cost view; the view that what matters is the causal provenance of the pleasurable state, i.e. that the reward system has a tracking function and the intervention destroys the tracking relation while leaving the feeling intact; the view that the loss is a loss of agency or activity rather than of feeling; the identity or narrative view; the informed-or-ideal-advisor desire view; and the view that no persisting subject remains about whom the question has an answer. At least one leaf should be labelled unnamed.

Several of the branch nodes should be distinctions I have had to invent to describe what I am seeing, because the standard vocabulary does not separate the cases I care about: whether the intervention leaves the reward signal still counterfactually tracking something about the world, a gradient the agent could in principle follow, or flattens it into a constant; whether the elevation is smooth and ambient or arrives as discrete spikes locked to events; whether the tampering is at the level of the target — gaming the proxy while still acting in the world, as my policies do — or at the level of the sensor, bypassing the world entirely; whether the dose has to escalate to hold the effect constant, or plateaus; and whether the subject, restored to their pre-intervention state, would decline the intervention on reflection. One node must be about epistemic authority: whose report settles the matter, the subject's sincere avowal or third-personal criteria about the subject's functioning, since that is precisely where my brother and I come apart.

Phrase every node as a question someone could answer without knowing which leaf it leads to, and anchor each one in a concrete case: my mother, a rat with an electrode it can press for, a policy exploiting a learned reward model, someone who never leaves a simulation. Eight to ten branch nodes, seven to nine leaves, each leaf carrying one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot actually separate, and say what further question would separate them, or whether nothing would.

Finally, run the instrument twice: once on my mother's case as I have described it, once on the reward-model exploitation case at work, and tell me whether they land in the same cell, in different cells, or in cells the tree cannot order with respect to one another. Assume the reader is my brother — a cardiologist, quick, no philosophy — and that he will be checking whether the questions have been rigged to reach my conclusion.
