hey — I need help finding the broken step in an argument I wrote for a metaphysics seminar (short paper on the whole "why is there something rather than nothing" question). I was pretty happy with it until a friend ran the same reasoning on a kitchen fire and got an absurd result, and now I can't tell which premise to drop. Our handout had a name for the key move — it called it the Enclosure Principle: an explanation of a fact may not cite anything that is itself part of the fact being explained, on the grounds that a whole can't be accounted for by drawing on its own parts, any more than a fact can explain itself. Here's what I built out of that, more or less as I turned it in:

(1) Explanation is irreflexive: if the fact that E explains the fact that F, then E is not identical to F. Nothing explains itself.
(2) Suppose the question has an answer: some fact E explains the fact S — the fact that there is something rather than nothing.
(3) S is the most inclusive fact there is. Whatever exists is a part of it; there is nothing outside it to explain it with.
(4) Any candidate explaining fact E is itself something that exists. So E is a part of S.
(5) [Enclosure] No fact can be explained by anything that is part of it.
(6) So E cannot explain S, contradicting (2). Nothing could have explained S: the question is not merely unanswered, it is unanswerable in principle, and not because we're ignorant but because of what sort of question it is.
(7) But now run the same five steps on a local fact. Take F: there is a fire in the kitchen. What explains it? The gas leak, the pilot light, the tea towel left on the hob.
(8) Those causes are things that exist, and an effect can't obtain unless its causes obtain — so their existing is, in the relevant sense, part of what F involves. By (5), then, they can't explain F either.
(9) Repeating (7)–(8) for arbitrary F: no causal explanation of anything is possible. Arson investigators, historians and doctors are not explaining anything when they tell you what caused the fire, the war or the rash.
(10) That's absurd, so something in (1)–(5) has to go.

I'd most like to keep (1) and the conclusion in (6) — I actually like the idea that the cosmological question is a structurally bad question rather than a hard one, and I don't want to give that up just because the argument overreaches. But I also don't want to be the person who says causal explanation is impossible, so I need to know what I'm really paying. Two things people said in seminar that I couldn't answer: one person said a *necessary* explaining fact escapes, because a necessary being or a necessary principle isn't part of the contingent furniture the question was about, so (4) fails for it and the argument only refutes contingent explanations. Another said (1) is too weak to support (5) and that I've quietly switched from "the explainer isn't identical to the explained" to something much stronger, but when I asked what exactly switched they just waved at the board. So: which step do you reject, why did it look innocent, and what does each of the other options cost me — including whether the necessary-fact escape hatch actually gets me the conclusion in (6) or quietly gives it away?
