I am trying to work out what I actually think about the authority of a community's memory when it collides with the documentary record, and I would like help extracting the principle — or, more realistically, the two or three candidate principles — that my verdicts on particular cases add up to. I am not asking for a bibliography or for the state of the literature; I have read enough of it to know the standard moves. What I want is the shape of the principle, stated precisely enough that I can test it against cases I have not yet considered.

The cases below are composites. I have changed names, places, and dates, and nothing in what follows needs to be looked up; please treat them as stipulations rather than as history to be checked.

1. A roadside marker in a hamlet in upstate New York, erected in 1921 by the local historical society, reads: *Near this spot the settlers of Harrow Ford drove off the Marlow gang, 1791.* The county militia pay rolls and a 1792 grand jury presentment tell a different story: four men were killed, they were unarmed debtors being pursued by a creditor's agent, and the settlers were fined. My verdict: the marker comes down. The town's memory gives way completely, and it should be replaced by something it does not want.

2. A steelworkers' local in Ohio has told, since 1977, that its foundry strike was broken when a man named Ray Kovac crossed the picket line. Payroll records and a hospital admission record put Kovac in surgery that week. His granddaughter teaches at the high school and has grown up with the story. My verdict: the local owes a public correction, and it owes it even though the story is the thing that holds its identity together, and even though paying it will cost the local the only heroic episode it has.

3. A K'iche'-speaking village in the western highlands remembers that in 1929 the landowner's militia killed a large part of the village at the river bend. The district judge's report for that quarter records *complete tranquility* and no disturbances anywhere in the department; the land registry shows transfers consistent with the families having fled. My verdict: the memory does not give way. The silence of that archive is not evidence against the memory; it is part of what the memory is about.

4. A village in the Carpathians remembers that in 1946 the people of the next valley burned its church and killed forty of its men. The parish register and the district court's 1947 indictments show the direction reversed: its own men went up the valley first. My verdict: the record governs, the village should revise its account, and the cost — that it stops being able to think of itself as the wronged party — does not exempt it. I hold this alongside case 3, and I am aware the two need a distinction I have not yet stated.

5. A town's national day of mourning commemorates *the Three Hundred* of the sack of Valmira in 1689. Tax rolls, hearth counts, and the burial register support something between sixty and eighty dead. My verdict, and this is the one I keep going back and forth on: the figure should be corrected in schoolbooks and on the civic monument, but the liturgy may go on saying "three hundred." Something in me thinks the prayer is not making the same kind of claim the monument is. Something else in me thinks I have invented that difference to avoid a cost.

6. My husband's family has always said that his great-great-uncle enlisted in a Colored Infantry regiment in 1863. The muster rolls put him in a quartermaster labor detail, and there he stayed. My verdict: the family should stop asserting it flatly, but it would be grotesque to demand they stop telling it. It should be told as what it is — a family belief the record does not support — and I do not think the record's authority reaches far enough into a Sunday dinner to require more.

7. An outport community on the Newfoundland coast has said since 1963 that the trawler *Sea Lark* was lost because the owner refused to replace a broken radio. The 1963 inquiry blamed the weather and exonerated the owner. In 2011 the owner's correspondence turned up in an estate sale and confirmed the refusal. My verdict: the community had been right, but more than that — the inquiry's authority was never as great as it presented itself as having, and it was a mistake, in 1963, to treat it as settling the question.

The principle I have been toying with has three moving parts. First: deference to the record is *earned*, not automatic, and it is earned by the record's independence from the interests at stake, by its counterfactual sensitivity — would this archive look different if the memory were true? — and by its willingness to speak against the interest of whoever made it. Second: even a fully earned record governs only the assertoric content of a memory, never its commemorative force. Third, and least developed: I keep wanting an analogy to adverse possession in property law, so that a memory held openly, continuously, and under claim of right for three generations acquires a standing the record cannot simply extinguish, however much better the evidence may be.

Here is what I would like from you.

State the strongest version of that principle, and at least one genuinely different rival, each precisely enough to generate verdicts. Motivate every clause by a case above, and say which case each clause exists to save. Then tell me, honestly, which of my verdicts your preferred principle cannot accommodate and which of them you would overturn — I would rather lose a verdict than keep a principle that only works by multiplying clauses. Apply both candidates to four cases I have not listed: a community that would prefer to forget an atrocity the record insists on; a four-hundred-year-old memory with no living person affected either way; a document of dubious provenance that surfaces and appears to corroborate a memory; and a case where memory and record agree on the event but disagree about who struck the first blow. Finally, tell me where the adverse-possession analogy clarifies things and where it quietly does damage — in particular whether it is doing any work that the other two clauses are not already doing.
