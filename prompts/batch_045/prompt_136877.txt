I cover artificial intelligence for a science magazine, and I keep having the same experience in interviews. When I ask people in the control camp — the ones who design deployment on the assumption that the model may be misaligned and try to make that survivable — what evidence would change their mind, they say: convincing evidence that the model is in fact aligned. When I ask people in the alignment camp the mirror-image question, they say: evidence that the model is actively trying to sabotage us. Neither side ever receives what it asks for, and after a great many hours of this I cannot point to a single conversation in which anyone moved. I want to write a piece diagnosing why.

Here is my hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than confirm. I do not think this is a disagreement about a probability, or even about a level of tolerated risk. I think the two research programmes have asymmetric success conditions that make them mutually unfalsifiable below deployment scale. A control regime that works produces no evidence that it was necessary: if monitoring, sandboxing and limited-trust deployment never surface sabotage, that outcome is equally consistent with a misaligned model being successfully deterred, with there being no misalignment at all, and with sabotage we lacked the resolution to detect. Conversely, a claim that a model is aligned is only genuinely refuted by a catastrophe, and a catastrophe is precisely the evidence nobody is willing to wait for. Each camp, on this account, is demanding from the other a kind of evidence the other's method cannot supply in principle.

I would also like you to consider a second, more specific possibility: that beneath the methodological difference lie two different pictures of what the bad outcome even looks like — a discrete defection by a model that has been biding its time, versus a gradual erosion of human control through capability, delegation and dependence — and that each side's preferred measures look obviously correct given its own picture and obviously misdirected given the other's.

What I want is a diagnosis of the structure of this dispute, with reasons: not a survey of the positions, and not a verdict on who is right. Tell me whether my asymmetry story is the right account, or whether the real fault line lies elsewhere — clashing standards of evidence, different tolerances for speculative harm, a hidden empirical question about how detectable deception actually is in models of the relevant size, or a verbal dispute conducted in a shared vocabulary. Then the part I most need: given your diagnosis, what would count as progress? I am particularly interested in whether deliberate demonstrations of sandbagging or scheming on today's models, or interpretability work that turns up goal-directed internal structure, really do the evidential work their proponents claim, or whether such results move the argument only rhetorically. If the honest answer is that nothing short of a serious incident settles it, say so, and tell me what epistemic posture that leaves a journalist, and a policymaker, in.
