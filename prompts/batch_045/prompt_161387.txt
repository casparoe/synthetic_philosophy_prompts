Hey, hoping you can settle something that's been bugging me since discussion section last week. I'm in an undergrad ethics seminar and we spent a whole session on the claim that an action has moral worth only if it's done for the right reason — the standard shopkeeper case: the guy who gives correct change only because cheating would hurt repeat business does the right thing, but it doesn't count for much morally. I get the point. But then we had a guest lecture from someone in psych about how bad people are at reporting their own motives: people confidently explain choices they never actually made, self-interest disguises itself as concern for others, that kind of thing. And now I think the moral-worth idea is in real trouble, because if I can't reliably tell what my operative reason is at the moment I'm acting, then I can never act *because it's the right reason* on purpose. And stopping to audit my motives mid-action seems to make it worse, since the checking becomes its own motive — wanting to be the sort of person whose actions have moral worth — which is itself self-interested. So I've started calling this the transparency condition: a motive counts as the right one only if the agent can tell, at the time, that it's the right one. Given what we know about introspection, almost nothing clears that bar.

Concretely: my neighbor shovels my driveway when I'm out of town, and he genuinely can't say why. His words were something like, 'I dunno, I was already out there, it just seemed like the thing to do' — no warm glow, no thought of reciprocity, no sense of duty. On the class standard his action apparently has no moral worth, which seems like a crazy result, so I've been toying with two repairs. Fix one: drop the first-person requirement and say the action has moral worth if a well-informed outside observer would attribute the right motive to him, judging from behavior and circumstances the way we always judge other people's motives anyway. Fix two: keep the first-person requirement but add a screening test — the action has moral worth only if he'd still shovel after an honest motive check, i.e., even after being told 'you're doing this for the good feeling, or to bank a favor.'

Which of those two fixes is the better repair, and am I right that the transparency condition is what's actually doing the damage here? I also want to know whether theres a version of the view that keeps moral worth from collapsing into just 'acted rightly and things went well,' because that's what I keep sliding into. Could you answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no intro and no wrap-up — and if you think I've set the whole problem up wrong, say that in one of the five.
