My half-formed idea: the two paradoxes are normally presented as one puzzle — a set of propositions each of which one is justified in believing, and which cannot all be true — but they differ in how the individual beliefs are evidentially sourced, and that difference decides which dissolution is available. In the lottery, every "ticket n will lose" is supported by one and the same body of evidence about the design of the lottery, so the step to the contradiction reuses that evidence many times over. In the preface, each claim in the book has its own evidence, and the "there is an error somewhere" belief rests on separate, higher-order evidence about the author's own fallibility. So the hypothesis is that a principle which blocks closure only for conjunctions whose support is evidentially redundant dissolves the lottery while leaving the preface intact, and that the preface then requires either a further restriction covering evidence that concerns the reliability of the very processes that produced the other conjuncts, or else the abandonment of the demand that first-order and higher-order justification be jointly satisfiable.
