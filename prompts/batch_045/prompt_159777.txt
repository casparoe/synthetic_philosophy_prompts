I'm a novelist — two books out, one of them did okay — and I'm about 60,000 words into a new one and stuck on something that I think is secretly a philosophy problem. Short version of the setup: it's maybe forty years from now, most people spend a big chunk of their lives in a persistent shared virtual world, they work there, they own things there, some of them get married there. The plot is a murder mystery: someone's avatar is 'killed' with an extremely rare in-game sword, and the whole book turns on whether my detective treats it as a homicide or shrugs it off. I've been reading around, and a friend of mine who does philosophy talked me into this position: the sword is a real object. Not a fiction, not an illusion — a real thing with real causal powers, grounded in real voltages in real hardware somewhere, bought and sold for real money, capable of really upsetting people. She also made some point about the sword not being *identical* to those voltages, the way a song isn't the same thing as the record it's pressed on, which I nodded along to but I'm not sure I could reconstruct.

Here's the problem. My writing group read a chunk, and one of them said: look, if you actually believe the sword is real, then you're committed to the killing being real murder, and if you flinch from that you've given the whole thing away. And I definately flinch, but I can't figure out how to flinch in a principled way. My prosecutor character keeps making the same move in slightly different words — the sword is real, so the place where it happened is a real place that my client went to, so it happened somewhere, so we have a jurisdiction and a victim and a corpse — and I can hear that some link in that chain is rotten and I can't tell which one. Everything I can think of to give the detective collapses into 'it's only a game,' which is the one line I refuse to write, because the entire book is built on this world mattering enormously to these people.

I'm at a residency up in the mountains with almost no signal, so I can't go chase anything down — please don't just hand me a list of things to read, I need the actual argument. Is the prosecutor's chain as broken as it sounds to me, and where exactly does it break? Concretely: can I keep the sword being genuinely real and still have my detective refuse to call this a murder without being a hypocrite, and if so, what's the sharpest way for her to put it in about two lines of dialogue?
