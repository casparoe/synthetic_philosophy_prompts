Hi — bit of an odd request, and I'll explain why before I ask for it. I spent thirty-odd years doing spectroscopy, retired four years ago, and since then I've drifted into philosophy the way my friends drift into birdwatching, except I'm now the annoying one who insists the group reads the hard bits. We're mostly retired scientists plus one engineer who is professionally unimpressed by everything. We've just burned two whole sessions on "why is there something rather than nothing?", and it went exactly as you'd predict: the physicist said the vacuum isn't nothing, the mathematician said the numbers are there whatever you do, somebody said "God" and got the look, and somebody else said the question is meaningless and got accused of dodging. So what I want from you is a map, not an essay.

What I'd like is a taxonomy of the positions on why there is something rather than nothing, drawn as a plain-text tree, every branch labelled with the claim that splits it. Think of it as one of those identification keys we used for rocks in field geology: you start at a node, answer a question, follow the arrow, and you land somewhere. The point is that someone in my group ends up at a leaf without ever being asked which "-ism" they fancy. So: each node has to be a question a retired chemist or engineer can answer on the spot, before they've any idea where it leads. No node that just asks "do you believe in God?", and no node that asks "is the universe contingent?" in those words — concrete case first, label only at the leaf.

Start node, fixed: I take it there is at least one thing, and asking "why?" at least looks like it makes sense. Anything that wants to reject that should come off the tree early and get named, not quietly ignored.

Three cases I want doing the real work, because my group only argues properly about examples:

1. The "frozen formalism" case: no concrete objects at all, ever, but the mathematical structure and the laws stay exactly as they are — the equations, the symmetries, the whole apparatus, with nothing for it to be true of. Is that a case of nothing?
2. The "books balance" case: a universe where everything that could be conserved sums to zero — mass-energy against gravitational energy and so on, all of it cancelling — so there's plenty going on but the totals are nil. Is that nearer something or nothing, and does it still stand in need of an explanation?
3. The "one lonely particle" case: empty space, one structureless point-mass, and no laws saying what it does.

Then some further splits to fit in wherever they actually belong, in the order that's logically right rather than the order I've listed them: whether the "why" is asking for a cause, for a metaphysical ground, or only for something that would satisfy us; whether the principle that everything has a reason is being applied to each item or to the whole lot at once; whether an explanation has to come to a stop or is allowed to be a loop (the universe bringing itself about, or grounding itself, if you can make that not sound like a conjuring trick); whether a selection effect does any work — if there are ever so many totalities and only some of them contain anyone to ask the question, does that answer it or just relocate it?; and whether nothing was ever on the table in the first place, i.e. whether there being nothing isn't a way things could have been.

Leaves: between ten and fourteen of them. Give the name where there is one and write "unnamed" where there isn't, with one sentence each on the most awkward thing that position is stuck with — the bit my group would go for. Among them, please make sure there are leaves for: a necessary concrete being of the classical-theism shape; the thinner view that something-or-other exists of necessity but nobody commits to what; the view that there's simply no explanation, it's brute; the view that the question doesn't get a grip on the totality and should be dissolved; the view that nothing was never an option; the anthropic or selection-effect deflation; the platonist or structuralist leftover, where the maths is necessarily there so the question was never about a bare nothing; a self-grounding or loop universe; the "it adds up to nothing" idea from case 2 above, if you think it can carry a leaf of its own; and the view that there is an answer but not one we could have in principle.

Two more things. First, be straight about where the key stops working: if two leaves differ in temperament but not in anything my group could actually answer differently, say so instead of inventing a question to pry them apart — I suspect the brute-fact leaf and the dissolutionist leaf are like that, but you tell me. Second, give me two or three combinations of answers the tree allows that nobody in fact occupies, with a line each on why: incoherent, unattractive, or just not tried.

And no reading lists, bibliographies or "as X argues in his 1987 book" — we're all on tablets, none of us can get to a university library, and anything that needs a reference chasing up is dead to us. I'd rather have your own framing anyway.

Around 900–1100 words, British English throughout (organisation, sceptical, favour, -ise endings), and keep it walkable: short nodes, one idea per branch, nothing that needs a paragraph of setup before a person can answer it. Also — my group definately will pick a fight with whatever you send, so where a fork is really a matter of taste rather than a genuine fork, mark it as one that doesn't bite.

Cheers — Alan.
