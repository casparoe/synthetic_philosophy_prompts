Hey — I'm writing the final for my upper-level decision theory / formal epistemology seminar (mixed bag of advanced undergrads and first-year PhDs, they've all had one semester of expected utility, it's an in-class paper and this is meant to be a 45-minute question out of two hours). The topic I want to hit is Good's theorem on the value of information — the result that a free signal can't lower an expected-utility maximiser's ex ante expected utility, so the value of an experiment is non-negative and is zero only when the signal is decision-irrelevant. The trouble is that last year basically everyone answered it in one line with the you-can-always-ignore-the-information argument, and I gave them most of the marks for it, which drove me up the wall. It's not false, but it isn't a proof and it hides exactly the two things I want tested: that the engine of the proof is a swap of the max and the expectation (equivalently, the convexity of the value-of-a-belief function, i.e. a Jensen argument), and that the step silently assumes the feasible action set is the same in every cell of the signal. A colleague in econ also told me over coffee that the ignore-it argument settles the whole matter, and I'd like the exam to be the place where that reply visibly runs out.

First, the question itself, self-contained, with a short notation preamble the students can work from (finite states, finite actions, bounded state-act utility, a prior, and a signal whose likelihoods may depend on the state but not on the action chosen — no one in the class has seen the variant I describe below, so spell it out). Split it into three parts, roughly 10 / 12 / 8 out of 30, and tell me if you'd weight it differently. Part (b) is the one I care about: give them a variant where the signal is observed not only by the agent but by a second party who then sets the agent's menu, so the feasible set is signal-contingent, and ask for a fully numerical counterexample with small numbers and the arithmetic shown in two or three lines where a free, perfectly informative signal has strictly negative value.
