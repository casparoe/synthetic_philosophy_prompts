Here's what's bugging me. When the 737 MAX was involved in two fatal crashes a few months apart, regulators grounded the entire fleet worldwide: every airframe of that type, including ones flown by carriers with completely different maintenance cultures, pilot training, and weather. Two events, and the response treated it as a fact about all of them. Now compare medicine. A handful of severe adverse events will not get a drug pulled; regulators want a statistical signal, usually from trials or big pharmacovigilance datasets. But then medicine turns around and does what looks like the exact opposite: one drug in a class gets a safety warning, and clinicians extend the worry across the whole class — different molecules, sometimes different pharmacologies — on evidence far weaker than what they demanded for the original drug. And in software: a single outlier spike on a dashboard gets waved off as noise, but one user-visible failure after a deploy gets the whole release rolled back.

So: what does this family of practices presuppose about what a mechanism is, and about when two events count as manifestations of the same one?

A colleague of mine has a test he calls the "fix-travels" criterion: two events share a mechanism iff an intervention you designed by studying only the first event would also have prevented the second. I like it, but I don't think it's what regulators are doing, because they ground the fleet *before* they know what the fix is. Another candidate: mechanisms are individuated by the space of anomalies they can generate, so grounding is justified when the two crashes sit in the same "could have been worse in these ways" region. A third: none of this is about mechanism identity at all, only about the asymmetry of error costs.

Which of these does the practice strictly *presuppose*, which does it merely sit well with, and where is it genuinely underdetermined — i.e., where would two rival accounts of mechanism identity license the exact same policy? Is the class-effect move in medicine inconsistent with the demand for a statistical signal, or do they quietly rest on different views? And if you swapped the implicit view for the fix-travels one, what concretely would have to change in how these decisions get made — would we ground less, investigate more, or just ask a different question first?
