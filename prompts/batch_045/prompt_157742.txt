Hey — I'm on the policy/trust team at a midsize company that ships a general-purpose assistant, and I'm rewriting our refusal guidelines. Right now theyre a mess: a list of banned topics plus one line saying "defualt to helping," and our raters are basically freelancing on everything in between. What I want instead is an actual decision procedure a front-line rater can run in about 90 seconds, with no philosophy training, to decide when the assistant should (a) just do it, (b) do it but say one thing alongside, (c) make it slightly harder — a delay, or an alternative offered first, (d) do a partial version, like giving the shape of a thing but not the specifics, or (e) refuse.

Constraints on our setup, since they matter: a rater sees the current conversation and the short memory file the user has explicitly saved, but no browsing or purchase history, and no web access. Every decision is logged and audited, and we have to be able to explain it to the user in a single sentence. Anything that requires guessing at facts about the user's life should stop and hand off rather than guess. Senior reviewers sign off on escalations within a day.

Where I want the theory to do real work: I've read around a bit and keep bumping into Mill's harm principle, hard versus soft paternalism, means versus ends paternalism, and the hypothetical-consent / "reasonable person" move — but I can't turn any of it into steps. So please build me the ladder of escalating friction and fill in the gates: what has to be true before the assistant climbs a rung, what stops it at a no, and where it has to hand the call to a human because it's a values question rather than a factual one. Be explicit about which step is doing which piece of philosophical work (which gate is the harm principle, which is the "are they actually choosing badly or just choosing unusually" check, which is the "is this their ends or just their means" check), and be honest about the places where the procedure just can't settle things.

Two things I specifically need handled, because they're where our current guidelines fall apart:

- Our assistant has "durable instructions": a user can save a standing rule for themselves, like "don't help me buy camera gear after 9pm" or "don't draft my resignation letters for me." Do those bind, and when does an earlier self get to overrule the person in front of us right now, given that we can't check whether they still endorse the rule?
- Requests that look self-regarding but actually land on an identifiable third party. That isn't paternalism at all, so I want it routed somewhere else in the procedure, with a test for when a third party really is identifiable versus just "someone, somewhere."
