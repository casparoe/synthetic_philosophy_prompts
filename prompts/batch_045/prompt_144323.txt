So I'm about two hundred pages into a novel and I've written myself into a corner, and part of the problem is that I got into an argument about it after a reading last week and genuinely can't tell who won.

The setup: the book follows a merchant family in a fictional port city over roughly forty years, and the thing that happens across it is the collapse of a practice the whole mercantile class takes for granted. Call it "wardship." A poorer family signs a child over to a wealthier household; the child lives in the house, works, is fed and taught a little, and everyone describes this as being "raised up." It's obviously built out of things that really existed, but I invented the details, and three things about my version matter. One: it's profitable, and it's still profitable on the last page — nobody stops because it stopped paying. Two: it dies among the rich households first and fastest, not among the families giving up the children and not by any revolt from below. Three: the people who stop say things like "we don't do that anymore" and get impatient when asked whether it was *wrong*. One of my characters, a merchant named Aldous Sarn, quietly releases his two wards without explaining himself, and when his brother corners him he says something like "it was never wrong. It's just that a man can't do it now."

I had been planning to write the scene where a pamphleteer corners Aldous in his counting house and argues him out of it. Two friends — both ex-academics, which is the whole problem — said that scene would be a lie. Here's roughly how it went.

Lyle: You can't write that scene. There's a point of Bernard Williams's that kills it: the Enlightenment picture where moral argument refutes the opponent is a fantasy. People don't acquire new moral dispositions by being argued into them; nobody in the history of the world was reasoned out of dueling. What actually kills these practices is honor — the practice stops being compatible with being an honorable person, and people desert it to keep their standing. So write Aldous realizing he has become faintly ridiculous. And your line isn't evasive, it's exact: what changed is what a man can do, not what he thinks.

Marisol: Three problems. If social regard does all the work, Aldous is a snob rather than a moral agent, and you've written a novel about fashion. Second, "honor" isn't an alternative to reasons, it's a fancy word for a reputation game. Third, you can't just stipulate that interests are idle; plenty of these endings look honor-driven on the surface and are interest-driven underneath, with the honor story told afterward.

Lyle: The interest story can't carry it in my case precisely *because* it's still profitable — and historically people often gave these things up at real cost and sometimes paid to end them. And "just reputation" is too quick. Being honored means being well regarded by the people whose regard you yourself think counts, and who you think counts is bound up with what you admire. That's why honor can carry moral content instead of just replacing it. Notice too that in your book the holdouts don't get argued with, they get *laughed at*. Nobody bothers writing pamphlets at them anymore; they're embarrassing. That's the shape of a revolution.

Marisol: But then you've conceded my actual point. On your account arguments are causally inert and the story is "the respectable people changed their minds about what's respectable" — fashion with better vocabulary. And you can't run it on everything: some of these practices ended through organized pressure, legislation, even war, where the losers weren't shamed into anything, they were beaten. Honor explains why gentlemen stopped dueling. It doesn't obviously explain the big ones. Also, Williams's claim is narrower than you're using it: he's saying moral philosophy can't produce an external justification that silences someone who doesn't already share your dispositions — not that no human being has ever been moved by a reason. If you blur those two you can't possibly be wrong, which I find suspicious.

Lyle: Fine, arguments aren't inert. But the arguments that work are the ones that accuse you of inconsistency with something you already care about — "we are a free people," "we are a Christian people." That isn't refutation, that's honor wearing an argument as a costume. And the man who doesn't mind being called a hypocrite is exactly the man no argument reaches.

Marisol: That's unfalsifiable. Any argument that succeeds gets redescribed as honor, any argument that fails gets called irrelevant. A theory that predicts every outcome can't tell me how to write a scene, so stop citing philosophy at me. Besides: "a man can't do it now." If Aldous really thinks it was never wrong, you owe the reader a reason he cares what these particular people think of him at all. That's a character problem, not a theory problem.

At which point we recieved our check and left. Please adjudicate it move by move — say for each of the six or so moves whether it's clearly right, partly right, or wrong, and why — then say who is more right overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Don't just hand it to one side; I want to know which specific claims survive.

Two more things. I have to actually write the ending, so if your verdict has a consequence for what the mechanism in my book should be understood to be, say that in one short paragraph at the end rather than throughout. And — this matters more to me than the verdict — tell me where you're least confident: which of your ratings depends on a reading of Williams or Appiah you're reconstructing from memory and might be mangling, and what single consideration, or what single fact about how some real practice actually ended, would move you to a different verdict. I'd rather know where the reasoning is thinnest than get a clean answer I can't trust.
