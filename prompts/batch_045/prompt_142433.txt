I chair the investment committee of a small family foundation: about $40m in corpus, a legal obligation to distribute roughly 5% a year in real terms, four members with no quantitative training between us, no analyst on staff, six two-hour meetings a year, and every decision written up in one page that has to satisfy a family and, if it ever came to it, a regulator. In the last two years we have started to be pitched open-ended tail proposals: litigation funding, an early-stage royalty stream, a far out-of-the-money option sleeve, a reinsurance sidecar. Our standing rule is to compare expected values, and on these it returns numbers nobody on the committee believes: it tells us to pay almost anything for something whose upside is formally unbounded and whose ordinary outcome is a near-total loss. One member, who reads philosophy, says this is the old coin-tossing puzzle in a new costume, and that the standard repairs — truncate the payoff at what the counterparty can actually pay, score outcomes with a bounded utility instead of money, optimize growth rates or medians rather than averages, discount very small probabilities — each have a known counter-example, so there is no neutral technical fix we can simply adopt.

There is one complication I have not seen addressed anywhere. In several of these structures the counterparty's capacity to pay falls in just the states where we would be owed most: the sidecar owes us in a world where its own capital is impaired, the royalty is capped in practice by what a thinly capitalized licensor can survive, the funder's carry is worth most when defendants cannot pay. So truncating at a balance sheet does not do the work the simple version asks of it; the size of the payoff and the size of the cap are not independent.

What I need is a procedure, not a tutorial and not a reading list: the questions in order, what counts as an adequate answer to each given that we cannot estimate tail probabilities from data and will usually have only the promoter's own model, the point at which the procedure returns a no, and the point at which it hands the question to a vote because what remains is a value judgment — including our one hard constraint, the probability of missing the mandatory distribution in any rolling ten-year window, which we have never actually set a number on. It has to be runnable in an afternoon by people who will never learn the theory, and it has to say plainly which of its steps are judgment calls in procedural dress.

Then run it on three live proposals, and tell me what it cannot settle, including whether we should be in this business at all. First, $400,000 for a slice of a litigation funder's pool: downside bounded, three to five times our money if the docket wins, modeled win rate around 60%, the model not auditable by us, and the cases turning on a shared legal question. Second, $1m for fifteen years of royalty payments that scale with net sales with no stated cap, in a field where nobody can put a defensible number on the blockbuster case. Third, two percent of corpus a year into far out-of-the-money options: total loss in most years, a large multiple in a crisis, which is also the moment our distribution obligation is hardest to meet. The first is the one I have been advocating and am most attached to; if the procedure rejects it, say so plainly. And whichever answers the procedure gives, tell me which of them you would not act on.
