I am a political philosopher who has, rather to my surprise, been seconded as a philosophical consultant to a provincial Reparations Review Board, and I have been asked to draft the reasoning for its next decision. I would be grateful for your help thinking it through. The Board's enabling statute says only that it may award "such remedy as is apt" in respect of "officially sanctioned injustice"; everything else is precedent, and my colleagues care a great deal about coherence with what the Board has already done. A colleague in the law school assures me that our second decision below settles the new case; I am not so sure, since the new case involves an identifiable asset and a beneficiary institution that is not the successor to the wrongdoer. Our track record, compressed:

1. A provincially authorized compulsory sterilization program (1954–1972): per capita awards to living survivors; claims by their adult children dismissed, on the ground that "the injury was done to a person, and the claim does not outlive her."
2. An 1879 cession signed under duress, the land still held by the province as a reserve: a perpetual annuity to the descendant community's council, sized by an assessment of the community's present condition rather than by the land's 1879 value; individual descendants' claims for shares dismissed, on the ground that "the injury was done to a people, and a people's claim is not a sum of persons' claims."
3. A 1961 urban-renewal demolition of a neighborhood, the land now a privately owned commercial complex, most original owners dead: a single lump sum to a community trust sized by the value of what the original owners lost; a claim by the present residents of the replacement neighborhood dismissed, although they are disadvantaged and although few are descended from the displaced.
4. Descendants of workers injured by a private company dissolved in 1931 with its assets dispersed and its shareholders long dead: dismissed, "the Board can find no present holder of the advantage."
5. A school district underfunded under a segregation-era statutory formula, 1920–1968: awarded, over the province's objection that subsequent equalization grants had already made the loss good; the Board held that "a benefit a claimant receives as a citizen does not discharge a claim for a wrong, and discharge requires a remedy the claimant is free to refuse."

The new case: in 1871 the province seized twelve thousand hectares under a treaty the Board has already found to have been signed under duress, and in 1874 granted that land by statute to a newly chartered college as its founding endowment. The college never took part in the seizure, but its charter recites the grant and its first treasurer recorded how the land was obtained; it is a legal person distinct from the province, and no one now associated with it had any part in the events. The land is today leased to a commercial developer and its rents supply roughly seven per cent of the college's operating budget. The claimant community is among the most disadvantaged groups in the province on every official index, has a council the province recognizes, was relocated twice after 1871, and now occupies territory assigned to it by the province; about fourteen per cent of the college's undergraduates are its members, and the college funds a liaison office and scholarships earmarked for them. It asks for three things: return of a forty-hectare parcel, within the original grant, that contains a burial ground still in use; a perpetual annuity to a council-controlled trust; and per capita payments to the roughly nine hundred living persons who can document descent from the 1871 signatories. The college resists with four arguments: that no present member of the community is worse off than she would otherwise have been, since but for the seizure her ancestors would not have met and she would not exist, so the annuity has no measure; that circumstances have changed so much that the claim has lapsed; that the scholarships and the liaison office have already discharged it; and that it is a beneficiary of the grant rather than the successor to the wrongdoer. The council, for its part, has said it will accept the parcel and the annuity, will refuse any one-time payment, and regards per capita payments as divisive.

What I need from you is this. Set out the two or three rules about who may hold a claim, against whom it may be held, how it is measured, and what discharges it, that fit all five precedents about equally well, and say what each predicts about the parcel, the annuity, and the per capita payments. Then give me your recommendation for what the Board should decide if it wants to be consistent with itself, identify which precedent is the closest analog and why, and say plainly what principle the Board would be adopting under each of the main alternatives — including what it would be committed to about whether an unbroken institutional identity matters when the institution is a mere beneficiary, whether a remedy the claimant refuses can still discharge a claim, and whether the ancestors'-non-identity argument has any force given that the Board has twice awarded relief without any counterfactual comparison of persons. Finally, tell me which single further case the Board should take next in order to separate the rules you have identified, since I would rather find out now which of them we are really running.
