I'm giving a forty-five-minute seminar paper next month at a Swiss university's center for security studies, and what I need is not help with the paper but a preparation sheet for the question period. Here is the thesis, in the form I will defend it. Armed neutrality is not, I argue, sustained by military capability in general but by a far more specific property that I call *offensive incapacity*: a force structure that is visibly incapable of being used across a border. Conscript and militia systems produce offensive incapacity as a by-product — short training and service terms, deployment constrained by law and by domestic politics, equipment and doctrine optimized for denial of terrain rather than for sustained operations at distance — and my claim is that this by-product, not the combat power, is what makes a neutral's neutrality credible to a potential aggressor. I formalize it crudely: a signaling setup in which the aggressor cannot observe the neutral's future alignment but can observe its force structure, and where the parameter doing the work is what I call the force's *redeployment elasticity*, the fraction of its military value that survives being moved several hundred kilometres from its own frontier. A territorial conscript army scores low; a professional, interoperable, deployable brigade scores high; and on my reading a state that raises its redeployment elasticity raises its measured capability while lowering the credibility of its neutrality. Call the resulting security gain the *unusability premium* — what a neutral buys by owning a military it could not plausibly use aggressively — and the result is a capability–credibility frontier with a threshold past which getting stronger makes you less secure. I then apply this to Finland, Sweden and Switzerland: the successive post-1990 rounds of restructuring that cut end strength, raised deployability, professionalized parts of the force and tied these armies to multinational structures were already eroding the neutrality signal well before the rupture in European security policy in 2022, and Finland's and Sweden's accession to NATO was, on my account, the culmination of that drift rather than a reversal of it. I know that last claim will irritate several people in the room.

The audience is about twenty-five people, and it is mixed in a way that matters. Three or four IR faculty work on small states and treat neutrality as a realist puzzle about credibility, commitment and audience costs. Two defense economists from the neighbouring technical university think in terms of substitutability, shadow prices and the opportunity cost of conscript labour, and regard conscription as an inefficient tax. There is a historian of Swiss military institutions from the Cold War period who knows the militia system's internal politics in detail. There is a militia major from a territorial formation who will be in the room and who I expect to be my toughest questioner, because he has spent his career inside the institution I am calling structurally bad at something. And there are half a dozen doctoral students working on NATO enlargement and Nordic security, several of them Finnish and Swedish, who have read the recent policy literature closely and will know the counterfactual arguments about Russian threat perception. Assume nobody present is a formal theorist: my signaling setup will be read as a heuristic device, not as a result, and I should not lean on it as if it had been proven.

What I want from you is the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage they would do, and each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it — the defense economist's version of "your premise is wrong" and the militia major's version are very different sentences, and I want both. For each objection give me a reply I can say out loud in about two minutes, a note on what I should concede up front rather than defend, and one line on where that reply is weakest. Flag which objections attack the model, which attack the historical application to the three countries, and which attack the policy implication. Tell me what I should preempt on the first two slides instead of waiting to be asked, and what I should abandon entirely if pressed.
