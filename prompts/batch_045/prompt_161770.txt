I teach a third-year seminar on confirmation and induction — 14 students, one 80-minute session — and I want them to run into Goodman's new riddle before I name it. Design an in-class activity that isolates projectibility rather than induction itself: they should be granted that the sequence is generated by some stable rule, so that all the difficulty sits in which rule the evidence supports and none of it sits in whether the future will resemble the past.

My candidate device, which you should develop or replace if something works better: a betting game with a hidden change point. A world fixes a rule of the form P holds up to some draw c and Q holds afterwards, where c is settled in advance and concealed; the class sees a run of observations, wagers on the next one at odds, and is scored by a proper scoring rule. If you use it, specify the observation sequence, how c is drawn, and the payoff scheme so that two things become unavoidable: that however much confirming evidence piles up, the live hypothesis set still contains both P forever and P until some later cutoff, Q after; and that the odds the class will accept between those two are set almost entirely by how the cutoff was drawn, not by the data. I would rather half the room be the world and half the bettors than watch me play the world for them.

Give me a debrief mapping the moments onto the riddle: why the disagreement survives every observation, why the reflexive answers — simplicity, no gerrymandering, the rival predicate is contrived — all turn out to lean on entrenchment or on a prior over hypotheses that itself needs defending, and why none of this is Hume's problem. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and which feature of the riddle the game cannot reproduce and I will have to state myself.
