So I've been reading up on anti-bribery law for work (compliance training, fun) and there's a thing in it I can't get straight, so help me out.

Here's the bit I keep circling. In the US federal bribery rules, as far as I rememeber, there's a carve-out for so-called facilitation or grease payments: small cash to a low-level official to do a thing he was *already* required to do, just sooner — stamp the form you're entitled to have stamped, hook the power back up, move the container that's already cleared. If I've got it right, the UK's 2010 bribery act deliberately has no such carve-out and counts the same payment as a bribe. Both systems are Western, both claim to be enforcing basically one idea, and the idea is roughly: public power used for private gain is corrupt, and that's what the big international corruption indices try to measure, a single scale that every country gets ranked on.

But then: a government will happily sell you the same speed out in the open. Pay a few hundred dollars extra and your passport shows up in a week instead of six. Pay a bigger fee and your visa or work petition gets a decision in fifteen business days instead of sitting in a pile for a year. There are fast-track lanes at airports and security checkpoints, priority slots for court dates in some places. The queue gets reordered by money in exactly the way the grease payment reorders it, except now it's posted on a website and the cash goes to the treasury. And there's a third thing in the same family: officials are usually allowed to accept cheap token stuff — lunch, a bottle of wine, a small gift — under some dollar threshold, but definitely not a holiday or a car.

So, the question: what theory of corruption does this whole package presuppose? The effect on the citizen waiting in the queue is identical in the grease case and the premium-processing case, so the theory can't just be about outcomes for the people affected. It looks like it has to be about something like authorization, or disclosure, or who the money lands on, or whether there's a fact of the matter about what you were owed before you paid. Reconstruct the implicit theory for me — the thing that makes the premium-processing fee clean and the envelope of cash dirty. Then: what does the practice *strictly* presuppose, as opposed to merely go along well with? Which rival views of corruption does it quietly rule out (a straight welfare or efficiency view would presumably treat both cases the same way; so would a view that cares about fairness in who gets served first)? And what would have to change in actual practice if you swapped in one of those rivals — what would a government that took queue-fairness seriously stop selling?

The case I really can't decide, and which I want you to chew on because I think it pulls the theory apart: suppose a country keeps the premium-processing scheme but rewrites one line. The fee is still capped, still fixed by law, still posted publicly — but it now goes to the individual officer who processes the file, and officers' base salaries are cut by the expected amount so their total take-home is about what it was. Same queue, same money, same authorization, same transparency; the difference is that the officer now personally gains by getting files done fast, and gains more if he hustles. Is that corruption on the theory you just reconstructed? If not, what would have to be added for it to be? If yes, which condition did it break, and why is that condition supposed to matter — what's the actual harm it's tracking?

Last piece, since this is for a project on whether these standards travel. The picture the theory seems to rest on has an office defined by rules that already fix what the citizen is owed, an official who is an agent, and a public that is the principal who authorizes him — so corruption is a kind of betrayal of that authorization. But a lot of what gets scored as corruption in the rankings is stuff like gifts inside kin networks, chiefs and local big men distributing resources to their people, payments to someone who is simultaneously a relative, a patron and an officeholder. Does the reconstructed theory have any non-question-begging way to say those are corrupt, or is it really a fairly parochial picture of where authority comes from that gets applied as if it were universal? And if it is parochial, does that undermine the practice above or just the export version of it?
