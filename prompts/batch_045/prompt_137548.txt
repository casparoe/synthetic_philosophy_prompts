hey — im in a social epistemology seminar this term and we just finished the block on epistemic injustice (the testimonial / hermeneutical split, credibility deficits vs. credibility excess, the identity-prejudicial vs. merely-mistaken distinction, and the claim that the distinctive harm is that someone is wronged *as a knower* and not just treated badly). Our final paper can be on a literary text, and I want to write about Chinua Achebe's Things Fall Apart.

My worry is this. The obvious reading is that the novel is a casebook of hermeneutical injustice: the ending, where the District Commissioner files Okonkwo's entire life as a paragraph in the ethnographic book he's planning to write; the missionaries, who have religion for themselves and only superstition for everyone else; the egwugwu and the colonial court as two systems that don't recognize each other's reasons as reasons. But every time I write that down it feels like I've just renamed colonialism and called it epistemology, and I can't tell whether the framework is doing work or just supplying vocabulary.

So please give me a reading that actually uses the distinctions instead of gesturing at them. Concretely:

1. In section our TA drew a distinction between (a) hermeneutical injustice proper, where the relevant concept or sense isn't yet in the shared pool, so that nobody — including the speaker — can quite make the experience intelligible to anyone, and (b) what he called credibility confiscation: the concepts exist, the group is recognized as having them, but they get reclassified when *this* group deploys them. So an elder's account becomes superstition, a grievance becomes custom, a story about land becomes myth. He said (b) is a different animal, because the failure isn't conceptual scarcity but jurisdiction — a fight over who gets to be a knower at all. I want you to run that distinction against the scenes I named and tell me which one is actually operating in each, and to give me at least one scene where the classification came out differently than you expected going in.

2. Does anyone in the novel have a testimonial-injustice grievance, or is this book almost purely about the hermeneutical kind? What keeps tripping me up is that the people with the credibility deficit mostly aren't trying to testify to the British in the first place — Okonkwo pretty much refuses to be a knower *for* them, and the novel gives him real reasons for that refusal. Does the testimonial picture secretly assume a speaker who is trying to be heard and gets blocked, and if so, is the novel a bad fit for that part of the theory, or does it expose a limit — a wrong done to someone as a knower that isn't a thwarted speech act at all?

3. The bit I keep going in circles on: Achebe writes in English, for a readership that includes the Commissioner's grandchildren, and the novel is obviously meant to fix the hermeneutical gap, to hand you the missing concepts. On the Fricker-ish line that just is hermeneutical justice being done. But the novel is also doing the translating, and translating looks a lot like where the confiscation happens. So is the book an instance of the remedy or an instance of the disease, or is that a fake choice that a good paper should refuse? I especially want to know what the strongest version of my argument has to concede here, because I think this is where my draft is weakest.

4. Where does the lens push back? I don't want to sand off everything in the novel that isn't about knowledge: Okonkwo's terror of being thought weak, the violence, and the fact that Umuofia's own elders are not neutral knowers either — the twins left in the forest, the osu, the people the clan itself won't hear. If epistemic injustice is supposed to track a wrong to someone in their capacity as a knower, what happens when the community whose hermeneutics are being erased has exclusions of its own? Does the framework flatten the book into something too clean, and if so can I still use it honestly?

Tell me plainly if this pairing is a bad idea, and if it is, what the least-bad version of the paper would still have to do — my prof's one hard rule is that you can't bolt a theory onto a text and call the result a reading. Please don't give me a plot summary and don't just tell me to go reread the Fricker chapters; I've done the reading, what I need is help seeing what the machinery actually does to this particular novel.
