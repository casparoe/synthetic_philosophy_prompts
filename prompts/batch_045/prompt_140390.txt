I'm teaching the three stances in my grad seminar on Thursday and, for the first time in about fifteen years, I'm finally writing up my own view instead of just walking them through Dennett's. Which means I've had to notice that my verdicts on individual cases don't obviously add up. So here they are, as gut calls, and I want to know what principle I'm implicitly working with — or what principle I should be working with instead, if mine is a mess. Please don't cite anything; I know the literature, I want the principle.

For each case: is there a real pattern here, and where there is, which stance am I entitled to?

1. The glider in Conway's Life. Real pattern, no hesitation. The physical stance (cell-by-cell update rule) misses it entirely, the design stance gets it, and I want to say the glider is there whether or not anybody ever runs Life.
2. Orion. Compresses that patch of sky beautifully, but I don't want to say there's an object out there. So: a real pattern in some thin sense, not a thing, and no stance past the physical is apt.
3. My cat wanting to go out. Intentional stance, and I'm a realist about it — she really does believe the door is shut, it isn't just useful shorthand for purposes of prediction.
4. A chess engine's "plan to sac the knight." Design stance first. Intentional talk is fine only if I can cash it in design terms — the search tree, the eval function — and stops being fine the moment it can't be cashed.
5. An LLM that says "I think the meeting is at three." I attribute beliefs to it constantly in ordinary use and then take it back in the seminar room. This is the one I flip-flop on and I'd like it settled.
6. A corporation's decision to raise prices. I'm entirely comfortable saying the firm intended to raise prices, and much less comfortable saying the firm believes anything, and I have no idea what justifies the asymmetry.
7. A curve I fitted to the last thousand data points that dies on point 1001. Not a real pattern — overfitting, no compression, just memorisation.
8. A regularity in a geophysical time series that compresses well but that no agent with realistic resources could ever have found, and that licences no intervention anyone could actually perform once found. My gut says: real, but only barely, and I feel guilty saying it.
9. A pattern in some data set that would only show up if you could run a computer bigger than the observable universe. My gut here says: yes, real — if you're a realist about compression at all, whether anyone can find it is beside the point. But that makes a joke of what I said about 8, and maybe of 7 too. This is the pair I can't reconcile.
10. Two descriptions of the same data that compress it exactly as well and that can't both be right. My verdict: no real pattern there until one of them wins — but I've no account of what would make one win.

My tentative principle, which I half believe: a pattern is real just in case (a) some description compresses the data better than the data compresses itself, and (b) that compression is one some agent situated in the world could, with tractable resources, actually exploit — predict with, intervene on, or choose differently because of. And I'm separately tempted to say you're entitled to the intentional stance over the design stance just when ascribing beliefs and desires buys you compression that function-and-design talk can't match. I think (b) is doing a lot of work and I suspect it's smuggling in something I don't want, given case 9.

Can you test that against the cases, repair it if it's repairable, or tell me to drop it and give me something better? I'd also genuinely like to know whether the stance-choice question is the same question as the reality question or a different one.

Give me, in this order:

1. "Candidate principles" — about 200 words. Two or three, stated precisely enough that I could apply one to a new case without asking you again; include my tentative one repaired if it can be repaired.
2. "Clause by clause" — about 300 words. For each clause of your preferred candidate, the case of mine that motivates it, and what breaks if you drop the clause.
3. "Fresh cases" — about 250 words. At least three cases I haven't listed, with what the principle says about them, so I can check the predictions against my gut.
4. "What I have to give up" — about 200 words. Which of my verdicts no principle saves, and which one you'd overturn rather than accommodate.
5. "Stance choice" — about 200 words. Whether picking a stance is the same question as whether the pattern is real, and if it isn't, the principle that governs it. Settle 5 and 6 while you're in there.
