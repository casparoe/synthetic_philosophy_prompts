I've been trying to work out what I actually think about when a community owes remembrance of a wrong and when it owes forgetting, and I'd rather start from my own gut reactions to cases than from theory — Margalit's duty to remember and Rieff's case for forgetting both sound right to me in the abstract, which is exactly the problem. So here are my verdicts:

- A genocide against a people whose descendants live in the same country now and are still measurably worse off: duty to remember, obviously.
- A fallen dictatorship's secret police files naming who was watched and by whom: duty to keep and open them, even forty years on, even though it humiliates families who'd rather not know.
- A company that killed customers with a product it knew was unsafe: duty to remember, permanently, even after compensation, reform, and the victims' own association asking everyone to let it go. I hold this one even though it makes me uneasy.
- Low-level informants who gave names under threat in a regime that has since collapsed: no duty to remember them. I think we owe them something closer to forgetting.
- A public act of cruelty committed at seventeen by someone who has lived decently ever since and hurt no one since: after twenty-five years, owed forgetting.
- The location of a massacre site, which became a pilgrimage spot for people who admire the killers once it was published: I think the right call was to stop publishing it and let it go unfindable. That feels stronger than "no duty to remember" — closer to a duty not to keep it alive.
- An atrocity recorded only in the killers' own paperwork — ledgers, transport schedules, not a single line of victim testimony: yes, duty to remember, no hesitation.
- The same kind of atrocity where everything known comes from a synthetic reconstruction: a model trained on fragments of testimony that produces continuous, plausible first-person accounts. We know some of the detail is generated rather than remembered, and we're certain the killings happened. Here I balk. I don't want commemoration built on it. But I can't explain why this differs from the paperwork case in a way that isn't just squeamishness about new technology.

Two that pull against each other:

- A wrong against a people who are entirely gone — no descendants, no surviving language, nothing left but the killers' records. I still feel a duty.
- A wrong against a people whose descendants are numerous, organised, and asking us to stop defining them by it. I feel the duty lapses.

And the one I keep changing my mind about: a credit-scoring system that shaved a fraction of a percent off tens of thousands of people's chances for thirty years, now discontinued. No individual was ruined. In aggregate, a whole neighbourhood was. Do we owe remembrance of that, and who would owe it to?

My working rule has been: a wrong is owed remembrance when remembering is what stands between us and its repetition, and when the wronged (or their descendants) haven't asked us to stop. That rule seems to give the wrong answer on the vanished-people case, where nothing can recur and nobody is asking, and on the massacre-site case, where publicity would supposedly deter repetition and I still want it buried.

So: is there one principle that gets these, or close, and if not, which of my verdicts would you overturn? I'd like each clause of whatever you propose tied to the case that motivates it, an honest list of the verdicts it can't accommodate, and its answer on the credit-scoring one plus at least one case I haven't listed. British English please, and lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the principle, or the verdict you'd drop — and then give me the reasoning, no preamble and no restating the question.
