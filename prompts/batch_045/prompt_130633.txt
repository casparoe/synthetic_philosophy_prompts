I'm a postdoc in philosophy, and in three weeks I have a single sixty-minute slot in a colleague's course on science in public: about thirty second-years, mostly non-majors, a mix of biology and literature students, one whiteboard, no assigned reading and no handout, their attention budget being roughly one board diagram. I've been asked to get across the crane/skyhook contrast and the companion notion of greedy reductionism — Dennett's vocabulary, from Darwin's Dangerous Idea — well enough that the students can apply both to cases they haven't seen. Don't explain the distinction to me; plan how I should explain it.

What I want, in four parts.

First, the dependency order, and why. Which ideas have to be in place before either label is introduced at all — I suspect the labels should arrive late, after the class has already felt the difference between an explanation that does lifting work and one that merely relocates the mystery — and, for each pair of adjacent steps, what breaks if I invert them. I'd rather know which dependence is load-bearing than which order is conventional.

Second, the one example that carries the whole hour, and the constraints that select it. I want a case in which the mechanism is visibly cumulative, in which every individual step is unimpressive, and in which the end product is far more organized than anything that had to be assumed at the start. Tell me what such an example must have, what it must lack, and why the usual candidates fail the brief — I'm thinking of the vertebrate eye, the bacterial flagellum, and a watch found on a heath.

Third, the misconception map: in the order the misconceptions will actually form, where in the sequence each should be headed off, and which ones I should deliberately let stand until the end because defusing them early would cost more than it saves. My guesses: that skyhook is a polite word for the supernatural and the apparatus is evolution-versus-creationism in costume; that greedy reductionism is a charge against reduction in general, so the moral is to distrust explanations that go small; the mirror-image error, that because greedy reductionism is a fault, any level of description is sacrosanct; that a crane must be a simple machine if it is to do honest work; that if cranes rest on cranes we get a regress, so eventually something has to hang from the sky; that it's random is a way of stopping the explanation; and that emergent is a place where explanation may stop. Add the ones I haven't listed, especially any that my own framing invites.

Fourth, a check at the end that separates students who can use the contrast from students who have learned two words — ideally something I can put on the board in thirty seconds — together with what a confident but wrong answer to it will look like.

Two additions I've been tempted to make, and I'd like your verdict on whether they earn their ten minutes and where they'd go. The first is a third category alongside crane and skyhook: the unfinished crane, in which a speaker names a mechanism — self-organization, emergence, a faculty, a drive — and it is genuinely open whether the name is a promissory note for machinery still to be specified or a terminus dressed up as a mechanism. I'd pair it with a test: what does the speaker do when you ask for the next level down? The second is a distinction between a crane's lifting power and its anchorage — the point that a grounded, cumulative process can build structures far more organized than anything that had to be assumed to start it, so that getting more out of less is not by itself evidence of a skyhook. I want this one because my literature students will otherwise take the cumulativeness itself to be the suspicious part.

Last: tell me what I should openly refuse to attempt in the hour, and say so to the students. Then mark the two or three places where this plan is most likely to fail with this audience. For each, name the observation — a question, a silence, a wrong answer of a particular shape — that would tell me to abandon the sequence on the spot, say what you would do instead, and say why that consideration should outweigh the plan as you have built it.
