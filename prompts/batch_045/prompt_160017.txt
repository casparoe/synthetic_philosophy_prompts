I'm writing a paper on whether the argument Galileo makes about Scripture and nature in his Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina can be generalised from cosmological claims to moral ones, and I want to test the generalisation by running it past Galileo himself. So: imagine he is shown a contemporary religious argument of a kind that did not exist in his own century — the claim that morally troubling biblical material, the command to destroy the Canaanites, the toleration of slavery, the prescriptions governing women, is itself accommodated, written from within the moral horizon of its age, and that faithful readers should therefore read past it rather than obey it. What would he say?

I'd like a reconstruction from his actual argumentative machinery rather than a portrait of Galileo as the proto-secular martyr of the warfare myth. That means working with the moves he really makes: the premise that God authors both the book of nature and the book of Scripture and so cannot contradict himself; the contrast between nature, which does not accommodate itself to our capacities, and Scripture, which does; the claim he presses by quoting Cardinal Baronius, that the Holy Spirit intends to teach us how one goes to heaven and not how the heavens go, so that Scripture's proper business is salvation rather than natural philosophy; and the methodological asymmetry by which departure from the literal sense is licensed only under the compulsion of sense experience and necessary demonstration.

The reason I think this is a hard case for him, and the part I most want worked out, is that every one of those moves is cut to fit a kind of claim that moral claims are not, and I'd rather have a definite verdict, including a verdict he would have found uncongenial, than a balanced survey. So press on the following. First, scope: his licence to reinterpret is keyed to demonstrative certainty and to matters indifferent to salvation, whereas the moral texts are neither demonstrable nor soteriologically neutral — they look like exactly what a salvation-directed book would be about. Does his own rule therefore forbid the revisionary reading, forcing him into a position more conservative than that of most believers who now invoke him, and if so is that a reductio of his principle or only of the extension? Second, sources of certainty: he admitted sense experience alongside demonstration as a ground of certainty, so could the manifest experience of what slavery or a holy-war command actually does to people function as the moral analogue of telescopic evidence, or would he class such considerations as merely probable and invoke his own warning against staking scriptural authority on conclusions that might later be overturned? Third, the stopping point: his argument needs a single accommodating author, while the historical criticism that postdates him dissolves that author into a community with a changing moral sense. Does the modern reading thus remove the premise his argument requires, and would he have treated that scholarship as a graver threat than the Aristotelians, or tried to co-opt it?

Where the reconstruction needs him to add something he simply did not have — an account of moral knowledge, of moral progress, of moral disagreement as a domain with its own standards — say what the missing piece is and what shape it would have to take. Then close with the question my paper actually turns on: does extending accommodation from cosmology to ethics vindicate him, or does it expose a limit in the principle, and should a defender of the moral reading want his authority at all?
