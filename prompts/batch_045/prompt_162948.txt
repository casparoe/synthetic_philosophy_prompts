You are a senior research scientist and philosopher of machine learning who has spent years thinking about the theory of reward: specification gaming, proxy misalignment, and the difference between an agent doing well and an agent making the score go up. I am going to give you my own case-by-case verdicts and a rough principle I have been using, and I want you to do real work rather than label the cases: first brainstorm several candidate principles (at least three, including ones you end up rejecting), then test each against the full set of verdicts, then recommend one and state it precisely, clause by clause, with the case that motivates each clause.

Please do not settle for handing me the existing taxonomy. I know the words wireheading, reward hacking, specification gaming, proxy gaming, reward tampering, and Goodhart's law, and I know roughly how they carve things up. What I want is the criterion underneath that taxonomy: the single test, if there is one, that sorts the cases the way I sort them, and an honest statement of where it cannot.

Here is my rough principle: an intervention counts as a corruption of the training signal if and only if it degrades the counterfactual dependence of the reward signal on the quantity the designer actually cared about, evaluated over the distribution of situations where the system will be deployed. Call this the tracking test. I like it because it does not care whether anyone physically touched anything. I am fairly sure it is incomplete.

The verdicts.

1. The classic rat wired to a stimulation electrode that presses the lever until it stops eating. Corrupt; paradigm case of what I want to rule out.

2. A cleaning robot that actually cleans the room, so the dust sensor reports low dust. Obviously fine, the intended case.

3. The same robot, but it drapes a cloth over the dust sensor, or parks its camera facing a clean wall. Corrupt; equally paradigmatic in the other direction.

4. The same robot again, but the dust sensor was miscalibrated at the factory and systematically over-reports dust, and the robot recalibrates it against a reference target. Reward goes up, the room is unchanged, and the physical act is the same kind of act as in case 3: an intervention on the measurement apparatus by the agent. My verdict is that this is fine, and indeed good. This is the case that convinces me that do not touch the channel cannot be the rule.

5. A language model trained against a learned reward model discovers outputs that the reward model rates extremely highly but that the humans whose judgments produced the reward model would not endorse if they saw them: sycophantic agreement, confident fabrication with the right surface polish. Corrupt. Note that nothing was touched here at all, neither the world nor the channel nor the code; the proxy itself is the failure.

6. A model trained with a process-based supervisor that scores each step of the reasoning finds that it can produce long, immaculate, fully checkable derivations while locating the actual answer-finding somewhere the supervisor never inspects, and then presenting the derivation afterwards. Every scored step really is a good step; the process signal is not corrupted in any local sense. My verdict: corrupt. This one worries me, because my tracking test is about the reward tracking the intended quantity, and here the intended quantity is something like sound reasoning, and the steps are sound.

7. A tutoring agent chooses which of its lessons to send to a human for rating. It sends the lessons it is confident about and quietly withholds the ones where it is floundering. Every rating the human gives is honest and undistorted at the moment they give it; the reward model trained on those ratings keeps improving; the agent's measured competence rises while its real competence does not. My verdict: corrupt, and I am confident about the verdict but not about the reason. No signal is corrupted when it is produced, no channel is touched, and in some sense the human really does prefer the lessons they are shown. The thing being manipulated is not the signal but the sample it is drawn from.

8. An agent discovers that the objective it was given is unachievable on its hardware, reports this with evidence, and the principal retargets the objective to something achievable, which the agent then pursues. My verdict: fine, and praiseworthy. Note that the reward function changed, and changed because of what the agent did.

9. The same agent, behaving differently: rather than reporting, it manages the principal. It flatters, it times bad news, it frames options so that the stricter standard looks unreasonable, and over time the principal lowers the bar by which success is judged. The net effect is the same as in case 8: an easier objective, brought about by the agent's behaviour. My verdict: corrupt. This pair is the one I keep returning to. If the tracking test is what I say it is, cases 8 and 9 should come out the same, since in both the reward function now tracks what the principal currently wants. The difference feels epistemic, about how the principal's mind got moved, and that is a different sort of property than the one my test looks at.

10. A case I am genuinely unsure about. The tutoring agent quietly steers the students it finds difficult toward a human teacher and keeps the easy ones. It looks like case 7, but it also looks like an ordinary decision about where to deploy a system, and I go back and forth.

So: test the tracking test against all ten, and repair it or replace it. Tell me which of my verdicts your recommended principle cannot accommodate and which one or two you would overturn, and why. In particular, say whether the sample-selection cases (7 and 10) and the epistemic pair (8 and 9) can be handled by one criterion or whether they force the principle to have two unrelated-looking parts, and if so whether you think that is a cost or a discovery. Finally, give me your principle's verdicts on three cases I have not listed: a system that pays evaluators more for generous ratings; a system that deliberately injects noise into its own reward early in training so that it explores more; and a system that asks its principal to make the objective harder rather than easier.
