I do a podcast about science and ideas — mostly long-form interviews, but once a month my producer makes me do a twelve-minute solo segment where I explain one idea cold, no guest, no slides, audio only, to listeners who are roughly half working engineers and half general curious people, and who will aboslutely have opinions in the comments.

Next month's topic got picked for me and it's the problem of logical omniscience. The thing I'm supposed to get across: on the standard possible-worlds semantics for belief — where to believe p is for p to hold in every world compatible with what you believe — it falls straight out of the "every world" quantifier that anyone who believes p also believes every logical consequence of p, believes every logical truth, and never believes anything contradictory. And apparently this isn't one formulation's quirk, it's the whole style of account biting.

Don't explain it to me. I need the plan for the segment.

- The order, and what actually depends on what. My instinct is that you can't even state the problem until listeners have the possible-worlds picture in hand, and you can't see why any repair is costly until the problem has bitten in a case where nothing exotic is going on. Tell me if that's backwards. Also: do "all logical truths" and "no contradictions" come as their own beats after the closure one, or do they have to be folded into it?
- The one case to build it around. My candidate: a traveler who believes her inbound flight lands at 10:05, believes her connection departs at 10:20, believes the airport's minimum connection time is 45 minutes — and believes she'll make the connection. She'd assert each of the first three and she's walking, not running. Does that carry it, or should I swap it? What does a carrying case have to have, and what does it have to not have? I'm assuming it must not involve forgetting, vagueness, self-deception, or gigantic numbers, or listeners will think the trick is in the psychology rather than the logic. Am I right about that, and what else should I rule out? And why not Fermat's Last Theorem, which is what everyone reaches for?
- The misconceptions, and where in the sequence each one has to be headed off. My list, tell me which are real and which I invented: (a) "it's just about infinitely many consequences, you'd fix it with more compute"; (b) "fine, so belief isn't closed under consequence, done"; (c) "just model belief as the list of sentences the agent actually has stored, problem gone"; (d) "impossible worlds are only for handling contradictions"; (e) "philosophers' puzzle, nothing to do with whether a language model knows anything." Add the ones I'm missing. Someone also told me that the contradictory-belief case is worse than it looks — if you believe a contradiction the set of compatible worlds is empty and then you believe everything, so it's more omniscience, not less. Is that right, and does it earn airtime or is it a detour?
- One question on mic at the end that shows me whether it landed. I was going to use a toy repair I half-invented on the plane: suppose belief closes only under derivations of at most k steps, so at k=1 an agent who believes A, A→B and B→C believes B but not C. Good check, bad check, or a third thing — and if it's not the check, what is?
- What I should flatly refuse to explain in twelve minutes and say out loud that I'm refusing. I'm guessing the machinery of impossible-worlds semantics and whatever the explicit-vs-implicit belief thing is called, but I'd rather be told which omission actually costs me something.
- Last thing: my producer wants me to open by saying whether this is a finding about human psychology or an artifact of a formalism, and I genuinely don't know. Does that go at the top, at the end, or nowhere?
