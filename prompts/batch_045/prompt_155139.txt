Me and a friend have been argueing for weeks about the whole replace-your-brain-a-bit-at-a-time-with-little-machines thing, and I think we keep going in circles because neither of us has ever said what a theory of this would actually have to do to count as a decent one. So I want the spec first, not the theories.

What it's for, so "adequate" means something: say there's a clinic offering the procedure, done over about two years, one small batch of cells at a time. I have to decide whether to sign the form. Afterwards, everyone has to decide whether my flat, my debts and my promises belong to the person who walks out at the end, and whether that person is bound by the consent I gave on day one. A theory that can't help with those decisions is no use to me however elegant it is.

So: the requirements. The cases it has to handle, with why each one earns a place on the list:

1. all of it swapped in one operation versus one tiny bit every few minutes — and if the difference matters, what the rate is even measured in.
2. a patient whose mental life runs much faster than mine, or much slower. Is "gradual" a matter of how many parts get swapped, how much clock time passes, or how much life they subjectively live through?
3. it being done while I'm asleep, versus done while I'm awake and watching it happen.
4. half finished, then stopped for a year, then finished.
5. parts swapped in a scattered random order, versus one neighbouring region at a time.
6. parts that do exactly the same job but whose inner state nobody can read off or copy, so no scanning and no backup is possible at any point.
7. every swap nudging the person's tastes slightly, so that by the end they want nothing the person at the start wanted, including the operation itself, and they want the rest of it stopped.
8. the bits that get taken out being kept alive and wired up into a second working brain, so there end up being two of us.
9. it being stopped at 40% with no plan to go further, and a contract that needs interpreting right now.

The distinctions it isn't allowed to collapse: whether it's me versus whether it matters to me (and whether caring about the outcome is the same thing as surviving it); being the same person versus being treated as the same person for practical purposes; there being an answer versus there only being a decision we have to make; and the difference between a claim about what happens and a claim about what to do when you're not sure what happens.

Things it can't contradict: anaesthesia, deep sleep, the ordinary fact that people change enormously over a lifetime and still get counted as one person, and the fact that I couldn't tell from the inside whether it had been done to me.

Then the interesting bits: mark which requirements are non-negotiable given what I want the theory for and which are just what one camp happens to like; flag any pair you suspect can't be satisfied together (I've got a feeling "give a definite yes or no in every case" and "don't invent a fact where there isn't one" are in trouble together, but you tell me); and say which single requirement does the most work in knocking candidates out.

At the end, a small table is fine, but keep each entry to a line or two: how the obvious candidates do against the list — the psychological-continuity one, the "you're just the living animal" one, the "you're the pattern, not the stuff" one, and the "there's no fact of the matter, just more or less continuity" one. I've described them clumsily on purpose, and please dont bother telling me who came up with which, I genuinely don't care. British English throughout, cheers.
