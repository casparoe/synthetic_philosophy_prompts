QUESTION 3 (25 marks)

(a) Set out the Wilt Chamberlain argument as Nozick actually runs it, and say what it is meant to establish. Be careful about the conclusion: does it show that patterned principles of distributive justice are unstable, or that they are unjust? Explain why, on Nozick's view, the second is supposed to follow. (8 marks)

(b) Does the argument also tell against end-state or current time-slice principles such as utilitarianism? Keep the distinction between patterned and end-state firmly in view, and if you think the Chamberlain example itself does not do the work, say which argument would be needed instead. (9 marks)

(c) Now a variant. In Nozick's example each spectator actively hands over a quarter. In ours, a streaming platform's default setting routes twenty-five cents of every subscriber's monthly fee to whichever creator that subscriber watched most that month; any subscriber can switch this off in their account settings, and hardly anyone does, so twenty million subscribers end up sending their quarters to a few thousand creators. Nobody is threatened, nobody is left worse off, and the resulting distribution D2 departs from the pattern that everyone liked at D1. Two things: (i) does Nozick's conclusion still follow here? (ii) Notice that the patterned society could preserve its pattern in this case without forbidding anyone from doing anything — it could simply require the platform's default to be opt-in rather than opt-out. Does that matter to the argument? Identify the premise the variant puts pressure on. (8 marks)

STUDENT ANSWER

(a) Nozick asks us to start with a distribution D1 that realises whatever pattern the patterned theorist likes best — take strict equality of holdings if you like. Wilt Chamberlain then signs a contract under which twenty-five cents from the price of each ticket at his home games goes to him. A season passes, about a million people cheerfully turn up, and we arrive at D2, in which Wilt has a very large sum, much more than anyone else, and everyone else is twenty-five cents poorer than they would have been. Every holding in D2 traces back to D1 through transfers that nobody has a complaint about: each spectator preferred watching Wilt to keeping the quarter, and Wilt gave everyone exactly what he promised. So if D1 was just, and each step from D1 to D2 was itself just, D2 is just. But D2 does not fit the pattern. Hence the pattern cannot be what makes a distribution just.

The conclusion is stronger than mere instability. Nozick's point is not that it is a nuisance to keep re-imposing the pattern — though it is — but that the only way to stop the pattern dissolving is to stop people transferring what they own to others who consent to recieve it, and he holds that this violates their rights. The instability is only a symptom: what it reveals is that the patterned principle would prohibit acts which violate nobody's rights, so the interference needed to preserve the pattern is itself impermissible. The argument is also a reductio rather than a begging of the question, because Nozick helps himself to the patterned theorist's own favourite distribution as the starting point. He is not assuming anything contentious about who is entitled to what at D1, only that everyone agrees D1 is just.

(b) The argument generalises to end-state principles as well, and in particular to utilitarianism, because utilitarianism is itself a patterned principle: it says that holdings are to vary along the natural dimension of utility, or of marginal utility, so it specifies a pattern just as to-each-according-to-moral-merit does, only along a different dimension. And then the same thing happens to it. Once people are free to make voluntary transfers, the utility-maximising distribution will be upset by their transfers just as equality would be, so the utilitarian has to interfere continuously to drag things back to the best current time-slice, and that interference is exactly as much a violation of rights as the egalitarian's. So Nozick has disposed of every distributive principle besides his own: patterned ones because liberty upsets them, end-state ones because they are patterned too. One can add that the utilitarian is in an even worse position, since on his view there is no fact about who is entitled to what at all — he has only the current time-slice, no historical dimension — and that is precisely what the example exploits.

(c) The variant changes nothing. What does the work in Nozick's argument is that the transfers are voluntary, and these are: nobody threatened anybody, and every subscriber could have switched the default off in a few clicks. A transfer is voluntary so long as the person could have done otherwise and was not coerced, and having to take some small step to avoid a transfer does not make it coerced. So the twenty-five cents are voluntarily transferred, the creators acquire them by just transfer from people who were entitled to them, and D2 is just by exactly the same reasoning as before. The answer to (i) is yes.

As for (ii), it does not matter that the patterned society could preserve its pattern by requiring opt-in rather than opt-out defaults. A default is just part of the background set-up, and what Nozick objects to is interference with what people do with their holdings; if the society sets the default at no transfer unless you opt in, that is a restriction on what subscribers may do with their own money, and so it is interference under another name. Besides, the Lockean proviso is satisfied either way: nobody is worse off than they would have been if the platform had never existed, and Wilt has not appropriated anything from anyone — he has only recieved what others voluntarily gave him. Since the proviso's worse-off test is met and everyone consented, there is nothing for redistribution to bite on, and the pattern has to go.
