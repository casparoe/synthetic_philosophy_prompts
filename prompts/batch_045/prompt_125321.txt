You are a professional philosopher of mind — someone who has actually argued about higher-order theories rather than merely read the survey article — and I want you to work slowly: brainstorm the candidate mappings out loud for a paragraph or two before you commit to a verdict, and say explicitly when a step depends on a contested assumption rather than on the theory itself. I write literary fiction, and the reason I need you is a scene I cannot get right. My novel is set in Trieste in 1911; my protagonist, a young cavalry lieutenant, takes a saber wound across the sword arm during a duel and, being wholly absorbed in the fight, feels nothing at the time. Hours later, when the regimental surgeon probes the wound, the pain arrives all at once — blinding, unmistakable — and he says, and believes, that it has been hurting him the whole time and he simply wasn't attending to it. The narrator, an army physician given to theorizing, insists he is wrong: the pain began the moment he noticed it, because on the physician's view a mental state is conscious only when a further, higher-order state takes it as its object. I need to know whether the theory really forces that verdict, or whether my lieutenant's intuition survives it.

At my writers' group a cognitive scientist put the theory to me as an analogy, and it is the analogy that has been doing all the work: first-order mental states are actors on a dark stage; a state becomes conscious only when a higher-order state — a spotlight — is trained on it; the beam itself is never seen, only what it lights; the beam swings away, as in distraction, and can also swing onto an empty stretch of stage, as when someone seems to himself to be grief-stricken while, as the narrative plainly shows, he is not. She offers this both as a verdict on the duel scene and as a rule for my prose: write the unlit stretches as pure immediacy, she says, with no sentence of the form I noticed that I was in pain, since no such noticing is occurring. I am suspicious of the picture for reasons I can only half articulate. It seems to presuppose fully formed actors waiting in the dark, i.e. that an unnoticed pain is already a pain with a determinate what-it's-like, which is exactly what the theory's opponents deny; it seems to need someone to aim the beam, which invites a regress; and it seems to make the lit actor look different under the light, whereas the theory is supposed to leave the first-order state's qualitative character untouched and merely make it conscious. I have drafted two replacements of my own: a caption card inserted into a silent film (it adds nothing to the images, but only the caption makes them legible to an audience — and yet who reads it?), and a ledger entry recording a transaction nobody need ever read (which kills the regress but seems also to kill the for-the-subject-ness that was the whole point).

So here is what I want. Give me the correspondence table explicitly — what plays the role of the stage, the actors, the beam, the darkness, the operator, and the empty stage in the actual theory — and then audit the analogy: which features of a spotlight the theory genuinely has (its selectivity, its independence from what it lights, its capacity to misfire), which features the analogy quietly imports that the theory lacks, and which disanalogies are harmless for my purposes and which are load-bearing. Say plainly whether the empty-stage case — a higher-order state with no first-order target — is a problem the spotlight picture merely dramatizes or one it makes worse, and what the friendliest response on the theory's behalf actually is. Then tell me whether either conclusion the analogy was wheeled in to support survives: the verdict that my lieutenant was not in pain until the surgeon touched him, and the rule about how to write the unlit stretches. Finally, rank my three analogies — spotlight, caption card, ledger entry — by how well each carries the theory without smuggling anything in, tell me what each one costs, and, since I have to write the passage either way, give me two or three sentences in each direction: what the scene sounds like if the physician is right, and what it sounds like if my lieutenant is right.
