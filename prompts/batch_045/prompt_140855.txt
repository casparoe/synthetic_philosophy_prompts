I am finishing a paper on vagueness and would like to open it with a compact statement of where I stand. Before I commit the package to print, I want it audited for internal stability. Please assume familiarity with the standard menu of positions: I do not need a survey of theories, a map of the debate, an account of who has held what, or suggestions for further reading. What I want is an audit of the set below.

My commitments:

(1) Semanticism. Vagueness is a feature of representations, not of what they represent. There are no vague objects and no vague states of affairs; a complete description of the world in a vocabulary free of vague predicates would leave nothing indeterminate. I hold this partly as a methodological constraint: I do not want to revise my metaphysics to accommodate a semantic phenomenon.

(2) Truth-conditional and classical. I want a compositional truth-conditional semantics, and I want classical logic to come out as the correct account of validity for the object language. I understand consequence as local, that is, as preservation of truth across all admissible precisifications, not as preservation of truth at each precisification taken separately. I do not want to buy a non-classical logic or a degree-theoretic semantics.

(3) Object-level gaps, and they are not ignorance. In a borderline case, the sentence that is a heap is neither true nor false, and this is a semantic matter: there is no further fact of the kind that would settle it and that we are merely ignorant of.

(4) Supervaluationist truth conditions, with a penumbral constraint. Truth is truth on every admissible precisification, falsity falsity on every one. The admissible precisifications are those compatible with what our use fixes, constrained so that they cannot disagree wildly: I take it to be inadmissible for one admissible precisification to place the cut-off for heap at a thousand grains and another at ten million.

(5) Determinacy without knowability one level up. This is the part I suspect nobody else holds. I want to say that there is a perfectly determinate fact of the matter about which precisifications are admissible, while adding that this fact is unknowable in principle: no investigation could reveal whether the admissible cut-offs for bald top out at 4,742 hairs or 4,743. So my sharp cut-offs sit in the metalanguage. The indeterminacy of (3) is real and semantic, but what is indeterminate is drawn from a determinately delimited range whose edges we can never locate. I am unsure what the determinate fact is fixed by, and I would rather say whatever our practice in fact determines than commit myself to a story about how it does so.

(6) Genuine higher-order vagueness, as a non-negotiable desideratum. There are borderline borderline cases. It is not merely that we cannot tell where the penumbra ends; there is no sharp boundary between the clear positive cases, the borderline cases and the clear negative cases. Any operator of the definitely family is itself vague. I am not willing to trade this away, and I regard a theory that yields sharp boundaries at some level of iteration as having failed here.

(7) A verdict on the sorites. The argument is valid but unsound. No single conditional is the culprit; a whole range of them fail to be true, and I also want to say that it is indeterminate which of them fail, so that the penumbra is not divided into the premises that fail and the premises that hold. I explain the pull of the tolerance premise by our inability to tell where the admissible cut-offs lie, not by anything in the conditionals themselves.

(8) A verdict on penumbral cases in law. When a court applies a vague statutory term to a borderline instance, a bequest to employees who have served a substantial period, a by-law excluding vehicles from the park, the ruling is, within the penumbra, an exercise of discretion rather than the discovery of a semantic fact, and an appellate court should treat it as such. I take this practice to be evidence for (3): if there were a fact of the matter to be got right, I would expect the law to handle penumbral questions as questions of fact.

What I would like from you:

Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equivalent. For each, say precisely which commitment has to be weakened, what the cheapest weakening is, and what that weakening costs me elsewhere in the set, since I expect some of these to be fixable only at the price of a commitment I care about more. Where a commitment is stated loosely enough that it can escape a tension by being made more precise, say so, give the precisified version, and tell me whether the precisified version still does the work I need from it. Tell me also which of the tensions I am likely to worry about are not tensions at all, and why not: I would rather have two real problems than eight apparent ones. If a verdict in (7) or (8) cannot be delivered by the principles I have stated, say which principle fails to deliver it and whether the failure is one of detail or one of principle. Note any place where the audit turns on a substantive philosophical choice rather than on what follows from what, so I can see where I am deciding rather than deriving.

Finally: if the package turns out to hinge on a single commitment, tell me which one, and tell me what I become if I give it up, whether that lands me on a view in the standard menu and, if it does not, what the residual position is and whether it is one anyone could reasonably hold. I would rather be told that two commitments are jointly untenable than be offered a reading of them on which everything comes out fine.
