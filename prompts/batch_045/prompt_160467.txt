Hi — I'm a PhD student in history working on commemoration disputes, and this term I've been taken on as research assistant (really the rapporteur) to a standing commision that rules on exactly this sort of fight: when a community's memory of its own past and the documentary record come apart, the commission decides what happens to the monument, the plaque, the museum panel, the school text. I have to draft the options paper for our next case and I keep going in circles, so I'd like an outside read. Please don't give me citations or a reading list — I've done the literature, I want the argument.

Here's the track record as I've reconstructed it from the minutes. (1) A town-hall mural showing the women of Carrowmore defending the wall in the 1571 siege: the town's own militia muster rolls list no women among the defenders, and the story first turns up in an 1860s novel. Ruling: mural removed and replaced with a display on how the story was invented. (2) A plaque calling a market-day gathering of 1789 the Twelve Days Riot and the start of the town's fight for self-government: the town's own tax and court records show forty-odd guild elders handing over a petition, no violence, nobody hurt. Ruling: plaque kept, a single sentence added noting what the records show. (3) The Bend: an upland community's elders remember a massacre at a bend in the river; nothing was ever written down on either side, because the district wasn't administered at the time, and test excavation found mass burials. Ruling: memory accepted as authoritative, memorial commissioned and funded. (4) A treaty plaque with the signing date eleven days off; the treaty instrument itself survives. Ruling: date corrected, wording untouched. (5) A regimental memorial to a unit remembered locally as liberators: the unit's own commander's after-action report — his own report, damaging to him — records reprisal killings of villagers in three parishes. Ruling: memorial moved into the county museum with a contextual panel, not destroyed, descendants' objection noted and overruled. (6) A midsummer Long Summer festival whose origin story is about a saint and a spring, and which nobody actually asserts as history: ruling: declined jurisdiction, on the ground that the commission rules on contested historical assertions and not on legend that claims nothing.

The new case is the Annerdale Walk. Since 1901 the hill families of Annerdale have walked, over three days, the route their ancestors are said to have walked in the winter of 1799–1800 after being cleared off the land — ninety-odd miles to the coast. The Walk is the centre of their identity, it's the one occasion the dispersed families come back for, and it's load-bearing for a grazing-rights claim now before a tribunal. A newly deposited estate archive (the landowner's papers, the coastal town's poor-law settlement register, the burgess minutes) shows that the removals were spread over three years rather than one winter; that about a third of the households left voluntarily after being offered cash; that only two families actually walked, the rest being moved by cart and coastal ship; and that the coastal town didn't take them in at all — it billeted them into workhouses and petitioned the county to send them back. The catch, and the reason I can't just apply precedent 2: every one of those documents was written by the party that removed them or the town that rejected them; the displaced appear only as numbered poor-law entries; the clearances were lawful at the time, so nobody recorded their side of it; and the estate's factor was seperately caught understating the number of households removed in an unrelated compensation dispute. Meanwhile a local history society down on the coast is already using the new archive to argue that the Annerdale families were never driven out at all and that the grazing claim is built on a story.

What I want from you: set out the two or three rules about which should give way — memory or record — that would generate all six of the earlier verdicts, and say what each of them predicts here, including which precedent each treats as the closest analog. Then give me your best guess at what the commission will actually do and, separately, what it should do if it wants to be consistent with itself, with the reasons you prefer that reading. Also tell me what further case, or what further piece of evidence, would actually separate the rules — because if the precedents genuinely underdetermine this, I need to be able to tell the commissioners what principle they'd be adopting with each option. Be honest about confidence: say which parts of this you think are really up in the air. And if I've set the problem up badly — if the gap between "the record is silent" and "the record is one-sided" can't bear the weight I'm putting on it, or if an options paper is the wrong instrument for this kind of decision — say so plainly instead of handing me a tidy answer.
