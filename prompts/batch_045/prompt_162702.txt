I left school at eighteen and have no training in biology or philosophy; I have been reading about genetics on my own, and I have got stuck on a comparison that everyone around me leans on without, it seems to me, ever examining it.

The comparison is that DNA is a code. A documentary I watched described the genome as the instruction book for building a person. My brother, who has written software for thirty years, puts it more strongly: DNA is not merely like source code, it is source code, the cell is the machine that compiles and runs it, and that is why it is legitimate to speak of genetic information being stored, copied, corrupted and now edited. He uses the picture to defend three claims:

1. A genome is information, and information is portable, so a stored sequence plus a synthesized genome would, in the sense that matters, reconstitute the organism or the species. This is what he says makes genome banking and the various de-extinction projects more than publicity.
2. One can read off what an organism will be from its sequence, at least in principle, the way one can read off what a program will do from its source; so the older talk of a blueprint, and of genetic determinism, was roughly right and merely ahead of the technology.
3. Nothing about a code implies an encoder. When a neighbour uses the very same picture to argue that the genome must have had an author, my brother says that is an abuse of his analogy; but he insists that 1 and 2 are fair uses of it.

I want to know whether he is entitled to draw the line there. What makes me doubt it is a handful of things I noticed while reading, which I cannot fit into the code picture:

(a) When DNA is copied, each base appears to be matched with a partner that physically fits it, rather as a mould matches a casting; the sequence serves as its own template. But when a gene is turned into a protein, bases are taken three at a time and matched to an amino acid by an adapter molecule, and that pairing looks arbitrary, more like a dictionary entry. The same molecule seems to be doing two quite different kinds of work, and I cannot tell which of them is supposed to be the coding part, or whether one analogy ought to cover both.

(b) The adapters, and the molecular machinery that does the reading, are themselves built out of products of the very process they carry out. In every code I know, the reader and the codebook come from outside the message and exist before it. Here they appear to be manufactured by the message.

(c) As far as I can make out, one stretch of DNA can be read starting at different points, and the working copy made from it can be cut apart and rejoined in different combinations in different tissues, so that the same stretch contributes to different products depending on which cell is reading it. There does not seem to be a single fixed text existing prior to the reading.

(d) I have also read that the assignments are not quite the same everywhere, that mitochondria in our own cells assign a few of them differently. So the mapping is neither a law of nature nor a convention fixed once for all, but something that itself has a history.

So my question is this: please audit the comparison. Set out explicitly what plays the role of the alphabet, the words, the source file, the compiler, the machine, the input, the output, the specification and the programmer; say which of those correspondences are load-bearing for claims 1 and 2 and which are decoration; then separate the mismatches that actually matter for those two claims from the ones that do not. Can my brother reject the author inference while keeping the rest, or does the code picture stand or fall as a package? And if some other comparison does the jobs he wants better — a recipe, a musical score, a punched card, a mould, a contract, a game of telephone — tell me which, what it maps onto what, and where it in turn breaks down. I would much rather be told that the analogy is sound for one purpose and hopeless for another than be told simply that it is only a metaphor.
