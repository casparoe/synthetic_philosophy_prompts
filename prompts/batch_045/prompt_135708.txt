I got into a long, slightly drunk argument with a friend last night and I need help settling it, or at least figuring out what we were actually disagreeing about. We were talking about Mary Shelley's Frankenstein — we both read it years ago and my friend is doing a PhD in synthetic biology, so the conversation drifted — and I claimed the creature is a genuinely hard case for etiological ("selected effects") theories of biological function and a friendlier case for causal-role ones. His reply was that obviously the creature's heart has the function of pumping blood and that I was being clever for no reason. But the thing that makes the case good, I think, is that the three sources of function that normally coincide in an evolved organism come apart here: the parts were scavenged from corpses and slaughterhouse material, so each organ does have an evolutionary selection history, but the system they've been assembled into is brand new and was never selected for anything; Victor has intentions, so there's an artifact-style designer story available; and the whole thing demonstrably does things — it walks, learns language by eavesdropping on the De Laceys, survives an Arctic winter, outruns Victor — that no arrangement of human parts was ever selected for. I also invented a variant to try to break the tie: imagine Victor had grown the body from scratch in a vat so that not a single cell in it had an evolutionary history, and imagine further that he'd walked out of the lab halfway through and abandoned the design. My friend says the heart still pumps in every version and that's all anyone needs; I think the fact that our answers might drift between the versions is itself the interesting result, because it suggests etiological function travels with parts the way a transplanted organ carries its function into a new host, while causal-role function is a fact about what the whole system currently does and would be totally undisturbed by any of my changes.

So what I'd like is a reading of the novel that uses it as a test case rather than as decoration. Where does each account give the intuitively right verdict and where does it give a silly one? In particular: does the creature's strength and cold-tolerance count as a function of anything, or only as a lucky system-level effect; is its hideousness a malfunction in the strong sense that only an etiological account can underwrite, given that the parts are individually fine and Victor intended beauty; and does Victor tearing apart the half-finished second creature do anything to the functions the first one has, if you think the designer's abandoned intentions were ever in the business of conferring them? I'm also curious whether the novel resists this reading somewhere — whether Shelley is doing something with the creature's own sense of what it's for (it wants a mate, i.e. reproduction, which is exactly the one function Victor refuses to give it) that neither account has room for. I'm giving a short talk to a sci-fi-and-philosophy reading group in a couple weeks, so I want something that's faithful in both directions and doesn't just collapse into "it depends which theory you prefer": tell me where the reading has to admit the novel isn't cooperating, and, if there's anything here, what the novel shows about functions that the journal literature's stock examples — hearts pumping, sperm swimming, spotted moths — can't.
