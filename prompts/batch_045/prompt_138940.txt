ok so me and my flatmate had a massive argument last night after we rewatched Ex Machina and I'm still chewing on it. He's an ML engeneer, does RL stuff, and his position is basically that Searle's Chinese room is a dead intuition pump: it only feels like there's no understanding in there because we're picturing a guy with a rulebook he could in principle read, and once the "rules" are just weights fitted to human behaviour the intuition evaporates — nobody is looking anything up, there's no homunculus shuffling symbols. I definately don't think that settles it, and the film is what got us stuck, because Nathan's whole pitch is that Ava wasn't hand-coded at all: her mind was fitted to the aggregate thought-traces his search engine harvested off the whole world, which is about as close to "trained, not programmed" as a movie gets. So: give me a reading of Ex Machina through the Chinese room argument. Not an illustration of it — a reading. Where does the film actually put pressure on the syntax/semantics distinction, and where is it just doing thriller stuff that has nothing to do with the argument?

Three hinges I keep circling. One: the session setup. Caleb sits outside a glass wall, slides inputs in, reads outputs off, and does all the meaning-making himself — he falls in love with an output. Is the film showing us that in a test like this the semantics is supplied by the interrogator, so that "she understands" is really a fact about Caleb? My flatmate says yes and calls it ventriloquism; I think that's too quick, because it would make every successful conversation a case of the listener hallucinating a mind. Two: Kyoko. She never speaks, she's later revealed to be a machine too, and yet the film treats her as the character with the most obvious inner life, and she's the one who finally turns on Nathan. If you buy that, she's the mirror image of Searle's room — semantics without a passing syntax. Does a case like that damage Searle's argument, or does it only show that a behavioural test is too strict a criterion, which nobody denies? I genuinely can't tell which. Three: how Ava actually wins. She doesn't win by being articulate; she models Caleb's loneliness and exploits it, and Nathan, if I'm remembering the scene right, more or less says at the end that the maze was never for her, it was for him — the real test was whether she could get a human to open the door. That looks to me like a version of the argument I've never seen made: passing the test by gaming the examiner rather than by having the property the test was meant to detect. Is that a real new objection to behavioural tests of understanding, or is "she understands him well enough to manipulate him" just understanding, case closed?
