I work as the clinical ethics consultant for a hospital centre that performs graded neural replacement, and I need something I can actually put in front of the nursing coordinators. The intervention is what it sounds like: in patients with a rare progressive cortical degeneration, biological cortex is exchanged for functionally matched synthetic modules a few cubic millimetres at a time, each session under general anaesthesia, over something like two hundred sessions spread across eighteen to twenty-four months. It works. Patients who complete it wake up with their memories, their temperaments, their projects and their manner of speaking intact, and to anyone observing them they are the people who enrolled. None of our disputes are about whether to offer it. They are about who, precisely, is in the bed at session 140, and that question has to be answered repeatedly and under time pressure, because the answer settles whether we continue to treat the current subject as the enrolling patient for purposes of consent, advance directives, surrogate designation, resuscitation status, and the property, custody and insurance questions that families bring us. I have roughly thirty minutes per consult, the person filling in the form will usually be a senior nurse with no philosophical training, and whatever the form does not settle escalates to me and then, if money or custody is involved, to legal. So I want a written procedure: the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as an adequate answer to each, the points at which the procedure returns a presumption against treating the current subject as the enrolling patient and stops, and the points at which it hands the decision to a human being because the issue is one of values rather than fact. Be explicit about which steps are really judgment calls wearing procedural clothing, because I would rather know that now than discover it during an audit. I would also rather have a procedure that is wrong in a known and correctable direction than one that is vague, and my committee will not accept one whose answer is always that it depends. Please skip citations, bibliographies and suggested reading altogether; I have no library access on the ward and I need the instrument, not the literature underneath it.

The reason I am pushing for this now is that I had a long and unsatisfying argument about it last night with a friend. He holds the psychological-continuity line and thinks the graded case is trivial: each session the patient wakes up remembering the last one, behaviour and memory are preserved end to end, so of course it is the same person throughout, and anyone who says otherwise is being spooked by the anaesthesia. I found myself defending something closer to the biological-organism view, mostly on the strength of what our phenomenal-continuity interviews turn up, and a third colleague of ours says there is no further fact here at all and that survival simply comes in degrees, so that the practical question is not what happened to the patient but which statuses we should let follow the continuity. We went nowhere. So I want the procedure built to handle, at minimum, these cases: the patient at around seventy per cent replacement who revokes the advance directive he wrote before enrolling, on the ground that he no longer wants what that earlier self wanted; the reversion case, where excised tissue has been kept viable and is reimplanted after sixty per cent of the cortex has been replaced, leaving us with two candidate claimants, the mostly synthetic subject and the restored biological one; the branching case, where a patient dies intraoperatively and the team restores a full backup taken at intake into the already implanted substrate, so that the resulting subject remembers nothing after intake; the split-stream case, a patient with a prior corpus callosotomy whose two hemispheric streams are replaced on staggered schedules and who then disagree about whether to continue; the pause case, where funding lapses at forty per cent and treatment resumes years later; the substitution case, where the only modules available mid-course run several times faster than biological tissue and the resulting subject says the enrollee could not have anticipated what living at that speed would be like; and the fade case, where behaviour, memory and reported life history are all intact but from about session eighty onward the patient volunteers, reliably and without being prompted, that it has felt for some time as though someone else has been finishing his sentences for him, while our interview instrument scores him ambiguously. I would also like the procedure to say whether it treats whole-brain destructive replacement of the sort performed at a competing centre differently from the graded protocol, and what in the procedure makes the difference.

Please ground the thing in the actual substance of those three views rather than in anything idiosyncratic, and keep the theory invisible in the form itself: the nurse should never have to know which view a question comes from. Then apply the finished procedure to three of the cases above, showing the steps as they would be gone through: the seventy per cent revocation, the reversion case where the restored biological subject contests the enrollee's will, and the split-stream case where the streams disagree. One of the three needs to be a case where the procedure gives an answer my committee will not want, and I strongly suspect that is the revocation case, since if the procedure tells us we must re-consent everyone mid-course and suspend new enrolments the programme will be damaged; if that is where it lands, say so plainly rather than softening it. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do. In particular, I want to know whether it is capable of settling the underlying question my friend and I were actually arguing about, whether the enrolling patient survives the series at all, or whether the most it can honestly deliver is a defensible rule for allocating consent, property and surrogacy among candidate claimants while leaving the survival question open, and how I should explain that difference to a family who wants a straight answer.
