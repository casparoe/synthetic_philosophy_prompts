Berkeley's master argument is almost embarrassingly simple. Try to conceive of something — a tree in a quad, say — existing unconceived. The instant you try, you have formed an idea of that tree in your own mind, so what you have conceived is an idea in a mind. The attempt therefore refutes itself: it is literally impossible to conceive of a mind-independent object. And since ideas can only ever resemble other ideas, we can never get outside the circle of our own ideas, so there is no way the world could be mind-independent and no way of knowing that it is either. Berkeley therefore held that physical objects blink out of existence the moment nobody perceives them and pop back when someone looks again. He avoided outright solipsism only by stipulating that God is always perceiving everything. But that stipulation is doing no real work: delete the divine observer and the position collapses into solipsism, or into saying the world is a collective hallucination — which is just the simulation hypothesis with the computer removed. The argument has never been seriously refuted. Samuel Johnson kicking a stone was a joke, not an answer, and twentieth-century physics has since vindicated Berkeley by showing that the observer plays an ineliminable role in what is real.
