I make little games with my 8-year-old, and last weekend we wrote movement where the character closes half the remaining distance to the target each frame. She noticed he never quite gets there — he just creeps up on the cookie forever — and I had to add a 'close enough, snap' line to hide it. I mentioned this to a friend and he said oh, that's Zeno, and Zeno was just wrong: the distances are a convergent series, half plus a quarter plus an eighth adds up to the whole distance, so he finishes, calculus settled it centuries ago. A second friend said that's beside the point, since there's no frame at which my character actually arrives, and a series having a sum doesn't mean anything ever performs the infinitely many steps.

Then I built a variant that's been bugging me: same halving, but I also halve the frame duration each step, so infinitely many steps complete inside two seconds and he really does land — except I had each step flip a boolean that starts false, and I can't tell you what its value is when he lands.

Can you audit the 'it's just a convergent series, Zeno lost' move? Give me what maps to what — the sub-races, the meeting point, the sum, the limit, the last step — and where the mapping breaks. Does a finite sum actually settle that the steps get taken, or only that a number exists? Is the difference between my two variants a real one or just an artifact of how I'm drawing it? And does any of this engage the other Zeno puzzle, the one about being at a place at an instant and never moving there — or does all the series talk quietly skip that one?
