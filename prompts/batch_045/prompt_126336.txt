I write about science and ideas, and next week I am defending, in front of a technical audience, the central claim of a long piece I have been working on: that pleasure and pain are not measurable on a single scale. My version is a lexical-threshold view. Below some intensity, pleasures and pains genuinely trade off against one another; above a threshold, pain becomes lexically dominant, so that no amount of mild pleasure, however long it lasts, compensates for a given stretch of severe pain. I locate the threshold in the structure of attention rather than in reported intensity: pain crosses it when it can no longer be backgrounded, when it commands attention whether or not the subject wants it to. My reason for putting it there is that trade-offs are only possible between things you can weigh against each other, weighing requires being able to turn attention away from one of the items, and what cannot be backgrounded cannot be weighed — so it enters deliberation not as a very large quantity but as a claim of a different order. The practical upshot I am there to defend is methodological: any cost-effectiveness framework that nets positive and negative experience into one cardinal currency, in the manner of health-utility and quality-adjusted-life-year style calculation, is not merely imprecise about severe pain but wrong in kind, and it will systematically under-fund interventions aimed at the worst suffering.

The room is a closed one-day roundtable that a large health foundation has convened to decide whether to fund a portfolio of chronic-pain programmes: about six health economists and measurement psychologists whose working lives depend on a single commensurable unit of experience, two pain scientists, one bioethicist, and three programme officers who need a defensible ranking by the end of the day. Draft me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I will actually get from this group, in their vocabulary rather than in a philosopher's, ordered by how much damage they would do, each with a reply I can deliver in roughly two minutes and a note on where that reply is soft; tell me which objections have genuine force and what I should concede before I am asked; and include the question I am hoping nobody raises.
