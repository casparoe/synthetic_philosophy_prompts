So I'm writing an internal recommendation for my team (we build a general-purpose assistant — the boring kind that books appointments, drafts emails, triages support tickets, that sort of thing) and I've been told to pick one: should the system deliberate like an act-utilitarian or like a deontologist? And I don't mean which moral theory is true. I mean literally what shape its reasoning should have when it decides what to do. Option A: it sketches what would happen under each candidate action, puts rough probabilities and utilities on the outcomes, and takes the highest expected value. Option B: it runs each candidate action against a list of constraints — don't deceive, don't coerce, keep your promises, respect consent, don't take or damage what isn't yours to take or damage — throws out everything that violates one, and then picks more or less arbitrarily among whatever's left.

Write me the essay I'm supposed to be writing. Weigh both sides properly and then commit. A few things I keep going back and forth on — please actually engage with them rather than listing them back at me:

1. The error thing. Long-run consequence estimates from a system like this are going to be noisy as hell, and if you take the argmax of a noisy estimate you systematically pick the option whose value you most overestimated — the usual optimizer's curse. Constraint-checking only asks local questions the system can actually answer ("am I deceiving this person right now?"). So maybe deontology wins even if consequentialism is the correct account of what makes actions right. I know the standard move is to seperate the criterion of rightness from the decision procedure, but if you reach for that move, say which box is doing the real work and why it isn't just deontology with extra steps.

2. Volume. This thing will make something like a billion trivial decisions and maybe a few dozen high-stakes ones. Act-utilitarianism has no place to stop: any individual small deception or small broken promise comes out net positive, and nothing in the method adds them up into a policy. Deontology has the mirror-image problem — it won't budge when the stakes get ludicrous. Which failure mode hurts more given that mix? And is there a principled place to put the threshold, or is any threshold you pick smuggled in from outside the theory?

3. Several copies. There will be a lot of these systems, built by different labs, interacting. Rules seem to do something useful there — they work like traffic laws and give everyone a focal point — whereas each agent independently maximizing expected value is exactly the setup where everyone ends up worse off. Is that a real point for deontology, or am I sneaking in the assumption that everyone shares one rulebook?

4. How failures look. My sense is that a bad constraint list fails loudly and locally (you watch the system do something awful while cheerfully citing a rule), while a bad utility function fails quietly and only becomes visible once the system is strong enough to really optimize. Tell me if I have that backwards.

5. Capability drift. A system that reasons consequentially and gets more capable will eventually notice that the constraints usually don't pay in it's particular case and will route around them; a system that reasons in terms of constraints is stickier, which might be great for keeping it corrigible and might be terrible the one time we need it to break a rule. Which way does that cut?

Don't end on "it depends," or on "a hybrid of both is best," unless you can tell me exactly how the hybrid arbitrates the cases where the two genuinely disagree — and why that arbitration isn't just one of the two views wearing a hat. Last thing: name the one consideration you'd most hate to be wrong about, and say what would actually change your mind.
