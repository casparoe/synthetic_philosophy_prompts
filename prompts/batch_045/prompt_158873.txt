I'm a quantitative ecologist, and most of my working life is spent fitting hierarchical models to long-term monitoring data: random effects for sites and observers, penalised likelihood with ridge or lasso on the fixed effects, model comparison by AIC or WAIC, grouped cross-validation when I want something I can defend to a sceptical reviewer, and increasingly a kind of pragmatic model averaging, because in my field no single model has ever won honestly. I am fluent in the mechanics of all of this — effective degrees of freedom, bias-variance bookkeeping, two-part or minimum-description-length codes, the M-closed versus M-open distinction between cases where the truth is one of my candidate models and cases where it plainly is not. What I do not have is any clear view of why simplicity should be expected to pay off, beyond the accumulated empirical fact that it usually does.

A philosopher I have been reading with for the past year claims that Solomonoff induction settles the question: there is a canonical simplicity-weighted prior over all computable hypotheses, it dominates every computable alternative up to a multiplicative constant that depends on the true environment, and this is what Occam's razor was always groping toward. I want you to restate that whole cluster of claims — the universal prior, the dominance result, Kolmogorov complexity's role in it, the relativity of complexity to a choice of universal machine, the incomputability of the prior itself, and the surrounding argument about whether simplicity is thereby justified a priori or merely vindicated pragmatically — in the vocabulary I actually use, as faithfully as you can. Please do not quietly substitute my framework's assumptions and call the result a translation: if, for instance, the universal prior cannot really be represented as a prior over a countable list of candidate models, or if the dominance result is a statement about cumulative log-loss rather than about anything resembling my finite-sample predictive scores, I want that said explicitly rather than papered over. Where a term has to be stretched to fit — simplicity as a penalty term, model complexity as description length, the truth as computable — mark the stretch.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Three particular suspicions of mine should be tested in the process. First, I have been experimenting with what I call a description-language audit: before comparing two candidate models I rewrite both in a deliberately different but equally expressive notation — moving, say, from a linear-predictor-plus-link formulation to a symbolic routine that computes the same fitted values — and check whether their ranking under a two-part code survives the rewrite. Sometimes it does not. I had hoped this was a small, finite, honest surrogate for the relativity of Kolmogorov complexity to a choice of universal machine; my worry is that it only measures my own ineptitude at writing compact code, and that the philosophical worry about language dependence is either much larger or much smaller than my little audit suggests. Second, much of the simplicity in my models is there for convenience rather than because I think the world is simple — a smooth spatial surface instead of a wiggly one, three variance components instead of eleven — and I suspect no a priori argument for simplicity covers that case at all, that what is really doing the work is a bias-variance tradeoff at a fixed sample size, and that a defender of the Solomonoff line has a reply I have not heard. Third, in our reading group I have been claiming that the dominance result is essentially a log-loss result, and that if you rescore predictions by squared error on a bounded horizon or by whether the next observation lands in a stated interval, the claim that simplicity-weighted prediction is optimal 'up to a constant' loses much of its content; my colleague says this is simply changing the subject. Tell me which of us is confused, whether the confusion has a name, and, at the end, whether what fails to translate into my vocabulary points to something my vocabulary is missing or to something the philosophers' claim was never clearly saying.
