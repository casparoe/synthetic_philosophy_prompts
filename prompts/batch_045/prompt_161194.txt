hey — I'm writing a short blog post (not for publication, mostly to get it straight in my own head) about whether death can be bad for the person who dies, and I want to sanity-check the Epicurean side before I commit to it.

The version I've ended up with — I'm calling it the no-window argument:

1. Nothing can be bad for you unless there's a time at which it's bad for you, and you exist at that time.
2. The only candidate times for death to be bad for you are while you're alive and after you die.
3. While you're alive, death hasn't happened yet, so it can't be bad for you then — and besides, whatever goods you've got at that point, you've got them.
4. Once you're dead you don't exist, so (1) rules it out.
5. So death isn't bad for you.

Premise (1) feels obviously true to me — how can something be bad for you if there's no time at which it's bad for you? — and it seems like once you grant it, the whole "death is bad because it deprives you of the goods you'd otherwise have had" line is dead in the water. So I don't really get why people still write about it.

The bit I'm least sure about is an addendum I want to stick at the end, which I actually think is the strongest part: that the argument doesn't just show death isn't bad for you, it shows there's no fact of the matter about how bad your death is, or about whether dying at 40 is worse than dying at 90, because a comparison needs two states to compare, and once you're gone there's no state you're in to compare to any other. Saying "you lose forty years of goods" is comparing a life to nothing, and that isn't a comparision at all. Am I pushing the argument too far there?

Two smaller things: I'm using the Lucretius symmetry point — the time before you were born wasn't bad for you, so why should the time after be — but only as a rhetorical flourish, since (1) does the real work on its own. Yeah? And if all this holds, then fearing death is just straightforwardly irrational, so I should be able to stop, right?
