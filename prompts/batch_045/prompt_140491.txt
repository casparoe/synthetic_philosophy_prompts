You are a philosopher of biology who works on theories of function, and I'd like you to audit a position paper I'm writing for a graduate reading group. Please reconstruct each commitment below in its strongest form before you start looking for trouble — I'd rather be beaten by the best version of my own view than by a strawman — and then tell me whether the package hangs together. I already know the standard dialectic between selected-effects and causal-role accounts, so please don't spend the response surveying it; what I want is the audit. My supervisor skimmed an early draft and said I was equivocating somewhere, but she couldn't say where, and I genuinely can't see it either.

Here is the set, more or less in the order I defend it in the paper: (1) The core claim is etiological: a trait's function is what it was selected for, and for artifacts the very same notion of function is conferred by a maker's or designer's intention. Function is a historical property, not a matter of what something happens to be doing. (2) The reason I prefer this to the Cummins-style capacity-decomposition account is normativity: function attributions are what make sense of defect, malfunction, and "supposed to." A heart that cannot pump is a defective heart, not a heart with a different causal role, and I don't see how a purely role-based account gets a failing organ rather than a differently-described one. (3) At the same time I want function attributions in physiology and medicine to be genuinely explanatory in the Cummins way: saying what a component contributes to the containing system's capacity really does explain how the system works, and not merely heuristically. (4) Methodologically, I think disputes like this should be settled by looking at what working scientists actually do — physiologists, molecular biologists, clinicians — rather than by armchair intuitions about imagined cases, and I appeal to practice at several points in the paper. (5) On exaptation I hold that when a trait was selected for X but is now maintained and elaborated because it does Y, we should say its function is Y (or at least includes Y), not X: if feathers were first selected for insulation in a flightless lineage and later co-opted for flight, I want to say flight is among their functions. (6) I reject forward-looking "propensity" accounts: bare present usefulness, or the fact that a trait would promote survival in future environments, does not by itself confer a function or generate a "supposed to." (7) I also want to be able to say of some traits — spandrels, vestiges — that they have no function at all, and to treat that as a substantive empirical claim rather than a placeholder for our ignorance. (8) For artifacts I'm committed to something that feels immediate rather than historical: a device I design and build this afternoon has its intended job as its function right away, with no track record of successful use behind it, and I want this to be the same notion of function, not a convenient homonym. (9) Finally, and this one came out of an argument with a physiologist friend, I want clinical function attributions to be sensitive to the individual organism: when a patient's anatomy is idiosyncratic and a structure has taken on a role it was never selected for, I want to say what that structure is *for* in this organism, given how it is actually organized, and to count that as a real function attribution rather than as loose clinical shorthand.

What I'd like from you: go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it, and don't flag everything as equally bad — I can produce that list myself. Rank the real tensions by severity. For each one, say what would have to give, what the cheapest available repair is, and what that repair costs me elsewhere in the set (in particular whether fixing it forces me to give up some explanation I was relying on). I especially want you to discriminate between three kinds of verdict: a genuine incompatibility, a case where I'm quietly using "function" for two different things and that's actually fine, and a merely verbal dispute. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Please also separate, where it matters, tensions that arise from the content of my views from tensions that arise from my methodological commitment in (4), since I suspect that commitment is doing more work in the package than I noticed.

One more thing on how I want the answer pitched. For each tension, tell me how confident you are that it is real, using something finer-grained than "maybe": say whether you'd defend the charge in print, whether it rests on a contested reading of one of my commitments, or whether it depends on an empirical claim about biological practice that neither of us can settle here. And please say what would change your verdict — which argument, or which finding about how physiologists or molecular biologists actually use function talk, would lead you to re-rank the tensions or to concede that the package is in better shape than you first thought. Finally, flag the place where your own audit is most likely to be wrong: the step you're least sure of, or the assumption about my commitments you'd most want to check with me before publishing the objection.
