Next thursday I'm presenting at the monthly meeting of a pro-housing group in a expensive city — the room will be about 40 people, mostly supply-side types who work in tech, a half dozen tenant organizers who come to every one of these to argue, one land-use lawyer who does this for fun, and a city planner who has to be there. I've got 20 minutes and then questions. I'm going to argue something this room will hate, and I want to be ready.

My thesis: the neighborhood veto is not just homeowners protecting asset prices, and the pro-housing movement is wrong to treat every instance of it as selfishness or astroturf. The core of it is legitimate — people whose daily life is staked in a place have a claim to a say over what happens to it, and that claim should scale with how much of their life is staked there, not with whether they hold the deed. On my version, long-term renters and small resident landlords in the neighborhood would have more of a say than absentee owners, and also more than the people who'd move into the new units. I call it stake-weighted say, and I want to argue that any "right to the city" worth wanting has to include something like it: the abundance agenda is right about how many units we need and wrong about who gets to decide.

The argument in brief: (1) what people are actually protecting at a zoning hearing isn't mostly resale value, it's that their kid's school, their commute, their landlord, the corner store and their neighbours are all in one place and can't be picked up and moved; (2) that continuity is a large part of what makes a city worth living in at all; (3) the current institution attaches the say to ownership, so owners over-claim it and renters — who often have the most precarious stake — get none; (4) so the fix isn't to abolish the say, it's to detach it from the deed and reattach it to stake; (5) and yes, that means some people who would have moved in don't get to. That's a real cost of the view and I'm not going to pretend otherwise.

For each: the objection as that person would actually put it, the two-minute version of my reply, and one honest sentence about where the reply is soft.
- "Give-aways and hills" (~120 words): what I should concede in the first two minutes to buy some goodwill, and the two things I should refuse to give up even if the room turns on me.
- "The one that has to land" (~120 words): the single reply that, if it doesn't work, makes the whole talk a waste — tell me which one it is and how to say it in under a minute.
