Bit of a random one, but here goes. I'm on the committee of the housing co-op I live in (14 flats, shared garden, the usual low-grade war about the bin rota), and we keep hitting the same argument in miniature: who actually gets a say on a decision? My mate who's doing a PhD in political theory says this is the boundary problem, laughed at me, and then couldn't actually answer it either. So I've started writing down my gut verdicts to see whether there's any principle behind them of whether I'm just making it up as I go along.

Here's the list. Members vote on the garden rota, obviously. We voted on a rule banning overnight guests — the guests are bound by it and I'm fine with them not getting a vote. We voted on whether to sack our part-time caretaker; she's bound by the house rules but isn't a member, and I think she should have had a vote, or at the very least a veto. Our parish council votes on a bypass whose noise and air will land almost entirely in the next village down the valley, and I think those people should get a say. But a firm whose entire business would be wiped out by a new emissions rule should not get a vote on that rule, even though it's affected far more severely than the villagers are. Fifteen-year-olds should get a vote on a binding thirty-year carbon target. Prisoners serving short sentences should vote. The long-term residents on our street who aren't citizens: I want to say yes for the parish, no for the general election, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's principled or just habit. Patients whose operations get cancelled because of a union strike ballot: no vote.

The thing I keep reaching for is something like — you owe someone a say just when the decision is going to be imposed on them by force or penalty and they can't reasonably walk away from the arrangement. I've been calling the second half the no-exit clause. But I can't work out which half is doing the work, because the guest case versus the caretaker case pull one way and the villagers versus the firm pull the other. Two further things I want a principle to handle rather than dodge: the people who count get to decide who counts, so what's to stop a majority trimming the franchise down to whoever will vote with them (we half-joked about doing exactly this over the bins, which is what made me think of it), and we're toying with a rule that any decision binding non-members has to be countersigned by a rotating panel drawn from those non-members — is that a real fix or a fig leaf?

So: give me the principle clause by clause, with the case that motivates each clause, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it can't deliver and which single clause is doing the most work. Is whatever I end up with closer to an all-affected view or an all-subjected one? And what does it say about our au pair — she lives here, she's bound by every rule, one-year fixed term, can leave on a month's notice — because I've gone back and forth on her about six times now. Please no citations or reading lists, I just want the shape of the thing; I'll go and find the literature myself if it survives.
