I host a monthly reading group drawn from my local rationalist-adjacent meetup — mostly software engineers plus two biology grad students, all of them steeped in the Sequences, none of them with any philosophy of biology. I have sixty minutes and a whiteboard to get them to the point where they can see that the question of what a gene is for is really two different questions that can come apart. Please take the role of a professional philosopher of biology who has taught this many times, and plan the explanation rather than giving it: I want the etiological (selected-effects) and the causal-role (contribution-to-a-containing-system's-capacity) accounts of biological function laid out in the order they have to be introduced, with the dependency structure explicit.

Here is the example I am inclined to carry the session with. A plasmid gene G was spliced into a bacterial strain by a lab because it conferred antibiotic resistance on the medium they were using; in the soil where the strain now lives, G's protein binds a heavy metal, and that detoxification is what currently keeps the lineage spreading; and in the standard assay G also makes colonies red, which is why everyone in the field calls it the red gene. Three candidate answers to what G is for, one per story, and I am hoping the divergence does all the work. Tell me whether it does, and what the case would have to gain or lose if it doesn't.

Misconceptions I expect: that the two accounts are rival answers to a single question rather than answers to different ones, so one of them has to be refuted; that the etiological answer is fixed by the original effect, or by the intention of whoever engineered the thing, rather than by what the lineage is currently being maintained for; and that a causal-role account has no way to call anything a malfunction. Say where in the sequence each should be headed off, and add the one I have missed that actually bites with this audience.

Close with the single question I can ask at the end that would tell me whether the distinction landed rather than whether they memorized it, and with what I should openly refuse to explain at this level — I am tempted to open the units-of-selection and artifact-function cans, and suspect I shouldn't.

Brainstorm as long as you need, then return exactly seven numbered items, one or two sentences each: (1) the sequence of beats with what each later beat depends on; (2) your verdict on the example and the minimal change if it fails; (3) through (5) my three misconceptions, each with the point in the sequence where it should be defused; (6) the missed misconception; (7) the closing check and the refusal. No introduction, no conclusion.
