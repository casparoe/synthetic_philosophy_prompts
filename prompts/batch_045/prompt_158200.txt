I'm leading a discussion section on George Eliot next month for a course on religion and secularism in the nineteenth-century novel, and before I put a frame in front of students I want to see whether it survives being pressed. The biographical spine is familiar enough: the young Marian Evans, who translated Strauss's *The Life of Jesus, Critically Examined* in 1846 and Feuerbach's *The Essence of Christianity* in 1854, gave up the doctrines and kept the religious psychology — God, revelation and an afterlife go, while reverence, duty, renunciation and the sense of being claimed by something outside the self stay on, re-attached to humankind, in something like the Comtean idiom of a religion of humanity. My worry is that kept the psychology hides a problem I have started calling the freestanding-reverence problem. On the Feuerbachian picture Eliot herself translated, worship is best described as a structure with a slot in it: an object treated as absolute, making a total claim, around which a whole affective and moral life gets organized. If demythologizing removes the object but leaves the structure standing, then either the structure slowly atrophies for want of anything that can fill the slot, or it finds a surrogate and quietly re-endows that surrogate with exactly the absoluteness it was supposed to have surrendered — a contingent, finite, not-always-lovable human being asked to bear a weight that only God could carry. I want to know whether that is a genuine dilemma for Eliot's position or something I have manufactured by over-formalizing what is really just a habit of feeling that never needed an object in the first place.

The text I most want read through it is *Silas Marner*, and the details I keep coming back to are these. Silas's loss of faith at Lantern Yard is not a theodicy crisis at all: nobody suffers, what destroys him is that the chapel's practice of drawing lots, treated as a way of letting God decide, returns a verdict he knows to be false, and it is that procedural failure rather than evil in the world that empties his faith. Once at Raveloe he fills the slot with gold and adores it, which looks like a deliberate picture of the slot occupied by the wrong thing. Then the plot itself performs the substitution: on the night the gold is stolen, Molly Farren dies in the snow on her way to the Casses' New Year party, her toddler wanders in through Silas's open door, and Silas, in one of his trances, reaches for what he takes to be his returned gold on the hearth and finds a child instead — around whom a whole life then grows. Two things strike me about how the novel gets there. First, the machinery is unmistakably providential: the theft that empties the slot and the child who fills it arrive the same night, the stone pit is drained years later to give up Dunstan's skeleton with the gold and restore Silas's name, and the whole plot runs on the kind of timing a believer would call Providence, in a book whose whole premise is that Providence is not available. Second, Eppie is an extremely favourable sample: the object of the religion of humanity is here a golden child, while Godfrey Cass's cowardice, Dunstan and Molly are what human objects look like when they fail. So: is the providential plotting irony, a nostalgic leftover, or evidence that a religion of humanity needs the shape of Providence while denying its author? And does the happy case do any argumentative work, or does the novel merely display one fortunate attachment and hope nobody asks about the others? I would also like your reading of Eppie's refusal at the end — she declines Godfrey and Nancy's offer, the adoption and the inheritance that go with it, choosing Silas and Aaron Winthrop — since the renunciation there has no religious rationale left whatever: is that the old structure surviving on a new object, or something different in kind?

I want the frame tested against *Middlemarch* too, in one specific respect. Dorothea Brooke has reverence in abundance and doctrine only loosely attached to it, and the novel's one scholar of comparative religion, Casaubon with his never-finished Key to All Mythologies, is a withered pedant working in the very tradition of German biblical and mythological criticism that Eliot herself had worked in, and is specifically represented as having failed to keep up with current German scholarship. Eliot thus makes the demythologizer a cautionary figure inside her fiction while having been one in her own early career, and I cannot tell whether that is self-knowledge or self-protection. Then there is the ending and the complaint students always make, that Dorothea's life comes to less than the novel seemed to promise her, which on my frame is exactly the right complaint — the freestanding structure quietly failing to fill — except that I suspect the novel already knows this and is asking whether it is nevertheless enough. So what I want from you is a reading of both novels through the freestanding-reverence frame, with an honest account of where it illuminates and where the novels resist being read that way. Where the dilemma is anachronistic or a category mistake — a much later suspicion about disguised surrogates projected back onto a Victorian who thought she was honestly preserving an inheritance — say so plainly. Point me to the three or four scenes you would put in front of a seminar, and finish by telling me what, if anything, the novels add to the philosophical question of religion without God that the arguments on that subject do not.
