I'm putting together the section on Sen's impossibility result for a graduate seminar handout on social choice and rights, and I keep getting the same objection from students: the original illustration — two readers and a certain novel — only works, they say, because both parties have prurient, meddlesome preferences about what the other person does in private, and no liberal principle worth defending commits us to honouring preferences like that. A colleague of mine thinks the objection is fatal and that the result is an artefact of prudish busybodying; I want to know whether the conflict survives once the preference profile is scrubbed clean of anything of that kind. So I need a case built to order.

The case: two people (three at most, and only if you can make the third person's sphere load-bearing — otherwise keep it to two), each with a recognised personal sphere, and a small menu of social alternatives, around four, that differ only in what each person does within that sphere. Assume the standard principles: minimal liberalism, i.e. each person settles the social ranking on at least one pair of alternatives differing only in what they themselves do; and the Pareto principle, i.e. if everyone strictly prefers one alternative to another, then so does society. Together they must generate a cycle — one leg from each person's decisiveness and one from Pareto, or some other arrangement if you prefer, provided every leg is forced by one of the two principles rather than asserted. Constraints: (i) realistic and ordinary — neighbours, flatmates, colleagues, a clinic, a village — with no fictional technology and no contrived stipulation doing any work; (ii) all rankings strict, so the Pareto leg is unambiguous, and the profile itself should be one an economist would call mundane — ordinary self-interest plus the material consequences of other people's conduct — and certainly one that unrestricted domain admits; (iii) nobody may rank two alternatives differently because they approve or disapprove of another person's tastes, character, lifestyle, or morals — wherever a person's ranking discriminates between alternatives that differ only in what someone else does, it must be because that other person's conduct makes a real material difference to what happens to them or to an outcome they share; (iv) each decisiveness pair must differ only in that person's own sphere, and you must argue that the sphere is one a liberal would actually recognise rather than stipulating it; (v) nothing may be stipulated about the social ranking — I want to recompute every social-preference judgment myself from your stated rankings and the two principles.

What I want on the page: first the scenario in a short paragraph, with a preference table giving each person's strict ranking over all the alternatives and, where it helps, the rough payoff structure behind those rankings; then the leg-by-leg derivation, naming which principle forces each judgment and checking the pair-difference condition for each; then the diagnosis, stating which single feature of the case does the work and what happens to the cycle if that feature is removed — in particular, if you delete every preference that ranges across another person's sphere, does the conflict survive?; then the strongest attack a defender of Paretian liberalism can mount, working through the escape routes I can think of — refusing to count cross-sphere preferences at all when assigning rights, denying that the sphere you picked is genuinely personal, observing that real people in that situation would sign a binding agreement or delegate the decision, or giving up a complete and transitive social ordering for something weaker — and saying which is least damaging and what it costs; and finally, in two or three sentences, whether a case like this shows the liberal paradox to be a distinct problem about rights or a familiar problem wearing a different hat, and what I should honestly tell my students.
