I sit on the technical staff of a public body that has to recommend a statutory emissions target running to the end of the century. A colleague in the economics unit sent me the argument below. He offers it as a reason for treating all benefits accruing more than about eighty years out as morally inert — not merely discounted to a small number, but excluded from the deliberation altogether. I think the conclusion is false, and I think it is false for a reason my colleague has not noticed; yet when I go through the steps one at a time I cannot find a place where he cheats. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

(1) A policy can be criticised on account of what it does to a future person only if that policy makes that person worse off — worse off, that is, than they would have been had we chosen differently.

(2) Any policy that changes energy prices, infrastructure, migration, or the pattern of consumption over decades also changes who meets whom, who moves where, and when children are conceived. So every policy actually on the table changes the identity of at least some of the people who will be alive in a hundred years: those particular individuals would not have existed at all under the alternative.

(3) If a person owes their existence to the policy we choose, then there is no alternative course of events in which that same person fares better. So, by (1), that person is not made worse off by our choice, however hard their life turns out to be.

(4) Provided the lives that result are lives worth living, (1) to (3) exhaust every way in which a policy could be bad for the people who live under it.

(5) A choice that is bad for no one, that breaks no promise, and that violates no one's rights is not morally objectionable.

(6) Therefore no policy we can now choose is morally objectionable on account of what it does to the people alive a century from now. It can be objectionable only on account of what it does to people who already exist, or who are already conceived.

What makes me think something has gone wrong is that the argument seems to prove much more than my colleague needs, and more than he himself accepts.

(i) It generalises backwards. The large historical injustices — the slave trade, say, or the dispossession of a people and the forced removal of their children — determined who later met whom and who was born. If (1) to (5) are sound, then no living descendant of those events was made worse off by them, and so on this argument there is nobody left to whom anything is owed by way of repair.

(ii) It generalises downwards. A firm that knowingly exposes its workers to a toxin that will cause serious but non-fatal disability in the children those workers go on to conceive has, on this argument, wronged no one, provided those children's lives are worth living and no other children would have been born in their place. My colleague concedes this and says he accepts it. I do not see how a view that accepts it can then bear the weight of a target on which a great deal turns.

(iii) Everything is left resting on the proviso in (4). If the only thing that can go wrong a century out is that lives stop being worth living, then a policy that yields lives barely above that line is unobjectionable and a policy that yields lives barely below it is a catastrophe. My job is to recommend a number, and I do not see how to choose between two numbers whose moral difference is supposed to be a cliff at a place I cannot locate.

(iv) It turns the whole question over to something nobody can know. We cannot now pick out who will exist in a century under each policy, so if the argument is right the permissibility of a target depends on a fact about conception dates that no one will ever be able to verify. My colleague says that is simply what the argument implies. It seems to me more like a sign that a subject has been lost rather than found.

I have tried two repairs and trust neither.

First repair: keep (1) but read worse off non-comparatively — a person is made worse off when their life falls below the level of a decent human life, regardless of what the alternatives were. This blocks (3) and restores a verdict about the distant future. But it seems to imply that a life below the line is a harm to its subject even when the only alternative was never existing, which is a version of the claim that existence itself can harm the one who exists; and the line looks conventional, so my target would be set by a convention rather than by the welfare of anyone.

Second repair: keep the requirement that the comparison be made in terms of persons, but drop the requirement that it be made in terms of the same persons. Since we cannot know who will exist, compare policies by the expected welfare of a randomly drawn member of the population each would produce. This is personal in flavour without being impersonal in substance — or so I hope. But a randomly drawn member is not anyone, and I cannot shake the sense that I have smuggled an aggregate back in under a personal description.

So: which step do you reject? If you were writing the memo, where would you say the argument breaks, and what exactly would you have to accept in order to say that? Is the argument valid with a narrower conclusion than (6), and would that narrower conclusion still do any work for someone who has to recommend a number?

Two preferences, in case they help, though I would rather be right than keep either. I want to keep (2) if I can: the fragility of who comes into existence strikes me as a sociological fact rather than a philosophical thesis, and a reply that simply denies it will not survive contact with demography. And I would prefer not to give up (5): abandoning it seems to hand the whole question to an aggregative calculus that I do not accept and that my colleague would reject even more firmly than I do.

Please work the argument itself rather than pointing me at the literature — I have no library access from here, and a list of names would not settle the memo.
