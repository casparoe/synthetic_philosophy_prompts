My friend and I spent about three hours last night arguing about whether culture can be a cause and we got absolutely nowhere, which is annoying because we agree on most of the history. His position: the Weber thing about protestantism and the spirit of capitalism is basically dead, because (a) the quantitative literature reran it and found the effect actually runs through literacy and human capital rather than anything like a work ethic, so at best its a mechanism mix-up, and (b) "culture" is just the word you use for whatever your institutional explanation can't explain, i.e. a residual with a story attached. My position was that (a) looks like a specification of his mechanism rather than a refutation, and (b) cuts both ways, since "institutions" is also a bucket for whatever your culture explanation can't explain. Neither of us moved.

It's obviously not a new argument — people have been at it since Weber's book, and it keeps resurfacing in new dress every couple of decades, and I'm trying to work out why it never closes. My working theory is that it isn't really one debate: I think there are at least three things tangled together, and nobody says which one they're doing. There's a verbal bit, since "culture" and "cause" and "value" shift meaning mid-sentence constantly. There's a different-questions bit, where one side wants an explanation of what an action meant to the person doing it and the other wants counterfactual dependence between two population-level variables, and those have different success conditions. And then there's what I'd actually bet on, which is a measurement problem wearing a theory costume: almost every test I've seen only has denomination or region, not what anyone believed or how intensely they practiced, so every "test of culture" is really a joint test of a bad proxy and a theory, and the two failure modes are indistinguishable in the data.

So: diagnose it. Which of those is doing the real work, is there a fourth thing I'm missing, and where does each side's actual argument commit them? I've been kicking around two things I half-invented to try to break the tie — the carry-on test, where the effect should travel with the belief when people migrate rather than with the birthplace or the denominational category, and the blocked-expression test, where the effect should shrink in settings where institutions prevent anyone accumulating anything and reappear where they don't. Do those actually discriminate between a values-cause and a category-marker-cause, or do they collapse back into the same joint test? And be honest about whether anything — a dataset, a natural experiment, a conceptual distinction, a change of question — would actually end this, or whether "can culture be a cause" is just badly posed and I should dissolve it and go home.

Write it as continuous prose please, like an email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
