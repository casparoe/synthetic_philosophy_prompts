I'm writing the position paper for my third-year seminar on the ethics of belief — we've worked through Clifford's "The Ethics of Belief," James's "The Will to Believe," and a few contemporary evidentialist and pragmatist responses — and before I hand in something I'll have to defend in discussion, I'd like an audit of whether the package I've assembled is actually stable. I got there by taking what I found convincing in each reading, and I have a standing worry that some of it is held together by luck. The set:

(1) Evidentialism about epistemic justification: whether my belief that p is epistemically justified is fixed entirely by my evidence. How much I want p to be true, or what believing p would cost me, never bears on justification.

(2) No direct doxastic voluntarism. I can't believe at will in the way I can raise my arm at will. What I control is my inquiry, my attention, who I spend time with, and what I do.

(3) A Cliffordian prohibition, narrower than Clifford's own. Believing on insufficient evidence is pro tanto wrong when the belief concerns other people's conduct or character, or the public and historical record. I reject the fully general version — that it is always, everywhere and for anyone wrong to believe anything on insufficient evidence — as forbidding far too much. But what I find compelling in Clifford is the argument rather than the verdict: the thought that no belief is a private matter, that a carelessly formed belief enters the world as testimony and as precedent, and that the habit of forming them that way spreads.

(4) A Jamesian permission for what I'll call bootstrapping options: forced, living and momentous in his sense, where the evidence is by stipulation insufficient, and where the truth of the proposition is partly downstream of one's own confidence in it, because trusting, committing or expecting is what elicits the cooperative response. Here passional factors may permissibly decide. I want to ground this in the mechanism — in the fact that here believing helps make it so — not in any general right to believe what helps.

(5) Uniformity: no special epistemology for religion. The right treatment of religious commitment has to fall out of (1), (3) and (4) applied to its actual features, not out of a sui generis category.

(6) What I'll call the scaffolding constraint: it is objectionable to acquire or sustain a belief by arranging one's information and one's interlocutors — surrounding oneself with people who already believe it, avoiding the strongest statements of the other side, rehearsing the considerations that favour it. I take this to be the mechanism behind most motivated reasoning, and to be what makes a belief epistemically disreputable even when it happens to be true.

(7) Verdicts. (a) Permissible: the graduate student who, on thin evidence, believes her senior colleague is on her side, where that confidence changes how she approaches him and makes his support likelier. (b) Permissible: the organisers of a first union local who believe, on thin evidence, that they will win recognition, where the belief is part of why people keep turning up. (c) Impermissible: the research scientist who, on thin evidence, talks herself into certainty that her long-shot hypothesis is correct, because the certainty is what will carry her through a decade of work on it. (d) Impermissible: campaign volunteers who let themselves become certain their side will win, granting that the belief raises turnout and that turnout partly decides the outcome — and stipulating that they have engaged in no scaffolding at all, that they have read the same polling as everyone else and have simply allowed themselves to be sure.

My own guesses about where the strain is: I suspect (2) and (4) cannot both be true; I suspect (1) leaves no room for the mechanism in (4) to matter at all; and I genuinely cannot tell whether (7d) is something my principles should permit or a counterexample to them. I am not confident about any of these.

So please audit the set rather than summarise the literature, and don't give me a reading list. Which pairs are actually in tension, ordered by severity; for each, what exactly would have to give, and what that costs me elsewhere in the set — not just "drop (4)," but what (4) was doing for me and what I would lose. Flag the items that only look like tensions and say why they dissolve. If one revision would fix the most at the least cost, name it, but set out the alternatives too rather than assuming which one I would take.
