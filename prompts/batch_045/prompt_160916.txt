I spent a career in sensory neurophysiology — visual psychophysics and single-unit recording, mostly — and in retirement I have been reading my way through philosophy of mind with what I take to be a well-equipped but stubbornly empirical mind. The proposal that keeps drawing me back, and frustrating me, is Dennett's heterophenomenology: collect the subject's utterances as texts, reconstruct from them the world as the subject takes it to be, and discharge the scientific obligation by explaining how those texts come to be produced, all without conceding that the subject's reports are authoritative about what is really going on in them. I am attracted to it because it looks like the only version of a science of consciousness I could actually have run in my own laboratory. I also think it has a hole in it, and I would like you to argue for that hole as carefully as you can.

Here is the hole as I see it. In my old field we never interpreted a stream of reports without a control on the reporting channel itself. Signal detection theory existed precisely because a subject's yes-no stream is a joint function of sensitivity to the stimulus and the subject's response criterion, and because the only way to pry the two apart is to include occasions on which the experimenter, and not the subject, knows what was presented: catch trials. Those give you an independent handle on the channel, and with it a decomposition of what changed when the stream changed.

My complaint is that heterophenomenology has no analogue of the catch trial, and cannot have one by its own rules. Its data are utterances; its official subject matter is the heterophenomenological world rather than whatever is really happening in the subject; and its explanatory task is to account for the production of the texts. But the texts are a function of at least two things — the subject's internal condition, and the subject's disposition to report. When a corpus changes, whether across subjects or within one subject over time, nothing in the method licenses attributing the change to one rather than the other, because the method has, on principle, no independently specified stimulus with which to calibrate the reporter. Call this the no-catch-trials problem.

What I want from you is an argument for the position that this is not a benign instance of ordinary underdetermination but a structural defect: that heterophenomenology, as stated, cannot discriminate a change in what a subject is like from a change in how that subject reports, that every working application of it (to pre- and post-lesion patients, to children versus adults, to animals, to the reports people produce about their own imagery only once a vocabulary for its absence is available, to subjects before and after a psychedelic or a course of meditation training) quietly borrows a theory of the reporting channel from somewhere else, and that the only place it could get such a theory is from the investigator's own case — which is first-person authority of exactly the sort the method was built to disown. I would also like you to consider, and either defeat or concede, the reply I expect from a defender: that the objection asks the method for a verdict about real experience it explicitly refuses to give, that it therefore misfires, and that the underdetermination you are pointing at is simply the familiar theory-ladenness of any data.

Please keep the argument disciplined about the mapping: say precisely what counts as the data, what the analogue of the stimulus is supposed to be here, and what the method is and is not entitled to infer. Distinguish the no-catch-trials problem from the neighbouring complaints it resembles — the hard problem, worries about introspective unreliability, the question of which reports to take seriously — so that I can tell whether you have handed me something new or a familiar objection relabelled.

Two further requests. First, a matter of intellectual hygiene that I care about as a newcomer to the field: separate, as you go, what is settled among people who actually work on these questions from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested claim as the consensus. Where your argument turns on a premise that specialists themselves dispute, say so and say what happens to the argument if that premise is given up. Where you are yourself unsure, mark the confidence rather than smoothing it over. Second, please do not give me citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I have no interest in chasing references, and I want the reasoning, not the apparatus. Around twelve hundred words, in prose a working scientist can follow.
