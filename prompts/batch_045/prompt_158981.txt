The funders aren't philosophers; what they actually want to know is whether policy work (cost-benefit analysis, welfare accounting) is entitled to use utility numbers at all. So I owe them three or four questions worth those nine person-years, ranked.

Can you give me that shortlist with the reasoning attached? I care much less about the list than about what puts something on it: what stays stuck until a given question is anwsered, what a philosopher with three years and no data can realistically settle, and what would move something up or down.

Two candidates I keep circling, in case they're the real cruxes or in case they're rabbit holes. One: more and more of the choice data we'd actually have is produced by systems choosing on someone's behalf — defaults, recommenders, assistant-style agents acting for a principal — and it isn't clear to me whose utility a revealed-preference argument is recovering there, or whether what's recovered is an objective function rather than a preference at all. Two: when the lotteries aren't handed to us, choice seems to pin down beleif and utility only jointly, and I want to know how badly that undercuts cardinality and interpersonal comparison.

Also, please mark which bits are settled results — the representation theorems, the identification limits — and which are live disputes among people who work on this. I don't want to hand in a contested claim wearing a consensus costume.
