I'm on the rotating duty-editor shift at a small public-health evidence service — twice a week, and three or four times a shift I have to write the one-line "bottom line" that sits on top of an evidence summary, in about ten minutes, and a second person has to sign it off before it goes up. The catch is that the same line is read by members of the public, by clinicians deciding what to do that afternoon, and gets lifted verbatim by reporters, so I can't tune how strong it is to one audience.

Can you write me a procedure for deciding when it's OK to come out and state a claim, when to hedge it, and when to say nothing? Ground it in whatever philosophy of language actually says about the norms of assertion — knowledge vs. truth vs. reasonable belief vs. whatever else is on the table — but in a form someone with no philosophy and ten minutes can run. So: the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure just returns "don't assert", and where it has to hand the decision to a person because it's a values call and not an epistemic one. Two wrinkles I'd like it to actually handle: (1) I'm usually speaking for the service, and I personally have not read the studies — so who is asserting, and whose epistemic position has to be good enough? (2) sentences that are true and that I do know, but which reporters will read as saying something stronger.

Then run it on three: an n=200 preprint finding that a common supplement cuts hospitalisation; the same-day question "is this new variant spreading through the air?" where all we have is one small observational study; and a line about a booster rollout expanding next week that I know from an embargoed government briefing but can't give a reason for. Don't soften the second one — I have a feeling the procedure is going to tell me to shut up, and I want to know whether that really follows.

Finally, say which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and where the whole thing will misfire.
