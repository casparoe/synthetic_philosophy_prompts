ive been chewing on this for like a week and I think ive talked myself into a corner, so:

Setup as I understand it, in case Im getting it wrong. In the "program game" version of a one-shot prisoners dilemma, you dont pick C or D directly, you each submit a program that gets the other persons source code as input and outputs C or D. The famous move is roughly "cooperate iff the opponents source is byte-for-byte identical to mine" (you quine to get your own source), and the claim is that this pair is a Nash equilibrium with mutual cooperation even though the underlying game is a one-shot PD, because if you deviate to anything else the other copy defects on you and you drop to the mutual-defection payoff.

Heres whats bugging me.

(1) The programs are just functions from source code to action. Two opponent programs that output the same action against me are payoff-identical to me. So the only thing that can rationally matter about the opponents source is its behavior, not its syntax. Comments, variable names, dead branches — irrelevant by construction.

(2) So the principled version of "cooperate with your twin" isnt the byte-identical one, its: cooperate iff the opponents program is behaviorally equivalent to mine, i.e. would output the same thing as me on every input. Call it Semantico.

(3) Semantico seems strictly better than the syntactic version. Its unexploitable — a behavioral twin that defected against Semantico would be defecting against something its behaviorally identical to, so it wouldnt be a twin — and it cooperates with any of its twins, not just verbatim copies of itself.

(4) And the mutual cooperation is stable for the usual reason: deviate from Semantico to anything else and your twin defects on you, so you get the mutual-defection payoff, which is worse.

(5) But Semantico cant exist. "Is behaviorally equivalent to Semantico" is a nontrivial semantic property of a program, and Rice's theorem says no program can decide any nontrivial semantic property. So no computable player-program can implement it.

(6) So the only implementable version of the idea is the syntactic one — which means whether you cooperate or defect turns on whitespace and variable naming. Which means the whole program-equilibrium thing never gave a principled account of when cooperation is rational; it just relocated the coordination problem onto syntax, and the "solution" to the PD is a coincidence of how you happened to format your code.

(7) And it generalizes: if I cant settle behavioral equivalence for a program whose source I literally have in my hands, I obviously cant settle it for another human from the outside. So no amount of modeling each other can ever rationally ground one-shot cooperation, and everyone who says otherwise is smuggling something in.

I dont believe (7) — partly because people plainly do cooperate in one-shot-ish situations, and partly because I have a foggy memory of bots that cooperate with themselves and with edited versions of themselves via proof search and Lob's theorem, which sounds like it would be computable. But I cant reconstruct it and im not sure im remembering right.

So: which step above do you reject, why does it look fine right up until it doesnt, and what does rejecting it cost me? If the answer is "you have to give up (3)", I want to know exactly what I lose — do I keep cooperation with myself and lose it with twins I cant verify, or does something worse happen? And does whatever fixes this still cash out the thing people want program equilibrium to cash out about decision theory, or is that part just dead?
