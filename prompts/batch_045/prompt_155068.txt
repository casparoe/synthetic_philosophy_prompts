hey — I could use a structural diagnosis here rather than a verdict. I'm a PhD student in philosophy of biology (working mostly on collective agency and individuality) and our reading group has been going round in circles for about a year on whether an ant colony can have a mind — we started from Hofstadter's Aunt Hillary, the ant colony he has conversations with in the Ant Fugue chapter, and then drifted into the empirical literature. Two people in the group, Wes and Lena, have basically staked out the two camps and neither has moved. Here's roughly how it goes:

Wes: the colony is the right unit of description. Individual workers only ever sample tiny local fragments, but the colony-level state — trail chemistry, patroller distribution, the rate at which foragers bump into each other, how much of the midden is being worked — reliably covaries with colony-scale features of the world (whether that cavity is the right size, whether the neighbours are pushing into the foraging area), and the colony's behaviour is steered by that. No single ant knows any of it. If that isn't representation, nothing in a brain is either, since a brain is also just cells shoving chemicals at each other.

Lena: brains have an architecture colonies don't — a place where information from any channel is available to any other and to action selection, rapidly and globally. In a colony the 'state' is smeared across ten thousand animals and no part ever accesses the whole; coordination falls out of local rules. Give me one variable the colony reads and writes as a whole. Her favourite analogy: the market tracks the economy, steers investment, and even has a neat aggregating variable — price — and nobody thinks the market believes anything.

Wes: (a) you can't demand a fused centralised architecture or you've excluded Aunt Hillary by definition; (b) colonies do have bottlenecks — nest relocation decisions funnel through encounter rates on a handful of trails, and the response to a perturbation passes through narrow channels; (c) integration is a scalar, so asking for the threshold isn't a test, it's a veto.

Lena: bottlenecks are cheap — a price is a bottleneck, a thermostat's set point is a bottleneck — so bottleneck-hood can't be what separates a mind from a market, and neither can self-maintenance, since markets have feedback loops and homeostatic targets too. What's missing isn't integration, it's a subject: a state whose being wrong matters to something, a perspective.

Wes (his strongest move, I think): colonies are units of selection; colony-level traits got selected because of colony-level outcomes. So a colony-level state can have the proper function of tracking a colony-level feature, and can therefore misfire — misrepresentation without anyone's feelings. That's content, and content is the core of cognition.

Lena: content on that criterion is everywhere — chemotaxis, stomata, the lac operon. So either you hand out 'beliefs' all the way down (and then the colony question was trivial all along), or you've quietly switched from cognition to consciousness and are coasting on colonies feeling more mind-like than bacteria. Plus the which-thing problem: workers jointly, the queen, or a nested crowd of subjects — nothing in the empirical story chooses.

We also tried three cases and none of them helped. (1) The relay: split a colony in two halves with a barrier, but run a robotic pheromone relay between them, sampling traffic on each side and reproducing it on the other, so the halves keep behaving as one colony — joint foraging, joint relocation. Then switch the relay off. Does one Aunt Hillary become two? Were there two all along? Wes says fission, and reminds us that split-brain cases don't show brains lack minds. Lena says the point is what we couldn't tell beforehand: with the relay running, how many colonies are there wasn't settled by the dynamics at all, so 'does the colony have a mind' inherits an indeterminacy that no amount of ant neuroscience will fix. (2) The swap: slave-making ants already run mixed-species colonies, so imagine replacing workers one at a time with enslaved heterospecifics while holding the colony-level dynamics fixed. Wes says Aunt Hillary survives total turnover of her parts. Lena says then the mind just is the dynamics, and dynamics are substrate-neutral, so a simulation has the same claim — either accept that, or say what the substrate-y ingredient is. (3) The supercolony: two colonies chemically coupled into one cooperative unit, Argentine-ant style, with genuinely interdependent dynamics. Is there now a further mind over and above the two? Yes gives you nested minds all the way up an introduced range; no needs a principle for where the nesting stops, and integration only gives graded verdicts that don't cut anywhere.

My own hunch, offered as a hunch: this is a proxy war about individuality, not mentality. The mind-talk is a bet about whether 'the colony' is one object with a boundary at all. I proposed settling that operationally — measure whether the colony has a Markov blanket, a real integration profile, a causal bottleneck — treat that as the test for colony-hood, and then see whether the mind question has any content left over. Wes liked it. Lena said a thermostat has a Markov blanket too, so this just moves the bump in the carpet.

So: what kind of stalemate is this actually? Verbal (about 'mind', 'representation', 'individual')? A hidden empirical question about integration or about what selection acts on? A difference about which cases get to count as data? Bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for? Two or three questions (cognition / consciousness / composition) run together under one heading? Or ill-posed? I want the diagnosis built out of what each side actually says above, not a survey of positions on collective mentality, and I'd like you to say what each side would have to give up if they're wrong, and whether anything — a measure, a distinction, an experiment, a reframing — would count as progress, or whether we should expect none and why.

Format: prose for the diagnosis, then one sentence beginning "Progress would be:" and, as the very last line, a machine-readable tag choosing one label even if it's imperfect: \answer{verbal}, \answer{hidden-empirical}, \answer{different-questions}, \answer{bedrock-intuition}, or \answer{ill-posed}.
