I'm writing a long-read on what makes it into the historical record and what doesn't: whose lives get filed, and what it means when a person's entire presence in an archive consists of a single tax-default notice, or of nothing at all. What I need before I start drafting is a specification rather than a tour of the literature.

Please write the desiderata that a method for reasoning from archival silence would have to meet to be fit for use in journalism — good enough, that is, that a reporter can make and defend a claim in print about what a particular gap does and does not show. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it. Then sort the list: which requirements should I treat as non-negotiable, and which are really commitments of one tradition that I am free to reject — for example the demand that a method presuppose the archive to be an instrument of state power, versus the rival demand that it stay agnostic about what the producers of the record were trying to do.

I want the list tested against at least these cases:

- Four different ways a thing can be missing: never produced, because no one wrote it down or no one was in a position to; produced and then destroyed, whether by weeding, appraisal, war, or a retention schedule; produced and surviving but unreachable, through sealing, classification, redaction or cost; and produced, surviving and available, but illegible as evidence — a person who appears only as a taxable hearth, a dependant, a defendant, an unnamed female child, or a number in the ledger of the institution that confined them. Say whether a usable method must keep these apart, or whether collapsing some of them is harmless.
- The discipline for inferring from absence: when a gap supports a claim, what has to be true about how the record was made — the watchdog-that-did-not-bark pattern and the ways it fails.
- Silence in born-digital collections, where absence is generated by whatever gets indexed, crawled, de-duplicated or auto-deleted, and by the categories a filing system imposes rather than by any decision about this record. A method that only works on paper-era archives is no use to me.
- The opposite failure: subjects who are over-documented — surveillance files, police records, the paperwork of confinement. A method that tells me only how to read absence and not how to read glut is incomplete for my purposes.
- The guard against romanticising: I do not want to slide into reading every silence as resistance, or as the trace of a voice that was suppressed. The method should let me tell suppression from indifference from accident, and say what I owe the people in the gap either way.
- What counts as repair: counter-archives, oral testimony, reparative description, re-indexing — and how much weight each can bear.

Finally: which single requirement knocks out the largest share of candidate methods, and which pair of requirements do you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. I have a guess — that a method which lets a silence speak for itself is in tension with one that never over-reads a gap — but I would like your view on where the real trade-off lies.

Please answer in British English, and do not cite anything or suggest further reading: I cannot check references, and guesses at them are worse than none.
