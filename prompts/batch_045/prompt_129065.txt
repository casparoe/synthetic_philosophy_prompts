I work in formal methods — I build and use proof assistants — and I have been trying to connect what I do to Benacerraf's dilemma about mathematical truth and mathematical knowledge. I would be grateful for your assessment of a way out that has occurred to me: specifically, whether it is a genuine third option or only one of the horns of the dilemma in disguise, and, if the latter, what the strongest version of that horn would have to deliver in order to be credible.

My understanding of the problem is this. Our semantics for mathematical sentences looks continuous with our semantics for the rest of language: numerals behave as singular terms, number-words and quantifiers range over a domain of objects, and truth conditions are given objectually. That is what makes "there are two primes between 10 and 20" true in the same way that "there are two apples on the table" is true. But our account of knowledge is causal: to know that p one must stand in the right causal or information-bearing relation to the objects that p is about. Numbers, if they are abstract, are causally inert. So the semantic picture appears to rule out the epistemology, and any revision that restores the epistemology — by making the objects perceivable, or by paraphrasing them away — appears to spoil the semantics. Platonism buys truth at the price of knowledge; anti-realism buys knowledge at the price of truth. That is how I have been reading the dialectic.

The proposal I want to put to you, which I have been calling certificationism, is that the contrast is illusory because mathematical knowledge was never knowledge of objects. When I accept a theorem, what I am in causal contact with is a formal derivation: a finite inscription, or, in the case that interests me, a finite object that a checking kernel on my machine inspects token by token and certifies. Nothing abstract is touched. Identify the truth of the theorem with the existence of a certified derivation of it, treat the apparent reference to numbers as a façon de parler that does no semantic work, and the causal constraint is satisfied exactly where my belief is actually grounded — in the derivation and in the machine's certification of it. Nothing that working mathematicians or engineers rely on seems to be lost: the theorems are all still there, the certificates are public and independently re-checkable, and applications go through because what gets applied is the derivation rather than a relation to an object. Proof assistants make the point vivid: a kernel that accepts a term consults no abstract object, and certification is the strongest warrant for a mathematical claim that we have.

I have two reservations, and I would like your help in weighing them.

The first concerns the soundness theorem. My warrant for believing what the kernel tells me is a proof that, for every derivation d, if the kernel certifies d then the formula at its root is true. That proof is itself mathematics, and not the sort of thing the kernel will hand me; it quantifies over all possible derivations, an infinite domain, and it is precisely the kind of claim I was supposed to be worried about. So the epistemological difficulty appears to have reappeared one level up, and I cannot tell whether that is a fatal regress or only the banal observation that no one justifies everything at once.

The second is that colleagues who know the literature better than I do say that what I have described is close to if-thenism or deductivism, and that the standing objection to those views is that they make all consistent mathematics equally acceptable: if the axioms are never asserted but only hypothesized, there is nothing to choose between number theory and a rival theory with the consistency of number theory and the content of nonsense. I do not see why that objection should stick to me, since my derivations are concrete objects and my truths are checkable facts about them rather than consequences of unasserted hypotheses. But I may be missing why the concrete/abstract distinction is supposed to carry the weight I am putting on it.

So: is the certificationist picture a dissolution of the dilemma, or does it amount to taking the anti-realist horn while relabelling the taking? If the latter, what would the view owe us — about applied or mixed statements, about the apparent determinacy of claims we cannot prove, about soundness and consistency — in order to be the best available version of that horn? And is the regress in my first reservation a real difficulty for the view, or only a confusion on my part about what a causal or reliability constraint on knowledge requires?
