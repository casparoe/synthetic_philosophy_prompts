I have been trying to restate in my own vocabulary the Epicurean case for thinking that a person's own death is not bad for that person, and I have produced a version that I cannot fault step by step but whose conclusion I find incredible. I would like a diagnosis.

The argument, with names for the two load-bearing moves:

(1) The Timing Constraint. If something is bad for a subject S, then there is some time at which S exists and at which it is bad for S. Something's being bad for someone requires a someone who is in the bad condition in question, and being in a condition is something that happens at a time.

(2) A human being exists at no time at which she is dead, and at no time before she is conceived.

(3) So being dead is not bad for the person who is dead. The symmetry argument usually traced to Lucretius reinforces this: our prenatal nonexistence was not bad for us either, and the direction of time should not matter to whether a stretch of nonexistence can be bad for someone.

(4) The Bridge. If X is not bad for S, then S has no prudential reason of her own to avoid X. Reasons of self-interest run through how things go for the person whose reasons they are; if an outcome costs me nothing, I have nothing, from my own point of view, to avoid in it.

(5) So no one has any prudential reason of her own to avoid dying at one time rather than another. Not the person who dies at twenty, and not even the person who, were she not to die now, would have sixty further years of an outstanding life. In neither case is her death bad for her, by (3), and what is not bad for her gives her nothing of her own to avoid, by (4).

Two consequences that I take to be unacceptable:

(6) Alya is seventy, healthy and contented, with no dependants and no project she minds leaving unfinished. She is offered, without her ever knowing the offer was made, the prevention of a condition that will otherwise painlessly end her life in her sleep at seventy instead of at ninety. Every moment of conscious experience on the two options is as good as the other; she will never miss the twenty years, since there is no one left to miss them; nothing else about her life, or anyone else's, differs. It seems to me that she genuinely loses something and that she has a strong reason of her own to want the prevention. By (5) she has none.

(7) Priya has one governing aim: to found and endow a school in her village, due to open the year after she dies. She works at it for thirty years and dies a week before the opening, at which point, for reasons no one could have foreseen, the school never opens. Given (1) and (2), nothing that happens after Priya's death can be bad for her, and her death itself is not bad for her either. So the failure of her life's central project costs her nothing at all, and was never, from her own point of view, worth an hour of her labour.

My suspicion is that something shifts between (3) and (5): that bad for in (1) through (3) means something like no state of hers at any time is a bad one, while (4) is only plausible if it means her life as a whole goes worse than it would otherwise have gone. But I cannot make that suspicion precise enough to see where the argument actually breaks, or what the repair costs.

So: name the step you would reject; explain why that step looks fine in isolation; and price the alternatives I could have taken instead. In particular I want to know three things. First, if the repair is to read bad for comparatively, what exactly is the comparison to — the life she would in fact have led, the best life then open to her, or something else — and what happens when that counterfactual is indeterminate, as it usually is? Second, is there any stable position that keeps (1), (2) and (3) and gives up only the Bridge, so that death is not bad for me yet I still have a reason of my own to postpone it: if so, what are those reasons reasons of, and does severing prudence from how things go for me leave prudence with any content? Third, a friend tells me the standard reply is something called timeless harm; I cannot tell whether that is a substantive position or a relabelling of the difficulty, so if that is where you would go, say what it commits me to beyond a name.

If it matters, I would rather keep (1) and (2) than (4), but I would also rather know whether that preference is itself the mistake.
