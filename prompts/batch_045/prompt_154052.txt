I am a retired physical oceanographer who has lately been sitting in on seminars in history and philosophy of science, and I have agreed to give a five-minute after-dinner toast at a small interdisciplinary meeting on deep time and historical explanation — mostly earth scientists, a few philosophers, a scattering of historians. I would like one joke about the longue durée.

The diagnostic I have been playing with, and which I would like the joke's punchline to turn on, is this: take any historical explanation, strike out every proper name of an individual and put 'someone' in its place, and replace every precise date with 'eventually.' If the explanation survives with none of its force lost, you were describing a structure of geographical time — a coastline, a wind system, a pattern of landholding, a route. If it survives but goes vague and hand-wavy, you were describing a conjoncture of a few decades. If it collapses into noise, you were writing the history of events. Call it the granite test.

So: a joke, tellable in well under a minute, whose humour depends on that three-way difference actually doing work rather than on deep time being long or historians being slow. Ideally the joke should land on a case where the granite test returns an answer that is correct and slightly humbling — something drawn from my own discipline, or from the conference itself, or from what an audience of this sort quietly assumes it is up to. It should also be intelligible to the philosophers present, several of whom will know Braudel only by reputation, so the setup has to carry the distinction on its own.
