I am a novelist, and I have written myself into a difficulty that more reading of Machiavelli has not solved. The premise of my novel is this: the Niccolò Machiavelli who was driven out of Florence in 1512 and spent his last productive years writing *The Prince* and the *Discourses on Livy* is brought forward into the present and retained under an assumed name — first as an advisor to a head of government, then, after a breach with her, as a consultant to the board of the firm that sells her government its behavioural-prediction infrastructure. The set pieces of the book are the memoranda he writes in each role. I want them defensible: not the cartoon realist of the maxim usually pinned on him, which I take to be a later paraphrase of his thought rather than any sentence he wrote, but a man reasoning in the vocabulary he actually had — fortuna set against virtù, the flood and the embankments built while the weather is calm, impetuousness weighed against caution, the ruin that follows from having arms in other men's hands, and the decay of a polity that is never dragged back toward its beginnings.

What I cannot settle is how much of that vocabulary survives four features of my characters' world, and I would rather have four concrete predictions than an essay on his thought.

The device I have been using to structure the scenes is what I call the responsiveness gap: the interval between a change in a prince's circumstances and the moment at which he is able to act on it. In his world that gap was fixed by the speed of a horse, a letter, a muster. In my characters' world it has been squeezed toward zero at some margins and stretched at others. My hypothesis — which I would like tested, not flattered — is that this single variable does most of the work in deciding whether the fortuna/virtù pairing still carves at the joints, and that where the gap collapses he would relocate virtù from seizing the occasion to pre-commitment: building the embankment rather than riding out the flood.

The four cases:

1. Latency. The firm's systems reprice and reallocate in microseconds; the government's political and security machinery reacts in hours; the woman at the top cannot respond at the tempo of the events that decide whether she keeps office. Does he treat this as an enlargement of fortuna's half of the ledger, or as a reason to say that virtù has changed its object — from the well-timed act to the design of the thing that acts — and would he, as I suspect, locate the princely function in the designer rather than the operator?

2. Reputational half-life. My head of government is polled continuously and every act of hers is on the record within minutes, so the interval over which a staged appearance survives one of her own acts is now very short. His prince is counselled to govern how he appears, and to be able not to keep faith when keeping faith would ruin him. As the gap between the act and the perception of it closes, does appearing collapse into being — so that he tells her to abandon semblance, or to purchase it by other means, through delegation, secrecy, or surrogate actors? Which trade would he call the better one, and on what grounds?

3. Rotation and the extraordinary remedy. Her constitution limits the top office to two short terms and forbids the exceptional, extra-legal act, while a permanent civil service and an arsenal outlast any office-holder. He held that a polity decays unless something periodically returns it toward its beginnings, whether a great example or one man with authority to reorder it. Would he read term limits as a machine for producing corruption, or would he find in them a mechanism for the renewal he wanted? And would he urge my character to take the extraordinary power anyway and break the limit, or to manufacture the shock from inside the rules?

4. Delegated foresight. The government buys its predictions — of unrest, of default, of where the next scandal will break — from the firm, and no minister can say why the model said what it said. He was blunt about mercenaries and auxiliaries and about the ruin of depending on forces you do not command. On his own terms, is dependence on an opaque vendor's model the same vice as hiring mercenaries, or is there a distinction he would draw between delegating force and delegating foresight? And here is the case I find hardest: suppose the predictions are accurate. Does accurate foresight shrink fortuna's share, or only relocate it into the residual that no model prices — which, on his view of sudden floods, is where the ruin comes from anyway?
