I'm a postdoc giving a work-in-progress talk next week in our joint EEB/philosophy seminar and I want the Q&A to go well. My thesis is this. The fight over whether the gene or the group is really the unit of selection has been conducted as if it were an empirical dispute, but the standard multi-level decomposition of selection is exact for any partition of a population you like, so no measurement could settle it: choose your partition, run the bookkeeping, and the numbers come out. What differs between partitions is not predictive content but which interventions the model makes visible. So I want an intervention-relative criterion for when a level of selection is a real thing rather than a convenience: a candidate level counts when the entities at that level are connected to their offspring entities by a transmission bottleneck that resets heritable variance at the lower level — germline sequestration in a sexual life cycle, single-cell or single-founder propagation in a colony transfer, that sort of thing. No bottleneck, and lower-level variance leaks through, leaving you an aggregate with no counterfactual grip; a bottleneck, and you get genuine higher-level adaptation. I'm therefore committed to the consequence that group selection should be easy to get in microbes propagated by single-colony transfer and essentially unavailable in most models of human or other vertebrate groups.
