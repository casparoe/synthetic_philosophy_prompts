I've been turning over trolley cases and double-effect stuff for ages now and I have a pile of verdicts I'm fairly confident about, plus a rough principle I think is doing the work, and I want to know whether the principle survives the verdicts or whether some of the verdicts have to go. Please don't point me at papers or books — I'm somewhere with no library access and I can't chase anything down, so just give me the shape of the thing in your own words.

My rough principle: it's okay to bring about a death you don't need, and not okay to need a death you bring about. The death has to be dead weight as far as the good outcome goes — if the five are saved partly *because* he dies, or because his body happens to be there, I've done something different in kind. Call it the load-bearing test.

Here are the verdicts, roughly in the order I noticed the strain:

1. Standard switch: five on the main line, one on the spur. Fine. His death isn't load-bearing; the five are saved by the trolley not being where they are.
2. Footbridge. Not fine. His body is the brake; the death is doing the work.
3. Loop version: he's already standing on the spur, and if his body didn't stop the trolley it would carry on round to the five. I still say fine, and this is where the load-bearing test starts to strain, because his body *is* load-bearing and yet it feels like case 1, not case 2.
4. Just like case 1, except a second trolley is going to kill the man on the spur about thirty seconds after I'd divert. Fine — and it feels *more* clearly fine than case 1, even though nothing about the means has changed. Which makes me think something like "don't leave him worse off than he was already going to be" is also running in there, doing separate work.
5. Footbridge again, except the fat man is the guy who tied the five down and set the trolley rolling, and he's on the bridge getting away. Pushing him: fine. But then whatever is wrong with case 2 can't be the load-bearing bit, or not only that.
6. Bombing: hitting the munitions plant and foreseeing the workers' deaths, fine; hitting the housing estate to break morale, not fine. Now the wrinkle — the plant only stops producing because the workers die, so their deaths are load-bearing, and I still say fine. Same wobble as case 3.
7. Hospital: heavy painkillers that will shorten a dying patient's life, fine. Lethal injection, not fine (I know plenty of people disagree; that's my verdict). Switching off the ventilator because the treatment has become burdensome, fine. Switching off the ventilator because another patient needs the machine and will live, also fine, but it feels like it needs defending in a way the first doesn't, and I can't say why.
8. Numbers: one to save two, fine. One to save one, not fine — I don't think I'm allowed to flip, and I definately don't think I'm required to. One to save a million, fine, and somewhere up there it stops being merely permitted and becomes something I'd be blamed for not doing. I've no idea where that threshold sits, or whether the load-bearing test has anything to say about numbers at all.
9. Two I go back and forth on. The fat man can't get himself over the railing and asks me to push him — part of me says consent settles it, part of me says I shouldn't put my hands on him either way. And the man on the spur in case 1: if he sees me and shouts not to divert, does that change anything, or is that just noise?

So: is there one principle that gets 1 through 8, and if it's my load-bearing test, how exactly does it get repaired so that 3, 5 and 6 come out where I put them?
