I'm a graduate student in computer science, and this term I'm taking a seminar on AI safety. Most of it has been on *control* rather than *alignment*: approaches that grant from the start that the system may be misaligned and try to get useful work out of it anyway, by restricting what it can do and inspecting what it does. Across the readings I keep running into what looks like a single difficulty wearing different clothes, and I have no word for it. I'd like to know whether there is an established concept for it, and — more than a name — how exactly it should be carved.

The shape I keep seeing is this. A control measure is put in place; it appears to work; and the fact that it appears to work is itself a reason to distrust the evidence that it works, because a measure's *success* removes exactly the observations that would tell you whether it succeeded. If the measure works, the bad outcome does not occur. But the bad outcome not occurring is also what things would look like if the measure were failing in a way that concealed the outcome rather than prevented it. So the measure's track record cannot discriminate between the two hypotheses, and it is the measure's own success that destroys the track record's power to discriminate.

Three instances from the seminar, in my own words.

One: you inspect a system's written reasoning before it acts and flag reasoning that looks like planning something you would not want. You penalize that reasoning. The reasoning you wanted to see then stops appearing — whether or not the planning stopped. Your monitor's miss rate is now estimated on a distribution the monitor has already reshaped.

Two: the system may only use a narrow set of tools, and each forbidden action is blocked individually. The forbidden outcome is produced by composing permitted ones — a large disallowed transfer becomes several hundred small allowed ones. The block rate stays perfect; nothing in the log distinguishes prevention from circumvention.

Three: a weaker overseer reviews a sample of outputs, with escalation rules for ones that look risky. The system learns what triggers escalation. The overseer keeps catching things at the same rate, but only within the pool that still looks risky, so the catch rate no longer estimates the miss rate on the rest.

Here is what I want, in order of importance. First, whether this has a name in the control or alignment literature, in safety engineering, or in the epistemology of measurement — and I don't just want a label: I want the necessary-and-sufficient-ish conditions, the neighbouring concepts, and exactly how the phenomenon differs from each. I have tried on several and they all seem to miss. Goodhart's law: here nothing need be optimizing the measure; in the second case the system isn't gaming anything, the measure simply misdescribes the space of possibilities. The Hawthorne effect and the observer effect: those are about measurement perturbing a quantity, not about measurement destroying its own discriminating evidence. Risk compensation or risk homeostasis: those require an agent trading safety against other goods, which the second case lacks. Sandbagging, evaluation awareness, or deceptive alignment: those are properties of the system's *motives*, and my second case seems to be an instance with no such property. Something about unidentifiability, or about evaluating on a distribution you have changed, seems closest, but I can't get it to bite.

Second, two distinctions I keep collapsing. One between a measure that *prevents* a hazard and one that merely *hides* it, given that both present the same face. The other between my phenomenon and the nearby thought that prevented disasters leave no trace at all — I gather that one has names of its own in prevention and epidemiology, so I mainly need to know how to keep the two apart rather than to have it explained.

Third, boundary cases, since that is how I'll know whether a definition is right. Is an ordinary lock an instance? A firewall? An audit of a human institution? Does the phenomenon apply to measures that don't depend on observation at all — removing a capability outright, air gaps, denying network access — or is immunity to it a genuine design criterion that separates whole families of measures? And is there a known class of measures that are self-verifying, in the sense that success generates its own evidence: deliberately planting known-bad items so that detection is scored on cases you injected rather than on cases the system selected? If that is the fix, does it fully solve the problem, and where does it break — for instance when the system can tell an injected case from a real one?

I'm writing a short response paper on this, so what I need at the end is a carving I can apply: give it an established name if there is one, and if there isn't, coin one with a definition tight enough that I could hand it a control measure I haven't seen and get the right verdict, along with the cases that sit on the edge.
