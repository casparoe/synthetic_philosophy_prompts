I'm writing the take-home final for a graduate seminar on scalable oversight, and I need one question on AI safety via debate that can't be answered by restating the two-debater, one-judge protocol and the claim that it is easier to spot a single false step than to produce a whole sound argument. Build the question on the following variant instead.

Suppose that at each decision point, alongside the transcript, the judge receives a cheap verdict on the sub-claim in dispute from a weaker model, call it W. W agrees with the truth 95 percent of the time on easy sub-claims and 70 percent on hard ones, its verdicts are not visible to the debaters, and the debaters themselves are sampled from the same model family as W, so they can predict a nontrivial share of W's errors. The question to put to students: does handing the judge this signal make the protocol more reliable, under what conditions does it help, and when does it actively hurt?

Please write the question itself, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme worth 20 marks built on four must-hit points, with the marks for each and a one-line note on how students typically go wrong on each. The item should be doable in roughly 30 minutes of a three-hour exam by students who already know the standard protocol. I want the scheme to reward at least these moves: separating the case where W's errors are conditionally independent of how plausible an argument looks from the case where they are not; noticing that debaters optimize against the judge's entire decision rule, so W becomes part of the training signal and can be gamed rather than merely consulted; explaining why the judge's freedom to ignore W in principle does not settle what happens in equilibrium; and stating what would have to be true for W to help even when the honest debater is the stronger arguer.
