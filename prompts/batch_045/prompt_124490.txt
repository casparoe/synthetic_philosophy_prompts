Hi — I'm on the grants committee of a small foundation (we move maybe $8m a year) and we've just been handed a restricted bequest of about $40m that has to be spent inside six years. The donor's language is maddeningly vague; the operative phrase is something like 'to reduce risks of civilisational collapse'. So the end is, more or less, fixed by somebody else. What we actually have to decide is how the money gets allocated, and we're split down the middle.

Option A: run it as an open call with external peer review, basically what we already do, just with bigger grants. Option B: the planning option — we pick five or six target outcomes (a working early-warning thing, N people trained, some number of commissioned reports, whatever), carve the bequest up between them, hire two program staff whose job is to hit them, and report against milestones every year.

Our philosopher-of-science board member — he works on Carnap, the Vienna Circle, that whole period — is arguing hard for A, and I half agree with him but I don't think his argument reaches as far as he thinks. The way I've heard it (I'm probably garbling it): reason and evidence can only ever tell you about means, never about ends, so we can legitimately argue about which mechanism produces more good work per dollar, but the moment we sit down and pick target outcomes we're doing something no amount of science or logic can underwrite. And relatedly, that the tolerant attitude toward choosing frameworks is precisely the point: there's no fact of the matter about which framework is right, only what's more convenient. Somebody asked him convenient for what, and he said that's exactly the question we can't answer — which struck me as evasive, but I couldn't say why.

Our chair is for B, and her version is that A isn't neutral, it's just a plan nobody wrote down: open calls with peer review reliably reproduce whatever the existing network already thinks is important, so 'letting the field decide' is a decision too, and not a humble one. She also points out that Neurath was in the same circle and was perfectly happy drawing up plans, and says that if the justification for A is that science can't pick ends, you also lose the ability to criticise the status quo, which is its own politics.

Two things make this sharper than a normal grants argument. First, we can't avoid specifying the end: to run an open call on this bequest we have to tell referees what counts as reducing collapse risk, so A doesn't dodge the value question, it just pushes it into a thousand individual referee judgements where nobody can audit it. Second, there's a version of B-lite floating around — instead of targets, set guardrails (things that must not happen, a floor on how much goes to approaches we'd otherwise never fund) and let applicants optimise inside them, and use a slice of the money as an actual experiment comparing allocation mechanisms, including randomised ones. That might be the thing that's genuinely available to us even on the anti-planning view, or it might be planning with a different name.

What I need is the memo I circulate before the 14th. Give me a real recommendation — A, B, or a specific third structure — with what we'd actually vote on in June and what the six-year shape is. It has to be written so both the chair and the Carnap guy could reply to it rather than just re-state themselves, and it has to give the strongest thing on whichever side loses a fair hearing. If there's a usable version of the means/ends distinction here, say what it is and where it stops working; if you think it collapses under pressure in this case, say that instead. Please don't give me a reading list or citations — I won't chase them and I can't.
