Ive been following the algorithmic fairness thing since the 2016 ProPublica piece on COMPAS, and what I want is a diagnosis rather than another explainer, so please skip the survey of defintions and skip the bibliography. Ive read the surveys; I just cant tell what argument they are supposed to be settling.

Here is where it looks stuck to me. ProPublica said black defendants were almost twice as likely as white defendants to be incorrectly labelled higher risk; the company that built the tool said no, a given score means the same thing for both groups, the scores are calibrated. Then the impossibility results came in and showed both sides were basically right. The one everybody cites is Kleinberg's 2016 paper, where you cannot have calibration within groups and balance for both the positive and the negative class at the same time except in highly constrained special cases where the base rates line up. Since then: a decade of new fairness criteria, a fresh incompatibility theorem every few months, toolkits that let you choose which point on which trade-off you would like to sit at, and as far as I can tell nobody has moved an inch. The field now produces taxonomies and dashboards instead of verdicts.

My hypothesis, which I want stress-tested rather than flattered: this is not mainly a value disagreement about how much we care about false positives versus false negatives. Its a disagreement about what kind of object fairness is even a property of — a score distribution, a thresholded decision rule, the whole pipeline including which features and which target label you picked, or a repeated process over time — with a hidden empirical question about where the base-rate differences come from doing most of the real work while everyone treats them as a fixed parameter.

The test Ive been trying to apply, which I made up, so tell me if its confused: run an invariance audit on each proposed criterion. For each one, ask which changes to the setup leave the verdict unchanged — permuting people within a group, swapping which group counts as protected, moving the decision threshold, adding or dropping a feature, redefining the target as arrest rather than reconviction, splitting a group into subgroups and demanding the criterion hold there too, swapping the deployment population for the training population. On this reading each camp is demanding invariance under the changes it takes to be morally inert and tolerating variation under the rest, and the impossibility theorems are what falls out when two sets of demands together generate everything, the way two symmetry conditions can jointly force the trivial solution. Is that a useful redescription of the standoff, or am I just relabelling it?

And separately: how much of this is verbal, with one camp using fair for something like actuarial or descriptive accuracy and the other for something substantively moral? Are all the impossibility theorems even doing the same kind of work, or do the ones that only bite when base rates differ need to be kept apart from the ones that bite regardless?

What I actually want is the diagnosis, argued from what each side really says, plus what would count as progress given that diagnosis. A theorem about which invariances can coexist, a change of question from which criterion is correct to who gets to choose at deployment and under what mandate, genuine causal work on the base rates, a distinction nobody has drawn yet — or an honest argument that these are bedrock intuitions and no progress should be expected. British English please, and no citations.
