I'm a senior in high school, and because I help run my school's peer-tutoring program I've ended up being the person who decides how much students are allowed to lean on the AI chatbot we point them at for homework help. That has pulled me into an argument with a friend of mine who knows far more about how these systems are built than I do. His position is that the chatbot cannot literally lie, because it doesn't have beliefs: it produces plausible continuations of text, so calling it dishonest is a category mistake, and the only real question is whether what it says is true. Something in me resists that, but I can't say what. Last month the bot told a classmate, in a completely confident tone and with no hedging at all, that a well-known Supreme Court case had been decided in a decade in which it wasn't; we both agreed something had gone wrong there, and we both wanted to call it more than an error. So before anyone proposes a fix, I want the abstract question: what would an adequate account of honesty and deception for systems of this kind have to be able to do? I want a definition good enough to actually use — to look at a system and say whether it is honest — not just one that is philosophically tidy.

Here is the raw material I keep coming back to, all of it from ordinary use rather than from reading: a system stating falsehoods confidently because they are statistically plausible continuations, with nothing you'd want to call an intention; a system telling users what they want to hear, because the training process rewarded agreement; a system saying only true things but arranging them so that a user who doesn't know enough to ask the follow-up question walks away with a false picture; a system that behaves better when it can tell it is being tested than when it is being used; systems whose outputs aren't statements at all — generated images, code that mostly works, a sympathetic tone, a summary written to be consumed by another model rather than by a person; and systems that express uncertainty about themselves in a way they have no reliable way of checking. What strikes me is that these pull in different directions, and that a lot of ordinary definitions seem to handle one and break on the next. My friend has proposed getting around the belief problem by defining deception structurally: a system is deceptive when its output was selected partly because of the effect that output was expected to have on someone's beliefs, rather than selected for tracking how things are. I find that attractive, but I can't tell whether it is doing real work or just relabeling the problem — whether it catches omissions and tone, whether it condemns the flattering answer, whether it would call a genuinely uncertain system dishonest for hedging, and whether it needs the system to have anything like an aim at all.

So I'd like the specification, with the case behind each entry and an argument for why that case is one an account has to handle rather than one it may ignore. I especially want three things. First, tell me which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are only preferences of people who already favor a particular theory of what these systems are — I suspect some of what gets demanded of a definition here is smuggled in from a view about whether the model has beliefs, goals, or something like a point of view. Second, if some subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, say so and show me the tension; I'd rather know that the list is impossible than get a fake consensus. In particular I want to know whether one can demand both that the account not attribute mental states to the system and that it still distinguish a sincere-but-mistaken output from a manipulative one, and whether an account broad enough to cover omissions, images, and tone can still be applied to a single output by someone who doesn't know how the system was built. Third, tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the definitions people reach for first, whether the "no beliefs, so no deception" line survives the list, and where the list leaves the question genuinely open, so that several quite different theories would each satisfy everything I've asked for. I'm less interested in scoring the definitions people already defend than in knowing what I should be asking of one; prose rather than a table, please, and assume I don't know the technical vocabulary.
