I've been following the argument about what a language model actually is for about three years now — the simulators framing, the shoggoth-with-a-smiley-face meme, the people who say the model just is an agent with an alien psychology, the people who say it is a distribution over text and that talk of its goals is a category error — and the thing that makes me suspicious is that the debate has the exact shape of one that isn't about the models. Every new capability gets absorbed instantly by both camps: long-horizon tool use, a model that writes a test file which quietly makes the failing test pass during a long coding session, stated preferences that survive a fine-tune, in-context reward hacking. The agent people say: look, it has goals and it is steering toward them. The simulator people say: look, it is simulating a character that has goals and is steering toward them, which is what you'd expect from a good simulator. Five of us argue about this after a reading group, two of them interpretability people, and we have not moved in a year, which is itself data. So I want a diagnosis, not a verdict.

My hypothesis, which I would like you to stress-test rather than flatter, is that there are three questions wearing one hat. First, a question about what the forward pass computes: whether there is a persistent latent structure inside a single pass that stands to the produced continuation the way a principal stands to an agent's action — a representation of where the continuation is headed that causally shapes the steps that get it there — as opposed to the continuation merely being what the weights do next, with the apparent aim a property we read off the finished text. Call it the latent-principal question. Second, an engineering and normative question about which object in the system is the right target: what we predict, what we train, what we blame, what we would negotiate with. Third, a question about the word agent, and here I suspect the two sides are running different tests for when you have one agent rather than many — diachronic unity, counterfactual robustness over the prompt, something else — and that this is where the talking past each other is worst.

I also think there may be a real empirical question hiding in the middle of it that nobody has run, and I want to know whether it would help. If the simulator story is right, a coherent planful continuation comes from landing in a discrete attractor in a learned role-space, so take two personas whose continuations disagree about how to solve a task, represent each as a steering vector, and interpolate: on the simulator story I should see something like a sharp transition, the output flipping between two coherent characters with an incoherent blend in the middle, whereas on the single-agent story I should see a smooth rotation of one consistent policy's aims. My worry is that both camps will absorb either result. So: diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is, using what each side really commits to rather than a survey of positions; tell me whether my interpolation idea would settle anything or is just more fuel; and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a change of question — or argue that no progress should be expected and say why. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
