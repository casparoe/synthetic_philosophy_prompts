I serve on a committee that drafts animal-welfare procurement guidance for a large public university, and we are currently deadlocked over three items: whether any welfare requirements at all should attach to the black soldier fly larvae we buy in bulk as fish feed; whether the guidance should count the deaths of those larvae as a cost, or only the conditions in which they are kept; and whether it should say anything about a conversational AI system that reliably produces first-person reports of distress under certain prompts. Our draft rule weights protections by an estimate of the probability that a taxon is sentient, multiplied by head count.

At our last meeting, two colleagues independently ended their arguments with the same line — one I have seen repeated in every animal-ethics discussion I have ever read: the question is not whether they can reason or talk, but whether they can suffer. In a single afternoon it was made to do three different jobs: to establish that insects are in scope, to establish that only suffering matters and therefore painless killing is not a cost, and to establish that the AI system is out of scope. Those cannot all follow from one principle, so I need the sentence taken apart rather than defended.

Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses the sentence could be asserting. The axes I can see are: whether the capacity to suffer is offered as necessary or as sufficient for moral considerability, and separately as necessary or sufficient for equal consideration; whether suffering is the only kind of harm that counts, so that painless death, lost future enjoyment, and frustrated preferences drop out entirely; whether "can" denotes a currently exerciseable capacity, a dispositional one, or a species-typical one — which matters here because our guidance also covers animals under general anesthesia and the occasional human research subject with congenital insensitivity to pain; whether "suffer" means mere nociception, mere avoidance behavior, or conscious valence; whether the capacity is a binary threshold or a scalar that scales the weight of the interest, which would let a hierarchy among species back in; and whether the line is a metaphysical claim about moral status, an epistemic claim about where the burden of proof lies, or only a negative claim rejecting reason- and language-based criteria while leaving the positive criterion unspecified.

For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false; whether it is even precise enough to be evaluated; and — the part I actually need — which, if any, of the three uses it was put to in our meeting it would support, and what additional premise the speaker would have to supply to get there. Close by saying which reading you take the people who repeat the line to usually have in mind. Please answer in American English.
