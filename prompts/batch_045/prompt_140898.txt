I'm a junior at a public high school, and I help edit my school's philosophy club zine, which is read mostly by people my own age who have never taken a philosophy class. For the next issue I want to build a quiz along the lines of "Which free will would you actually want?", and I'd like your help designing it.

Here's the angle I care about. Every treatment of this topic I've run into turns into the same argument about whether determinism is true, and my classmates tune out within a paragraph. So I want to sidestep that. The quiz should work like a menu: imagine a machine — I've been calling it the Modifier — that can rebuild your will in various ways before you face a hard choice, and each question asks which rebuild you'd take. The point is to sort readers by what they would actually choose, not by what they have heard free will is supposed to be.

What I'm looking for:

Nine or ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three options, worded so a sixteen-year-old with no background can answer in under a minute. No question should ask anyone to pick a label, and nothing phrased in technical terms like whether you could have done otherwise in exactly the same circumstances — that just makes people guess at the approved answer.

The cases should be the ones that genuinely split the views. Some I've been playing with: a Rehearsal Room where you can run a decision a thousand times in simulation and only the run you endorse counts as your real action; a Valet that has watched you for years and now executes your choices better than you would yourself, so that you always do what you most wanted but never deliberate; a Tuning Fork that lets you edit the desires you will have next year, but only using the values you have right now; a decision settled by a genuinely random neural coin flip versus one settled by your settled character; a hard choice made by someone whose entire value system was installed by the community they were raised in; and a case where you alone lose the capacity while everyone around you keeps it. Please use these, drop any that aren't doing real work, and add whatever is missing — especially cases that separate positions I haven't thought of.

Then the taxonomy. Before you tell me which position each answer pattern maps onto, I want the space of possible answers laid out first: which commitments are doing the branching (whether the ability to do otherwise matters at all, whether what matters is that the will is really yours rather than that it was uncaused, whether randomness threatens agency just as much as determinism does, whether what is really at stake is blame and desert or instead something like authorship, meaning, love, or achievement, whether this is the kind of thing you can have more or less of). Then place the named positions into the cells: the view that freedom is just being able to do what you want, the view that it is about responding to reasons or about wanting to want what you want, the view that moral responsibility survives even without alternative possibilities, the views on which genuine freedom requires some indeterminism in the process of deciding, the skeptical view that we lack it and that the practices built on it need rethinking, the revisionist view that we should rebuild the concept around what we actually care about, and a leaf for someone who would say the illusion is worth keeping. Where a combination of answers has nobody living in it, say so and explain why: incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unoccupied because nobody bothered.

Also: flag any two leaves the quiz genuinely cannot tell apart. I would rather print an honest note about the overlap than have the thing look tidier than it is. And please make sure the key can't be gamed by picking whichever option sounds most sophisticated — say what a tied or messy answer pattern most likely means, and what it means when someone's honest answer is that they don't know which they'd want.

One constraint: the zine has no footnotes and my readers won't look anything up, so please don't cite papers, name-drop philosophers, or suggest further reading. Just the questions, the scoring key, and the map.
