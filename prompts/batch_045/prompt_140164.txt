me and a coworker keep going in circles about what counts as "deception" for the support agent we're shipping, and I realised I've accumulated a bunch of half-thought-through positions that might not fit together. figured I'd write them down and get someone to poke at them before they end up in a design doc. I'm an engineer, never took a philosophy class in my life, so please skip the survey of the literature — I don't need the history of the concept, I need to know where I'm contradicting myself.

where I've landed:

1. Deception should be defined functionally — by what a reasonable user ends up believing after interacting with the thing — not by anything going on inside the model. I don't want to build policy on claims about model beliefs or intentions that nobody can test.

2. But hallucinating isn't deception, it's a bug. A model that invents a refund policy isn't lying, it's malfunctioning, same as a service returning garbage from a bad cache. I think we should judge this stuff at the level of what the system is set up to do, not at the level of individual outputs.

3. Sycophancy — tilting the answer toward whatever the user seems to want, because that's what got reinforced — is deception, and it's the worst kind, worse than a confident hallucination.

4. Selective omission counts too. If it surfaces only the two cases that support what the user already thinks, that's deceptive even though every sentence it emits is literally true.

5. I'm against the blanket "by the way I'm an AI" disclaimer. Our agent signs replies with a first name and says things like "I looked into your account and here's what happened," and adding the disclaimer just makes people bounce in testing. A good answer should be a good answer; I don't think we owe people a nag. (Legal made us strip out "I think" for a related reason, which I also think is silly but I lost that one.)

6. Tone is seperate from content. Writing "your refund was processed on the 3rd" in a flat confident voice when the model is maybe 60% on the date is just decent UX; hedging everything drops task completion and then people don't get the true information at all. my coworker calls this "confidence laundering," which I think is a bit much — nothing false got said.

his actual line is that I'm mixing up honesty (something agents have) with accuracy (something outputs have). maybe! but he also keeps saying the whole list is a mess and then can't point at where.

so: audit it. which of these actually conflict, which only look like they conflict, and for each real conflict what's the cheapest fix and what does it cost me? rank them by how bad the conflict is rather than flagging all of them as equally bad, be specific about which claim has to give, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most. if one of them is load-bearing for the rest, say which. I'm not attached to any particular item, I just want to know which one is doing the work.
