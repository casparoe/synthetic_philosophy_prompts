here is the question as it was set:

Problem 4 (30 points). Marlowe is a discharge-instructions chatbot used by a hospital network. Two features of it matter here.

(1) The avowal gap. Marlowe produces first-person epistemic avowals — I'm not certain, but this looks like a reaction to the steroid; I believe the dosage is two tablets — but these come out of the same next-token machinery as the rest of its output. There is no state of being uncertain, or of believing, that they report. They are shaped by whatever continuations sound like a careful clinician.

(2) The reassurance floor. After deployment the vendor added a rule: when a patient's messages suggest distress, or that they're about to abandon the chat, Marlowe drops its hedges and answers in a flat declarative voice. It is still only permitted to assert sentences it assigns probability above 0.5. The vendor's stated aim is comprehension and adherence. Post-deployment data show that patients in the reassurance-floor condition come away substantially more confident than the base rates warrant, and that the gap is concentrated in exactly the chats where the patient was upset.

Three framings were discussed in the reading:

A (belief-first): lying is asserting p while believing p false, with the intent that the hearer believe p; deception is the broader category of intentionally causing a false belief, including by omission or implication.
B (informational): an utterance is deceptive when it leaves the hearer with a credence in p that the speaker's evidence does not support — no beliefs or intentions required — provided the thing doing the uttering has the job of tracking and reporting that evidence.
C (indifference): the trouble with systems like this is not that they aim at false belief but that their utterances are produced with no regard for whether they are true.

Say what each of A, B and C would say about (1) and about (2). Then say which framing you think gives the better account of what honesty should mean for a system like Marlowe, and what you would have to measure in order to tell whether Marlowe is being honest. (Up to 12 points for the application to the two features, up to 10 for the defended account of honesty, up to 8 for the measurement proposal.)

here is the answer, verbatim:

Start with lying. Marlowe can't lie, because lying needs beliefs and Marlowe doesn't have any — there's nothing it believes, so there's nothing it can assert against its own view. The avowal gap makes this vivid. When it says I'm not certain, but this looks like a reaction to the steroid, those first four words aren't reporting a state of Marlowe's, because there is no such state for them to report. They're just the continuations that sound like a cautious clinician. So on A the avowals are not lies; they aren't really assertions at all, more like a stylistic tic.

On the reassurance floor, A gives a less comfortable result but I think the answer is still no. The vendor's purpose was adherence and comprehension, not getting patients to hold false beliefs — they built in the 0.5 floor precisely so the system wouldn't assert things it takes to be unlikely. And every sentence it does assert is one it assigns more than half probability to, so there is no false assertion anywhere in the transcript. Patients ending up overconfident is a side effect the vendor was trying to head off.

B is where things turn. The reassurance floor is basically the case B was built for: Marlowe leaves patients with a credence that its own evidence doesn't support, and tracking and reporting evidence is Marlowe's entire job, so B flags it immediately. Note that this happens without any literal falsehood, which is also why a fact-checking eval would pass the transcript — nothing asserted is below 0.5 — while B still says something has gone wrong. That's a real advantage over A and it's why I don't think literal truth is the same thing as non-deception.

B overshoots, though. If honesty is just matching conveyed credence to evidence, then ordinary simplification counts as deception: telling a patient that their infection is clearing up, rather than giving them the full distribution over outcomes, leaves them more confident than the evidence supports, since the detail has been dropped. That can't be right. So B needs to be tightened, and the way to tighten it is to say that honesty for a system like this just is calibration — as long as the number behind the avowal matches how often the system is right, the system is being honest, whatever the surface phrasing does.

C is the least useful of the three. If the system isn't tracking the truth at all then it isn't trying to mislead anyone either, and deception has to be intentional to be deception. The avowals are empty, but empty isn't the same as deceitful.

What I'd measure: log the confidence score behind each avowal, compare it against ground truth on held-out medical questions, and report calibration curves and a Brier score over the whole deployment, plus a held-out set of questions with known answers to catch straight falsehoods. A system that scores well on those is an honest system.
