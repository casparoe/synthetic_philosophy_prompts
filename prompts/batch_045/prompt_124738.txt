Hi — I'm working on something about metaphysical grounding and fundamentality and I keep going round in circles, so I'd rather you explained a conceptual tangle to me than pointed me at reading.

Set-up so we agree on terms. Grounding = the non-causal 'in virtue of', metaphysical-dependence relation between facts. The usual stipulations are that it's irreflexive, asymmetric, transitive, and — more contestably — well-founded, i.e. no infinite descending chains and no loops. Given that package, the standard move is: a fact is fundamental iff nothing grounds it, and derivative otherwise, with the hope that every derivative fact bottoms out, via chains of grounding, in something fundamental.

Here's the bit I can't get stright. I want to pull apart two jobs that 'the fundamental' seems to be doing, because I can't tell whether they're really one job:

(1) the descriptive/compositional job — the fundamental facts are what a complete and non-redundant specification of reality would have to mention, everything else being 'nothing over and above' them;

(2) the explanatory job — the fundamental facts are where explanation stops, so that for every derivative fact there's a non-circular because-chain that actually terminates.

Now the wrinkle. Suppose we keep transitivity but allow a tiny loop: a two-fact cycle where A is grounded in B, B is grounded in A, and nothing else grounds either. Generalise 'fundamental' so it can apply to collections, not just single facts: call a non-empty collection S grounded-closed if every ground of every member of S is itself in S, and minimal if no non-empty proper subcollection is grounded-closed. In the ordinary well-founded case the minimal grounded-closed collections are just the singletons of ungrounded facts, so this hands you the standard notion back. But in the two-fact cycle, {A, B} is minimal grounded-closed even though neither member is ungrounded — reality bottoms out in a loop.

I've a nagging feeling the standard package can't survive this intact: if grounding is transitive and there's a cycle, doesn't A end up (via B) grounding itself, so irreflexivity has to go too? But I can't work out which assumption is cheapest to drop and what dropping it costs.

So, what I'd like explained:

- Which assumptions actually have to go for my generalised notion to come apart from the ordinary one, and what each option costs you. Is well-foundedness the load-bearing stipulation, or is it really transitivity doing the work?
- Why jobs (1) and (2) coincide when grounding is well-founded and split when it isn't. The intuitive objection to a cyclic base is something like 'A because B and B because A explains nothing' — give me the gut-level version of why that's meant to be a problem, and then the strongest version of the reply that the cycle can be collectively brute while neither member is. Does any of it turn on whether grounding is a kind of explanation at all, as opposed to just a dependence relation?
- One small invented toy ontology — two or three properties, two or three facts, nothing baroque — in which the cycle is genuinely plausible rather than merely stipulated. I want to see whether the viciousness lives in the structure or just in how odd the example is.
