I teach a first-year module on probability and rational belief, and I want to use the 2016 American presidential election forecasts as a worked example of the Principal Principle — the constraint, roughly, that your credence in an outcome, conditional on the chance of that outcome being r and on your admissible evidence, should be r. To keep us off contested ground, stipulate the facts: on the morning of the vote the best-known poll-based model put Clinton at something like a two-in-three chance; she won the popular vote but lost the Electoral College; and a large part of the public commentary afterwards was that the forecast had been 'wrong'. I don't need the exact figure or the exact model, and I'd rather you didn't rest anything on them. What I want is an analysis that runs in both directions: a disciplined mapping of the episode onto the Principle, and then a verdict about what the episode does to the Principle.

Three things I want built in rather than gestured at. First, the mapping has to say who the deferring agent is — a member of the public updating that morning, the forecaster who built the model, or the pundit passing judgement afterwards — because the answer seems to change what the Principle requires: for the forecaster, after all, the polls the model is built out of look like inadmissible evidence about the outcome itself, so either the admissibility clause is doing something subtler than I think it is, or the Principle simply isn't addressed to someone in the forecaster's position. Second, the status of the number: a poll-based forecast is not obviously an objective chance of the kind the Principle is stated for, since it is a model output and there are rival models, so tell me what would have to be true for the Principle to get a grip here at all, and if it doesn't get a grip, what nearby principle does speak. Third, a wrinkle my seminar came up with that I can't settle: when the chance itself is uncertain and several models disagree, you might defer to one forecaster's number, or mix over models yourself and defer to your own mixture, or defer at a higher level to an aggregation of forecasters — and my hunch is that deferring to a mixture is not the same thing as taking a mixture of what each model would have you believe once you also condition on the fact of the disagreement.

I'd also like the backwards direction done properly: is the post-hoc 'the model was wrong because he won' complaint exactly the failure the Principle is meant to forbid, which would make the election a vindication of it, or is it rather a case the Principle never anticipated — model-generated chances, a single unrepeatable trial, evidence about the outcome arriving after the forecast — and therefore no evidence either way? Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no preamble and no closing summary, in this order: (1) the mapping — the agent, the putatively chance-like quantity, the admissible evidence, and the options; (2) what the Principle strictly prescribes, both on the morning of the vote and after the result; (3) which features of the episode it explains well; (4) which features it misdescribes, or about which it is simply silent; (5) your verdict on the episode as evidence for or against the Principle, and on whether my mixture-of-deference puzzle is a real gap in it or a confusion on my part.
