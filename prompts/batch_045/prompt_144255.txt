Pain asymbolia is a neurological condition in which patients report feeling pain and can describe its location, intensity and sensory quality (stinging, burning, and so on) as accurately as before, but say that the pain no longer bothers them, and show no inclination to withdraw from or stop the stimulus. Philosophers have used this case to argue about where the badness of pain is located. In your answer: (a) state the case accurately; (b) reconstruct the argument from it as strongly as you can; (c) present and evaluate the strongest objection you can find; (d) say whether the argument establishes (i) that pain's unpleasantness is a distinct component of pain, separable from its sensory character, (ii) that pain's badness is extrinsic to the experience rather than intrinsic to it, both, or neither. Two views were introduced in the readings: the two-component view, on which the sensory and affective dimensions of pain are distinct components that can come apart, and the inseparability view, on which pain's unpleasantness is not a separate component but is identical with, or at least inseparable from, its sensory character.

Student's answer:

Pain asymbolia is a documented neurological condition (I think the damage is to the insula, or possibly the anterior cingulate — I'm not sure which) in which patients still have pain in the ordinary sensory sense. They can say that it stings or burns, they can point to where it is, and they rate it at the same intensity they did before the injury. What they lose is the awful, get-me-out-of-here element. They'll say the pain is there but that it doesn't bother them; they don't flinch, they don't pull away, and they don't ask for anything to make it stop.

The argument is basically a matter of holding one thing fixed while another one varies. Keep the sensory character fixed — same burning, same intensity, same location — and the unpleasantness drops out. Anything that can disappear while the other thing stays the same can't be the same thing as it. So unpleasantness is a separate component of pain, not just the sensory quality described in different words. Now, what makes pain bad is its unpleasantness. So the badness of pain isn't intrinsic to pain: it comes from a further reaction to the sensation, an attitude of minding it. Which means the badness is extrinsic to the experience itself — the very same sensation, in a subject who didn't mind it, wouldn't be bad at all. And that has a practical upshot: what we should be trying to remove is not the sensation but the subject's dislike of it, and a drug that induced asymbolia would be a genuine solution to the problem of suffering, provided we replaced the information that pain carries with some non-painful signal.

The most common objection is that we shouldn't trust what these patients say — maybe the lesion damages their capacity to report their own experience, or maybe they're just being stoical. I don't find this convincing. The patients are otherwise lucid and consistent, they pass the standard neurological tests, and the dissociation shows up in imaging as well, not just in what they say.

The serious objection is the inseparability theorist's. The inseparability theorist claims that pain just is its own unpleasantness, so that a pain you don't mind isn't a pain at all. But this is simply refusing to accept the case. Asymbolia happens, so the inseparability theorist is stuck denying that these patients are in pain — which is absurd, given that they can describe the sensation perfectly well and it's the same sort of sensation you or I would call painful. So the inseparability view is refuted by the data, and the argument goes through.

Conclusion: the asymbolia argument establishes both (i) that unpleasantness is a distinct component of pain and (ii) that pain's badness is extrinsic to the experience — the sensation itself is value-neutral, and everything that matters morally is contributed by the subject's attitude toward it.
