Three of us run an informal philosophy of science reading group — me, Wes (he does phil of physics), and Marisol (she does ML). We spent a session on the problem of old evidence, I thought we'd settled it, and then Marisol emailed me an argument that seems to show our solution lets you confirm basically anything, and I've been going in circles for a week. Can you find the step that breaks?

Background, short version. Standard Bayesian confirmation: E confirms H for S only if P(H|E) > P(H), equivalently P(E|H) > P(E). But if S already knows E, then P(E) = 1, and P(E|H) = 1 too, so nothing S already knows can confirm anything. That's the problem of old evidence. The stock illustration is the one about the orbit of Mercury, but the case I actually care about is a different one.

The fix we liked — call it the rollback — is this: don't evaluate confirmation at S's actual state; evaluate it at the state S would have been in before E came in. Strip E out of S's evidence, hold the rest fixed, and ask whether conditionalizing on E *there* would raise P(H). In that state E need not have probability 1, so old evidence can confirm after all. We liked it because it handles:

THE TAPE CASE. A survey group has a decade of raw data sitting on a drive in a cabinet. Nobody has reduced it; nobody knows what pattern is on it. Years later someone fits a model, and the model predicts a pattern that is sitting on the drive. Intuitively that data confirms the model. The rollback delivers it: in the rolled-back state the team had the drive but not the numbers, so P(E) < 1 there, and the model entails E, so P(E|H) = 1 > P(E).

Marisol's argument that this proves too much:

(1) Confirmation is exactly what the rollback says it is: E confirms H for S iff, in S's total evidence with E deleted and everything else held fixed, P(H|E) > P(H).
(2) The tape case is a genuine case of confirmation, and the rollback is what explains why: at the rolled-back state P(E) < 1 even though the evidence was in the group's physical possession the whole time.
(3) Nothing in the rollback appeals to how E was acquired, when, or whether anyone went looking. It uses only two facts: that P(E) < 1 in the rolled-back state, and that H entails E. So if it works for the tape it works for anything with those two features.
(4) For any hypothesis H and any contingent D, H entails H-or-D. So take your favourite H — that the expansion rate is such-and-such, that my neighbour's dog ate my hedge, whatever — and take a boring D you already know: "either H or my bike has a flat tyre." You know that disjunction, so it's old evidence; H entails it; and in the rolled-back state its probability is below 1. By (1) and (3) it confirms H.
(5) The usual defences don't touch it. It isn't ad hocness or overfitting: H entails the disjunction with no adjustable knobs, whatever the world is like. It isn't that the evidence is irrelevant in the ordinary sense — its probability does go to 1 under H. "But you'd have to actually learn E" is answered by (2): the tape case works precisely because we evaluate from a state where E hasn't been learned. And if you object that you can't delete the disjunction without also deleting the known disjunct that makes it known, fine — delete that too; the inequality still goes through, and there are plenty of D's you don't know anyway, where the deletion is clean.
(6) So on our own fix, every hypothesis with nonzero prior is confirmed by infinitely many propositions its holder already has in hand, for free, with nobody doing anything.
(7) But confirmation isn't free, and the tape case isn't free either: what makes it worth something is that someone went to the cabinet, reduced the data, and noticed the fit.
(8) So the rollback is either trivializing or it has to lean on something beyond the counterfactual probability assignment — something about what the agent was actually in a position to find out. Which is worrying, because that something looks like it rewards ignorance: someone who sees at a glance that H entails H-or-D gets no confirmation, and a logically ideal agent, who sees every consequence of everything, would get confirmation from nothing at all.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I want to keep (2) — the tape case really is confirmation, and really is the same phenomenon as the Mercury case, not a separate thing — and I'd like to keep the basic Bayesian identity from the setup if at all possible. I'll give up nearly anything else. My own guess is that the trouble is in (1), and that "delete E, hold everything else fixed" isn't a well defined operation, or at least isn't well defined in the cases (4) needs it to be — but I can't say what the constraint is or where it would come from. If the right answer is that confirmation has to be relativized to something other than a single proposition — a partition, a possible observation, a method, whatever — say so, and tell me what that costs the tape case.
