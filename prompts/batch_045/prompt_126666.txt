I teach a final-year and MA seminar in the philosophy department on rational choice and game theory, and I am writing one question for this year's unseen written paper. The students have met strict and weak dominance, iterated deletion of strictly dominated strategies using pure dominators, mixed strategies, and Nash equilibrium. In one lecture I mentioned in passing that a pure strategy can be strictly dominated by a mixed strategy even though no pure strategy dominates it, and I went through the standard three-line textbook illustration of that fact. I now want a question that cannot be answered by reproducing that illustration or by writing down the definitions.

The question will be built around the following two-player game, which I have constructed for the purpose. Rowena chooses among A, B and C; Colin chooses among L, M and R; the first figure in each cell is Rowena's payoff, the second Colin's, and both are to be read as von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities.

               L             M             R
   A         4, 6          0, 4          3, 2
   B         0, 2          4, 1          1, 3
   C        1.5, 1        1.5, 5        1.8, 5

The game is designed so that: no strategy of either player is strictly dominated by a pure strategy at the outset, and indeed none is even weakly dominated; exactly one strategy is strictly dominated by a mixed strategy, and the set of mixing weights that do the job is a proper, non-degenerate interval; once that deletion is made, the remaining deletions are pure and the procedure terminates in a single strategy profile, which is therefore the unique Nash equilibrium. You should verify all of this rather than take it from me.

Please produce three things.

First, the question as it will appear on the paper: 25 marks, about forty-five minutes, set out in clearly labelled parts. It should ask the students to (i) establish that deletion with pure dominators gets nowhere; (ii) exhibit the strategy that is dominated by a mixture, verify the domination, and give the range of mixing weights that work; (iii) carry the procedure through to the end, stating each surviving profile, and say in a sentence or two whether the order of deletion could have made a difference and why; (iv) answer an objection I want them to confront head on, namely that Rowena is a single agent who will perform one action and cannot improve her position by tossing a coin, so that deleting one of her options because a mixture of the others beats it must be illegitimate; and (v) say whether the mixed deletion would still be licensed if we knew only Rowena's ordinal ranking of the nine outcomes together with Colin's cardinal payoffs, and if not, to give an explicit ordinally equivalent payoff table for Rowena in which no deletion goes through at that step.

Second, a model answer of roughly seven hundred words, with the arithmetic shown and the interval of mixing weights stated exactly.

Third, a marking scheme giving the marks for each part, totalling 25, and for each part naming the specific move that separates a first-class answer from an upper-second answer and the characteristic mistake that should cost marks. Two points matter to me especially and should be weighted accordingly: the epistemic reading of a mixed dominator, on which what the mixture shows is that the deleted strategy is not a best reply to any belief about the other player, so that its removal requires only rationality and not any independence or equilibrium assumption and commits the analyst to no prediction that the player randomises; and the fact that domination by a mixture, unlike domination by a pure strategy, is not invariant under order-preserving transformations of the player's own payoffs, so that the prediction is genuinely cardinal information and not merely ordinal. For the second of these, an answer of the very highest class will notice that uniqueness of the equilibrium is not preserved either, and will exhibit a second equilibrium of the transformed game.

Please write in British English throughout, keep the question self-contained so that no reading, source or named theorem is required of the students, and avoid anything that would reward a candidate merely for reciting terminology.
