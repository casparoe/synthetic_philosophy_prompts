My friend and I rewatched Interstellar last night and then argued for like two hours in the parking lot, and I need someone to settle it before I see him again. His take is that it's basically a longtermist tract: dying Earth, one narrow window to move the species off it, Cooper abandoning his kids, and the twist at the end where the beings who built the wormhole turn out to be our own far future descendants. He thinks the film is straight-up saying that securing an astronomically big future outweighs everything near-term. I think that's way too quick, because the one character who actually talks like a longtermist -- Mann, with his bit about overcoming the survival instinct to save the species -- is the villain who faked his planet's data and killed people to keep the mission alive. The film seems to want us to be horrified by exactly that reasoning.

So: who's reading it right? But please actually read the film rather than just use it as a peg, and please don't hedge into "it's a rich and ambiguous text." Take a side and argue for it, and be explicit about which scenes you're resting it on. A few things I want you to deal with rather than gesture at:

Brand's Plan B -- a few thousand fertilized eggs and one person to raise them -- is the film's version of an existential-risk hedge, and he knowingly chooses it over saving everyone currently alive. I've been calling the distinction "existence-securing" vs "option-preserving": making sure there are people at all, versus making sure the future keeps the capacity to notice its mistakes, revise its values, and build more than a bottlenecked survivor colony. Does the film care about that difference? On its own terms is Plan B a triumph or a quiet catastrophe, and does the ending on Cooper Station change the answer?

Also, Earth in this film isn't about to be wiped out by an asteroid; it's a slow civilizational collapse with billions of people still living in it, farming and getting dust in their lungs. The headline argument for strong longtermism is about sharp catastrophes that permanently foreclose the whole future. So is the film even depicting the case the view is built around, or is it resting on a weaker, uncontroversial "we owe something to the next generation" idea that nobody in the debate would deny? If it's the latter, that's a point against my friend and I want to hear it said plainly.

The "they are us" thing is what he keeps coming back to: if the future people are the ones who reach back and save us, then the value of the future literally causes the present that produces it, which makes the sacrifice self-justifying in a way real-world longtermism never gets to have. Is that a genuine philosophical point the film is making, or just a time-travel gimmick?
