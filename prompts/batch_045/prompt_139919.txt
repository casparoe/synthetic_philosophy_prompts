I'm a fourth-year PhD student finishing a dissertation chapter on the unity of consciousness and the metaphysics of subjects, and I've reached a point where I need help reconstructing a view that isn't mine.

Over the past two years I've assembled the verdicts of a senior philosopher — I'll call her V. — on how many subjects of experience are present in a range of cases. I have them from her published examples, a guest lecture she gave here, and some correspondence. As I have them:

1. Ordinary adult human, neurologically intact: one.
2. Adult in the first year after complete surgical section of the corpus callosum, speech in the left hemisphere only: two.
3. The same kind of patient fifteen years on, when the hemispheres have learned to exchange information through the environment — one hand arranges letters or objects, the other reads the arrangement, and the patient reports no sense of conflict: still two. She says explicitly that environmental cross-cuing does not merge subjects, however fluent it becomes.
4. A patient with complete callosotomy whose right hemisphere has never supported language or independent purposive action — no alien hand, no intermanual conflict, only coarse affective and approach-or-withdraw responses: two. So the verdict is not being driven by behavioral disunity or by there being two wills.
5. A patient with only the posterior portion of the callosum sectioned, with documented loss of interhemispheric visual transfer and an intact anterior commissure: one.
6. An adult born with complete agenesis of the corpus callosum, language competent in both hemispheres, no intermanual conflict, and demonstrable but slow and coarse transfer of simple visuomotor and affective information through the anterior commissure and subcortical routes: one.
7. A hypothetical pair of ordinary adults joined by a bidirectional cortical interface that transmits, involuntarily and without voluntary gating, perceptual detail, affect, and current intentions in both directions at high bandwidth: one — a single fused subject. She adds three provisos: the sharing must be bidirectional, it must be subpersonal rather than something the subjects do, and it must not be switchable at will. A unidirectional link does not fuse, and neither does one whose output the recipient can selectively attend away from.

Here is the case I want to put to her. A 41-year-old man had a complete callosotomy at 22 for intractable epilepsy; unusually, and because of a left-hemisphere injury in infancy with reorganization, he has language in both hemispheres. Fifteen years after surgery he is implanted with a bidirectional commissural prosthesis: paired arrays and a subcutaneous processor that, roughly every 100–200 ms, extracts from each hemisphere a compressed state vector — the currently dominant action-goal read off prefrontal and premotor activity, a scalar arousal-and-valence value, and a coarse category label for the dominant perceptual content (something like face, indoor scene, speech, tool) — and delivers it into the other hemisphere as a subpersonal input that the receiving hemisphere cannot gate, attend away from, or switch off. No spatial detail, no fine-grained perceptual content, no transfer of the experience itself. With the device on, the chronic intermanual conflict disappears and both hemispheres, probed separately, report a single undivided will and no sense of split experience; neither can describe the other's current perceptual detail; both can still be driven independently in forced-choice tasks that turn on fine perceptual discrimination. When the device is switched off for a day, the conflict and the divided-agency reports return.

What I need from you:

First, the candidate rules or principles that fit verdicts 1 through 7 jointly, stated precisely enough to be applied to a new case rather than gestured at. I want at least two that come apart on the prosthesis case, and I want to know where exactly they agree and why the earlier verdicts can't separate them. In particular, tell me which feature is doing the work in case 6 — residual neural sharing as opposed to merely environmental cuing, chronic developmental reorganization as opposed to acute surgical disconnection, bilateral language, or something else — and whether the prosthesis supplies that feature.

Second, what each rule predicts when the device is on: one subject, two, or some third verdict such as one subject with two experiential fields, or two subjects sharing a workspace.

Third, your best guess at what V. will actually say, with the reasons for preferring that rule over the others. Note one complication: with the device on, both hemispheres report unity, so first-person evidence stops discriminating, and I don't know which she weights when report and behavioral dissociation come apart.

Fourth, the single variant that would best discriminate the surviving rules — raising the buffer's content until fine perceptual detail is transmitted, making it unidirectional, making it voluntarily gateable, transmitting perceptual detail without conative or affective content, or transmitting goals alone — and which of those I should build the chapter around.

Fifth, and frankly most important to me: whether her verdicts commit her to the number being determinate here at all. If the honest reconstruction says the prosthesis case is borderline in a way that admits of no further fact, I need to know that, and I need to know what my chapter would have to concede in that case, because my argument requires her to give a determinate answer and I would rather reorganize around a different case than build on a reconstruction she'll reject. Relatedly: if she says one when the device is on and two when it is off, does she owe me an account of subjects dividing and reuniting, or of subjects coming into and going out of existence with the flip of a switch, and do her other verdicts constrain what she can say there?

Please don't spend words on who has defended which of these positions or on what I should be reading; the literature side is handled. What I need is the reconstruction, the prediction, and the discriminating case.
