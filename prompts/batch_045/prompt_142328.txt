I run a fortnightly lunch thing at work called Hard Problems, Cold Pizza — mostly backend and data folks, usually 10-14 people, 50 minutes in a room with a whiteboard and a projector I never bother with. Nobody there has touched philosophy since a freshman elective, maybe half of them are comfortable with Bayes' rule, and the other half check out the second a formula goes on the board. No real money is allowed (finance gets weird about it), so stakes have to be chips or candy or some stupid trophy that lives on the winner's desk.

I got nerd-sniped by this thing called the Judy Benjamin problem. From what I can tell the setup is: someone is dropped into an area split into a 2x2 grid, and the only message she gets back is a conditional — something like "if you're in the east half, you're in the south row" — and then people argue about what she should now believe. I probably have the original story half wrong and I honestly don't care about getting the canonical version right. The thing that hooked me is this: it feels like the message "tells you" one definite thing, but what it actually licenses depends on how the message got produced. At work I keep banging on about the same thing with logging — a log line tells you nothing until you know what else the service would have logged, and half our incident reviews are people treating a log line as a bare fact instead of the output of a particular branch. I want the session to land exactly there, but through something people do instead of something I say.

So: design the 50 minutes.

What I have in mind, but improve it if you can:

- Small groups, each one getting a different *protocol* — a codebook saying what the radio operator would have said if she were in each of the four cells. All the codebooks should produce the same sentence for the actual cell they're told about, but they should differ in what they'd produce elsewhere. Groups update from the codebook, not from the sentence.
- One group gets a straight yes/no version: she radios in asking whether the conditional is true and gets an honest yes. Another group gets the plain non-conditional message that rules out the same single cell. I do *not* want anyone told in advance that those two come out the same — I want someone in the room to notice it and say "wait, those are the same thing," and then have the room argue about whether that's a discovery or a bug.
- One group should get a noisy channel: the radio is dodgy, so there's a known chance the sentence got garbled into something else. That's the version closest to what we actually deal with at work, so I care most about it.
- Credences get bet, not stated. Give me a payoff scheme that makes honest reporting the best strategy, explained in one line with no calculus, using something like 20 chips spread over the four cells.
- Give me the worked numbers for each group's update so I can sanity-check what they're doing while they do it, plus a minute-by-minute timeline, what the facilitator says, and — more importantly — what the facilitator shuts up about. Nobody should hear any philosopher's name or any term of art until after the debrief, and possibly not even then.
- The debrief should map specific moments onto specific ideas: the moment two groups come back with different numbers from the same sentence, the moment someone insists the conditional just *is* the negated conjunction, the moment the noise group's answer stops looking like either of the others.
- Then the honest part: what will definately go wrong the first time I run this. People turning it into a logic puzzle, someone splitting evenly across all four cells out of spite, someone trying to game whatever scoring rule I use, the noise group doing something I didn't anticipate, and the person who demands to know which answer is the *right* one — I don't know, and I'd like a sentence I can say that isn't a dodge.
- And what the game structurally cannot show. Some part of the disagreement is presumably invisible to an exercise like this, and I'd rather hand it to them as a flat statement at the end than pretend the activity delivered it. Tell me which part that is.

Two more asks. First, re-skin it however you want — drone lost in a warehouse grid, request routed to one of four regions, whatever makes engineers lean in. I only care about the 2x2 grid plus a conditional message. Second, if you can make it work, give me a 5-minute closing variant where the message is about her *belief* rather than the terrain — roughly "once you've updated, you'll be more than 60% sure you're in the west half" — so they see the same machinery show up when the signal is about credence instead of geography. If that variant is a bad idea or collapses into something trivial, say so instead of forcing it. And please skip any reading list or citations at the end; I can't check them and nobody here will follow them.
