I'm auditing a graduate seminar on early modern philosophy this term, and we've just started on David Hume's 1757 essay Of the Standard of Taste. I have a background in art history rather than philosophy, so I'm relying a lot on secondary summaries; my brother, who works as a museum educator and who has read a good deal of popular aesthetics but no early modern scholarship, sent our reading group the following confident gloss of Hume's argument. He wrote: 'Hume begins by pretending to take seriously the proverb that there is no disputing about taste, but his real aim is to refute relativism. His headline claim is that beauty is not a quality in objects; it is just a feeling of pleasure in the mind of the observer, which sounds thoroughly subjectivist. To avoid the consequence that anything anyone enjoys is as good as anything else, Hume then says there is a standard of taste, and this standard is nothing but the shared sentiment of qualified critics. A qualified critic needs delicacy of taste — a fine-grained emotional sensitivity — together with good sense, practice, freedom from prejudice, and wide experience of comparison. If enough of these critics agree that a work is beautiful, their collective pleasure is the standard, and that is what makes beauty objective: it is the property of producing the appropriate pleasure in the appropriate sort of person. The upshot is that, on Hume's view, expert opinion about art is effectively infallible, and the essay's main achievement is to replace naive subjectivism with a tidy dispositional theory of beauty. The obvious problem is that this is circular: we identify the true experts by their liking the right things, but we identify the right things by the experts liking them. So Hume's attempted escape from relativism collapses back into it.'

I had always assumed that Hume was a straightforward subjectivist about beauty — that on his view beauty is just in the eye of the beholder — so the claim that he ends up with a standard sounded odd to me, and my brother's talk of 'objectivity' and 'infallibility' made me even more suspicious. I suspect the summary is unfair in places, but I don't know enough about the essay to say where. Could you please go through the gloss and give me an accuracy audit? Please don't just tell me to read secondary literature — engage the summary line by line and tell me what to trust.
