I'm 71 and spent most of my working life in experimental physical chemistry — X-ray work, mostly, so I'm thoroughly used to operational definitions. In my old lab we'd have happily said the lattice spacing just *is* what the diffractometer reads, and nobody lost sleep over it, because two different instruments gave you the same number. Since retiring I've been going to a U3A discussion group that's wading through happiness and wellbeing, and last week it went badly for me. We'd got onto utility, and one of the group, a retired economics lecturer, kept wheeling out "de gustibus non est disputandum" — meaning it, I gathered, as something like "people's choices reveal their preferences, so there's no fact of the matter about whether anyone's tastes are correct." He used it twice: once to say there's no sense in which my grandson's taste in music is worse than mine (fine, I'll grant him that), and once to brush off my suggestion that nudging — fruit at eye level, default enrolment into pensions, that sort of thing — might be justified on the grounds that what people actually pick often isn't what they'd want on reflection. It's that second use I can't get straight, because it seemed to be doing enormously more work than the first, and I couldn't make him say how.

My worry is this. Utility had been introduced to us as something like happiness or wellbeing, and by the end of the evening it had turned into a mere numbering of choices, and I genuinely can't tell whether that was a discovery, a definition, or a bait-and-switch. So what I'd like is a list — a proper menu, not one favoured reading explained at length — of the distinct precise claims the slogan could be making, from the flatly trivial to the strong and interesting. For each one, say whether it's a platitude, whether it's substantive and defensible, whether it's just false, and above all whether it would actually get my economist his conclusion about the nudging case. My own stab, which he waved away, was that "reveals" might mean "is constitutive of" (preference just is the choice behaviour, so there's nothing to dispute but equally nothing to discover) or "is evidence of" (choice is our best window onto something independent of it), and that the slogan slides between the two. If it's the first, I don't see how the theory could ever be wrong, which as an experimentalist makes me suspicious; though I gather economists bolt on a consistency requirement, that choices have to line up across different menus — the "strong axiom", I think it's called? — which is a bit like demanding that two instruments agree, so maybe that's where the content actually lives. Please say if that's right.

Two cases I'd like each reading tested against, since these are what got me going. First, my friend at the astronomy club: he'll take the cheaper of two eyepieces when a third, obviously rubbish one is sitting on the table beside them, but he'll take the pricier, better one when only the two are there. Did he "reveal" a preference for the cheap one, or for the good one, and does your favourite reading have anything to say about which is the real one — or does it just say that whatever he picks is ipso facto what he prefers, in which case I want to know what work the word "prefers" is still doing. Second, me at eleven at night eating a biscuit I definately don't want, in the sense that I'd rather not be eating it: is the slogan committed to saying that in some sense I prefer the biscuit? And if some readings say yes and others no, which is the one that could carry "hands off, people's own choices are the measure of their own welfare"? Also tell me plainly if either of my cases is a dud — something the slogan was never meant to cover — because I suspect the biscuit might be. Housekeeping: British English throughout (behaviour, colour, whilst), no papers or citations thrown at me, our library doesn't carry journals and I'm not chasing them up, and close with one straight line on whether "utility" in this man's mouth has anything left to do with happiness.
