Me and a mate have got into a bit of a loop over this and I could do with someone poking holes in both of us. He's very into the big-numbers side of things — effective altruism, tot up the good, save the most you can — and I've been reading on my own (no philosophy background, just whatever I stumble across) about the objection to utilitarianism that starts from the fact that people are separate: that you can't just bung everyone's happiness into one pot, because there isn't anyone who has all that happiness, there's just a lot of different people each having their own. Someone called Rawls gets credited with the phrase, I think, though I may have that wrong. Anyway, I re-read Never Let Me Go last month and I've got this feeling the book is doing something with that idea, but I can't tell whether it's the thing I think it is or whether I'm making it up.

The bit that got under my skin is what I've started calling 'the rota'. The donation programme doesn't grab one person and take everything; it takes a bit from each, then a bit more, on a schedule everybody roughly knows about. Tommy, Ruth, Kathy — they each go in their own order, each with their own carer, their own favourite tape, their own little pile of stuff. Nobody is merging them into one big heap. So my first thought was: the separateness point can't really be about the merging, can it? It must be about what the trade lets you do. But then I thought: if it is a straight trade — a few dozen donors finishing early so thousands don't — then what I object to is the trading, and the version of the separateness point that says the numbers don't count at all seems to commit me to not caring how many people get saved, which I do care about, a bit. Whereas the other version I've seen floating about — numbers matter, but not by adding them up, because each person is somebody you'd owe an explanation to — I can't work out what it would actually say here. Is my 'rota' thing pinning down something the philosophers miss, or is it just the same metaphor in a new hat?

And I want to be fair about where I might be cheating. You never meet the people who get the organs, you never see the arithmetic being done, and the folk running things never really defend it as more happiness overall — they seem to half-believe the donors aren't properly like them at all (that business with Madame collecting the artwork and then looking at Kathy like she's frightened of her, and the whole Hailsham project of proving there's a soul in there). So maybe it isn't a case of utilitarian aggregation at all, and using the book to beat up on utilitarianism is a bit of a swerve. And nobody runs: Kathy never says the programme should stop, and the nearest anyone gets is Ruth, near the end, trying to wangle a deferral for the two of them — an exception for two people, not an end to the thing. I don't know what to make of that.

So: give me a reading of the novel through this objection. Does it dramatise it, and which version — the 'numbers don't count' one or the 'they count, but not by totalling' one — and does the rota complicate either? Where does the book resist the reading: where would an honest critic say I'm using a novel about being lied to, being used, and never being asked, to carry an argument it isn't making? And if it does say something about adding people up, is it something the argument on its own wouldn't reach, or is it just the argument with nice wallpaper? I've got a book club in three weeks and I'd rather not stand up and talk confident rubbish.
