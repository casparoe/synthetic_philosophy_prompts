I'm working through a survey article on the realism/antirealism dispute, and its section on Fitch's knowability paradox has left me unsure how much to take at face value. The passage runs as follows:

> Antirealists hold that a proposition's being true amounts to nothing more than our being able, in principle, to find out that it is: every truth is knowable. Take some truth we do not in fact know — whether there is extraterrestrial life, say. The conjunction *there is extraterrestrial life and nobody has ever established this* is then also true, so by the antirealist's principle it is knowable as well. Yet nobody could know that conjunction: anyone who knew it would know that there is extraterrestrial life, and would also know that nobody had ever established this — which would be false, since they themselves would have just established it. So the conjunction is an unknowable truth, and the principle collapses. Since there are truths we do not know, there are truths that no mind could know in any circumstances, and truth cannot consist in verifiability. Antirealists have tried to salvage the principle by restricting it to propositions that do not mention knowledge, but that simply exempts the principle from whatever cases embarrass it, and it is motivated by nothing except the wish to avoid the argument. The argument is also more general than it looks: nothing in it depends on the details of knowledge. Any operator that is factive and that distributes over conjunction yields the same collapse, so the real moral is that no factive notion can be what truth consists in. It should be conceded, finally, that the argument assumes knowers are logically omniscient — that they know every consequence of what they know. Almost no one defends that now, and some writers accordingly read the paradox as a problem for idealized epistemology rather than as a refutation of antirealism.

From this the article draws two conclusions: (i) that at a minimum the argument establishes that truth outruns all possible evidence, so that realism about truth is the only position left standing; and (ii) that the restriction strategy, as stated, is circular.

Two things make me suspicious, though neither amounts to an argument. First, every proposition the argument actually produces as unknowable is one that already talks about somebody's knowing something; I cannot see how the conclusion is supposed to reach a first-order claim about galaxies or the past, but perhaps that is only the example's fault. Second, I have seen antirealists of the Dummettian sort described as claiming something about verification conditions, warranted assertibility, or provability rather than the flat modal thesis that all truths are knowable, and I cannot tell whether the article is attacking a position anyone actually holds.

Would you audit the passage claim by claim — what someone working on this would accept as stated, what is a tolerable simplification, what is subtly wrong, and what is just false? I am especially interested in four points: how much closure the argument really needs, and whether its reliance on knowing the conjuncts of a known conjunction is anywhere near full logical omniscience; whether the argument is essentially tied to a fixed agent and time, or whether it survives the reading on which *knowable* means knowable by someone or other at some time or other; whether the generalisation to arbitrary factive, conjunction-distributing operators is correct, and whether that generalisation supports the moral the article extracts from it; and whether the restriction strategy is as unprincipled as the article makes out, given what the antirealist is supposed to be saying about first-order subject matter. Finally: do conclusions (i) and (ii) survive once the passage is cleaned up, and does the article's own concession in its last two sentences sit comfortably with (i)?
