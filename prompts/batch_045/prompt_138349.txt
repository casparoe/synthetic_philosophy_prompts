I'm on the steering comittee for our lab's scalable-oversight pilot: two AI systems argue opposite answers to a hard technical question (does this patch close the vuln, is this benchmark contaminated, whatever), and a human judge with about 40 minutes and no domain expertise picks a winner. The idea is that the back-and-forth drags the wrong side's mistakes into the open. Last week's final round went badly: one debater produced a 60-page case, the other kept insisting there was a false step somewhere in section 4 but couldn't locate it in the time allowed, and the judge went with the long one for looking more complete. Over the weekend, with a whole afternoon, we found the bad step on page 31 — the short side had been right all along.

The thing I keep going in circles on is whether the protocol's premise is even coherent. Debate is supposed to help because spotting a flaw is meant to be cheaper than fabricating an entire fake case, but if you can bury one wrong step in 60 pages, then generating confusion looks cheaper than dissipating it, and the format just rewards whoever can produce more text. What I can't tell is whether that's a bug we can fix with rules or the whole thing collapsing.

Two fixes are on the table. One is a compression round: after the debate, each side restates its entire case in 500 words and the judge votes again on the short versions only. The other is a bounty rule: a debater who claims an error has to name a specific sentence, and loses on the spot if that sentence turns out to be fine. Draft the memo I'll circulate before friday's meeting. It should recommend one concrete course of action — let the result stand, rerun this round, adopt a standing rule, or some combination, with a time frame — and give reasons the judges and the participants could actually act on rather than general worries about AI safety.

Be honest about the strongest thing on the other side, which to me is that both rules just punish long or genuinely complicated reasoning, and that under them a slick, short, wrong arguement beats a messy, long, correct one. If that trade-off is unavoidable, say so and say why your recommendation is still worth it; if you think it means the pilot should be measuring something else entirely, say that instead and tell us what.
