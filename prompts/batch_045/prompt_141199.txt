I spent the better part of last night's walk home from a department drinks thing arguing with a friend—an unreconstructed hedonist—about a view I've been trying to build a thesis chapter on, and I came away with the unsettling sense that I may not have one position but several that I rotate between depending on who is pressing me. The view is a version of tranquilism, in my own formulation: what makes a life go better for the person living it is only the absence of craving and the absence of suffering, with nothing positive on the ledger—pleasure, enjoyment, and getting what you wanted count for nothing in themselves. I also add what I call the ceiling-is-zero clause: a life devoid of both craving and suffering isn't mildly good, it is at the very top of the scale, so there is no positive level of welfare for time to accumulate. Around that core I hold a cluster of further things, and I want to know whether the cluster is a package or a pile. (a) The core plus the ceiling clause, as stated. (b) In the case of the Settling—a slow-release intervention that damps the intensity and duration of wanting without touching the capacity for enjoyment, and that strips pain of its unpleasantness while leaving the sensory discrimination intact—I bite the bullet: someone who receives it at eighteen has a life that goes better for them, even if what it displaces would have been a career, a marriage, and a great deal of ambition; and I want to be able to go on saying, without contradiction, that I would refuse it myself and not be irrational to do so. (c) A doctor who slips the Settling to a competent patient who has refused it wrongs that patient, and the wrong isn't fully captured by any welfare loss. (d) What we owe a person, when we act for their sake, is exhausted by making their life go better for them; autonomy, truthfulness, and self-direction matter only as things that usually protect welfare. (e) Death is bad for the person who dies and not merely for those left behind—I am not an Epicurean about this—and it is worse the more good years it takes, which is why a child's death is a graver harm than a death at eighty-five. (f) It is better, in one respect, that the world contain contented people who never wanted much, and that betterness is a reason of some weight to bring such people about, even though it benefits no one, since existing is not a gain in welfare on my view. (g) In the applied chapter I want to keep operationalizing welfare with the standard self-report instruments—life-satisfaction ladders and positive-affect batteries of the sort health economists already use—because that is the only data anyone will fund me to collect. (h) The moment of relief, the first cold drink after the climb, is one of the great goods in a life; my friend says this is me smuggling pleasure back in, I say it is compatible with (a) because relief just is a craving ceasing. (i) Consider two retirees: Aldo, who never wanted much and is content, and Bea, who spent forty years wanting to build a boat, built it, and is now content. I hold both that the strenuous, aspiring life is the one worth choosing and the one I would want for my children, and that Bea's forty years of wanting are not, on net, a welfare cost to her, because the wanting is partly what makes the finishing what it is.

What I want is an audit, not a defense or a summary of the literature. Go through these and tell me which of them actually pull against each other and which only look like they do, and please rank the real tensions by severity instead of listing them as equally bad. In particular: say whether three or four of my problems are really one problem with a single load-bearing commitment, and if so name it; give me, for each tension, the cheapest available fix and what that fix costs me elsewhere in the set; and tell me which single revision would clean up the most of the mess at once. Flag clearly anywhere I am merely stipulating rather than arguing, and anywhere a tension I have listed dissolves on a distinction I have not drawn. Do not assume in advance which commitment I would give up—I genuinely don't know—and resist the temptation to dissolve everything by telling me tranquilism is only a theory of prudential value unless you also say what that move does to (d), (e), and (g). Finally, attach to each of your diagnoses a confidence level, and single out the one claim in your audit you are least sure of, with the reason why.
