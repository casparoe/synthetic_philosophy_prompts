**Q4 (25 marks).** In Everettian quantum mechanics every outcome of a measurement is realised in some branch. That raises a puzzle about probability which is standardly split into two parts.

(a) Distinguish the *incoherence problem* from the *quantitative problem*, and explain why a solution to one is not obviously a solution to the other. [8]

(b) Say what is wrong with "naive branch counting": assigning probabilities by counting branches and dividing by the total number. [7]

The answer, verbatim, typos theirs:

---

(a) First, the Born rule problem is not the same as the preferred basis problem. Decoherence fixes the branch structure for all practical purposes — the pointer basis gets picked out by the interaction with the environment — but it says nothing about how much probability each branch gets. The Born rule problem then splits in two. The incoherence problem: since every outcome happens somewhere, in what sense can one outcome be said to be more probable than another? There is nothing chancy in the physics, and no fact of the matter about which branch "I" will end up in, since I end up in all of them. The quantitative problem is separate: even if we grant that some kind of probability or uncertainty is coherent here, why is the measure |c_i|² rather than |c_i|, or equal weights, or anything else? These are independent, and that is why solving one doesn't solve the other — you could give a perfectly good account of self-locating uncertainty and still have no constraint at all on the numbers.

(b) Naive branch counting fails because the number of branches doesn't track the amplitudes. For a measurement with n distinguishable outcomes there is one branch per outcome whatever the c_i happen to be, so counting gives 1/n each, which contradicts experiment. Worse, the number of branches isn't even well defined: branching is continuous, and how many branches you get depends on how coarse-grained your description is, so there is no fact of the matter about how many there are.

(c) I'll take the second option. Briefly on Deutsch first: his 1999 argument is the cleanest route, and its really a result in decision theory rather than physics — any agent whose preferences obey the usual rationality axioms must bet as if the chances were the Born weights, and the only thing the argument takes from quantum mechanics is that measurements produce branching. Wallace's version just fills in the technical details.

The quantitative part is also easier than people make out: amplitudes are additive, and probabilities over exclusive alternatives have to be additive too, so the probability assignment has to be the square of the amplitude — no other function is additive in the required way.

My own proposal does better than either. Make the branching discrete. At a measurement with amplitudes c_1 ... c_n, produce N_i branches of outcome i, where N_i is proportional to |c_i|² rounded to an integer, the constant of proportionality K being enormous — 10^100, say — so that the rounding error is far below anything we could measure. The number of my future copies who see outcome i is then proportional to |c_i|², and probability is just self-locating ignorance: before the measurement I don't know which of my descendants I'll turn out to be, and when most of them see outcome i, that's what to bet on. So the Born rule falls out of the dynamics, and the incoherence problem is solved at the same time, since probability has been reduced to ordinary ignorance about where I am located, of the sort already familiar from anthropic cases. And it is barely ad hoc: one new constant is much less machinery than an extra postulate about probability.
