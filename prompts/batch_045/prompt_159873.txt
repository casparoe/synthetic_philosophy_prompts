Write a dialogue between two philosophers of mind who both accept a higher-order theory of consciousness but disagree about whether the higher-order state has to actually occur.

A holds the occurrent reading: a first-order sensory state is phenomenally conscious only when, and for as long as, the subject is actually in a higher-order state that represents her as being in it; if that higher-order state lapses, the first-order state may persist, but it goes dark. B holds the dispositional reading: a first-order state is conscious in virtue of being available to, or poised for uptake by, the subject's own higher-order system, so a state can be conscious throughout a stretch of time in which no higher-order state is actually occurring.

Give them one concrete case to fight over, stipulated rather than cited: a subject's visual system registers a briefly presented array while her capacity for introspection is wholly absorbed by a demanding concurrent task, so no higher-order state about the array occurs at the time; a moment later the load lifts and she reports the array's content accurately and says it looked like something to her. Have each say what her reading implies here, and then have them answer three further pressures rather than talk past them. First, the symmetry worry: introspection manufactures the higher-order state on arrival, so the two readings may never differ in anything observable — they should either say what would still tell them apart or concede that nothing would. Second, the external-monitor worry: a first-order state is also disposed to be represented by a device attached to the skull or by another person's thought about it, and that plainly does not make it conscious, so they must say what the subject's own system has that the device lacks. Third, the cost each pays: for A, that experience winks out whenever attention is entirely absorbed elsewhere; for B, that a subject can be conscious of a state she is in no position to register as her own.

Keep both speakers equally competent and have each respond to the other's actual argument. Close with them identifying what the disagreement really turns on — what consciousness would have to be for mere availability to be enough — without either side conceding. Keep the argument self-contained, with no appeal to published sources.
