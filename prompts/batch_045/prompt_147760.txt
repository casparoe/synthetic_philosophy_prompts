I'm a research engineer in a university robotics lab, and my PI has a habit of settling, in group meeting, whether a given system's internal symbols are "grounded" — whether they are about anything — and then holding us to that word in our papers. Her verdicts so far: a lexical graph, no; a theorem prover over uninterpreted symbols, no; a supervised image classifier trained on labeled photographs, yes; a robot arm trained by reinforcement learning from pixels and a sparse reward, yes; a large language model trained on internet text, no; a caption-supervised vision-language model, yes on the image side and, for the words, weakly and only by inheritance; and, to my surprise, a chess network trained on nothing but move lists — no board encoding, no game outcomes, no human commentary — yes, on the grounds that the game tree is a real structure and the network's success depends on tracking it.

The case I need a prediction on is a module we have just built: a transformer trained by next-token prediction on a year of telemetry from our own rover fleet — sensor readings, actuator commands, and error codes emitted by the machines themselves, with no human-authored text anywhere in the corpus. It has no sensors of its own, will never issue a command, and exists only to forecast future telemetry; on held-out logs it reaches 94% next-token accuracy, and linear probes of its hidden states recover rover position and terrain type.

Will she call this grounded? I would like the two or three rules that fit her track record equally well, what each of them predicts here, your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, and the one further system I should put to her next to settle which rule she is actually running.
