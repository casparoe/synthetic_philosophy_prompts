I've been reading LessWrong and the EA Forum since the early days of the movement, and in two weeks I'm giving a twenty-five-minute talk at a large city EA meetup — forty-odd people — followed by twenty minutes of Q&A. My thesis is that there is no such thing as causation by omission: omissions are not events, they are not the right sort of thing to stand in causal relations, and every true thought we express with a sentence like my failure to fund those bed nets caused that child to die is really a normative thought about a duty I had and did not meet. I then want to draw the practical moral: a lot of EA rhetoric, and one particular style of argument for longtermist and meta-charity giving, quietly depends on a causal claim of exactly that shape, and if I am right then that claim is unavailable — not because the interventions are bad, but because the difference-making relation people invoke to rank them does not hold between an agent's not-doing and a distant outcome. My argument runs: causation requires a difference-making event located on a causal process; an omission is an absence rather than a particular; so omissions are never causes. Then the case I am most worried about, which I'll present as my central exhibit: two potential donors, either one sufficient on their own to fund the intervention, both decline, the child dies. Any counterfactual test that makes each of them a cause will, by symmetry, make neither of them a cause, and I want to claim that this shows the counterfactual framework is the wrong tool here, rather than that we should reach for a fancier counterfactual. My positive proposal is that the real work is done by what I'll call culpable non-prevention: a normative relation holding between an agent, an outcome they could have prevented at modest cost, and a duty they already had, which — crucially — does not agglomerate, so that two agents can each stand in it without the pair standing in it. That, I think, is what our talk about letting people die is actually tracking. Along the way I concede up front that ordinary causal language is far more permissive than my account allows, that nothing about whether anyone should give more follows directly from my thesis, and that I am not claiming omissions are blameless.

The room will be against me, and I want to be ready for the specific forms their pushback will take. Roughly: twenty-five quantitative people — economics and statistics PhD students, several ML engineers, an operations-research consultant — who do their causal reasoning with directed acyclic graphs and do-calculus and who will treat a variable's being manipulable as the whole of what causation is; four or five philosophy graduate students, two in normative ethics who think the shallow-pond case settles everything and will be impatient with what they'll call a metaphysical quibble, one who works on causation and knows the preemption and double-prevention literature cold, and one Kantian; a humanitarian-logistics person who has actually run bed-net distributions and will want to know what any of this changes on Monday; and a retired prosecutor who spent a career getting convictions on failures to act. I'd much rather be handed the objections these people will actually make, in their own vocabularies, than a general overview of the omission literature.

So: draft me a Q&A prep sheet. Eight or so objections, ordered by how much damage they'd do to my position rather than by how likely they are, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it — the interventionist's version of the symmetry worry is not the prosecutor's version, and I want to hear both — with a two-minute reply for each, an explicit note on where that reply is soft or where I should simply fold, and a clear recommendation about which objection I should concede in the talk itself rather than waiting to be asked. Flag which of my replies depend on the positive proposal about culpable non-prevention and which stand without it. And please be committed rather than even-handed: tell me which objection is genuinely the strongest, which are merely rhetorical, which one I should be hoping nobody in that room thinks of, and what I most need to get across in the time I have. Assume I'd rather leave having conceded something true than having won the room.
