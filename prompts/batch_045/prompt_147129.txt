I am writing the decision-theory chapter of my dissertation and need a diagnostic handout for a graduate reading group: a taxonomy of the positions on the Death in Damascus case, built as a decision tree in which every branch node is a question a student can answer without already knowing which view it leads to, and every leaf is a named response together with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Please build it from the logical space rather than from a list of theories — first the separating questions and the answer combinations they permit, then the actual positions placed in that space, with any unoccupied combinations noted and briefly diagnosed as incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Aim for eight to ten branch nodes and six to eight leaves; assume an audience that knows Newcomb's problem but not this literature, and make sure no node asks anything as direct as "are you a causal decision theorist?"

Fix the background so the instability is measurable: two cities, survival worth vastly more than the small cost of travel, and Death as a predictor of where you will be rather than a pursuer who causally tracks you. Then run the tree over these variants, which are the cases I want the positions sorted by: Death's accuracy at 99% versus 70% with a known bias toward predicting flight; Death's prediction legible to you before you choose, because it is written on a card in a sealed envelope that cannot afterward be altered, versus opaque; the option, before choosing, to irrevocably delegate your destination to a device whose output you cannot predict and which degrades Death's accuracy to chance; and the option, after opening the envelope but before departing, to take a drug that erases your memory of what it said.

The leaves should include, at least: causal, evidential, and functional treatments; the requirement that a permissible act maximize expected utility conditional on your making it, and the question whether any act satisfies it here; mixed-strategy and deliberational-dynamics responses; the view that no act is rationally permissible and the case is a diachronic tragedy; and the view that the case is ill-posed because near-perfect external prediction of a deliberating agent is inconsistent with the agent's deliberating at all, so that the instability is an artifact of stipulations that cannot jointly hold. Finally, flag the pairs of leaves your tree cannot actually separate — my suspicion is that the causal and functional treatments diverge only outside this case, and that the ratificationist and mixed-strategy responses nearly coincide at 99% accuracy — and tell me which further variant would pry them apart.
