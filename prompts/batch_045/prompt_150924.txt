I'm in a graduate seminar on philosophy of biology, cross-listed with the ecology and evolutionary biology department, and we've spent the last four weeks on the adaptationism debate — Gould and Lewontin's 1979 Spandrels paper, the exaptation literature, and the methodological replies about how you're supposed to test an adaptationist hypothesis instead of just telling a story about one. Our professor is broadly sympathetic to the critique but isn't a caricature of it: she insists on a three-way verdict on every trait we discuss — adaptation, exaptation, or spandrel (by-product) — and she has now ruled on enough cases that I think she's running a single rule, even though she's never stated one. Her verdicts so far: a moth population that darkened after industrial soot blackened the tree bark and lightened again after clean-air legislation, adaptation, uncontroversially; human reading, exaptation rather than adaptation, because the visual machinery was built for other work and later press-ganged, and there's no evidence selection ever acted on reading ability specifically; male nipples, spandrel, a free rider on a developmental program shared with females; the panda's-thumb style case — a wrist bone that predates bamboo-feeding, got co-opted for stripping it, and was later slightly improved — exaptation for the bone-as-thumb, adaptation for the later enlargement, and if you ask about the thumb without saying which aspect, she says the question is badly posed; a hypothetical she invented herself about a beetle with an iridescent flank patch whose optical structure is a beautiful fit to a predator's visual system but which turns out, on comparative evidence, to predate the function entirely and to be present in an outgroup where it does nothing, verdict not an adaptation, on the explicit ground that the appearance of good design is not evidence of selection; a trait that was selected for function F in an ancestral environment, no longer does F, now does G, and is currently maintained by selection for G, verdict adaptation for G, with the F history demoted to a constraint on what was available; and a costly sexual display that lowers the bearer's survival but increases mating success, verdict adaptation, so whatever rule she's running isn't just about survival.

Next week I have to give a ten-minute response on a case she's assigned, and I want to predict her verdict before I write, because my response changes depending on what she says. The case is a desert lizard with an enlarged, flattened crest along the skull roof that it uses to wedge itself into rock crevices to escape predators. The stipulated facts are these. The crest is used for wedging now, and individuals with taller crests escape more often; a field experiment in which crests were shaved found reduced survival, but shaving also reduced overall head depth. Comparatively, the crest begins as a low ridge that in three sister lineages does nothing detectable and looks like a consequence of how the skull roof ossifies as the skull elongates. In the focal lineage there is strong evidence of selection on overall head depth, because deeper-headed males win male-male contests, and crest height scales almost perfectly with head depth. But what actually does the causal work in wedging is not the crest's height: it's the thickness and the angle of the crest's leading edge, and shape tracks head depth so tightly that nobody has found variation in it independent of depth. And yet the shape is an extremely good fit to wedging — precise, economical, and it fails when experimenters alter it.

Here's what I want from you. Predict her verdict on whether the crest is an adaptation for crevice-wedging, and separately on its height, on the shape of its leading edge, and on the wedging behavior, since she may well carve. Then give me the rule you think she's implicitly running, stated sharply enough that I could test it, and at least one or two genuinely different rules that fit her track record just as well but come apart on this case — say what each predicts and which one you'd bet on and why. Then tell me what further case or what further piece of evidence would actually tell the competing rules apart: which manipulation, which comparison, which population would I need? And finally, flag the one earlier verdict of hers that looks least stable under her own rule, since that's where I'd rather press in the response than on the crest itself.
