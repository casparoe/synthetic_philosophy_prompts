I write for a general readership on science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about why epistemologists have never settled whether knowledge is closed under known entailment. From the outside the debate looks frozen: since Nozick's tracking account and its sensitivity- and safety-style successors, the same moves have been repeated for decades. Closure defenders run the Moorean argument — I know I have hands, I know that having hands entails not being a envatted brain, so, closure granted, the skeptic's conclusion is false. Anti-closure theorists reply with barn-facade, lottery and disguised-mule style cases in which a subject seems to know p and to deduce q from it competently, yet we balk at crediting knowledge of q. Each side then accuses the other of leaning on intuitions it has no independent argument for, and nothing moves.

My working hypothesis is that the word 'closure' is holding together at least three different principles, and that the impasse is partly an artifact of that: (i) single-premise closure with an actual competent deduction, which almost nobody contests; (ii) multi-premise closure, where knowledge is aggregated across several known propositions and uneliminated risks seem to accrete across the inference; (iii) the anti-skeptical use, where the deduced proposition is the denial of a skeptical hypothesis, and where all the theoretical payoff lies. If the anti-closure intuitions are generated by the second and the motivation for closure comes from the third, the sides may not be denying one another's claims at all. I suspect that once the principles are separated, what remains is a real but different dispute: whether knowledge is a threshold notion marking that a belief is good enough to assert and act on — in which case closure functions as bookkeeping about what we are already committed to — or an anti-luck notion marking whether a belief's truth is attributable to the subject's tracking of the fact, in which case closure becomes a substantive question about whether those modal conditions compose.

Diagnose this for me rather than surveying it: which of these distinctions, if any, is doing the real work; is any part of the disagreement verbal, or a clash over which cases count as data, or a hidden question about how standards shift across contexts; and given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a formal result about the composition of modal conditions, a cleaner separation of questions, an experimental finding about the folk concept, or nothing at all? Please flag how confident you are in each claim and single out the point you are least sure of; if I use your diagnosis in the article I need to know where you are reasoning and where you are guessing.
