I'm trying to work out where a utilitarian of Mill's particular shape would have to stand on the experience machine, and I keep going back and forth. What I want from you is the answer derived from Mill's own commitments, not a tour of what anyone has written about him since — the argument, not the literature.

The case, fixed so we're talking about the same thing: Nozick's 1974 thought experiment imagines a machine that stimulates your brain so that you have, from the inside, an utterly convincing life of the sort you would most want — writing the novel, keeping the friendship, hearing the music, seeing it matter. You are floating in a tank; nothing you seem to do has any real effect on anything. Most people, asked, say they would not plug in permanently. Mill died a century before the machine was described, so I want you to tell me what he would have said had it been put to him — working from the qualified hedonism of Utilitarianism, with its distinction between higher and lower pleasures and its appeal to the preferences of competent judges, and from the argument about individuality and self-development in On Liberty.

Three variants, because I think they come apart for him:

1. The plain machine, but stocked only with the higher pleasures — the experiences of reasoning well, of love, of making something. Does Mill's quality test, applied from inside the machine, return "yes"? And if so, is that a reductio of his position or a consequence he should have welcomed?

2. The discriminating machine: two programs, one of higher and one of lower pleasures; the subject lives through both and then chooses, and simulated competent judges, having tried both, always prefer the higher. Does this satisfy Mill's criterion? And if a Millian wants to say that those judges are not competent because they have never known reality, is that a legitimate move — or is it cheating, given that on his own account the superiority of the higher pleasures is supposed to be constituted by just such preferences and by nothing behind them?

3. The transparent machine: you are told exactly what you are in, you know nothing you do reaches anyone, you may unplug at any moment, and you stay. Ask here whether what Mill describes recovering in his Autobiography — the crisis in which he found himself unable to feel anything at all, and the restoration of his capacity for feeling through poetry rather than through argument — is available to someone in the tank, or whether what he needed was precisely the feeling's being his, connected to a real self and a real world.

Two standing requirements. Where Mill's texts leave something genuinely open, say what you are attributing to him and on what basis, rather than closing the gap silently. And at the end, turn the case back on the theory: does the machine show that Mill's talk of the quality of a pleasure was always secretly a claim about the activity rather than the feeling? Does the answer he gives depend on his being a hedonist at all — would it be the same for someone who kept the On Liberty doctrine of individuality and dropped the doctrine that happiness is the sole end? And is the machine, for him, a counterexample, an anomaly, or a case his theory was never built to touch?
