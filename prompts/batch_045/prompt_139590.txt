Something's been bugging me. My bank refuses me a loan on the basis of a model, and under the GDPR I'm entitled to "meaningful information about the logic involved" in that decision. But I'll happily board an Airbus whose design no single engineer holds in their head, and take a drug that turned up by screening thousands of compounds, and nobody thinks Boeing or the chemist owes me an account of their reasoning. So we demand comprehension from the algorithm and not from everything else that's obviously competent without it.

Say the banks model is a black box that beats the human underwriters on accuracy and on measures of disparate impact, and say the only explanations on offer for its verdicts are what I've started calling explanatory placebos — they read well and reassure, but they don't predict or constrain what the model does on the next case. And suppose it genuinely tracks the right reasons (flip the relevant feature and the verdict flips, in the pattern a good underwriter's reasons would generate) without ever being able to state them.

What does this reason-giving requirement actually presuppose? Is it committed to the thought that a decision is legitimate only if someone can say why — that tracking the reasons can't come apart from telling them — or can it be reconstructed as a rule about contestability, dignity, or catching errors, one that survives the Darwin/Turing point that comprehension usually arrives late if it arrives at all? Be clear about what the practice strictly requires versus what merely fits it, and about where it's underdetermined — wich rival pictures of reasoning would all happily sign up to it. Then: if we dropped the comprehension demand and licensed these systems on demonstrated competence instead, by adversarial testing, counterfactual probes and strict liability for bad outcomes, what concretely would we loose, and who'd be worse off? Please answer in British English.
