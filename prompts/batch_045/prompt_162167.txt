I'm building a little side project with two friends (we're all CS grad students, no philosophy background) where the pitch is that we can figure out what people actually want from what they do instead of asking them. Self-report is garbage — people confabulate on surveys, they say they want to exercise and then don't — but behavior doesn't lie, and inverse RL looks like exactly the right tool: you watch the trajectories, you get back a reward function, and the reward function basically is the preference ordering. So we can skip all the mushy "value elicitation" stuff and read preferences straight off the clickstream. We're planning to try it first on the purchase logs from our department's coffee machine.

The part my friends think is a non-issue is the degeneracy worry — that tons of different reward functions can rationalize the same observed behavior, so the recovered reward isn't unique. My fix, which I'd like someone to sanity-check, has two parts. First, I only run the pipeline on what I've started calling a *saturated* demo set: every state-action pair that could matter gets visited at least once, and I also have the demonstrator's full action distribution in each state, not just the single action they happened to take. My reasoning is that if I know the whole policy rather than samples from it, the log-probabilities hand me the advantages and the reward falls out up to potential-based shaping. Second, I don't care about potential-based shaping and I don't care about the overall scale, since neither changes which policy is optimal — so the ambiguity that's left looks like exactly the harmless kind, and I can declare victory and treat whatever reward the solver spits out as the person's true preferences.

But the other night one of my friends threw two cases at me that I can't shake. Someone who genuinely wants to stop drinking, says so, means it, and drinks anyway every evening. And a guy who picks the cheap bottle of wine because he's misread the shelf label and thinks it's the expensive one. Run my pipeline on either person and I get a reward function on which drinking, or cheap wine, is what they're maximizing — and by my own argument that's their real preference, and the thing they say out loud is just noise. That feels wrong, but I can't locate the mistake, and it definately bugs me because the whole appeal of the project was that behavior is harder to fake than words. So: is the two-part fix actually sound, and if it is, why does it give such a creepy answer here? And if it isn't sound, which part breaks — can I patch it by setting the temperature β (the bit that controls how noisy we assume the demonstrator is) more carefully, or is the problem upstream of that?
