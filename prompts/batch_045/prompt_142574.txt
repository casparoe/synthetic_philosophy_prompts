Two people in my metaphysics reading group got into it after our session on the identity of indiscernibles and Black's two-spheres argument. They asked me to referee afterwards and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning. Roughly how it went:

Setup they agreed on: the only things that exist are two exactly similar spheres in an otherwise empty Newtonian space — absolute space, so rotation is a genuine absolute fact, no Machian worries. Both spheres spin at the same rate. In the version they were fighting over, the first sphere's spin axis is tilted 30° from the line joining their centres and the second's is at 90° to that line, i.e. perpendicular.

Raj: So the principle is fine here. The two spheres differ in a perfectly qualitative, structural respect — the angle between its own spin axis and the line joining it to the other one is 30° for one and 90° for the other. No haecceities, no bare thisness, no cheating with 'being identical to a'. Black's case only looks like a counterexample because he rigged the setup to be perfectly symmetric; add spin and the symmetry breaks.

Mei: Two problems. First, you haven't answered Black, you've changed the subject: point both axes along the line joining the centres, or both perpendicular to it and parallel to each other, and the symmetry is straight back, counterexample and all. Showing some two-sphere worlds aren't counterexamples doesn't show none are. Second, worse: your example runs on absolute rotation, and absolute rotation means Newtonian absolute space. But the identity of indiscernibles is a Leibnizian principle, the people who want it are usually relationists about space, and they're exactly the people who deny that rotation is absolute. You're defending the principle with a resource its own friends reject.

Raj: Fine, drop the spin. Give each sphere an internal marked axis instead — identical internal structure, all the same intrinsic properties, just pointed differently in space, one at 30° to the line joining them and one at 90°. No absolute space needed, same result. And on your first point: I'll grant that a genuinely symmetric arrangement is a counterexample if it's possible, but the test isn't whether the picture looks lopsided. Tilt them so the first one leans toward the other and the second leans away — 30° and 150° — and a reflection through the midpoint swaps them, so that one's symmetric after all; and whether they spin the same way or opposite ways makes no difference either. What matters is whether there's any isometry of the whole setup that swaps the two spheres, and in the 30°/90° case there isn't one.

Mei: Even the marked-axis version needs more than relationism gives you. If all spatial facts are relations among bodies, then with two bodies there's basically just the distance between them, and there's no room for facts about the angle between an axis and the line joining them — that 'line' isn't a body and isn't a relation between bodies either. So you've smuggled space back in as a thing. And once space is a thing, the symmetric case stops being a counterexample too, because then the two spheres differ by standing in different relations to different points or regions of space. So pick one: relationism, and your example can't even be stated; or substantivalism, and the principle is rescued by brute location, which Leibniz shouldn't have wanted either.

Raj: Brute location is exactly the kind of thisness the principle is suposed to rule out, so 'they occupy different points of space' isn't a rescue, it's a restatement of the problem. And on possibility: you keep saying the symmetric arrangement is possible and therefore the principle is false, but that's the very thing at issue. At least my case shows the argument needs an extra premise — that a perfectly symmetric arrangement of two objects is possible — and that's where the work is, not in the spheres.

Mei: Then here's what your view owes me. Take the symmetric arrangement and ask: would swapping the two spheres give a different possible world or the same one? If you say different, you're a haecceitist and you never needed the principle in the first place. If you say the same, tell me how there are two of them.

Can you go through it move by move and say who's right about which specific claim, and what — if anything — both of them are missing? I care less about who won overall than about which bits actually hold up.
