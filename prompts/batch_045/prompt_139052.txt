You're a philosopher of mind who works on perception. Take your time and think it through properly before answering — I want your real number, not a tour of the literature or a hedge like "it depends what you mean by perception."

The question: what's your credence that any of the well-known alleged cases of cognitive penetration — effects of what you're told, of learned categories, of language, of fear or desire on how things look — are genuine cases of cognition getting into perception, rather than effects on where attention goes, on how the scene gets organised, or on post-perceptual judgment and reporting?

Here's a distinction I want you to use, since I think a lot of the debate slides over it. Grade 1: the cognitive state changes what you notice or pick out. Grade 2: it changes how the scene gets structured — grouping, figure/ground, what counts as one object. Grade 3: it changes the values of the basic feature representations themselves, the colour and shape and size and motion the visual system attributes, the kind of thing that would show up in forced-choice discrimination or in an adaptation aftereffect and not just in people's ratings on a scale.

Give me exactly five numbered items, no intro, no conclusion, one or two sentences each:

2. your credence that the actual empirical cases clear the boring confounds — response bias, criterion shifts, task demands — and show a real change in experience at all
3. your credence, conditional on 2, that what's happening in those cases is Grade 3 rather than Grade 1 or 2
4. how those combine into your headline number, and which single term that number is most sensitive to
