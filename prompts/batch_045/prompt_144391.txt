I am trying to work out what I actually think about the bearing of practical stakes on knowledge, and I would rather start from my own verdicts than from a theory. Every case below has the same subject, Ada, and the same evidence: a fortnight ago she saw her bank branch open on a Saturday, she remembers this with the ordinary confidence one has in such things, she has not checked since, and the branch's Saturday hours have not changed. The question in each case is solely whether I am willing to say that Ada knows that the branch will be open tomorrow. I have listed my verdicts, with an asterisk where I am shaky.

1. Nothing rides on it. She would like to deposit a cheque; if the branch is shut she will come back on Monday. — She knows.

2. A great deal rides on it, and she could still check tonight: if she is wrong she misses a deadline and loses something she cares about deeply. — She does not know.

3. Late stakes. The belief is formed on Friday with nothing riding on it. On Saturday night, once the opening hours are irrelevant to anything she can still do, she learns that something enormous turned on whether the branch had been open that morning — and that it had not. — She knows. I cannot see how stakes that could never have guided her inquiry or her action could retrospectively take knowledge away from her.*

4. Invisible stakes. Unknown to Ada, a stranger has wagered a very large sum on whether the branch is open on Saturday. She never learns of it; nothing in her situation feels any different. — She knows, though I hold this less firmly than 1.*

5. Moral stakes. Nothing is at stake for Ada's own interests, but if she is wrong, a blameless colleague will be blamed for something she did not do, and Ada's acting on her belief is what would prevent it. — She does not know.

6. Exhausted inquiry. High stakes as in 2, but Ada has already done everything a reasonable person could do to check: the app, the notice in the window last week, a phone call that went to voicemail. There is no further evidence available to her. — She still does not know. I realise this is the point where the ought-implies-can pressure is greatest.*

7. Bad aims. A great deal rides on it, but only because Ada wants to use the Saturday opening to do something seriously wrong; her own project is what makes so much turn on the question. — She knows. A rotten aim should not raise the bar, and it would be stranger still if it lowered it.

8. Attribution. Ada's own stakes are as low as in case 1. Her colleague Bob, for whom a great deal rides on the branch being open, says of her: "Ada knows it is open." I want to say that Bob's remark is false, or at least that he should not make it; yet I also want to say that Ada herself may perfectly well say "I know it is open." — Conflicted.*

What I cannot reconcile is the pair 4 and 8. In 4, high stakes belonging to a third party leave Ada's knowledge untouched. In 8, high stakes belonging to the person making the attribution appear to spoil it, even though nothing about Ada has changed. That seems to pull me away from the view I was initially attracted to — that the standard for knowledge is fixed by what is at stake for the subject, with the meaning of "knows" itself held constant — and toward a view on which the standard is fixed by the context in which the attribution is made, or by the context in which it is assessed, if I am to keep both halves of 8. But then 4 is a problem as well, unless the contexts that matter are those of speakers and assessors rather than of anyone whose interests happen to be in the offing. Cases 3 and 6 pull against each other in a quieter way: stakes that arrive too late to be responded to do not count, while stakes that cannot be responded to at all apparently still do.

The principle I have been trying to make work is this: S knows that p if and only if S's evidence makes p sufficiently probable that, given what is at stake for S with respect to p, it would be rational for S to treat p as settled in her practical reasoning.

Please do the following with it.

Test it against 1 through 8 case by case, and where it fails, repair it if a small modification will do, or replace it with a candidate that does better. Name the view each repaired version belongs to — stakes fixed by the subject's situation with an invariant meaning, stakes fixed by the ascriber's context, or truth fixed relative to a context of assessment. Tell me plainly which of my verdicts the best version forces me to give up, and which of the starred ones it merely explains away. I would also like predictions on three cases I have not listed: an eavesdropper who overhears Bob's remark in 8 without knowing what is at stake for Bob; a continuum of cases in which the stakes rise by tiny increments, which raises the question whether there is a determinate threshold or whether the account leaves "knows" intolerably vague; and a case in which Ada's evidence is as good as evidence gets — she read the posted hours that morning — but the stakes are catastrophic, which raises the question whether the bar can be raised without limit, so that no finite evidence ever suffices.

One further thing. I would like to know how much of my pattern a purist can absorb without conceding anything about stakes: could 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 7 be explained by my tracking something else — shifts in what I take Ada's evidence to be, or conversational pressure on what may be asserted rather than on what is true? If so, say which verdicts are doing the real work and which are idle.

For what it is worth, I find arguments from cases most persuasive when the evidence and the subject's situation are held fixed and only the standpoint of the attribution varies, and I am unmoved by the thought that knowledge must be action-guiding in order for stakes to matter to it. If the honest answer is that no single principle captures all eight verdicts and that the pattern fragments into two different phenomena, say that too, and tell me where the fracture runs.
