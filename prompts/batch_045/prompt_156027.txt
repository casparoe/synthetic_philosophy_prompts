You are a professional philosopher who has spent a decade brokering adversarial collaborations and drafting the joint statements that come out of them. You are known for formulations that are neither vacuous nor quietly slanted: every sentence you write could be signed by both parties without either feeling sold out. Work carefully and do not rush to the document. Begin with clearly marked scratch notes: brainstorm candidate agreed claims, candidate cruxes, and candidate disagreements, and tag each candidate disagreement with your working guess about whether it is substantive or merely verbal (and if verbal, what distinction would dissolve it). Only then write the deliverable. Do not adjudicate, do not referee, do not add your own verdict on who is right, and do not hedge the agreed claims into mush. Assume you have no library access: argue from the positions and the cases themselves, and do not cite, name-check, or build bibliographies of secondary literature.

Task. Draft the joint statement that a Cliffordian evidentialist and a Jamesian permissivist about the ethics of belief could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Clifford's strict line is the one he summed up in the dictum that it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone to believe anything upon insufficient evidence; James's reply is that our passional nature not only may but must decide an option that is genuine — forced, live, and momentous — and that cannot be settled on intellectual grounds, since declining to decide is itself a decision with the same risk of losing truth. Call the two signatories E (evidentialist) and J (permissivist).

Scope stipulation, which both parties have accepted in advance. The collaboration restricts itself to what they jointly call outcome-dependent propositions: propositions P such that, holding the agent's evidence fixed, the agent's believing P (or her believing it with a distinctive confidence) alters the objective probability that P is true — typically by way of persistence, recruitment of others, or discouragement of rivals. Examples: this campaign will win, I can stop drinking, our department will rally behind the proposal, this patient will recover. Outcome-independent propositions (that a given vessel is seaworthy, that a coin landed heads) are not in the primary scope, but both parties must record explicitly whether the strict rule governs there without exception, or whether J reserves some outcome-independent cases as well, and if so which (for instance where the evidence is intractable but the option forced). If, once this is recorded, the live dispute turns out to be mostly a dispute about how large and how important the outcome-dependent class is, say so plainly.

The document must contain the following, in this order.

1. Agreed claims. Eight to ten numbered claims, each a single sentence, each something a reasonable outsider would have thought contested. No filler: do not include claims like both sides care about truth or evidence matters sometimes. Candidates to consider include whether belief is directly voluntary; whether there are norms governing belief as opposed to only norms governing inquiry, assertion, and attention-management; whether a belief's permissibility can depend on effects on third parties and not only on the believer's own accuracy; and whether the parties share a common standard of what would count as improving or degrading a person's long-run calibration.

2. A disagreements table, with columns: issue; the formulation E would sign for his own view; the formulation J would sign for hers; and substantive or verbal — and, for the verbal ones, the distinction that dissolves them and the residual question that is left once the words are disentangled. Pay particular attention to whether the parties are arguing about belief, about credence, or about a third state: acceptance, understood as treating a proposition as true for the purposes of action, planning, and reasoning while withholding belief in it. State which of J's favored goods can be purchased with acceptance alone and what, if anything, is left over.

3. A localization ladder. Place the disagreement on a five-rung ladder of increasingly strong theses, from the claim that there are no norms on belief at all up to the claim that no passional consideration ever bears on the permissibility of any belief, and record for each rung whether E accepts it, whether J accepts it, and whether the rung does any work — that is, whether moving it one way or the other would change either party's verdict on any case. Identify the rung at which they actually part and say which rungs had been doing the rhetorical work in the historical exchange while doing no logical work.

4. Rulings on four cases, in neutral wording, where each party must state its verdict and the one sentence of reasoning that carries it. (a) Clifford's shipowner, who quiets his doubts about his vessel rather than having her surveyed and sends trusting emigrants to sea. Both must classify the case: is the proposition that the ship is sound outcome-dependent? Is the proposition that my doubts are groundless partly self-fulfilling, by way of suppressing inquiry? Where exactly does each side locate the wrong? (b) James's own investigator: the chemist who finds a hypothesis live enough to spend a year verifying it, whom James counts as believing it to that extent. Both must say whether this is belief, acceptance, or something else, and whether the permissivist's best cases thereby collapse into the category the evidentialist already allows. (c) A prognostic clinician who adopts a confident belief that a patient will recover when the evidence supports a substantially lower figure, where the confidence predictably improves adherence and mood and may itself shift the outcome. Both must address whether the benefit is obtainable by acceptance, what is lost when the clinician must disclose an honest prognosis, and what the costs are to her future calibration. (d) An organizer or founder whose venture succeeds partly because she believed, before the evidence supported it, that it would. Both must address a wrinkle neither usually handles: whether permissive belief in such cases needs a consent or opt-in condition, since the belief's self-fulfilling mechanism works by recruiting others who have not themselves chosen the venture, and whether third-party effects of the kind Clifford stressed about credulity's social spread make the case different from the purely personal one.

5. Cruxes, with predictions registered in advance. Give at least three. For each: the sub-question; why it is a crux (which side it would move, and in which direction); the test, argument, or case both parties agree bears on it; E's prediction; J's prediction; and, for each side, the sign-flip condition — the result that would move that side and not merely be explained away. Cruxes worth considering include: whether training in confidence-first belief cultures degrades calibration on unrelated, outcome-independent forecasting items, and how both parties would read a null result; whether acceptance can substitute for belief in the motivational and interpersonal roles J cites, and what the failure mode looks like when the agent knows perfectly well that she is only accepting; and how large the outcome-dependent class really is, given our tendency to over-attribute success to our own antecedent confidence, with both parties stating what measurement design would separate self-fulfilling confidence from selective memory.

6. What would move whom, and what will not be settled. A short section in which each party names the single consideration most likely to change its own mind, plus an honest joint note on which cruxes are in principle testable but, given institutional realities, are not going to be tested — and what follows for how a reasonable person should act under this unresolved disagreement.

7. What we are not saying. Three to five lines blocking the predictable misreadings: that J endorses wishful thinking, that E forbids hope or action under uncertainty, and so on.

Aim for 1,200 to 1,600 words for the finished document, excluding scratch notes. Write every disputed formulation twice, once as E would sign it and once as J would, and do not let either formulation contain a term the other side would reject as question-begging.
