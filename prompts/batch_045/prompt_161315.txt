hey — I'm a PhD student (phil, decision-theory side of things) and I'm TAing our upper-level "Agency over Time" seminar this term; I've been asked to write one of the three questions on the take-home midterm and I want it to be on akrasia and hyperbolic discounting. But I defintely don't want another pile of essays that just say "present bias means we overweight the present, so we eat the cake even though we planned not to, the end." Every single person in that room can recite that. The question needs to force them onto the bit that's actually hard: whether a present-biased agent ever really acts *against her own judgment*, or whether the model is just relabelling an ordinary change of mind as a failure of will.

Some material I want built into it — use it, but reword/reorder however reads better on an exam paper:

- A case with two characters rather than one. Mira is a textbook hyperbolic discounter: at 9am she ranks finishing her dissertation chapter above going to the pub; by 4pm she's reversed and she's at the pub, and at 4pm she'll cheerfully tell you the pub is the better option *now*. Sam is time-consistent — no reversals, roughly exponential — but still ends up at the pub at 4pm while saying, perfectly sincerely, "I know I should be writing." So reversal-without-conflict and conflict-without-reversal. I want students forced to say which of them, if either, is the weak-willed one and on what grounds, instead of shrugging "both, kinda."
- Make them be explicit about *whose* judgment and *at what time* the action is supposed to be against. I'm pretty sure most of them are silently equivocating between the 9am judgment and the 4pm judgment, and that's where the whole "hyperbolic discounting explains akrasia" story quietly does its work.
- A thing I've been calling the relocation test in section: hold the gap between the two options fixed and slide the entire decision a week later (or imagine the same 9am/4pm pair happening tomorrow instead). Mira's 4pm preference flips straight back to the chapter. So: does the fact that her preference is unstable under pure time-shifting show that the 4pm preference isn't really *hers* / isn't her all-things-considered judgment — or does it just show that she has time-indexed preferences and that's the end of the story? That's the crux I want them stuck on.
- The "sophisticated" variant: a discounter who perfectly anticipates her own reversal. Make them handle the awkward bit everyone skips — that knowing you'll cave can make you cave *earlier*, or otherwise leave you worse off than the naive agent who at least forms the plan to resist; and that Ulysses-style precommitment only does any good if you can bind yourself before the reversal point hits. Anyone who treats sophistication as just strictly better than naivety should lose marks for it.

Constraints: take-home, 48 hours, answer capped at 900 words, 20 marks. It should be the kind of question where a strong B and an A are both "right" but the A makes one extra move.

What I need from you:

1. The question exactly as it'll appear on the paper, with the word cap and the marks stated on it.
2. A model answer of roughly 700–800 words — the sort of thing a top student would actually hand in, with a real argument and at least one concession in it, not an encyclopedia voice.
3. A marking scheme: five must-hit moves, marks for each (totalling 20), and one line per move on how people typically fudge it. Then say explicitly which single move is the one that seperates an A from a strong B — the one thing whose absence caps someone at, say, 15/20.

Two more things: don't put any references or further reading on the question (I don't want them scrambling for papers), and go easy on named positions and names generally — describe the views in plain terms. The class is mixed phil/econ/psych and about half of them freeze up the moment somebody drops a name.
