I'm retired now — ran a field ecology group for most of my working life — and for the last couple of years I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group down at the university, where I'm basically the old guy who keeps asking for definitions. We've been doing norms of assertion, and I can't let go of an idea of my own that I think is getting ignored. Call it the calibration norm: an assertion is permissible only if, across the class of assertions the speaker makes at a given confidence level, the proportion that turn out true roughly matches that confidence. If you assert things flatly, with the manner of someone who is certain, and only about half of what you say in that manner is true, you are asserting badly. Its a norm on the practice or the policy, not on the single act, and I know that is half of what is in dispute — don't smooth that over for me, use it.

What I want is a case to bring to the group. One paragraph, ordinary life (a field station, a survey crew, a lab meeting, a clinic, whatever you like — nothing science-fictional, no perfect predictor, no demon), in which:

- the speaker flatly asserts that p;
- p is true;
- she knows that p — plainly, with no Gettier machinery, no fake barns, no lotteries; on this occasion she did the work and checked;
- her believing it is reasonable, even exemplary;
- and still the assertion strikes us as impermissible, and the reason is not tact, politeness, confidentiality, or the avoidance of some bad outcome. It is epistemic: her track record is bad, and the people hearing her have no way to tell which of her claims are the ones she actually checked.

What the case is for, so you know what counts as cheating: the man in the group who defends the knowledge norm keeps insisting that every case where someone knows and yet shouldn't assert is really about etiquette or consequences, so it never touches the norm of assertion itself. I want a case where the shouldn't is epistemic the whole way down. So don't let the impermissibility come from rudeness, secrets, or downstream harm, and don't stipulate the verdicts — I want them falling out of the four norms.

Then check it for me. State the proposition asserted, the basis she has for it, and the reference class and hit rate you're using for my norm — and tell me which of the two obvious reference classes you picked and what that choice costs you (everything she asserts, versus only what she asserts flatly at that level). Then say what the truth norm, the reasonable-belief norm, the knowledge norm, and the calibration norm each deliver and why. Tell me which feature of the case is doing the real work, and what happens if I remove it — say, if the hearers could tell which of her claims were the checked ones. And give me the strongest thing my knowledge-norm friend can say back; I'd rather here the real objection than a strawman.

Start with the scenario itself, one paragraph, no lead-in, no prefacing sentence like Here's a case that works, no restating what I asked for. Put the analysis underneath it, and open that with a single line saying whether the case actually does what I wanted.
