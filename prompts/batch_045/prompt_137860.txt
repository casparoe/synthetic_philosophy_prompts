Hey — I'm in a reading group doing a couple of sessions on Frankfurt cases, and I've ended up defending something I can't figure out how to get evidence against. The view: the classic set-up really does refute the principle of alternative possibilities — Black watches Jones, would force the vote if Jones were about to waver, Jones votes on his own anyway, no intervention occurs, and Jones is responsible even though he couldn't have done otherwise. I actually think I have a stronger version, which someone at the group threw out and I've been calling the coin-flip case: Black doesn't wait for any prior sign that Jones is wavering, he just flips a fair coin (or watches a Geiger counter) and forces the vote iff it comes up heads. Now there's no reliable prior sign for anyone to exploit, nobody could have predicted in advance whether the vote would be Jones's own doing or Black's, and in the branch where Jones votes on his own he couldn't have done otherwise — but it is his vote, made for his own reasons. If the original case works, this one seems to work better, and it makes the "there must be a flicker of freedom somewhere" reply feel like it's reaching for a technicality.

So: what would count against this? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want the ones that a committed actual-sequence / reasons-responsiveness theorist would concede are genuine problems, not just the ones their critics like to raise. A few things I'm especially unsure about, in no particular order: whether the argument only works for blame and not for praise or credit (if it doesn't generalize to "couldn't have done better," I'd take that as evidence something's off); whether the flicker-of-freedom reply and the retreat to "robust" alternatives are real advances or just moving the goalposts one level down; whether the intuition in these cases is tracking the absence of alternatives at all, or is tracking the fact that Black's presence makes Jones's agency look bypassed or manipulated — I find that variations in how the intervention works move me more than variations in the metaphysics, which seems like it might be bad news; and whether my coin-flip version is a genuine improvement or secretly a different (and worse) case, since it makes whether Jones acts on his own a matter of luck.

I'd also like an honest verdict on how exposed the view is. Every time I push on it I end up saying "well, PAP was never really about that kind of alternative possibility," and I can't tell whether that's a discovery or a way of insulating the position so that nothing could count against it. If it is insulated, is that actually a problem for a view of this kind, or is it fine given what the view is supposed to do? Two side things if they're relevant: I tried telling a version of the case where the "agent" is an LLM whose output is monitored and would have been rewritten if it started going off-script, and nobody is tempted to call the model responsible — but I can't tell if that's about alternatives or about it not being the right kind of thing to be responsible at all, and I'd like to know whether that comparison actually tells us anything about the Jones case. And I'd be more moved by arguments and cases than by survey data about what ordinary people say, though tell me if you think that's a mistake. Aim it at someone who's read the literature — no need to explain what PAP is.
