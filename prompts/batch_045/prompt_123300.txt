I am a novelist, and the request I want to make is closer to engineering than to commentary. I should be grateful for a response that treats it as a design brief.

The book I am finishing turns on a scene I have not yet managed to make honest. My protagonist is caught inside a small, closed system of rules — a game, a procedure, a liturgy of permissions — and the drama requires that she get out of it. Not outwit it, not find the clever sequence; get out of it. She must stop playing for a reason that is a reason and not a mood. Every version I have drafted makes the exit a matter of temperament: she gives up, or she has an intuition, or a second character tells her. I want the exit to be forced by the structure of the thing she is inside, so that a reader feels the necessity of it in the same way one feels the necessity of a good proof.

What I need from you, then, is a puzzle built to order. I have in mind the lineage of Post production systems — Hofstadter's MIU puzzle is the familiar instance, with its unreachable target and the invariant that settles the matter from outside — but I do not want a retelling of that puzzle, and I do not want an exposition of what jumping out of the system means. I want a new instance, designed so that three positions on what such a jump consists in return three different verdicts about my character's situation, and designed under constraints that make the thing usable in a novel.

The object: a miniature formal system, statable in four rules or fewer, with no randomness, together with a target state the character is trying to reach. My character is not a mathematician, and my reader is not one either, so the rules and the target must be describable in a paragraph of ordinary language. I am happy to receive the system as bare string-rewriting and do the dressing myself.

The specifications:

1. The target is unreachable by any legal sequence of moves, but not trivially so. The space of reachable states should be large enough that a person working by hand could not settle the question by enumeration, and small enough that the question does not turn on computation. I want the in-system search to be genuinely hopeless but not visibly hopeless.

2. There is at least one near miss: a reachable state that differs from the target in one feature, of a kind that a reasonable person working inside would read as encouragement. My character should be able to believe, for good internal reasons, that she is close.

3. No move within the system, and no quantity of cleverness within it, can establish unreachability. The system has no vocabulary in which the claim that the target cannot be reached can be stated, so that for someone playing, not yet found and impossible are indistinguishable.

4. From outside, unreachability follows from a single property that every rule preserves — something countable, colourable, or arrangeable, expressible without jargon — in an argument a patient reader could verify in half a page. No oracles, no completed infinities, no appeal to a machine, no step that is merely asserted to be obvious.

5. The jump is forced rather than optional. Given the first and fourth specifications, continuing to play is provably a waste, and so the character's exit is an achievement of knowledge, not a decision of temperament. This is what the scene is for, and it fixes what would count as cheating: a system in which the verdicts are stipulated rather than proved is useless to me.

After the system, I want the design shown to work. Give me the verdict of each of three positions, and why each follows from the rules rather than from your say-so. First, the view that reasoning is rule-following: for it, my character can never know she is stuck, and the puzzle is, for her, exactly as it appears — a hard one. Second, the view that she can reason about the system: for it, naming the invariant and arguing over the length of a possible derivation yields a proof that no amount of play could, and the exit is real and is the only exit there is. Third, the regress worry associated with Carroll's dialogue between Achilles and the tortoise: for it, the step that licenses the outside argument is not itself licensed by the system's rules, so the outside is only a larger inside, and nothing has been escaped.

Then, briefly and honestly: which single feature of your system does the work; what the case collapses into if that feature is removed; how a defender of the first position and a spokesman for the third would attack your design; and whether any part of the outcome is stipulated rather than derived.

Finally, two things for the book itself. Give the puzzle a name my character could use. And give me one sentence — hers, at the moment the jump lands — that a reader would feel rather than need to be told.

Please keep the whole response under about nine hundred words.
