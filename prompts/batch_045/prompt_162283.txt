I don't have any academic background — I work in insurance claims and I moderate a neighborhood Facebook group — but I keep walking into the same kind of argument, and what I want is a tool for it rather than a reading list. I have no library access, so please don't send me toward papers or authors or titles; I need something I could actually run at a kitchen table. The shape is always the same: two people agree on every fact anyone could check, agree that they agree, and still won't stop. My brother-in-law and I went round for an hour on whether his sourdough starter is alive, having already settled everything about metabolism, reproduction, and cells. Our group burned a whole meeting on whether the vacant lot on Fourth Street counts as blighted, which matters because the wording of a city grant turns on it. And when I read online that enormous old questions — whether numbers exist, whether there is really a table there in addition to the particles — might be nothing but disputes about words, I can't tell whether that's a deep point or a way of changing the subject.

So here's the ask: write me the specification for a test that tells you whether a disagreement is merely verbal, meaning a clash of meanings dressed up as a clash about the world, or genuinely substantive. The purpose fixes the standard: this has to be usable by the people having the argument, in real time, with no philosopher in the room, on disputes where the facts are already settled. Give me each requirement together with the case that motivates it — a case I could actually hand to someone — and why the test is broken without it. What it must handle: disagreements that survive after both sides stipulate the same definition; cases where one person is simply using the word incorrectly rather than using it differently; disputes about where to draw a line on a continuous scale, like whether a lot is blighted or a room is clean; terms whose meaning is fixed in advance by a contract or statute; disputes about things everyone agrees don't exist; and cases where the two sides are covertly arguing about which meaning we ought to adopt instead of about the facts. What it must not do: rule everything verbal just because the parties agree on the evidence, or treat a verdict of verbal as the same verdict as pointless. It also has to say what is left of a dispute once it is declared verbal, whether the real argument has moved to which usage we should adopt, and whether the test should give the same verdict for the sourdough case and the existence-of-numbers case or whether the stakes are allowed to change the answer. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable for my purpose and which are preferences that someone with different aims could reasonably drop.

The part I most want is the tensions. I suspect there is a real conflict between requiring that the verdict depend only on what the two speakers mean and what facts they have, and requiring that the test never dissolve a dispute whose classification carries money or legal consequences, but tell me if there is a worse pair, and if some subset of the list cannot be satisfied together then say plainly that the specification itself has a hole in it. Then: which single requirement does the most work in knocking out the obvious candidate tests — including the one my friend keeps proposing, where you ask whether each side would take a small bet on their claim — and one paragraph on how the two approaches I can picture hold up against the list, the one where you replace the contested word with a made-up word and see whether the argument survives, and the one where you say the disagreement was really about which usage to adopt all along. No scorecard beyond that paragraph; the criteria are what I am paying for.
