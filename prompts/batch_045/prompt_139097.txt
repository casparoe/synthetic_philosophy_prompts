I teach a graduate seminar on the nature of well-being, and for roughly ten years I have been arguing with a colleague in the medical school — a palliative-care physician who does ethics consultation — about what exactly is bad about pain. I've been trying to reverse-engineer her, and I now have a table of her verdicts on the cases we've discussed, each on two questions: is any of this bad for the patient, and is any clinical intervention indicated. (i) Ordinary severe post-operative pain, high intensity, high unpleasantness, patient wants it gone and cannot follow a page of text: bad for her; analgesia indicated. (ii) The classic pain-asymbolia cases: the patient reports a full-intensity burning pain, localizes it precisely, and is entirely unmoved — says it does not bother him, does not want it to stop, goes on with his crossword, and declines an analgesic if one is offered: nothing bad for him here, no analgesia indicated, there is nothing we owe him relief from. (iii) Opioid analgesia in which intensity ratings barely budge but the patient says the pain is still there and no longer matters, stops grimacing, and returns to reading: the bad-making feature has been removed, continue the drug, the residual sensation is not itself a welfare cost. (iv) Congenital insensitivity to pain: badly off, but only derivatively, because of the accumulated unchecked injuries; she denies that pain is bad in virtue of its warning function, and has said that a subject with a pain that warned of nothing and accompanied no damage could still be badly off in virtue of the experience. (v) Air hunger in a paralyzed, ventilated patient — no tissue damage, no pain, overwhelmingly awful, wholly absorbing: bad for him, opioids indicated, and the fact that it isn't pain makes no difference. (vi) A man at 9/10 intensity and 9/10 unpleasantness who cannot hold a conversation, who says he knows it is awful and would like it to stop, but who refuses medication and does not want to be asked again: bad for him and analgesia indicated; she treats the refusal as a question about consent, not as evidence about welfare. She has also said more than once that the good of a pain's ending is proportional to how bad the pain was, and that where there is no relief there was nothing to be relieved of. I take the record to have already ruled out two views: that the intensity of the sensation as such is the bad-making feature, and that pain matters only as a signal. What I cannot tell is whether she is running something like a valence view (badness is the experience's felt unpleasantness, its bother or sting), something like a demand or capture view (badness is an experience's involuntary seizure of attention and disruption of ongoing agency), or something like a desire view (badness is a matter of what the subject wants, occurrently or on reflection). On her earlier cases these all say the same thing, so I built a case to separate them.

The case is invented — a congenital channelopathy I use in seminar and call Ardley–Okono syndrome, so please don't try to look it up. Patients have entirely normal detection thresholds and normal intensity scaling: for a standard noxious stimulus they rate 8/10, localize it, describe it as burning or stabbing, and their ratings track stimulus intensity just as controls' do. But they score 0 or 1 on unpleasantness scales and say things like it doesn't bother me, I feel it fine, it just doesn't matter. They never request analgesia and decline it when offered, including after being told the drug has no cognitive side effects. They withdraw reflexively, protect injured limbs, and do not accumulate injuries, so there is no derivative harm of the sort that drove her verdict in (iv). Here is the complication: during an episode they break off mid-sentence, their performance on a concurrent reading-comprehension and working-memory task falls to exactly the level of controls in equal-intensity pain, their pupillary and skin-conductance responses and facial action units match controls, and afterwards they report having lost the thread and cannot say what they had been thinking about. When the episode ends they report no relief and show no positive contrast; before a scheduled stimulus they show no anticipatory anxiety and no avoidance learning. Several of them, having noticed the disruption, have asked that painful procedures be scheduled outside working hours, and they describe the episodes as bad for my schedule but not bad for me.

So: what will she say about whether anything here is bad for these patients, and about whether anything is clinically indicated? Which of the rules I listed — or some fourth one I haven't thought of — best fits the track record, which of them fit it equally well but come apart here, and what does each predict? Give me your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, and say how you would handle the two details that pull in opposite directions, namely the absent relief and the scheduling requests. Finally, tell me which single variation on the syndrome, or which further case, would most cleanly tell the surviving rules apart: I want to know whether to add a variant with intact relief, one with genuine occurrent aversion, one in which the attentional capture is pharmacologically blocked while the unpleasantness is left in place, or something else entirely. No need for citations or reading recommendations — I want the argument, not the literature.
