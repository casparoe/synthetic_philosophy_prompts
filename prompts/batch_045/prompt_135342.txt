Hi. I need some help drafting something, and I'm out of my depth on the philosophy side of it.

I'm on the management committee of a small nature reserve trust — around 200 hectares of old gravel workings, reedbed, scrub and some wet grassland. I spent about forty years as a field biologist before I retired (freshwater invertebrates, mostly), and since then I've got into reading philosophy, which is where I've picked up an argument I can't shake and also can't properly refute.

Here's the case. The arable field next to us, thirty hectares, is up for sale. Half the committee wants us to bid for it and turn it into wet grassland and shallow pools, joining it to the reedbed. A developer also wants it — distribution shed, parking, the usual. One of our younger committee members, who's read some welfare biology, says we should let the developer have it. His argument, as I understand it: thirty hectares of good wetland doesn't just hold animals, it manufactures them — huge numbers of invertebrates, amphibians, small mammals and birds started every year, and the overwhelming majority of those lives end within weeks or months, in ways we'd call awful if they happened to a domestic animal. If those lives are on balance bad ones, then creating the habitat does harm on a scale we wouldn't accept anywhere else, and thirty hectares of shed and tarmac prevents all of it. He's not being flippant about it and he's not anti-wildlife.

The bit I keep coming back to, as the person on that committee who's actually counted things in trays, is whether the breeding populations on that field are habitat-limited at all. If the numbers are set mainly by something density-independent — a hard winter, a dry spring, whatever it happens to be in a given year — then taking the habitat away doesn't reduce the number of lives started, it just rearranges where and when they end, and the "prevented lives" argument quietly collapses even though the one-off deaths from clearing the site are real and immediate. If they are habitat-limited, his argument has real force and I don't know what to do with it. I'd rather the memo turned on that, and on the difference between the deaths caused by clearing the site once and the ongoing background mortality of whatever lives there afterwards, than on general stuff about how harsh nature is.

What I want is the decision memo I can put in front of the committee at the March meeting. 800 to 1000 words, recommendation in the first two sentences, a concrete course of action with dates attached: do we bid or not; if we bid, what management regime, what we'd actually go out and measure, and when we'd revisit the decision and on what trigger. Give the don't-create-more-habitat case its strongest version, not a strawman — he'll be in the room — and then say plainly which way you'd go and why, including the strongest thing on the side you're rejecting. Please no citations or reading lists: none of us can look anything up, we'd only argue about whether you got the names right, and half the committee are retired teachers and a farmer, so keep the English plain.

One more thing. There's a middle option floating around — buy the field but manage it as closed scrub and woodland rather than open early-successional wetland, on the theory that fewer lives get started per hectare per year under mature, low-turnover habitat. Tell me honestly whether that's coherent, or whether it's just splitting the difference because arguing is uncomfortable. I suspect it's definately the second, but I can't quite say why.
