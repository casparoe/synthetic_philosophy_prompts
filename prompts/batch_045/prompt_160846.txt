The setup they should be arguing about is a variant of the classic Ellsberg urn that I made up for the handout, so please don't quietly swap in the original: there are two urns, each with 90 balls. In each urn 30 balls are red for sure, and the other 60 are blue and yellow in some proportion nobody tells you. The twist is that the urns are mirrors of each other — if urn A has k blue and 60−k yellow among those 60, urn B has 60−k blue and k yellow. You win $100 if the ball you draw is blue. You can bet on blue from A, or on blue from B, or you can let the experimenter flip a fair coin to pick which urn you draw from and then bet on blue from that urn. Two things I want them to notice: the coin flip tells you nothing and gives you exactly the same chance of blue as a straight bet on red (1/3, no matter what the unknown proportion is), so the hedge manufactures a known-risk option out of two unknown ones; and yet a rule that evaluates an act by its worst case over a set of priors strictly prefers the coin flip, because mixing the mirrors pins the chance of blue at 1/3 whatever the composition is, while each unhedged bet has a worst case of zero.

So: exactly five numbered exchanges, alternating between "Sam", who thinks any uncertainty worth acting on should be represented by a single probability and that the sure-thing principle (independence / no taste for mixing as such) is non-negotiable, and "Rob", who thinks a robustness or worst-case rule is the right normative ideal for one-shot decisions under real ignorance. Neither should be a strawman. Sam has to do better than "you're being irrational": he should get to press that the coin flip is objectively worthless, that Rob would pay for an information-free randomization, and that a preference for hedging can't be a preference over consequences. Rob has to do better than "I just feel safer": he should get to press that the worst case tracks the quality of his evidence rather than some hidden fact about the world, that the mirror design blocks the usual "the subject just assumes the experimenter is lying to them" story that arguably explains the original one-urn results, and that things get worse for Sam once you allow that you might not even know the full set of possible compositions. They should actually answer each other's points rather than trade slogans — I'd like Sam to concede something like that the disagreement is partly about what a decision rule is for, and Rob to concede something like that the hedge preference is a cost he accepts rather than a free lunch — but don't let either collapse into the other; it should still be a standoff at the end.
