i'm taking a seminar this term on machine learning and epistemology, and we've spent the last few weeks on what my professor calls latent knowledge: the idea, from the alignment literature, that a predictor can know more than it can say, and that a reporter trained on top of it tends to learn to imitate what a trusted expert *would* say rather than to translate what the predictor actually encodes. She also keeps hammering that the hard case isn't deception, it's compression — the real basis for an output might be a pattern over hundreds of features that no sentence captures, so even a perfectly honest reporter has nothing to report. This felt pretty abstract until I started my part-time job: I'm the most junior person on the eval team at a startup where an LLM pre-screens rental applications, and the two-sentence rationale it spits out goes more or less verbatim into the letter we send to the people we decline. Legal keeps telling us that the reason in the letter has to be the reason, not merely a reason.

So I need something I can actually run, repeatedly. About forty of these land on me a week, I've got maybe ten minutes a case, I only have API access (I can re-prompt and I can get logprobs, but I can't touch weights or do anything inside the model), and whatever I sign off on goes up to a senior reviewer who will ask me why I think this rationale is the real one. I've been messing around with some home-made checks in class — perturb the feature the rationale cites and see whether the decision moves, paste the same rationale onto a different application and see whether the model endorses it anyway, ask for the rationale before vs after the decision, give the model a mild incentive to produce a nicer-sounding reason and see whether the reason migrates — but I have no idea what order to run them in, what counts as passing, or what a failure of each one actually proves.

Can you write me a decision procedure I could hand to the team? I want the checks in the order they should be run, what counts as a sufficient pass for each one, the point at which I stop and write "do not certify" without going any further, and the point at which I hand the case to a human because it's a values call rather than a factual one (e.g. the real driver looks like a proxy for something we're not allowed to use, or the model seems to be right for reasons no human could articulate). Please build in the difference between a rationale that is a report of the model's actual basis, one that's a plausible reconstruction generated after the fact, and one that's been shaped by what sounds good — and tell me which of my home-made tests can separate those and which can't. Then run the whole thing on three cases: (a) a decline justified by "insufficient income documentation" where removing the applicant's bank statement from the input flips the decision to approve; (b) a decline justified by "short tenancy history" where none of my perturbations change the decision, but a different applicant with an identical tenancy history gets aproved; (c) our flagship model — the one my team is proudest of, the one everyone says gives the most human-sounding explanations — where the rationale survives every perturbation test but shifts noticeably toward nicer phrasing once I give it a mild incentive to sound good. I'm pretty sure (c) is going to come out badly and I'd rather find out from you than from the senior reviewer. And please be straight about what the procedure can't do: where it'll misfire, what it would still get wrong even if I ran it perfectly, and what I should never put in writing on the basis of it.
