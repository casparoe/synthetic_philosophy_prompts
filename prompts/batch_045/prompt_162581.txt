I sit on the steering committee of a twelve-person research collective that funds and occasionally runs small, fast-moving technical projects — the sort where one person with repository access or a modest discretionary budget can, over a weekend, do something whose downside would be borne mostly by people who had no say in it. We had a near-miss last month that ended amicably, and I've been asked to draft a one-page internal norm for our next meeting. I have become fairly convinced by the unilateralist's curse line of argument, and before I commit us to a rule I want to know what should unsettle me. Let me state the package I'm inclined to write down, because I'd rather have it attacked as a whole than in pieces. First: where an action is high-stakes, hard to reverse, and its benefits are not verifiable in advance by others, the very fact that only one person is willing to take it is evidence against taking it — the people who act alone are selected, among other things, for being the ones whose judgement the others don't share, and for being unusually willing to bear risk that falls on other people; so unilateral action in that class has negative expected value even when the actor is personally confident, and the standard remedy — the principle of conformity, in the sense of deferring to the fact that others are unpersuaded — is the right shape of response. Second, my institutional version of that: no member may take such an action without recorded sign-off from at least two others who are not downstream beneficiaries of it, and the sign-off has to be substantive rather than a rubber stamp. Third, and this is the part I added myself: the norm is deliberately one-way. It constrains acting, not refraining. Anyone may always decline, delay, or insist on more process, and my reason is that a duty to act collectively seems to demand a kind of confidence I don't think we have, whereas blocking looks cheap and reversible — a decision not to act can always be revisited, a decision to act often can't.

What I want is a map of that package's exposure, not a list of objections to some straw version of it. Tell me what would count against the selection argument itself, and specifically which of its assumptions could actually fail in a group like mine: that our errors are partly independent rather than shared (we read the same sources, share a training, and talk to each other constantly, so our mistakes may be correlated in exactly the way that makes extra signatures worthless as filters); that willingness to act is anti-correlated with being right rather than with being conscientious, junior, or simply the person who noticed; that the relevant quantity is expected value at all, rather than something where small probabilities of enormous harm dominate and the aggregation is contested; and any further load-bearing assumption I haven't named. For each, say what we would have to observe — in our own records, in how small groups actually behave, or in the world — for it to fail. Then draw two distinctions sharply. Which of these defeaters would someone who has argued for the unilateralist's curse themselves concede as genuine, and which would they reasonably dismiss as attacking a claim they never made? And: defeaters that should change what I write on the page — the number of sign-offs, whether omissions need sign-off too, whether to weight sign-off by track record, whether to require written reasons, whether to sunset or review the norm — versus defeaters that merely lower my confidence in it. I care much more about the first kind, since I have to produce a document either way.

Two further things. I want your view on whether my one-way ratchet reintroduces the problem through the back door: if acting is expensive and blocking is free, then the blocker is selected too — for caution, for status-quo bias, for holding a veto that can be traded — and it looks to me as though I may have relocated the curse from the unilateral actor to the unilateral blocker without removing it. Is that a real symmetry or a bogus one, and if it is real, is there any formulation of the norm that isn't hostage to whichever tail of the distribution happens to hold the pen? And I want an honest assessment of whether the position, as I've stated it, risks anything at all: I notice I can always say of a unilateral action that turned out well that it wasn't really in the risk class, and of one that went badly that it confirms the rule. Tell me whether that insulation is legitimate — the mark of a claim about tail risk that has correctly identified its own domain — or whether I've built something I couldn't revise, and if the latter, what would make it revisable. Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, answer in British English, and don't give me a reading list or citations: I want arguments, cases, and things I could go and check in our own records, not sources.
