Here's roughly how it went. (1) Malik: sainthood crowds out non-moral excellence. A saint spends every discretionary hour on the highest-return altruistic thing available, so no cello, no long novels, no hiking unless it's instrumental; but those non-moral goods are real goods and part of what makes a life worth living, so maximal moral goodness isn't the same thing as maximal goodness, plain and simple, and it's not an ideal to aim at. (2) Jo: the crowding-out is overstated, because a lot of non-moral goods are complements rather than substitutes — a rested, socially embedded, disciplined person does more good across a lifetime, and skills transfer — and besides, a lot of what we value in our own hobbies is available second-hand; being the friend who genuinely delights in someone else's cello recital isn't obviously much worse than playing it yourself. (3) Malik: even granting that, the saint's relation to her own projects is corrupted, because she takes up the cello only because it makes her more effective and she'd drop it the moment the numbers moved; her commitments are all held on a leash, and being wholehearted about something — caring about it non-instrumentally and not auditing it — is itself a constituent of a good life, which sainthood structurally rules out. (4) Jo: that's a demand about motive-shape smuggled in as a demand about content; what matters for friendship and for a life going well is mostly the stable pattern of attention and action, and the leash objection proves too much anyway, since plenty of perfectly healthy commitments are conditional in the same way while the condition is just different, and being responsive to the impersonal good isn't a defect. (5) Malik, conceding the leash point and pivoting: here's what I actually think — sainthood is fine as an emergency setting, not as a character. You can install in yourself a disposition to flip into full-on maximising altruism when a threshold is crossed (a child in a pond, a pandemic, a moment when your particular skills are weirdly high-leverage) and otherwise live as a normal person with projects, partialities and hobbies; call it the reserve model, and it gets you the saint's benefits without paying for them across an entire life. (6) Jo: the reserve model has no principled threshold — whatever line you draw is just the line where the saint's own arithmetic says the extra good outweighs the cello, so the "normal" life is the saint's life with a rounding error, and a person who calibrates how much she cares about others to the marginal returns is exactly the cost-benefit manager you said you didn't want; worse, if you set your own trigger, self-interest will set it low and you'll be a reliably bad judge of when it's gone off.
