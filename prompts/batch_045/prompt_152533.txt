My mother retires at the end of the month after thirty-one years in infection prevention and control at a large urban teaching hospital — central-line and ventilator-associated infection surveillance, hand-hygiene auditing, and a great deal of arguing with attending physicians — and I have been asked to give the toast at her department's retirement dinner. About two hundred people will be there: her own team, ward nurses and several of the physicians she spent decades nagging, hospital administrators, our family, and four friends from the philosophy department where she took her first degree before she ever trained as a nurse, two of whom spent their careers arguing about whether absences can be causes and who will hear any cheat. I need six or seven minutes that land for the nurses and the family without turning into a lecture, and that her old classmates cannot convict of quietly asserting that someone caused something not to happen.

That last constraint is hers, not mine, and it is quite specific. She holds what I would call positive-event causal realism: only positive happenings can stand in causal relations, so while she will say without any hesitation that a resident's failure to check a chart caused a patient's death — on her view omissions cause things freely, especially where a norm has been violated — she denies that anything she did caused there to be no outbreak, and denies that eleven years without a cluster is something anybody brought about. She has also forbidden my sister, an epidemiologist, from putting a number on it: her unit's practices were heavily redundant, any of three or four people would have caught the same problem, and she thinks a but-for estimate of infections prevented quietly commits her to a necessity claim she regards as false. Her email listed four things not to say, and those are two. The other two: do not pretend the record was unbroken (last spring there was a four-patient cluster in an ICU she covered; she has not forgiven herself and the room knows), and do not skip the fact that her actual work was getting other people to do things — audits, teaching, arguments — so a toast that praises her as though she personally held the infections off is, to her, simply a false description of the job. (One of her friends said the word for this is double prevention, and that she has always thought praise travels badly across it.) She would also rather be blamed than praised for trying, so anything of the form "she gave it everything" is the one note guaranteed to make her wince.

So the puzzle is that nearly everything one wants to say about her career has the shape "because of her, something did not happen," and every honest-looking formulation of that seems to be exactly what she has ruled out. Please write the toast. It should say something substantial and true about a thirty-year career whose only visible product is the absence of events — without claiming she caused any absence, without a number, without erasing either the cluster or the colleagues who performed the actions she was arranging for them to perform, and without collapsing into praise of effort or good intentions. Then, in a short note to me below the speech, mark the two or three lines where you were most tempted to smuggle in a causal claim about something that did not happen, and say what you wrote instead and why.
