I am a novelist, finishing a book set in a small provincial town in the 1930s, and I am stuck on a word. Two scenes in the manuscript have, I now realise, the same shape. In the first, a young woman signs over the family workshop to a charming outsider who has been courting her: every figure he has given her is accurate, he has answered every question truthfully, there is no threat and no penalty attached to refusal, she is of age, sharp-tongued, and she says yes twice — once to him, once, alone, to her oldest friend. But no one in that town, in that year, refuses him. Not because refusal is punished, but because it has never been done: there is no sentence in circulation, no story anyone can tell, in which a woman in her position declines. In the second scene, an injured man agrees to an operation that will cost him a great deal and benefit him only modestly. He can recite the risks back to the surgeon; he is not confused; nobody is pressuring him. Yet saying no is, in his mind, not "declining an operation" but "being the sort of man who abandons his family" — and he says, much later, that his yes was the only thing he could have been. In both cases the usual three tests come back clean: no coercion in any sense that involves a threat or a worsened alternative, no communicated falsehood, no failure of competence. And yet the yes feels as if it belongs to the situation rather than to her, or to him: what they are choosing between is not quite what is on offer.

I have tried on the obvious words and none of them fit. Coercion is too strong — nothing has been made worse for them if they decline. Deception seems wrong too: I have read enough to know the common test is roughly "would she still have consented had she been told the truth?", and here the answer is yes, since they were told the truth about every fact anyone would call relevant. Competence is the wrong shelf entirely; both characters reason perfectly well. What I am after is a name for a failure of the *option space* rather than of the information, the will, or the threats: the agent's alternatives are all present, but the meaning each of them carries for her has been fixed by something other than her own judgement, so that one option is unthinkable and another is inflated into the only thing a decent person could do. A philosopher at a dinner party last month mumbled something that sounded like "hermeneutic" something, and I lost the thread; if that is the right neighbourhood I would like to be told, but a bare label will not help me. Please tell me what exactly this phenomenon is, how it differs from the three standard consent-invalidators and from anything else nearby that it might be confused with, whether it actually invalidates consent or is instead a separate complaint one can make about a transaction whose consent was genuinely valid, and where its edges are — I need to know which nearby cases are *not* instances of it, because my narrator has to name it in about four words of dialogue and I cannot have her name it wrongly. I am on a residency in a village with no library, so please do not send me to books or authors; argue it out in your own terms.
