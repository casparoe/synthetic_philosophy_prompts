I teach moral philosophy, and this term I was conscripted onto the committee that administers the Aldridge Medal, funded by an old bequest for conduct that goes beyond what could reasonably be demanded of anyone — the category we argue about in the literature that begins with Urmson's 1958 paper on saints and heroes, though the bequest's own wording predates all of that and simply describes deeds of which it can be said that they were good to do but not blameworthy to have left undone. The committee is not a theoretical body, but in fourteen years it has accumulated a track record, and because I am the philosopher on it I have been asked to draft the memo on this year's case. Our precedents are these. We awarded the medal to a night custodian who broke two ribs pulling two strangers from a car in a flooded underpass, with no professional duty to intervene and no compensation beyond his medical bills. We declined it, with a letter of commendation, for a campus police officer who carried an unconscious student out of a smoke-filled residence hall, on the ground that this is what his role required of him. We declined it for an administrative assistant who turned in a wallet containing roughly eighteen hundred dollars in cash and a passport, on the ground that returning it cost her nothing and was ordinary decency. We awarded it to an alumna who donated a kidney to a stranger she had met through a patient-matching forum. We awarded it to a professor of nursing who gave unpaid end-of-life care to a neighbour with no family for six years and turned down paid consultancy work in order to keep doing it. And we declined it for a colleague in the physics department who assigned the royalties of one of his textbooks to a nonprofit, on the ground that he had given up nothing he would otherwise have had. Two dimensions have never arisen: no case so far has involved an agent who was later and substantially compensated or honoured for the very same deed, and we have never said a word about whether the agent's motives matter.

The case before us is Dr. M., now an associate professor of chemistry. Over four years she developed a cheap point-of-care diagnostic for a neglected tropical disease, deliberately did not patent it, published the protocol open-access, and spent four summers training technicians in three countries to use it; she turned down a consultancy to develop a proprietary version, and colleagues in her field estimate the forgone licensing income in the low hundreds of thousands. Her tenure decision was deferred by two years as a result, and senior colleagues say there was genuine doubt at the time whether she would be tenured at all. She had no prior connection to the affected populations and was under no legal, professional or contractual obligation of any kind. Since then she has received a major prize carrying an unrestricted award of about half a million dollars, a named chair, and a trade book contract. In her interview with us she was candid that professional ambition and the expectation of recognition were substantial parts of why she did it, and she also observed that the open-access route generated the citations she needed for tenure considerably faster than a patent would have, and that she would not have taken this path had it meant leaving research altogether; she further said that she would have done it had no one ever noticed, which two members of the committee take to settle the question of motive and two take to be exactly what one would say in a medal interview. She also describes the project, unprompted, as the thing she most wanted to do with her career. We are split four to three, and I do not think the split is really about Dr. M.

So here is what I want from you. Reconstruct the rules that fit our precedents jointly, and I suspect there are at least four that do: one that measures the agent's cost prospectively and in gross terms, counting opportunity costs and risks borne at the time of acting; one that measures it net, so that later compensation and honours can retroactively remove the sacrifice a deed consisted in; one that adds a condition on the agent's reasons, so that mixed or partly self-interested motivation disqualifies or at least demotes; and one on which what counts as a cost is fixed not by forgone income but by the agent's own projects, so that four years spent on the work one most wanted to do is not obviously a sacrifice at all. Say what each predicts here, which precedent is the closest analog on each, and which of them you think the committee is in fact running. Then give me the recommendation you would put in the memo, and — this is the part I care about — say plainly what we would be committing ourselves to either way: whether the medal honours sacrifice or excellence in aid, whether being beyond the call is a property of the act at the time of acting or of the whole episode including its upshot, whether compensation can cancel supererogation or merely coexist with it, and whether there is any coherent position at all in the middle. Finally, tell me which single further fact about this case, or which variant of it, would best discriminate between the surviving rules, since I would rather ask Dr. M. one good question than argue with my colleagues for another three meetings.
