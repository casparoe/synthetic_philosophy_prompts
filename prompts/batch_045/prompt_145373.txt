I spent my career as a plant physiologist, most of it measuring ion transport across membranes, and since retiring I have been sitting in on a reading group in the philosophy department that is working its way through free will. I have become preoccupied with a distinction that nobody in that room can name for me, and that they keep collapsing into a single word I do not think covers it.

Three cases, which look to me like three different things. The first is a machine I knew intimately: an autosampler that fetched vials from a rack. If you shifted the rack slightly while the arm was away, the machine would bring the vial back to the start position, re-run its entire homing and calibration sequence, and then, because the sequence itself nudged the rack, discover the rack was not where it expected, and begin again. It could do this all night. What interests me is that the movements were not stereotyped in the least: it corrected for a sticking vial, routed around a jam, did something slightly different every pass. The rigidity sat somewhere else entirely, in the conditions under which it regarded a stage as completed and allowed itself to go on to the next. The second case is myself. I press save, then press it again, then open the document to confirm, then save once more, and the confirmation does not close the matter. Here nothing in the world was disturbed by anyone; the thing being reset is, apparently, a flag in me, and I cannot locate it. The third case I can only gesture at: a colleague's laboratory robot, which recovers admirably from shoves, dropped objects and closed doors, and which a student then drove into a permanent loop with two chairs and a length of cable. It occurs to me that for anything running on rules one could in principle contrive circumstances that drive it into just such a loop, so that rigidity of this kind may not be a property of a system at all but of a system together with circumstances someone has chosen; and if that is right, the word cannot be doing the work my reading group wants it to do.

I have seen the term sphexishness used for this, after the old story of the wasp that drags its prey to the threshold of the burrow and, if the prey is moved while it goes in to inspect, drags it back and inspects again indefinitely; but I would rather not rest anything on an anecdote that sounds as though it has been retold into smoothness, and in any case the word seems to me to be used loosely for anything mechanical. I am after something finer. The nearest things I have been offered all miss: the ethologists' fixed action pattern and related notions concern stereotyped motor sequences, and my autosampler's sequences were not stereotyped; the clinical notion of perseveration implies a pathology and a subject who should know better; and what psychologists call functional fixedness concerns how one conceives an object, not how one's routines terminate. So: is there an established concept whose home is the control architecture rather than the behaviour, that is, rigidity in the release and termination conditions of a routine combined with full plasticity in what the routine does while it runs? Are my three cases one phenomenon or three, and if three, how should they be separated and what are the borderline instances — a pilot working a checklist, a mathematician re-checking a proof, a program that retries deliberately because retrying is good design? Is a name the same as an answer here: a student suggested that the escape is a higher-level process able to inspect and interrupt the lower one, and I do not see why that inspector should not be loopable in exactly the same fashion, requiring an inspector for the inspector. I will not be satisfied with a bare label; I want to know what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, where its edges lie, and whether carving it this way changes anything in the argument about whether human beings are mechanical in the relevant sense.
