I am completing a paper in normative political theory in which I defend a scheme of my own devising: a bicameral legislature in which the second chamber is filled by lot in overlapping, staggered cohorts, holds no power of initiation over its own agenda, and exercises only an agenda-setting power plus a suspensive veto that the elected chamber can override by supermajority. My claim is that such a chamber is democratically legitimate and not merely epistemically useful — that it is a mode of self-government, not an advisory panel of well-chosen amateurs.

A reviewer, and increasingly the literature itself, keeps pressing an analogy against me: a citizens' assembly is nothing but a jury scaled up. The reviewer uses it to license a specific conclusion, namely that sortition is legitimate only where the task is judgment under a standard supplied by someone else — applying law to facts, not authoring law — and that my chamber is therefore at best a technocratic check whose credentials are epistemic (better-considered outcomes) rather than democratic (authorized by the people). She sharpens this with what she calls the authorship condition: a body may exercise political power legitimately only if it is competent to author, and not merely apply, the norms it enforces; a jury cannot, because the judge instructs it on the law, it answers a single question put to it by others, it gives a verdict without reasons, and it is not expected to defend the rule it applies.

I want the analogy audited rather than asserted or dismissed. Please do the following, in no more than 800 words including the table:

Give the correspondences explicitly, as a table: for each structural element of a criminal jury (the frame from which the panel is drawn, voir dire and challenges, the number of members, the single question, the judge's instructions on law, the burden of proof, unanimity or supermajority, the unreasoned verdict, sequestration, service for one case only, the possibility of nullification, appeal and reversal), state what would have to play that role in my lot chamber, and flag the places where the mapping is forced or where my chamber simply has no counterpart.

Separate the disanalogies that bear on the conclusion at issue from those that are decorative. I take three to be serious and want your verdict on them: the scale problem (twelve persons facing a binary question about the past versus several hundred facing an open legislative agenda); what I call the mandate gap (a jury's authority does not come from being authorized by anyone, whereas my chamber's is said to come from its being a descriptive microcosm of the demos, and it is unclear that the microcosm claim survives once the panel is stratified and the agenda is set elsewhere); and the authorship problem itself, softened by the fact that juries do in practice move the content of the law through nullification and through findings of fact.

Then tell me whether my conclusion or the reviewer's survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and what the analogy was quietly carrying. In particular: does the jury comparison, if accepted, actually argue for a design change rather than for my scheme's rejection — for instance a stricter verdict rule, in which the lot chamber answers only a single yes-or-no question drafted elsewhere, in one shot, without reasons, under something like a burden of proof — and would making my chamber fit the jury better make it more democratic or merely more tractable? Finally, if the jury is a poor source domain, name the better one (a constitutional convention, a moot court, an audit committee, a deliberative opinion poll, a board of trustees) and say in one sentence what it would license that the jury does not.
