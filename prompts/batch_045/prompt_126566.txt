I'm the graduate student representative on my university's Library and Archives Committee, and this term I'm also taking a seminar on evidence, silence, and the historical record, which is partly why the chair asked me to draft the memo that will go out before next month's vote. I'd be grateful for a draft I can revise and put my name to.

The case before us: Special Collections has been offered the working papers of a linguistic anthropologist who worked with a small upland community over roughly two decades in the mid-twentieth century. The collection runs to about sixty linear feet — field notebooks, genealogical charts, photographs, correspondence with a district administration, and some two hundred open-reel tape recordings, a significant number of them of ceremonies and oral genealogies.

Three things make this harder than a routine acquisition.

First, the community's elected council has made three requests: that the recordings be returned to them; that individual names be kept out of the public online finding aid; and that the community be permitted to deposit its own account of the researcher and of his fieldwork alongside the collection, as a series in the same finding aid rather than as a separate, lesser collection.

Second, the notebooks are very likely the only surviving documentary record of a forced relocation in the early 1960s, and the community is currently preparing a land claim that our own legal historian says would be substantially weaker without them. There is a real possibility that the redaction the council is asking for would damage the very claim it is meant to protect. I don't want a memo that quietly assumes the community's interest and the community's stated demands coincide.

Third, the donor's deed of gift stipulates that the whole collection be available to any qualified researcher on equal terms, without differentiation. If we refuse, the estate has said it will place the papers with an institution overseas that the community would have no practical access to at all. Declining may therefore not be the protective option it looks like.

There is also a time element. The tapes are suffering binder degradation, and the conservator's view is that we have roughly eighteen months to make preservation copies before a meaningful amount of content is unrecoverable. Our university librarian has proposed that we accept the collection now, digitize the tape immediately, and defer every access question until later — presenting this as a neutral holding action. The council's position is that copying the recordings is itself the prohibited act, so that "preserve now, decide later" already takes a side. I find that argument uncomfortable and I don't think the memo should wave it away.

What I need is a committee memo of about a thousand words, in standard form (To / From / Date / Re), that does the following:

Give one bottom-line recommendation — accept, accept with conditions, or decline — with a concrete time frame: what the committee resolves at next month's meeting, what happens within six months, and what happens at the eighteen-month mark.

Work through the four specific questions: whether to make preservation copies before the access question is settled; whether to redact names from the finding aid and how to handle the conflict with the land claim; whether the community's parallel series belongs inside the finding aid; and whether we should require, as a standing condition on this and future acquisitions, an "absence note" — a short mandatory paragraph in the descriptive record stating which categories of person appear in the collection only as objects of someone else's paperwork rather than as its authors, and which records were likely never created in the first place. I've been arguing for something like this in seminar and I want to see whether it survives being written as actual policy.

Give the strongest version of the option you are not recommending. If you recommend conditions, the argument I most want to see answered is that an archive whose access rules vary with who is asking and what they are asking for has stopped being an archive; and that since our descriptive categories already distribute authorship and anonymity in ways nobody voted on, we cannot claim a neutral baseline that your recommendation departs from.

Finally, say plainly what the recommendation costs, in terms of what future researchers will not be able to do.

Two constraints on style. The committee includes two members who stop reading at words like "subaltern" and "epistemic violence," so the reasoning has to work in ordinary language. And please don't spend the memo establishing that archives are sites of power — that is the premise of the seminar and it is not in dispute among us. What is in dispute is what follows from it in this case, for this vote.
