I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, working on a paper about whether the category of the supererogatory survives temporal extension — whether an act that is beyond duty when done all at once is still beyond duty when the same act is spread across a long sequence of small steps. I need a case built to order, not an illustration.

Build me a realistic scenario — caregiving, mentoring, hosting, neighborliness, something a person could actually be in; nothing heroic, no trolleys; the whole thing in one paragraph — in which an agent performs a long series of small acts, each of which is, at the moment she performs it, a textbook instance of supererogation: permitted, praiseworthy, genuinely beyond duty, and such that she could have omitted it without blame. The series as a whole should add up to a substantial sacrifice and a substantial benefit to someone else, even though no single step is very costly. Two stipulations do the real work and must be respected. First, the need being met has to stay roughly constant across the sequence: the duty, if one appears by the end, must not be explained by the beneficiary's situation getting worse. Second, the agent must never promise, consent, accept a role, or wrong anyone — no contracts, no offices, no culpable negligence. Yet by the end she must be unable to stop without real blame, and not merely without disappointing someone or being rude. The blame has to be moral, and it has to arise from what her own earlier optional acts did to the situation rather than from a duty that was present all along.

Then show that the case works, against three accounts I want kept separate: (i) the standard token-level account, on which an act is supererogatory iff it is permitted to do, permitted to omit, praiseworthy if done, and beyond what duty requires; (ii) a course-of-action account, which evaluates the temporally extended sequence and asks whether that sequence, taken as a whole, could have been omitted without blame; (iii) a sacrifice condition, on which no act counts as supererogatory unless it costs the agent something significant, so that a cheap act is at best a favor. For each, state the verdict about the individual steps and about the sequence, and derive it from the account rather than asserting it. The pattern I am hoping the case can be made to generate is that (i) treats the early steps as supererogatory and the final ones as obligatory, (iii) withholds supererogation from every step while owing an account of the total, and (ii) returns whichever verdict you think is actually correct — including one you think I will not like.

Close with three things: which single feature of the case is doing the work; what the case becomes if that feature is removed, which should collapse it back into an ordinary and uncontroversial instance of supererogation rather than into nothing at all; and the strongest available line of attack for whichever of the three accounts comes out worst, framed as that theorist would actually press it.
