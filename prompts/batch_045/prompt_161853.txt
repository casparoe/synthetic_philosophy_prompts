I keep running into the same one-liner in climate-policy threads on the rationalist blogs and forums I read, usually in some form like "you can't have infinite growth on a finite planet." It almost always functions as a conversation-ender: someone argues that a high and rising global carbon price, plus targeted regulation, gets us most of the way to where we need to be by mid-century, and someone else replies with the line and treats degrowth in rich countries as established. I want to know what the line would have to mean for that inference to be sound, because as it stands it looks to me like it quietly trades on an equivocation between physical quantities and value.

Please lay out, as a numbered menu I can work from in a post I'm writing, the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing, ordered roughly from the ones that are near-platitudes to the ones that are substantive and contestable. The readings I'd particularly like separated are:

1. The purely arithmetic one: no positive-rate exponential growth of a bounded physical quantity can continue indefinitely, whatever the bound is.
2. The finite-stock version: the planet has finite extractable reserves, so material throughput must eventually fall — with the question of whether declining extraction is compatible with continued output growth left explicit.
3. The finite-sink version: the operative constraint is not resources at all but the atmosphere's capacity to absorb waste, so the relevant claim is about cumulative emissions and a binding quantity cap rather than about growth.
4. The thermodynamic-flow version: the binding long-run constraint is the planet's ability to shed waste heat (and the incoming solar flux), not any stock of stuff — including how far away that constraint actually sits, and why the timescale matters for a policy argument about the next hundred years.
5. The decoupling version: sustained growth is impossible unless output can absolutely decouple from throughput, as opposed to merely getting less resource-intensive per unit — with relative and absolute decoupling kept apart, and the rebound effect given its due.
6. The value version: GDP is a price-weighted index of final goods and services, not a physical quantity, so nothing about a finite planet constrains it unless growth in value requires growth in throughput; I'd like the argument for and against that bridge premise stated carefully, along with the complication that shifting output composition toward activities with slow productivity growth may itself slow measured growth.
7. The hard-to-abate version: even at full internalization of the externality, some emission sources have no cheap substitute at scale, so any growing economy retains positive residual emissions and a price alone cannot do the work.
8. The use of "infinite": nobody in these threads is defending infinite growth; what is actually at issue is another century or two of a few percent a year, and I want to know how much of the line's rhetorical force comes from an "infinite" that no one is proposing.

For each reading, give a verdict: platitude, substantive and defensible, substantive and contested, or false or incoherent. Then, which is the part I most care about, say for each whether it would actually support the conclusion the line was being used for — that a carbon price plus regulation is insufficient and that deliberate degrowth in wealthy countries is required — or whether it only supports a different instrument (a binding cap, a higher price, public investment), or whether it is true but simply does not bear on the policy question, or whether it is really a normative view about what we ought to want that has been dressed up as a physical impossibility.

Two constraints, please. Don't cite papers, authors, or reports: I can't check them and I don't need authorities here, I need the arguments in your own words. And be explicit about your confidence: for each verdict, give me a rough probability where you have one rather than a flat assertion, and single out the one claim you are least confident in — I expect that will be whatever you say about the empirical prospects for absolute decoupling — and tell me what kind of evidence would most change your mind about it.
