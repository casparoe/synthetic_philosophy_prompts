I letter signs, place cards and the occasional pub menu by hand as a hobby, and once a month I run an evening for about a dozen adults in a meeting room at the public library. Roughly seventy-five minutes of actual working time. The skill in the room runs from people who print carefully and slowly to two or three who do brush lettering and know their way around type properly. Most of them have not met before. We have tables, paper, pencils, cheap markers, tracing paper, scissors, and my phone for photographing things. No projector. I would like one of these evenings built around something I have been chewing over on my own, and I would much rather the group ran into it than heard me describe it.

The thing, as I have it: what makes every a an a. The same question for g, or k, or a capital R — a is just where I noticed it. What got me going were some observations I cannot fit together. I can read an a in a typeface I have never seen, instantly and without effort, and yet I cannot say what I am matching the shape against; there does not seem to be any stroke or proportion that all the a's I can read actually share. My own a changes depending on what letter comes before it and how wide I am writing. If I take an a from one typeface and drop it into a word set in another, everybody still reads it as an a, but it now looks foreign — whereas the o from that second typeface never reads as an a however I adjust it. And a child's a, a circle with a stick on the right, is unmistakably an a to me even though it shares almost nothing with the two-storey a in the book I am copying from. Sometimes it feels like the answer is a list of features, and then I find a feature I can delete or one that belongs just as much to d or q, and the list has gone again.

What I want from you is a runnable plan for the evening: rounds or phases with timings, what I make or hand out beforehand, what people are asked to do with each other, and what they should be holding at the end. The target feeling I am after is that they can all still recognise an a in a style none of them has seen, faster than they can explain themselves, and that by the end they have tried and failed to produce the rule they are clearly using. I would rather that arrive as something they did — a sorting job they disagree about, a shape they have to complete or transplant or strip down, a constraint that quietly forces the issue — than as a question I put to them at the start. Ideally they should not hear the question in its general form until they have already been arguing about a particular case of it. Nobody should need to draw well; if a round only works for the two people who can, I will drop it.

Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the parts of the puzzle — including the bit I cannot get the activity to produce and will have to say in plain words myself — plus an honest list of what will predictably go wrong when I run it and how I would recover. Two things I do not want the evening to turn into: a short history of the alphabet, and a linguistics session about sounds and spelling. Someone will say it is really about the sound, and I would like that anticipated in the design rather than argued with in the room, since the one letter covers noticeably different sounds and the one sound gets written several different ways.

Last thing: tell me how confident you are that each phase will actually produce the effect I described rather than a different and less interesting one, name the phase you are least confident about with what I should watch for and what I would swap in if it falls flat, and flag anywhere your design quietly assumes a particular answer to what letter-identity consists in — I would rather know where you have committed me than find out from a room of disagreeing strangers.
