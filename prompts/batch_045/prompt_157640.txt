I host a small, kind of scrappy interview podcast — about 40 minutes, general audience, listeners who are mostly not philosophers but who don't mind being pushed a bit. I got into all this because my sister told me she "just knew" she wanted kids and I couldn't see how anyone could know that, so next week's guest is a philosopher who's spent about a decade arguing that choices like whether to have a first child, whether to retrain for a career you've never tried, whether to get a cochlear implant if you've been deaf since birth, are what she calls transformative: you can't know what it'll be like beforehand, and having the experience changes what you want, so ordinary expected-utility reasoning has nothing to plug into it. The claim I actually want to get at is her newer and more controversial one — that once a choice is transformative in this way, expected utility isn't just unusable but the wrong norm, and the rational thing is what she calls discovery-guided choice: you take the option that gives you the best shot at finding out who you'd become, on the grounds that you're partly choosing a future self rather than choosing an outcome. She's also willing to bite a bullet most people in this area won't: she thinks the same norm covers small everyday cases — trying a food you've never had, moving to a city you've never lived in, signing up for something you'll probably hate — because those are "micro-transformative", and people who quietly run a cost-benefit calculation on them are making the same mistake in miniature. And she has one escape line: whenever I've heard her given a case where you could in principle gather decent evidence or ask around, she says something like "oh, that's just decision under risk wearing a transformative costume" and moves on. I want questions that make that move progressively harder to lean on.

So: draft me eight to ten questions, with at least one each on — (a) where the line between transformative and merely big-or-unfamiliar is supposed to fall, and what happens to her view if there isn't a principled line; (b) the worry that discovery-guided choice is just expected-utility maximization with one extra item in the outcome list, namely the value of finding out who you are, so that it quietly collapses back into the thing she's attacking; (c) what to do with the fact that most people who become parents say it was worth it, and especially with people who seem a lot like you psychologically who say the same, since that looks like exactly the sort of evidence you'd normally be entitled to use; (d) whether choosing to have a child partly in order to find out what it's like is treating the child as a means, and how she squirms out of that; (e) why, if you genuinely can't imagine the new experience, you're in any position to judge which future self you'd want to discover — i.e. how the norm gets any grip without a target. For each question, tell me what she'll most likely answer and then give me the follow-up that presses on that specific answer instead of letting her move on to the next point.

Two more I want in there: one coming from a listener who reads a lot of behavioral economics and will want to know whether this is all just ambiguity aversion about your own future tastes under a fancier name, and what she'd say to that; and a closing one about what would actually count against her — what she could in principle run into that would make her give up the discovery norm rather than reinterpret the case. Last time I had a guest on I let them restate their book for thirty-five minutes and it was dead boring, so keep the questions pointed, give me the bit of pressure to apply after each answer, and flag any question that needs a sentence of setup so my non-philosopher listeners don't get lost.
