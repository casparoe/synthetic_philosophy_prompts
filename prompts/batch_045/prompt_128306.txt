I am a sixth-form student who runs a short interview series for my school's science-and-philosophy society: each episode is a twenty-minute recorded conversation with one researcher, posted for other students, most of whom have had a single year of biology and no philosophy. My next guest is a philosopher of biology — call her Dr. Nadia Hallam — who defends what she calls the strong reading of the genetic code: not that calling DNA a code is a vivid and useful metaphor, but that a stretch of DNA genuinely says something, in something like the way a sentence does, and that its content is fixed by the job the cell's translation machinery was selected, over evolutionary time, to do with it. She has had this conversation many times before, and she has one reliable way of absorbing an awkward case: whatever example I produce, she replies that it shows only that the code is read in context, not that there is no code. I want to be ready for that move rather than flattened by it.

Please draft ten interview questions for her (I will choose eight for the recording, so give me spares). Format each one as: the question exactly as I would say it aloud, in at most two sentences, with no technical term left undefined — if I say 'methylation' or 'alternative splicing' I have to be able to say in the same breath what it is; then a one-line note of the answer she is most likely to give; then a follow-up that lands only if she gives precisely that answer. Nothing that just invites her to restate the strong reading, and no suggestions of papers or further reading. The register should be mine: seventeen, polite, not deferential.

The ground I want covered:

1. A code, in the ordinary case, has a sender, a receiver and something that interprets it. Ask her who plays those roles for DNA, and what happens to the strong reading if some of the roles are simply empty. I expect her to say that the translation machinery is the reader and that natural selection supplies the history that makes one reading correct rather than another.

2. I have read that teams have rewritten every occurrence of one of the stop codons in a bacterium's genome and then reassigned that codon to an amino acid that living things do not normally use. Ask whether that codon now says something different, and in virtue of what — its new reader, or the old function it was selected for. Her likely answer is that the codon's content changed because the reading system changed; the follow-up should press on whether content can flip that fast, and what the now-unused old machinery was doing if it was not reading a message.

3. The parity worry: if a stretch of DNA counts as information-bearing because of the difference it makes to development, then so do chemical marks attached to it, messenger molecules the mother deposits in the egg, and the temperature of the nest in turtles, where it settles the sex of the hatchlings. Press her on what makes the nucleic acid special. If, as I expect, she says that only the genome is passed down the lineage in the relevant way, the follow-up should raise heritable states that do not involve nucleic acid at all, such as prions or inherited methylation patterns.

4. Alternative splicing: one stretch of DNA can yield several different proteins depending on the cell type, so it apparently says different things in different rooms of the same body. Her answer will be that the cellular context is part of what disambiguates the message, as context disambiguates a word; the follow-up should ask why the thing that carries the content is not then the whole cell or the whole lineage rather than the sequence.

5. The machinery that reads the code is itself built from instructions written in the code — transfer RNAs, the enzymes that load them, the ribosome. Ask what she makes of that circle, and be ready for the reply that this is unremarkable, since a compiler can be written in the language it compiles. The follow-up should ask whether a code that is read only by its own products has the arbitrariness that a real code needs, or whether it is closer to chemistry.

6. As I understand it, our own mitochondria read one of the stop codons as an amino acid instead, so the same three letters mean different things in different compartments of one animal. Ask whether a stretch of DNA has a content at all, or only a content relative to a translation system, and then what becomes of the claim that the genome contains the instructions for building an organism.

7. A case I have not seen her address, so it should be put to her cold: suppose we found a single-celled lineage with two translation systems that evolved independently, each with its own codon table, and a sixty-letter window that the first system reads as one peptide and the second as a quite different one, with which peptide you get settled by whichever system reaches the window first. Ask which of the two the window says. If she says both, relative to a reader, press her on what survives of 'the code' as opposed to two codes; if she says whichever one the system was selected to produce, press her on whether two chemically identical windows in two sister lineages can then say different things.

8. The word 'information' in the consumer sense — the companies that tell you your DNA contains information about your ancestry and your health risks — is presumably not her sense. Ask her to say exactly where the two come apart, and follow up on the fact that her own coding relation looks like a correlation plus a selected function, which invites the question of whether the difference is one of kind or of degree.

9. The code is redundant: several different three-letter words name the same amino acid, and some people argue the arrangement is near-optimal at buffering the damage done by single-letter typos. Ask her whether this supports her reading or instead shows that what we really have is an error-correcting channel in the engineer's sense, which is not about meaning at all — and then what molecular biology would actually lose if we dropped the semantic claim and kept only that one.

10. Finally, ask her what would count against her. My own candidate is this: if it turned out that which amino acid a codon picks out were chemically forced by how the molecules fit together rather than settled by an arbitrary convention, there would be no code, only physics. If she grants that, follow up by asking how arbitrary the mapping has to be for her — the standard table is not arbitrary in every respect, since similar words often name chemically similar amino acids — and whether a partly-arbitrary, partly-forced mapping is still a code.

Order the ten so that her 'it is read in context, not that there is no code' reply becomes progressively harder to reach for, and close with the question about what could count as evidence against her own view.
