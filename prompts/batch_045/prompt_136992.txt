I'm trying to work out when it is legitimate to decide on the effects I can actually estimate, and when I am merely kidding myself. My verdicts are as follows.

1. Which laundry detergent to buy: near-term reasons only. Ripple effects through supply chains and everything downstream get no weight, and I think they should get none.
2. A career in finance versus one in clinical psychology: here I think I ought to weigh labour-market and institutional effects, including who takes the finance job if I decline it. I feel uneasy but not paralysed, and I reach a verdict.
3. Ten thousand dollars to bed nets versus to reducing extinction risk this century: the remote effects dominate whatever I do, and here I feel genuinely unable to rank the options. I give to bed nets, but as a shrug rather than a judgement.
4. Whether to have a first child: equally large and equally remote in its significance — descendants, emissions, whoever that person becomes — yet I think this is the wrong kind of thing to deliberate over in those terms, and I resent the framing.
5. A policy that raises the probability of a very large, very long-lived human population: I am empirically less uncertain here than in case 3, and yet I feel more paralysed, because I do not know whether a vastly bigger future would be better.

Cases 3 and 4 have the same shape as far as I can tell, and I have no story on which they come apart.

My tentative principle: uncertainty about remote effects is inert when it is roughly symmetric across my options and uncorrelated with anything I can estimate, and significant when many agents face a structurally similar choice, so that the guesswork does not cancel but compounds. Test that against the five verdicts and repair it where it fails. Then say what the repaired principle predicts for: (a) adopting a standing policy of giving a fixed share of income to whatever looks best on near-term evidence, rather than deciding gift by gift; (b) funding something that widens the spread of remote outcomes without, so far as I can tell, moving the mean; (c) acting when the value of what I do depends partly on what a much richer and better-informed future generation will want. Finally, state which of my verdicts the principle cannot save, and whether the paralysis in case 5 is even the same phenomenon as the paralysis in case 3.
