Hi — I'm a second-year ML PhD student and I'm finally writing the "what are we actually assuming?" section of a paper on debate-style oversight, and I keep tripping over the fact that I can't say in one sentence what the method is even committed to. So here's the practice, concretely:

We have questions the judge can't answer directly. In our ablations the judge is a model strictly weaker than the debaters; in the human version it's a non-expert with limited time. Two debater models are assigned opposite answers and argue it out, the judge sees only the transcript and picks a winner, and the training signal is just "win the debate". Two design features, take them as given: (i) the protocol pushes debaters to break each argument into small claims the judge can check on it's own — a citation, a one-step computation, a single inference in a proof; (ii) the whole thing is supposed to keep working even when the judge is weaker than either debater, because judging, supposedly, is easier than producing.

What I want is the implicit theory reconstructed, in three parts:

1. What does this presuppose about the questions it can be run on? I've started splitting the question-space into what I call 'lego questions', where a wrong answer must somewhere rest on a checkably false or unsupported bit, and 'gestalt questions', where every local claim can be true and the answer still be wrong — is this proof strategy promising, is this translation faithful, is this the right abstraction for the codebase. Is the protocol strictly committed to the first kind, or can it be run on the second with some extra assumption doing the work? And if so what would that assumption have to be — something about the judge, about the debaters, or about the structure of true-but-misleading chains of claims?

2. What does it presuppose about the debaters themselves? It looks to me oddly indifferent to whether they understand anything at all: the epistemic work is done by the adversarial structure plus the checkability constraint, not by anyone's grasp of the subject. But the judge is required to be doing something closer to genuine assessment. Is that combination coherent, or does the method quietly need both sides to "get it" in the same sense? And when a debater wins by assembling locally true but globally misleading claims, is that evidence the debaters aren't doing what we hoped, or that checkability was the wrong constraint?

3. Is "judging is easier than producing" one thesis or several — an asymmetry of computational difficulty, of verification versus discovery, of understanding versus mere fluency? Which one does the protocol actually need, and are they seperable in principle?

I also want the underdetermination made explicit: the honest version, where several different theories of testimony, evidence or understanding would all equally license running debates this way, and where a debate "working" is compatible with more than one story about what is going on. Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with. And tell me what would have to change if we dropped the checkability requirement and let debaters argue holistically — what would that version be committed to instead?

Don't give me a literature review and don't cite anything; I know that literature and my supervisor will fill in references. I want the argument. British English please, and ideally under 1200 words.
