I am writing up my position on moral progress for a seminar I am due to present in next month, and I would like it stress-tested before I commit to it in front of people who will enjoy taking it apart. Here, as tightly as I can state them, is what I believe:

(i) There has been genuine moral progress over roughly the last three centuries. The abolition of chattel slavery, the retreat of judicial torture and of capital punishment, the enfranchisement of women, the extension of serious consideration to non-human animals: these are not merely changes I happen to approve of, and I want to say the later arrangements are better than the earlier ones, and better than simply better-by-my-lights.

(ii) The expanding-circle picture is the right shape for this story. Moral concern has widened — kin, tribe, nation, humanity, and now beyond — and the engine of the widening is impartial reasoning pressing on the parochial sympathies we are born with.

(iii) But scope is not where the important change happened. What has really altered is what registers as a moral injury in the first place: humiliation, psychological coercion, the withholding of recognition, neglect, cruelty to animals now show up as harms in a way they largely did not before. That change I also take to be an improvement.

(iv) None of this requires that persons have become better. I think we are probably no more virtuous than our ancestors, and that the improvement, if there is one, belongs to norms, practices and institutions rather than to agents.

(v) I expect that people two centuries from now will regard some of our own ordinary, unscrutinised practices as atrocities, and I do not think I can say in advance which ones. I do not think this undermines (i), but I would like to be sure of that.

(vi) I think the progress that has occurred is mostly a by-product of material and institutional change — literacy, print, commerce, urban density, state capacity, the sheer decline of everyday violence — and that moral philosophers have mostly supplied after-the-fact rationalisations for shifts that were already underway.

(vii) If you asked me for my single best piece of evidence for (i), I would point to the growth of concern for people who do not yet exist. Duties to future generations strike me as the clearest case of the circle having widened for real reasons rather than for fashionable ones.

(viii) A worry I cannot place. As the circle has widened, the concern extended to any single individual seems to me to have thinned: more beings, less per being. I cannot tell whether that trade is progress, or merely change, or something worse, and I do not know which of my other commitments it bears on.

What I want is an audit rather than an overview. Go through this set and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only look as though they are, and be willing to say that a pair I have presented as obviously coherent is in fact the source of the trouble. Rank the genuine tensions by how severe they are, and for each one say precisely what would have to be revised, and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and—if it belongs anywhere—what (viii) is trying to tell me. I have no settled view about which commitment I would sacrifice if forced, so please do not assume I am more attached to the ones I listed first; assume only that I would like to keep (i) if it can be kept honestly, and argue with me if it cannot.
