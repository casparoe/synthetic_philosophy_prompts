Next month I'm giving the guest lecture in a master's-level seminar on happiness and well-being. I'm a PhD student in clinical psychology; the room will be clinical-psychology and public-health students who have spent the term working with subjective well-being measures — life-satisfaction scales, affect balance, experience sampling — and who have read no philosophy. I have eighty minutes and a whiteboard, and I can't assign anything.

The thesis I have to teach is tranquilism: that, as far as a person's own good is concerned, only the absence of craving and of suffering matters, so that getting what you want matters only insofar as wanting it is itself a craving, or failing to get it hurts. Please don't explain the thesis to me and don't write the lecture; I want a plan for how I should explain it, and I want the plan's reasoning about sequence to be explicit.

Start with the dependency structure: what has to be in place before the thesis is even intelligible to this audience, and in what order. My worry is that the vocabulary fights me — these students hear "craving" as clinical craving and "suffering" as pain, and they have spent a semester treating self-report as the ground truth about a person's condition, so they will hear any claim about someone's good as a claim about what that person would say. Tell me which distinction has to be built first and what to build it out of.

Then give me the one case that should carry the whole session, with an argument for what it must contain and what it must not. I've been working with a pair: two people who both want recognition for their life's work, one of whom gets it, while the other undergoes a neurological change that quietly dissolves the ambition and leaves no disappointment, no sense of loss, and no residue. My instinct is that the carrying case must present a craving that is genuinely absent rather than suppressed or distracted from, and that it must not involve deception, because the moment anyone is lied to the room will argue about autonomy and stop arguing about welfare. Tell me whether I'm right about those two constraints, whether this case or something else should carry the session, and what the case has to be built so that the students can't dodge it by saying the person "would still want it if they knew."

I also want you to place, in the sequence, the point at which I introduce the two routes by which a craving can disappear: the world's coming to fit the desire, and the desire's quieting without being satisfied — adaptively, pharmacologically, or through practice. The distinctive bite of the view is that it treats those routes alike, and I want that to arrive as something the students notice they are committed to, not as a slogan I assert.

Give me a misconception map with a location for each entry — the point in the sequence where I should head it off and a single framing sentence that defuses it without my having to argue the full case. The five I expect are: that this is just hedonism with a quieter name; that it is just life satisfaction by another name; that it recommends sedation or wireheading; that it is refuted by the plain fact that people care about getting what they want even when they are content; and that it is a clinical or contemplative prescription for managing oneself rather than a claim about what is good for a person. Add any misconception you think I'm missing that this particular audience would form, especially any that comes from their measurement training.

There is one fork I can't place. Whether a craving that is currently absent but that flares the instant a cue arrives counts as a craving at all: I have a diagnostic — present the cue, tell the person the truth, see whether the craving appears — but I suspect the diagnostic quietly prejudges the question by equating "would crave under the cue" with "craves." Tell me whether to surface this before the room as an open fork that any version of the view must settle, or to keep it off the board entirely, and if I do surface it, where it belongs and how much time it deserves.

Finally: one check I can run in the last five minutes that would distinguish a student who actually has the thesis from one who has merely stopped objecting — something they have to apply the view to rather than restate — and two things I should openly decline to settle at this level, with the line I should use to decline them.

Please don't suggest readings, handouts, or citations; the students won't do them and I have nothing to distribute. Write the plan in American English, as an ordered sequence with approximate timings and a note of what each step is for.
