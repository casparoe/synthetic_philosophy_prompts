I'm a TA for a second-year epistemology course and I'm working through a stack of short-answer responses to one of our problem questions. We'd just done the contextualism reading (DeRose-style) and the pragmatic encroachment reading (Stanley/Hawthorne-style subject-sensitive invariantism), and I wrote a question that was supposed to force people to separate the two views rather than just restate the bank case. I also threw in a wrinkle I made up myself so they couldn't just reproduce the seminar discussion, and a final part on whether knowledge can still do its usual job of settling practical questions if it moves around like this. The question was worth 25 marks. The rubric I was given is pretty loose: accurate statement of each view, a correct account of what the two views actually disagree about, a defensible treatment of the wrinkle case, and a real engagement with the action-guiding point rather than a slogan.

Here's the question:

**Question 3.** (a) Set out the disagreement between epistemic contextualism and pragmatic encroachment. Both views predict that whether someone counts as knowing can shift with features of the context, so what exactly are they disagreeing about? (b) Dana has exactly the sort of evidence you'd expect in a bank case: she remembers banking there on a Saturday a few weeks ago, she glanced at the posted hours, and so on. As far as Dana is concerned, nothing much hangs on whether the bank is open Saturday. But unbeknownst to her, the bank's Saturday hours determine whether a grant she applied for months ago gets paid out this cycle — a great deal to her, if only she knew. Say what each view predicts about whether Dana knows, and say whether the case tells against either view. (c) Knowledge is supposed to be the thing that settles practical questions: if you know that p, that is normally enough to act on p. Does either view make knowledge unable to play that role?

And here's one student's answer, more or less verbatim (I haven't fixed the typos):

"Contextualism and pragmatic encroachment are both reactions to the same pattern. In the bank case you have the same evidence either way — you banked there on a Saturday a few weeks back, you've seen the hours posted — but on Friday, when it matters whether the check clears before Monday, it feels wrong to say you know the bank will be open, and on Saturday, when nothing much is riding on it, it feels fine. The contextualist explains this by saying that the truth-conditions of 'knows' shift with the context of the person making the attribution. In a high-stakes conversational context, the standards go up, and so one and the same sentence can be true in one mouth and false in another, roughly the way 'flat' and 'tall' work.

Pragmatic encroachment, or subject-sensitive invariantism, agrees that knowledge tracks practical factors but locates the shift somewhere else: what matters is how much is at stake for the subject, not which standards happen to be in force in the attributor's conversation. Importantly, encroachment is still a form of invariantism, so it doesn't have to say that the meaning of 'knows' changes from context to context. What changes is whether the subject's practical situation lets her count as knowing at all.

So the views disagree about whose situation does the work, and you can pull them apart. Suppose I'm attributing knowledge to Dana and a huge amount is riding on it for me, while almost nothing is riding on it for her. The contextualist says my high-stakes context raises the standards, so 'Dana knows' is false; the encroacher says Dana's own stakes are what count, so she knows and what I said is true.

The unbeknownst-stakes case makes the contrast sharper. Dana's evidence hasn't changed and, as far as she can tell, the stakes are trivial. Encroachment has to say she doesn't know, because the stakes really are enormous — a grant she cares about turns on it — and encroachment is a view about how much is actually at stake, not about how much the subject thinks is at stake. Contextualism, by contrast, has nothing to latch onto: nothing in the conversational context has changed, so it predicts that Dana knows. But this is bad news for the encroacher, because it shows that encroachment is really contextualism in disguise. Once the subject's knowledge can hinge on facts she has no access to, the encroacher is committed to knowledge depending on something external to the subject's own evidence, and that is just the contextualist's point about the attributor's context. The dispute is therefore much shallower than it is made out to be.

On (c), the worry is that if knowledge slides around with stakes, it can't be the stable thing that settles what to do. I think this is fatal to encroachment. On encroachment you can acquire knowledge just by ceasing to care whether p is true: stop caring whether the bank is open and you come to know that it is, with no improvement in your evidence whatsoever. That's absurd. Contextualism escapes this, because for the contextualist the standards are fixed by the attributor's context rather than by the subject's own attitudes, so the subject can't manufacture knowledge by talking herself out of caring. On the whole I find the encroachment reading more interesting, but (b) and (c) show it can't be right as it stands."
