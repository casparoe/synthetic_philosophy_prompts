I'm leading a single ninety-minute session for a philosophy reading group — about twelve adults, no mathematics beyond arithmetic, a flipchart and my laptop, no formula sheets. The topic is why a deterministic world can still be unpredictable: sensitive dependence, chaos, and what breaks in the inference from determinism to predictability. Don't explain the material to me; plan how I should explain it.

Two things I want built into the sequence. First, I intend to present predictability not as a property of a system but as a three-place contract: a target quantity, a tolerance, a horizon, and a budget of precision and computation — so that 'is this predictable?' is incomplete until you say to within what, for how long, and for whom. Tell me whether that framing should open the session or arrive only after they have already felt the puzzle, and what has to be in place before it lands. Second, I want the carrying example to separate sustained sensitivity from mere knife-edge instability: two runs started a hair apart that keep being re-separated, as against a pencil balanced on its tip, which is sensitive once and then just falls. Give me one setup I can run three times in the room — finite precision with the true rule; exact starting data with a slightly wrong rule, which has to come out wrong in a way that measuring the start better would not fix; and the coarse or statistical question — and say what it must have and what it must lack. If two different setups do the work better, say so and say why.

Then the misconception map, each with the point in the sequence where it should be defused: chaos as indeterminism; chaos as randomness or as merely random-looking output; sensitivity as requiring a complicated system; the butterfly-effect reading on which small causes matter in general; the thought that enough precision or a big enough computer restores the Laplacean picture; and the slide from this trajectory is unpredictable to nothing about it is predictable. For each, tell me whether to head it off before it forms or let it surface and then correct it, and why that ordering.

Close with how I will know it landed: one question or short task whose wrong answers are distinguishable from one another. And tell me what I should openly refuse to settle in ninety minutes — I am inclined to leave out the quantum or thermal noise floor, free will, and whether any real weather system is deterministic — and to say out loud that I am leaving them out. Please write your answer in American English.
