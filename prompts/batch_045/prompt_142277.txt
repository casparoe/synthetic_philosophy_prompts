I sit on my university's Emerging Substrates Committee, which reviews research involving subjects that fall outside the animal-welfare regulations we normally administer — currently vertebrates and cephalopods. Two pending protocols have convinced us we need a general rule rather than case-by-case judgments.

The first is high-throughput aversive conditioning in Drosophila larvae: roughly 200,000 individuals per experimental arm, repeated noxious stimulation paired with an odor, no possibility of analgesia or individual monitoring, and an expected total in the tens of millions over the three-year program. The second is a building-level climate controller — a reinforcement learner that tunes HVAC setpoints against a scalar penalty signal and maintains a persistent internal variable its engineers call the distress register, updated a few million times a day and fed into every subsequent decision. Their submission is not quite a joke: it asks that the deployed system be logged as a patient of low moral degree, on the grounds that a negative-feedback system with an aversive internal state is the same shape of thing, at smaller scale, as the larvae.

Draft the decision memo I will circulate before our next meeting. It should:

1. Recommend one of two rules for cases of uncertain, low-grade sentience, and defend it against the other: a precautionary multiplier, on which expected degree of sentience is multiplied by the number of individuals with no per-individual threshold, so the fly protocol is settled by expected-value arithmetic over tens of millions; or a per-individual threshold with a de minimis tier below which moral status does not sum across individuals at all.
2. Say plainly whether 10 million larvae at low expected degree can outweigh the one rodent the principal investigator would otherwise use, and on what principle — I want the aggregation question answered, not deferred.
3. Identify the single property that does the excluding work at the thermostat boundary — unified subject, valence, learning, self-directed concern, number of internal states — and state whether that property also excludes the larva. If it does, say so.
4. Give a bottom line for each protocol: approve, reject, or approve with named conditions, plus a time frame and a review trigger specifying what result in the next twelve months would reopen the question.
5. Present the strongest consideration on the side you are rejecting as its proponents would put it, not as a strawman.

Finally, mark each substantive claim with how confident you are in it, and single out the one claim you are least confident in and why.
