**The question (25 marks: (a) 8, (b) 10, (c) 7).**

**3. The Born rule without collapse**

**(a)** In an interpretation with genuine collapse, the Born rule can be laid down as a dynamical postulate: outcomes occur, and they occur with the frequencies the rule specifies. The Everettian is standardly said to have to *derive* it instead, or at least to earn the right to probabilistic language.

(i) Say precisely what it is that is supposed to need deriving, and why the fact that a quantity equal to the squared amplitude is right there in the formalism does not by itself settle it.

(ii) Assess the following reply: "Nothing needs to be probable. Every outcome occurs in some branch. The Born rule is a claim about how the branches are distributed in amplitude, and that is a fact about the wavefunction, not about anyone's expectations — so there is nothing here for the Everettian to derive." [8]

**(b)** Set out the decision-theoretic program for the Born rule: what it takes as given, what constraints it imposes on the preferences of an agent who is about to branch, and what it concludes. Then say how much of the Born rule it actually delivers, and whether that is enough for the Everettian's purposes. [10]

**(c)** A fallback: the *caring-measure* view. There is no probabilistic fact about which branch you will end up in; there are only your successors, and the respect in which they matter to you — the degree to which you should be concerned with each — is given by branch weight. (This is sometimes put by saying that a world has a "measure of existence.") Rational concern, on this view, takes over the job that probability was supposed to do.

Two amendments have been floated, mostly in comment threads rather than journals:

- **Floor:** you should be concerned with a successor in proportion to max(w, δ) for some fixed δ > 0, rather than to the bare weight w.
- **Rank:** you should be concerned with successors according to the ordinal ranking of their weights, with the magnitudes of the weights playing no further role.

Evaluate the unamended view and both amendments: which, if any, does the work its friends want it to do, and does either amendment buy its advantage by reinstating something the view was supposed to get rid of, or by creating a difficulty of its own? [7]

**One student's answer.**

> **(a)** What needs deriving isn't the number. The Everettian has the wavefunction, and the amplitudes are sitting in it; ask her for a quantity equal to the squared amplitude and she can produce one immediately. What needs deriving is the *role*: why that is the quantity an agent should bet on, expect, or proportion her concern to. Summing over a branching structure gets you a catalogue, not a prediction.
>
> It helps to separate two questions. There is the question why there should be anything probabilistic here at all, and there is the question why the right function of the amplitude is the square rather than something else. The first is the deep one. The second is comparatively easy: once you have additivity of weights across successive branchings and equal treatment of branches with equal amplitude, there isn't much room left to move.
>
> In a collapse theory the deep question doesn't arise in the same form, because there is one outcome and the rule is simply a postulate about how often outcomes of each kind come up. The Everettian declines to stipulate, so she owes us whatever work the stipulation was doing.
>
> The reply in (ii) fails. "Every outcome occurs" is true, but it isn't an answer to what was asked. When I say spin-up is probable I am not reporting a fact about how amplitude is distributed across the multiverse; I am recording what I should expect, and a fact about the multiverse has to be connected to that expectation by an argument. The obvious connection — I don't know which branch I'll find myself on, so my uncertainty about my own location does the work — isn't available in the naive form, because I know I will be in all of them, and there is no further fact about which one. Nor can the reply retreat to saying the Born rule is merely descriptive: even descriptively, the decomposition of the universal state into branches is fixed only up to the coarse-grainings that decoherence supplies, and those are approximate and to some degree conventional, so the descriptive claim is softer than the reply makes it sound. I don't think the Everettian should lean on that last point, though; it's a technical worry largely independent of the main one.
>
> **(b)** The program starts from preferences rather than probabilities, which is the right way round, since probabilities are exactly what's in question. Take an agent who knows the universal state and knows she is about to branch; let her have preferences over acts whose outcomes are, in general, branch-structures rather than single outcomes. Impose the usual rationality constraints — completeness and transitivity, a branching-appropriate analogue of the sure-thing principle, and diachronic consistency, so that what she prefers on behalf of her successors before branching lines up with what those successors prefer afterwards. Given the amplitude structure, the result is that her preferences are exactly those of an agent who assigns the branches their squared amplitudes as credences and maximises expected utility by those credences. The Born rule is recovered as a constraint on rational preference.
>
> What makes this more than a redescription is the order of explanation: branching structure plus rationality gives you the weights, rather than weights plus decision theory giving you what to do.
>
> It's worth being clear about what is delivered. The result is best understood as making the self-locating-uncertainty picture precise rather than as replacing it. Before the measurement I don't know which successor I will be; the successors are all equally real; nothing breaks the symmetry among them except amplitude; and the theorem then fixes the distribution over my location as the Born one. The derivation is what stops that picture from being hand-waving.
>
> I think it's enough. The Everettian isn't claiming that branching produces genuine single-case chances; she's claiming that everything we actually do with probabilities — betting, expecting, planning — is captured. And note how strong the result is: the Born rule isn't a leftover empirical postulate bolted onto the interpretation, it follows from rationality together with the branch structure, so an Everettian agent who violated it wouldn't be making a physical mistake, she'd be making a practical one. And because the amplitude structure is the only physical input, nothing contingent is left over: the rule comes out normative.
>
> The main thing the program needs is that the branching structure, and the agent-stages within it, are well defined enough for the preference axioms to bite. That's a real assumption, but it isn't an extra one: it's an assumption the interpretation is committed to anyway.
>
> **(c)** The unamended view has the right shape. It stops pretending that there's a fact about which branch I end up in, and it gives the amplitudes a job without having to call them probabilities. But it has an unattractive consequence: if a successor in a branch of weight 10⁻⁶ is in agony, the view says I should be concerned about her to degree 10⁻⁶, which seems to say that her suffering barely matters — and that sits badly with the thought that every branch is fully real.
>
> Floor fixes it. With a fixed minimum, no successor falls below a threshold of concern, so the reality of the small-weight branches is respected; and in ordinary cases, where every weight in play is comfortably above δ, nothing changes, so the view still reproduces Born-rule guidance where it matters. It's true that concern then no longer sums to one, but that's a feature rather than a bug, since caring was never supposed to be a probability distribution.
>
> Rank is hopeless. Discarding the magnitudes discards everything the view was for. If only the ordering matters, then branches of weight 0.500001 and 0.999999 differ no more than branches of weight 0.01 and 0.010001, and there is nothing left that could guide a trade-off between how much I care and how much is at stake. Rank can't be combined with anything in the neighbourhood of expected-value reasoning either, so it gives up the only thing the caring-measure view was buying.
