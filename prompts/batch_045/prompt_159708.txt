I have been carrying an argument about sphexishness around for a full term and I can no longer tell whether I have discovered something or committed a fallacy. I need you to locate the step where it goes wrong and then commit to a verdict about what the case can teach us.

The case is the standard one, which I stipulate rather than defend (I am told the original field reports are shakier than the textbooks suggest): a digger wasp paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the mouth of her burrow, leaves it on the threshold, goes inside to inspect the nest, comes back out, and drags the cricket in. If, while she is inside, the cricket is displaced a few inches, she brings it back to the threshold and then goes in to inspect again — and does so repeatedly, apparently without ever taking account of the fact that the cricket was already in position and that only the inspection still needed doing.

The usual gloss is that her routine is rigid: it tracks an internal stage counter rather than the state of the world, and that is what makes it mechanical rather than free. Two stipulations, which I introduce so that the argument can be stated exactly:

- Call a routine rigid-at-grain-g if, for any perturbation of the world occurring within an interval shorter than g, the later stages of the routine are unchanged.
- Call the sphex floor of a system the smallest g at which that system is not rigid.

Then the argument:

(1) Every system that acts in time has a sphex floor. Sensing, transmission, and deliberation all take time, so for any agent there is some interval below which it cannot take the world's current state into account. No system, then, is flexible at every grain; in that sense everything, ourselves included, is sphexish at some grain.

(2) But rigidity cannot be the defect the usual gloss takes it to be, because some rigidity is constitutive of agency rather than opposed to it. Call a system hyperplastic if it treats every perturbation, however slight, as a reason to reopen the whole plan rather than to update locally — the limiting case of an agent with no sphex floor at all. A hyperplastic agent never finishes anything: there is always some further perturbation to which it has not yet responded, and distal goals require holding intermediate stages settled in the face of considerations that would countermand them. So if the wasp is defective, her defect cannot be that she is rigid; it can only be that she is rigid at the wrong grain, or in the wrong place.

(3) Being rigid at the wrong grain is not a property of the behavior itself. Coarsen the grain of description and the same sequence is flexible: perturb the cricket at intervals longer than one full cycle and she provisions the nest successfully; coarsen further and she adjusts her hunting to whatever prey the season offers. Whether a stretch of behavior counts as rigid therefore depends on the grain at which it is described.

(4) Nothing in the creature settles the grain. The grain is fixed by whoever is asking — a field worker interested in the provisioning cycle, a neuroethologist interested in milliseconds, a philosopher interested in accountability. So sphexish does not name a property the system has; it names a relation between a system and an inquirer's interest.

(5) If (4) is right, then the contrast this case is always used to draw — mechanical rigidity versus genuine flexible agency — is not a contrast in the world at all, and the wasp cannot be evidence about free will. It can at best be evidence about how a description sounds.

(6) I cannot accept (5), for two reasons. First, the argument generalizes past the wasp: by the same reasoning a chess program's search, a pilot's checklist, and a person's resolution to keep a promise are each rigid at one grain and flexible at another, and we would have to conclude that no distinction between following a rule and exercising judgment survives. Second, if rigidity is grain-relative all the way down, the compatibilist who wants free action to be action produced by the right kind of flexible mechanism has nothing left to say to the incompatibilist, who can always redescribe at a finer grain.

(7) So either I have shown that the notion of sphexishness does no philosophical work, or one of (1) through (4) is false.

Where I stand: I would sooner give up (5) than give up (2). It seems to me clearly right — and more interesting than the tidy conclusion — that a creature which never completed anything would not thereby be more of an agent, and that commitment to a routine is part of what agency costs.

A friend thinks the trouble is an equivocation between a grain of description and a grain of the creature's own sampling of the world, with (3) true only of the first and (4) true only of the second. Neither of us can make that stick: the creature's own sampling grain looks like nothing more than the grain at which the mechanism in fact responds, and that in turn looks like something we fix by choosing a description.

So: which step do you reject? I want the diagnosis and not merely the correction — explain why the step looked sound enough to survive a term of my worrying at it — and then price the alternatives: what do I lose if I give up the step you name, and what does someone who disagrees with me gain from it? Take a definite position rather than laying out the menu. Say explicitly both what the wasp case can legitimately establish about free will and what it cannot. Finally, mark the place where your own diagnosis is least secure and say what a critic of your answer would press on there.
