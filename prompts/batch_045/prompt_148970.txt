I have been arguing with a friend since about midnight — she has formal training in decision theory and I do not — about what, if anything, is wrong with an agent whose preferences change over time, and I came away unable to tell whether the position I ended up holding is a package or a pile. So let me write down where I have landed and ask you to audit it. (1) Diachronic rationality is a genuine requirement, not a shadow cast by synchronic rationality applied at each moment: a person can be irrational across time even if every individual choice she makes is locally impeccable. And I want rationality to be a property of the person, not of her circumstances — it should not be able to change because of which trades happen to come her way, or because of how generous the people around her happen to be. (2) Nonetheless, I think the entire content of that requirement is exploitability: a pattern of preferences is diachronically defective just in case there is some sequence of trades, each of which the agent would accept at the time it is offered, that leaves her worse off by her own lights — the money-pump picture. If no such sequence exists, I do not think there is any residue of criticism left, however strange the pattern looks when written down. (3) On how one should respond to one's own future selves: I reject the resolute picture, on which you form a plan now and then make your later self carry it out even when she prefers otherwise, as either incoherent or a kind of self-tyranny, and I favour the sophisticated one, on which you foresee what your later self will want, reason backwards from that, and let her have it. (4) Case verdict: consider someone whose deepest tastes change, irreversibly, about once a decade, and who knows this about herself — she now thinks her present projects worth everything, and she can foresee that in ten years she will regard them as worthless, the way she now regards the projects she had as a teenager. I think there is nothing irrational in her and no reason for her to discount her current projects now; my friend says this is the worst part of my view, because on my own account the only thing that saves her is that she cannot be pumped, there being no route back to her earlier selves' preferences, and that this is a fact about the physics of time rather than a virtue in her. (5) Case verdict: I think it can be rational, and sometimes mandatory, to pay to have one's own future options removed — I pay a service to lock me out of my social apps for a month, knowing that on day three I would pay to be let back in. My friend says (5) cannot sit with (3): either foresight does the work and the lockout is pointless, or the lockout does the work and I have quietly become a resolute chooser who is coercing her later self. I think that is a false dichotomy, but I could not say precisely why. (6) Case verdict: an agent whose preferences cycle — she prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A — does deserve to be told that she is being irrational, even in a world where nobody ever offers her those three trades; mere pumpability is enough. And I do not think it helps her that, in that world, a philanthropist could equally have made the same trades in the reverse direction and left her holding her original option plus cash.

What I want is an audit, not a tutorial. Go through these and tell me which pairs genuinely conflict and which only look as though they do, ranked by how badly they conflict rather than listed as if they were all equally bad; for each real conflict, say what would have to be given up and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the set; and if there is one revision that would clean up the most, say which it is. Please argue rather than cite — I do not want a bibliography or a reading list, and this should be answerable without one. Finally, I would like you to say how confident you are in each diagnosis and to single out the claim you are least sure of, rather than hedging everything to the same degree.
