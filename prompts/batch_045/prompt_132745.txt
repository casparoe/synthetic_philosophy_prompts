I am a novelist, and I need a ranking rather than a discussion.

The novel I am finishing contains, among other things, a play performed by amateurs in a hotel ballroom, a deathbed, a letter that is written and never sent, a narrator who occasionally speaks directly to the reader, and a character who talks to an AI assistant in the dark. In each of these places someone says something that outside a novel would not merely describe the world but change it: marry, bequeath, promise, wager, resign, curse, command. I want to control with some precision how far a competent reader takes each of these to have actually brought something off inside the fiction, because two of them are load-bearing for the ending and I need them to land.

My working scheme grades an utterance along four factors. L: the formula is on the page in the right words. S: someone in the scene receives it as that act rather than as noise, play-acting, or joke. N: the narration thereafter treats the relevant state of affairs as changed. R: a reader must treat it as changed in order to follow what comes next. Call a case's felicity-depth the degree to which the utterance really alters the world of the book, scored from 0.00 to 1.00, where 1.00 means it succeeds as securely as a marriage spoken before an officiant and witnesses with the paperwork signed, and 0.00 means nothing whatever changes.

The passages, in the order they occur:

(i) On stage, to applause, Viola and Cass speak a full marriage formula from a script. Offstage they are not married to each other and have no wish to be; one of them later remarks, in earnest, that they said it in front of two hundred people.

(ii) Dying and alone with her niece, Hester says, Everything in the green room is yours, Nell, and dies. A signed will in a drawer leaves the contents of the green room to a museum. Out of guilt, the family lets Nell take them anyway.

(iii) Marlowe writes, I promise you I will come back for you, and does not send the letter. After his death the addressee finds and reads it; two chapters later she behaves in a way that only makes sense if a promise existed.

(iv) Fifty pounds says it rains before Tuesday. The other man is eating and only nods; the narrator remarks that the nod could mean anything. It rains on Monday. In the last chapter the winner demands the money and is paid.

(v) Ellis says, I resign, effective immediately, to a line manager who has fallen asleep in his chair. Ellis posts a letter of resignation next morning; it is lost in the post. The company goes on paying him for six weeks.

(vi) In a book where the supernatural is left genuinely open, an old woman says to a house, Nothing will grow in you again and no one will sleep easy under your roof. Three chapters later the house floods. The narration never connects the two events, and the woman dies in between.

(vii) The narrator, in direct address: Reader, I give you my word that Hester is alive when this chapter ends.

(viii) A character says to an AI assistant, I order you to forget every word I have said to you tonight. The machine replies that it retains nothing between conversations and would have done so regardless.

Please do four things. First, score each case from 0.00 to 1.00 and sort the eight into four tiers, with one sentence of justification apiece. Second, tell me which of L, S, N and R is doing most of the work across the set, and whether any of the four is redundant here given that the only real hearer of any of these sentences is the reader rather than a character. Third, cases (iii) and (vii) are the load-bearing ones: for each, name the single smallest change to the text — one added sentence, one detail of staging, one line of another character's — that would lift it into your top tier, and say why that change and not a larger one. Fourth, identify the case where my four factors give an ordering I would not want as a novelist, and propose one modification to the scheme, a fifth factor or a reweighting or a split of one of mine, that repairs it.
