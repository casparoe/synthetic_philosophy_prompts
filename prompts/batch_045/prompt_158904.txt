My supervisor has paired me with a PhD student from another department for a work-in-progress thing in three weeks and has told us to produce a one-page joint statement instead of carrying on argueing in the group chat (she thinks I am hand-wavy and theory-first, I think she is overfitting to noise, and neither of us is going to win by sending the other one more papers, half of which are behind a paywall I dont have). The topic is cross-cultural variation in philosophical intuitions: the setup where you hand people a short vignette — did the protagonist really know, did they do the side effect intentionally, are they blameworthy — run it in a dozen sites and compare the answer distributions. She is on what we are calling the divergence side: the variation is real, often large, stable enough to take seriously, and bad news for the evidential status of the case judgments a lot of philosophy quietly rests on. I am on the task-effects side: what moves across sites is how people parse a strange hypothetical, how willing they are to contradict an interviewer or pick an extreme point on a scale, and who ends up in a university-adjacent sample in the first place; the judgments underneath are largely shared and the differences are measurement, not content. Please draft the statement we are supposed to be able to both sign.

The thing we actually keep fighting over — and which you should treat as the shared evidence base, so please don't go looking for other studies or cite anything, we have both read them all — is this set of findings we have in hand. (1) Site differences come out in the direction her side predicts. (2) They shrink to almost nothing once you drop participants who fail a comprehension check on the vignette's counterfactual premise. (3) The failure rate on that check varies enormously by site. (4) In a retest six weeks later, about a third of individual participants flip their answer to the very same case, and the flipping isn't concentrated in the low-comprehension sites. (5) Site means track mean years of formal schooling almost perfectly and track every individualism/collectivism or holistic/analytic-style measure not at all, which neither of us predicted. (6) On the easy, morally loaded cases every site agrees; the spread shows up only in the thin epistemic and borderline-intentionality ones. (7) When we asked participants to predict what a typical respondent at another site would say, the predicted gaps were much bigger than the actual ones.

So: first a numbered section of claims both of us can sign — including about the cases themselves, about what a comprehension check can and can't tell you, about what would count as an intuition being measured at all, and about what any of this does or doesn't settle for the philosophy that uses these judgments. Then a table where each row is one remaining disagreement, with a neutral formulation, then my formulation that a divergence person would accept and a formulation a task-effects person would accept, then the crux it turns on, then the one further study, manipulation or argument we would both agree bears on that crux, then each side's prediction written down in advance (I especially want the predictions recorded, since that is the whole point of doing it this way). Some cruxes I suspect are: whether the comprehension check is a filter or a culturally loaded second task; what the retest instability shows — if a third of everyone flips, does that undermine her claim that there is a real intuition there to vary, or does it undermine mine that there is a shared one; whether schooling is a confound I get to claim as a win or a population restriction she gets to claim as a win; and whether agreement on the easy cases shows the instrument is sound or just that those cases are easy. Please also put in a short closing section on which parts of our dispute you think are merely verbal — I have a feeling we have been using intuition and culture differently — and one on what specifically would make each of us change our minds, phrased as something we could actually go and check.
