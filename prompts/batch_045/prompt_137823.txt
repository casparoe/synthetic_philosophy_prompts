hey — long one, sorry in advance. I'm about 200 pages into a novel and I've realised the problem I've got isn't a plot problem, its a philosophy problem wearing a costume.

Fictional country, fictional army of occupation, mid-century-ish. My POV guy is a small-town policeman in his thirties, reserves, not a joiner, not especially political. His unit gets sent into the countryside and ends up shooting a minority population — I invented the country and the group on purpose so I don't have to get any real history right; what I want is the *structure* of the thing. He goes along with it. Over the draft I've been quietly telling myself three or four different stories about why, swapping between them without noticing, and its making the middle of the book mushy.

So: build me a taxonomy, or a decision tree — whichever fits better — of the available explanations for why an ordinary-ish man in a unit like that ends up killing and then keeps killing. The two famous books (the "ordinary men, it was the situation" one and the "willing executioners, it was the culture" one) are way too coarse for me. I need more cells than that, and I need to know which cell my guy is in so I can write him consistently.

The constraints, because I think this is the part that makes or breaks it:

- Build it from the questions, not from a list of names. Every branch gets labelled with the claim that splits it from its siblings, and I want to be able to answer each node without already knowing what it leads to — so phrase nodes as claims about a case, or about what would have happened if X had been different, never as "is it obedience or ideology".
- Three things need to stay separate if they can be: why he fires the first time, why he keeps firing, and why he doesn't take the out when it's offered. If your tree can't tell those apart, say so rather than pretending.
- One actual scene from my draft has to show up as a node: the morning after the first shooting the company is told that anyone who doesn't feel up to it can step out, hand over his rifle, and go help with the trucks. Eleven men do. Nobody is punished, nobody is shot; one of them gets a bit of stick at cards a week later and that's the entire cost. My guy thinks about it for maybe four seconds and stays in line. Tell me how that scene sorts.
- Leaves I want present somewhere: conformity and not letting your mates down; obedience to a perceived legitimate authority; believing refusal would be genuinely dangerous when in fact it wasn't; careerism and material incentives; ideological conviction (he thinks they deserve it); gradualism, sunk cost and not being able to survive admitting you were wrong; role and division of labour, where the machine does the moral work; vice or character, he simply had it in him; and the deflationary one where there's nothing much to explain and the banality is the answer. Add cells if the logical space wants them.
- Where a combination of answers is empty, mark it and tell me why — incoherent, unattractive, or just nobody's bothered to stand there. Where two leaves make all the same predictions about my guy, flag them as unseparable instead of inventing a distinction.
- Each leaf gets one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and — the bit I actually need — one sentence on what would have to be *visible on the page* for that to be the right explanation. What does the scene look like? What's the reader meant to feel: dread, recognition, pity, contempt?
- I'd also like a split somewhere about whether the explanation is supposed to take him off the moral hook, because I can feel myself using the situational story to quietly forgive him and I'd rather do that on purpose than by accident.
- No footnotes, no reading list, no "see so-and-so 1996" — I'm not going to look anything up, I'd rather have your reasoning than your sources. Five to seven split questions, ten to fifteen leaves.

And if you've got room at the end: a six-question version I could hand my writing group, where they answer it about my draft and find out which explanation each of them has been silently assuming. I suspect we are not all reading the same book.
