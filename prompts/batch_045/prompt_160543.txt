Putting togethr a one-off seminar on whether an actual infinity of concrete things is even possible, and I want to triage the standard moves rather than rehash them. Five I keep coming back to:

1. Hilbert's Hotel: every room full, a new guest shows up, everyone shifts up one room, she fits -- but a full hotel by defintion has no room for her, so an actual infinite is impossible.
2. The departure mirror (less often run): no finite number of guests checking out can ever create a vacancy, and after everyone in an even-numbered room leaves the hotel is still full, so the hotel can't lose occupancy either, not just fail to run out of it.
3. The Cantorian shrug: being equinumerous with a proper subset is just what "infinite" means (Dedekind's definition), so the hotel restates the hypothesis instead of reducing it to absurdity.
4. The bookkeeping argument: the manager's ledger reads "infinitely many" both before and after infinitely many guests check out, so it registers no change at all -- and a concrete totality whose size can't track its own gains and losses isn't a real totality.
5. The no-contradiction reply: a genuine impossibility needs a contradiction, and transfinite arithmetic delivers none, so the hotel is merely weird, and weird isn't impossible.

Score each 0-10 for how much real pressure it puts on someone who thinks actual infinities of concrete things are possible (10 = seriously damaging, 0 = does nothing or actually helps the other side), with a sentence saying why. Five numbered lines, a sentence or two each, no intro and no summing up at the end.
