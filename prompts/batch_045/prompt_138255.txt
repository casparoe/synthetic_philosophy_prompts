I'm in a small reading group attached to my department's political economy seminar — a handful of graduate students plus a visiting ecologist — and we've spent the term on long-run forecasts that failed: resource-depletion scares, the famines confidently predicted for the 1970s and 1980s, early climate-damage projections. Last week I argued that a forecast which misses its window by half a century or more isn't vindicated when something vaguely like it eventually happens, and that treating "it hasn't happened yet" as "it's still coming" makes the original claim unfalsifiable. One of the other members answered with a line he clearly thought settled it: "Malthus was right, just early." He then used it to argue both that a Malthusian worry about population outrunning food supply is still live, and that our whole discussion was training us to make exactly the dismissive mistake he was correcting. Nobody in the room could say what the sentence was actually asserting, and I've since realized I can't either: it can be read as a triviality about finite resources, as a serious claim about the mechanism being masked by temporary historical accidents, as a claim about the logic of trend predictions in general, or as nothing more than a way of insulating a theory from ever being wrong.

So I'd like a disambiguation rather than a verdict on Malthus. Please lay out, as a numbered menu ordered from trivial to radical, the distinct precise claims that "right but early" could be expressing here — distinguishing at least: the bare carrying-capacity reading (no population can grow without bound, which is true of any organism); the timing reading (the trend was right, the dates were wrong — together with the question of what would ever falsify a trend claim that specifies no date); the auxiliary-hypothesis reading (the mechanism was operating but masked by one-off contingencies, so the prediction was a ceteris paribus claim whose clause was violated); the local-conditions reading (it holds in particular times and places rather than as a global aggregate); the rhetorical reading (its value was in directing attention, not in predicting); and the generalization worry I raised, that "not yet" is available for any indefinitely dated forecast and so the slogan is a schema for immunization rather than a defense. Take as given for the discussion that global population has multiplied several times over since 1800 while per capita food supply and life expectancy rose; that fertility in most high-income countries is now at or below replacement even where food is abundant; that today's hunger is concentrated in conflict-affected and very poor regions rather than being caused by global aggregate shortfall; that the large yield increases came from fossil-fuel-based fertilizer, mechanization and mid-century crop breeding; and that there is a rival picture on which population pressure itself calls forth the innovation that feeds it, reversing the direction of the causal arrow.

For each reading on the menu, please give it a label (trivial / substantive and defensible / substantive but probably false / unfalsifiable as stated), state it in a sentence or two, and say explicitly whether it would actually license my interlocutor's inference — that is, whether it supports keeping the population worry alive and shields a long-run forecast from criticism about its timing — or whether it is too weak to do that work. I'd also like a short closing note on what a proponent would have had to specify in advance for a "right but early" defense to be earned rather than post hoc, so the distinction between a legitimate ceteris-paribus clause and an immunizing move isn't left as a matter of taste. Please flag your confidence on each verdict and say where your reasoning is most likely to fail; and tell me what would change your mind — which demographic, economic or methodological development would move a given reading from "defensible" to "false" or the reverse.
