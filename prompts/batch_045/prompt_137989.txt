QUESTION

4. Risk and the reference class (20 marks)

The Department of Correctional Analytics uses an actuarial instrument validated on a sample of 40,000 people released from custody. For any class C of people in the sample, let f(C) be the proportion of members of C who were rearrested within three years. Call a class *undivided* (relative to the sample) if no attribute recorded in the sample divides C into two subclasses with different rearrest rates.

(a) [8 marks] The Department adopts a rule it calls Fixed-Order Narrowing (FON). Fix once and for all an ordering of the recorded attributes. For a new applicant, start with the whole sample, then work down the ordering adding attributes one at a time, and stop at the last class containing at least 250 members; output that class's f-value as the applicant's probability of rearrest. Does FON solve the reference class problem? In your answer, distinguish (i) the question which class one should use from (ii) the question whether there is any determinate probability of this applicant's being rearrested.

(b) [7 marks] Applicant D is a 29-year-old man with two prior convictions who is employed at the time of assessment. In the sample: f(men with two prior convictions) = 0.44; f(men aged 25–34 with two prior convictions) = 0.61; f(men aged 25–34 with two prior convictions and employed at assessment) = 0.23. The Department's file does not report the sizes of these classes, or whether any of them is undivided. Which of these numbers, if any, is D's probability of being rearrested? What does your answer commit you to about what 'probability' means here?

(c) [5 marks] A colleague remarks: the reference class problem is just the problem of induction wearing a lab coat — if the future resembles the past, the choice of class hardly matters, and if it doesn't, no class helps. Assess this remark.

STUDENT'S ANSWER

(a) FON does not solve the reference class problem, but it is on the right lines. Its defect is that it produces an answer by convention rather than by finding the right class: the output depends on the order in which the attributes happen to be listed and on the arbitrary cut-off of 250, and D belongs to indefinitely many classes with different frequencies, so nothing in the procedure selects one of them as the one that matters. That is the problem in form (i), the question of which class to use.

But the defect is really a data problem. The correct rule is to use the narrowest class available, since a narrower class is more like D, and the probability we want is the frequency of rearrest among people relevantly like D. FON has to stop at 250 only because below that the frequency is too noisy to trust. With a large enough validation sample the stopping point moves later and later, and in the limit we reach the class of people who share all of D's causally relevant properties. That class is undivided in the sense of the question — no recorded attribute splits it into subclasses with different rates — so its rate is not an average over a mixture of different kinds of case, and is therefore D's probability rather than merely our best estimate of it. The reference class problem is thus an epistemological problem about how much data we can afford; the fact of the matter is there, and we simply cannot always reach it. As to (ii), there is a determinate probability, namely the frequency in the correct class. A strict frequentist who holds that probability is nothing but long-run relative frequency will add that there is no single-case probability over and above that; but that is a metaphysical position, and FON's conventions neither support nor undermine it either way. FON fails, but for the practical reason that it is forced to stop narrowing too early.

(b) D's probability is 0.23. The correct class is the narrowest one that still yields a usable frequency, and the third class is undivided relative to the sample, so its rate applies uniformly to its members rather than averaging over dissimilar cases. The broader classes mislead precisely because they mix: 0.44 averages men of every age and employment status, 0.61 averages employed and unemployed men together, and the spread between 0.61 and 0.23 shows how much the mixing was hiding. Narrowing is monotonic in accuracy — every attribute we add makes the comparison class more like D, so each step can only bring us nearer the truth, and the fact that 0.61 lies further from 0.23 than 0.44 does is just a reminder of how coarse the broad classes were. Employment at assessment screens off the broader classes, so once we know that D is employed the other two numbers are irrelevant. And since the class is undivided, 0.23 is not merely our best estimate; it is D's chance of being rearrested.

(c) The colleague is wrong. The problem of induction asks whether the regularities we have observed will continue. Grant, for the sake of argument, that they will: let 0.44, 0.61 and 0.23 be perfectly stable from year to year. It is still unsettled what D's probability is, because D falls in all three classes and they disagree. So the reference class problem is not the problem of induction; it is a problem about which of several equally well-confirmed regularities attaches to a single case. The colleague has mistaken a question about whether there are stable frequencies for a question about how they apply. Nothing in his remark touches our case at all.
