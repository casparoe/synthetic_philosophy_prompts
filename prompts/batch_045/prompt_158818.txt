I am a PhD student in epistemology, and I am trying to settle on the modal condition on knowledge — the component that is supposed to do the work that the post-Gettier literature showed the tripartite analysis could not. Rather than start from the accounts already on the market, I want to start from my own verdicts on a batch of cases and see what principle they add up to. I have deliberately included two pairs of cases that I suspect pull in opposite directions.

(1) Ines checks the department room-booking page at 9 a.m.; it shows the seminar room free at 2 p.m.; she believes the room is free and books it. In fact the page was serving a cached copy: the room had been booked at 8 by another group, that booking was cancelled at 8:30, and the cancellation never propagated to the cache. The room is free at 2. My verdict: no knowledge.

(2) Driving through a region where all but one of the barn-looking structures are façades, Henry looks at the one real barn and believes that that is a barn. My verdict: no knowledge. Control: the same perceptual process, at the same distance, in an ordinary region — knowledge.

(3) Malik believes the 10:15 train is cancelled because a friend texted him that it is (the friend had misread the board) and because the station departure board itself says CANCELLED. My verdict: knowledge, even though his ground set contains an actively misleading element.

(4) Sofia believes the defence starts at 2 because the graduate coordinator's email says 2. She also, and irrelevantly, falsely believes that the coordinator has never once made a scheduling error. My verdict: knowledge — which makes me think a flat no-false-lemma clause is too strong as usually stated.

(5) Aisha posts to the lab channel that the compute cluster will be down from 2 to 4 for maintenance. No maintenance was scheduled; she wrote it carelessly. But the sysadmin reads the post and, to avoid confusing people, schedules maintenance for exactly 2 to 4. Her belief is true, and true because of her grounds. My verdict: knowledge — this is the verdict I am least confident about, and it is the one that sits worst with (1), since in both cases the grounds are misleading and the belief is true anyway.

(6) Theo asks a colleague about a grant deadline; the colleague is wrong about administrative deadlines nine times out of ten and is right on this occasion; Theo believes the deadline is Friday, and it is. My verdict: no knowledge.

(7) Priya asks a code assistant whether a certain function exists. The assistant hallucinates often, but its failures are confined to APIs introduced after its training cutoff; Priya verifies that the function is documented well before that cutoff, restricts her query accordingly, and the assistant says yes and is right. My verdict: knowledge — which sits badly with (6), since both sources are statistically unreliable in general and reliable only in the actual case.

(8) The case I keep going back and forth on: Tomas believes the seminar is cancelled because the department calendar says so; the calendar entry was auto-generated by copying the previous week's entry, so it would have said cancelled whatever the facts were; but the seminar is cancelled because the invited speaker saw the calendar entry, concluded the seminar was off, and did not travel.

My own tentative principle — call it anchored safety — is this: S knows that p iff (i) p is true; (ii) S believes that p on grounds G; (iii) there is no nearby possible world in which S has G, believes that p on that basis, and p is false, where the relevant neighborhood is generated by holding fixed, first, every condition S can discriminate and does discriminate, and second, the causal upshot of S's having those grounds, so that worlds in which the grounds themselves make p true are excluded from the neighborhood. The second clause is what I am relying on for (5); the first is what I am relying on for (7); and (6) is supposed to fail because neither clause applies to it.

What I would like from you:

- Does anchored safety, or something very close to it, deliver all eight verdicts? Where does it overgenerate or undergenerate? I am especially worried that clause (iii)'s first half collapses the neighborhood so far that it lets in too much, and that its second half lets in cases I have not thought of.
- If no single principle delivers all my verdicts, which verdict is the cheapest to overturn, and what is the simplest principle that survives that loss? Please say explicitly which of my verdicts your preferred principle cannot accommodate, rather than quietly reinterpreting them.
- What does your principle predict for case (8), and for two case types I have not listed: a subject who arrives at a true mathematical claim by a derivation containing an invalid step whose invalidity happens not to matter to the result; and a subject whose source is unreliable only in a subclass she could have identified and screened out but did not screen out, her belief being true?
- State the finished principle precisely enough that I could apply it to a further case without coming back to you, and note, clause by clause, which of my cases motivates each clause.
