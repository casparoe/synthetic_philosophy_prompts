In a seminar on the philosophy of mathematics we keep returning to the village barber: the barber shaves all and only those villagers who do not shave themselves, and the question whether he shaves himself is offered as Russell's paradox in ordinary language. One participant uses it to argue that the paradox is a cheap self-reference trick — just as we simply conclude that no such barber exists (or that he is a woman, or lives outside the village), so we should simply conclude that no such set exists, and naive set theory therefore needs only a local repair, a ban on self-referential membership conditions, rather than a general restriction on comprehension. I suspect the analogy is carrying that conclusion, and I would like an audit of it.

Give the correspondence table first: what plays the barber, the villagers, the act of shaving, the village, and the roster of villagers? Above all, what corresponds to the naive comprehension principle — the thesis that every condition determines a set — and to the guarantee that it hands us a set of exactly the non-self-membered sets before any contradiction is derived? My worry is that nothing does: with a fixed roster, the only conclusion available is that no villager shaves exactly the non-self-shavers, which is consistent and unremarkable. I have tried patching the story with a clause in the village charter — for any group of villagers you can describe, some villager shaves exactly that group — and I want to know whether the patched story is faithful to naive comprehension or whether it imports something else, the diagonal or powerset point rather than comprehension itself.

Then separate the disanalogies that matter for this use from those that do not: does it matter that shaving is something an agent does while membership arguably is not, that the barber story has easy outs the set does not, that a bounded roster replaces an open-ended universe? Does the patched story even deliver the corollary that there is no set of all sets, or does the barber framing make that corollary look trivial in a way it is not?

Finally, does the local-repair conclusion survive? And if the real moral is Cantor's diagonal argument, what analogue would make that visible rather than hide it?
