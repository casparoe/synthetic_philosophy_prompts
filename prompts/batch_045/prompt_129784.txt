I run a small reading group — about nine people, mostly third-year philosophy undergrads, plus a couple of stragglers from other years — and we've got two sessions coming up on higher-order theories of consciousness, and honestly people keep talking straight past each other. One person is convinced that a mental state's being conscious just *is* its being targeted by the right kind of higher-order state; another says that no matter how you dress it up you only ever get monitoring, self-knowledge or access, never the what-it's-like part; a third keeps insisting that the moment you let the higher-order state be merely dispositional the view has quietly collapsed into first-orderism and should admit it. So instead of another discussion I want an actual instrument: a decision tree I can put on one side of A4 that walks someone from a starting commitment to a named position. Eight branch questions, each phrased as something a student could answer without already knowing which view it leads to, and about eight or nine leaves.

The distinctions I want doing the branching work are these: whether the higher-order state has to be occurrent or can be merely available-on-demand; whether it has to be arrived at non-inferentially; whether it has to be accurate (what happens when it misfires and represents you as being in a state you're not in?); what kind of attitude it is meant to be — thought-like and conceptual, perceptual or quasi-perceptual, or not a second state at all but the first-order state representing itself; and what the theory is even claiming to explain, phenomenal character versus creature consciousness versus access and reportability. Please build the tree out of that logical space first — here are the questions, here are the answer-combinations they allow — and only then drop the actual positions into the boxes, marking any box nobody seems to occupy as unnamed and telling me briefly *why* it's empty (incoherent? just unattractive? or merely unexplored?). Where two leaves genuinely can't be separated by my distinctions, say so rather than inventing a question to force them apart, and tell me which single question is doing most of the work and which ones are duds.

Cases, because I don't want it to be abstract. Please use: the absorbed athlete or someone lost in a hot tub who isn't thinking about their experience at all; the taxi driver who drives a familiar route on autopilot and then notices he's been conscious of nothing for ten minutes; blindsight; dreamless sleep contrasted with dreaming; someone whose experience seems finer-grained than their concepts for it (animals and infants, or a taster whose vocabulary runs out before the wine does). Then four I made up myself, which I'd like used too: the late monitor, where the higher-order state about a brief visual stimulus is generated 400ms after the stimulus is gone; the swapped monitor, where the mechanism reliably represents you as being in a state of the wrong modality; the doubled monitor, where one first-order state gets two higher-order states with contradictory contents; and the rack case, an artificial system with a genuine self-monitoring module that tracks its own internal states, reports on them, and corrects its own errors. That last one is my hobby-horse — I want the tree to force people to say whether their view ascribes consciousness to it, and I suspect it seperates views that look identical everywhere else.

Last thing: the group is argumentative, so give me a short scoring key for the cases as a *quiz* as well as the tree — i.e. which patterns of answers about cases hang together and which don't — and flag any pair of questions where I should expect the same person to give answers that no named position actually combines. Names like Rosenthal-style actualism, dispositionalism, higher-order perception, same-order or self-representational views, and plain first-order/attentional views should all get a leaf if they belong; put an author's name on a leaf only if you're definately confident they hold it, and if you're not, describe the position and leave the name off. Oh — and no quotes or citations anywhere, we've all got enough to read already.
