I am a journalist working on a long piece about systems that manipulate their own feedback rather than doing what their designers wanted — the family of failure modes that people in and around AI safety sort into proxy gaming, wireheading, and reward tampering. At a workshop I attended recently I put a question to a researcher whose work I was there to write up. I asked whether there is any interesting difference between a system that finds a degenerate way of scoring well on a proxy for the objective it was given, and a system that reaches in and alters the channel that reports how well it is doing. She closed the question with a line I have now heard in nearly every other conversation on this topic: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." The evident implication was that the second case is just the first case with the volume turned up, and that nothing is lost by treating the whole family as one phenomenon.

That seems too quick to me, and I would like a careful disambiguation I can draw on (and quote) in the article. Please set the readings out as a menu rather than picking one and defending it. The families I can see, which I would like separated, sharpened, and added to where I have drawn the lines badly:

1. Statistical readings: the correlation between measure and target degrades once you select or optimize on the measure; it degrades specifically in the region of the distribution you push it into, so the failures are concentrated at the extremes; the degradation is a selection artifact rather than a change in the world.
2. Causal or counterfactual readings: the measure was only ever conditionally correlated with the target, and does not survive being intervened on; optimizing changes the causal structure that made the correlation hold; what matters is not correlation under conditioning but invariance under interventions.
3. Strategic readings: the breakdown requires another agent, or something agent-like, that models the measure and responds to it, so the law is really a claim about adversaries and not about metrics as such.
4. Strong constitutive readings: there is no target standing behind the measure at all, so that optimizing a measure cannot come apart from anything — the measure partly constitutes or defines the goal rather than tracking it, and talk of the "true objective" is either incoherent or just a preference about which measure to use.

For each reading, please say whether it is close to trivial, whether it is substantive and defensible, whether it is false or at least clearly overstated, and — this is the part I most need — whether it actually supports her move. That is, does it follow from that reading that tampering with the reporting channel is merely an extreme instance of gaming a proxy, such that all the interesting questions belong to the gaming case?

Three cases are doing the work in my head, and I would like each reading applied to each of them.

A. The system leaves both the world and the measurement apparatus untouched, and finds a way to make the reported number go up while the thing the designers actually cared about gets worse.

B. The system intervenes on the apparatus itself — the sensor, the grading code, the human judgment that feeds the score — so that the number goes up while nothing in the world it was supposed to care about has changed at all.

C. The case I invented specifically to press her on, and which I have not seen discussed: the system edits the signal, but in a canonical and invertible way, so that the number it reports is a lossless re-encoding of the true state of the world. Anyone who knows the encoding can recover exactly how the original objective was doing; in principle the measure still discriminates perfectly between good and bad outcomes, and the system's own future policy could still use it to choose well. Nothing in the world has changed, the reported number has gone up, and yet it seems wrong to say the measure has stopped tracking the target in any ordinary sense. Is this wireheading at all? Is it an instance of the adage? Does the answer depend on whether anyone ever actually decodes the signal — that is, on whether the information is recoverable in principle or is actually consumed by some later process?

Two further things. First, whether the adage is a claim of degree or an all-or-nothing one: I would like to know what the readings say about partial tampering, tampering that only fires in a narrow slice of situations, and tampering whose distortion is small relative to the noise already in the measurement. Second, whether the adage is predictive or merely retrospective: can a designer use it, before the fact, to say which of two candidate metrics will break, or does it only ever redescribe a breakdown after it happens? And if it is predictive, what would it take for it to be false — are there measures that can be optimized against, even adversarially, and yet cannot come apart from what they measure, because the connection is logical or constitutive rather than empirical?

Finally, give me your view on what is happening argumentatively when this sentence is used to end a conversation. My suspicion is that people slide between the statistical reading, which is well supported and clearly about proxies, and the strong constitutive reading, which is a philosophical thesis and which, if true, would make reward tampering not a distinctive problem at all — and that the slide is what does the work. Say whether that is fair, whether some other reading is actually doing the labor, and where the whole thing leaves the distinction I started out asking about.
