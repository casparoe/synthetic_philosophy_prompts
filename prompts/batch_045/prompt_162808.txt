hey, hoping you can help me untangle a fight we keep having in our reading group on the fairness impossibility results.

The setup: we've got a risk score, a binary outcome, and group membership, and the result everyone cites says a score can't be calibrated within each group *and* have equal false-positive and false-negative rates across groups at the same time — not unless the groups happen to have the same base rate, or the predictor is already perfect. The math isn't in dispute. What's in dispute is what it *means*, and one of the senior people in the group keeps reaching for Arrow to settle it.

His line is that this is basically Arrow's theorem in a hoodie: a short list of conditions that each look obviously reasonable on their own, jointly unsatisfiable, so the lesson is the same one social choice supposedly absorbed — stop hunting for the one correct fairness definition, accept that no definition is privileged by the math, pick one for your context, write it down in the model card, and move on. He'll even do the mapping out loud: groups are the voters, the fairness criteria are the axioms, the degenerate escape cases are the dictator. And he uses it to license a fairly relaxed political conclusion: since no notion is singled out as correct, it's fine — almost required — for each deployment site to choose its own, and we should stop treating the trade-off as a scandal or as something anyone needs to justify to the people on the receiving end, any more than a city needs to justify using plurality instead of Borda.

I don't buy it and I can't say exactly why, so: please audit the analogy properly.

- Give me the correspondence table. For each moving part on the Arrow side — voters, alternatives, individual preference orderings, the preference profile, the social welfare function, the social ordering that's supposed to come out at the end, Pareto/unanimity, independence of irrelevant alternatives, non-dictatorship, and the "domain" the theorem quantifies over — what's the supposed counterpart on the fairness side? And please explicitly flag the boxes that stay empty, because I suspect there are a lot of them and that he's quietly filling them in with nothing.

- Then the disanalogies, and sort them into ones that are cosmetic versus ones that actually do work against his conclusion. The four I half-suspect: (i) Arrow's is a necessity over *all* possible profiles, whereas the fairness result is contingent — it has escape hatches that are empirical facts about the world (equal base rates, perfect prediction), so it's an "almost always, given how the world happens to be" rather than a theorem about every possible input, and that seems to matter a lot if you want to move from it to "no definition is privileged"; (ii) the fairness result mixes two kinds of condition, since one of the two things it pits against the other (calibration) is arguably an *accuracy* property that only some people count as a fairness requirement at all, while Arrow's conditions are all normative desiderata of the same shape — so it's not obvious the fairness case is even a dilemma between two values; (iii) there is no aggregation anywhere — no voters, no inputs being combined, the groups aren't agents expressing anything — so whatever this result is, it isn't a social choice problem and the "who chooses" structure is entirely missing; (iv) the escape routes don't line up: Arrow's (restricted preference domains, cardinal intensities, dropping independence) have no counterpart here, and the fairness-side escapes (randomizing, abstaining and deferring to a human, changing the feature set, redefining the target outcome, moving the threshold) have no counterpart there. Tell me which of these actually bite and which are just philosophers nitpicking.

- A wrinkle I want folded in rather than set aside: in the system we're actually building, the model doesn't emit a probability at all. It emits a binary yes/no, and we're allowed to tune the threshold separately per group. My feeling is that this makes the Arrow picture worse than unhelpful, because the condition doing the heavy lifting on one side of the impossibility is a property of *scores* and isn't even well-defined for the artifact we ship — so the "dilemma" may be partly an artifact of modelling the output as a calibrated probability. Is that right? And if so, what *is* left of the trade-off in that setting, in your own words — what can and can't you have at once once you've fixed per-group thresholds?

- And the part I actually care about: does the relaxed conclusion survive? Grant the analogy for the sake of argument. Arrow's theorem is standardly read as "no rule is objectively best, so the choice is a political one" and that reading is fairly comfortable — but does it really support "each vendor or deployment site picks its own notion and owes nobody an accounting"? Or is the analogy smuggling in a story about *who* legitimately gets to choose, since in the voting case the chooser is the polity and the people bearing the consequences are the same people doing the choosing, whereas here the chooser is an institution and the people absorbing the error rates aren't in the room?

- Last: if Arrow is the wrong picture, is there a better one that's genuinely load-bearing — an operating-characteristic / sensitivity-vs-specificity curve, a Simpson's-paradox-style aggregation artifact, an incompatibility between conditional independencies — and what would *that* analogy license instead? Or is the honest answer "no analogy, it's just a fact about conditional distributions, and any analogy is doing rhetorical work"? If it's the latter, say what rhetorical work the Arrow framing is doing and why it's tempting.
