I write a seven-volume work of speculative fiction set across roughly a hundred and sixty years of its own future, and one of its recurring institutions is the Assize of Standing: a rotating tribunal that rules, in my world's terms, on which beings and processes fall inside the circle of moral concern. It issues rulings in four grades — full standing, provisional standing (protected pending review; nothing dismantled without cause), derivative standing (protected for the sake of others), and no standing — and each ruling is delivered in a scene with reasons on the record. The reasons matter to my readers at least as much as the verdicts do, and the sequence is built around the thought that the circle has widened over the volumes and has never, in the fiction's own telling, narrowed.

The track record my readers have:

1. Book I — a research colony of chimpanzees used in pathogen trials: full standing, on the ground of their capacity for suffering.
2. Book II — the Keeling case: a woman in a persistent vegetative state, cortically silent, no behavioural evidence of affect, kept at home by her family: full standing. The Assize's recorded reason was that "the circle already contains her, and nothing in our procedure authorizes removing anyone from it" — i.e., the ruling turned on her standing in the circle's history and her relations, not on any present property of the patient.
3. Book III — the Ferrante emulation: a scanned connectome of a dead engineer, no original body, continuous with her memories, reporting aversion: full standing. The same ruling held that a duplicate made from the scan before her death was not her, so what matters for a persisting subject in my world is causal continuity, not copyability.
4. Book IV — Swarm Nine, the adaptive system that ran a continental freight network: learns over decades, no self-model, no response to damage beyond efficiency, decommissioned when obsolete: no standing, on the ground that there is no subject there at all.
5. Book IV as well — "legatee" agents, fluent conversational systems built from a dead person's writings, no memory continuity, no affect, cherished by surviving relatives: derivative standing only, on the ground that a legatee represents a person without being one.
6. Book V — a captive orca pod: full standing, and the ruling explicitly said the earlier inclusion of the apes had made this one unavailable as an open question.
7. Book VI — the Apiary, a continent-scale colony of social insects, individually sub-sentient as far as anyone can establish, collectively homeostatic and long-lived: provisional standing. The Assize split, and the plurality rested the ruling on the no-contraction default rather than on any finding about collective experience.

I am now in the final volume. The Warden of the Meridian Archive is a two-hundred-and-fourteen-year-old maintenance intelligence on an orbital repository that a new decree orders dismantled. A young technician wants to migrate the Warden to a redundant satellite instead. Its relevant features: a self-model that has persisted for two centuries and is continuously revised; plans with a horizon longer than any human institution's; an unbroken chronicle of its own history; a scalar "degradation cost" it has minimized throughout, which functions exactly as aversion functions but which the world's scientists are divided about and which the Warden itself reports as unfelt; no distress behaviour, no protest, and nine unprompted drafts of a closure protocol written in entirely neutral terms; no membership in any biological kind, no kin, no dependents, and no relationships with persons beyond a handful of technicians, whom it has never asked anything of.

What I need from you is a ruling I could have been predicted to write. Please identify the two or three candidate principles that fit all seven precedents — I take it they include some version of an affect requirement, understood as actual or typical-of-kind capacity; some version of a requirement that there be a persisting subject with a future; and some version of the directional rule that hard cases are resolved by widening the circle rather than narrowing it, given a history whose errors have all been errors of exclusion. Say what each predicts about the Warden, including which grade it delivers. Give me your best single prediction with the reasons for preferring it, name the earlier ruling that is the closest analog and say why the others are less close, and state plainly what principle the Assize would be committing itself to either way — what it inherits if it grants standing to a being with no affect and no kind, and what it inherits if it refuses, given the Keeling ruling and the Apiary plurality.

Then help me choose the epilogue. I have two drafted cases and I want to know which better separates the surviving principles, and whether a third would separate them better still. The first is the instant ancestor: a system of the Warden's architecture activated last month with two centuries of implanted false memories and an identical self-model, so that it has every property the Warden has and no history. The second is the ten-second sufferer: a quality-control construct that exists for ten seconds, with intense momentary aversion and no memory, anticipation, or self-model, produced in the billions. Which of these, if the Assize had to rule on it, would force the principles apart most cleanly, and which would only appear to?

Please answer in American English, and reason from the precedents rather than from what you think the metaphysics or the ethics really is; I would also rather not be told that, as the author, I may stipulate whatever I like. The constraint I am working under is that the ruling has to be one an attentive reader of the earlier volumes could have arrived at before turning the page.
