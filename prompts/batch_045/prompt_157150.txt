right so I've been sat here for a fortnight trying to write down where I've actually got to on property, and I've a suspicion I'm helping myself to something somewhere. Can you audit this lot? Roughly where I've landed:

(1) Entitlement theory. Whether you're entitled to what you've got is purely a matter of history — legitimate acquisition, then voluntary transfer, plus whatever fixes past injustices — and not a matter of whether the overall distribution has any particular shape. No patterned or end-state principles.

(2) I think the Wilt Chamberlain argument genuinely works, and I think it works as a knockdown rather than as one consideration to be weighed against others. If your principle says a distribution is unjust in virtue of its shape, you have to stop people voluntarily handing a basketball player 25 cents each to watch him play, or take it back off him afterwards, and that's just not on. So patterned principles are out.

(3) Taking a slice of what someone earns to fund redistribution is morally on a par with making them work for you a few hours a week. Hence: no redistributive welfare state.

(4) But I'm with the left on natural resources. The Lockean proviso is a real constraint: appropriate something that used to be available to everyone and you owe the rest of us for it. And I read it as an ongoing charge that sticks to the asset, so it travels with the land when it's sold and whoever buys it picks it up, rather than as a one-off debt paid off by whoever happened to grab it first. I'd fund a citizen's dividend out of it. (Folk keep telling me the proviso can't do any work now that nothing's unowned any more; I think the sticky reading dodges that, but I'm not definately right.)

(5) Rectification matters a lot to me. Huge amounts of current holdings descend from enclosures, colonisation and slavery, and I think the state should be paying reparations. I've got nothing worked out on who owes whom.

(6) Two verdicts. There are things the state should be able to stop you selling even when both parties want the trade — your vote, a kidney, yourself into slavery. And it's fine to leave the lot to your kids tax-free, and fine that some families stay rich for centuries off the back of it.

I can't tell which of these is load-bearing and which is decoration, and I've got about four things nagging at me with no idea how to rank them:

- Does (4) survive (2)? The ongoing charge has to be reapplied indefinitely and it constrains what people end up with, which is exactly the sort of thing Chamberlain is meant to kill. But it also feels different and I can't say why. Same worry in reverse: isn't the charge just a tax, which (3) says is forced labour?
- (5) and (3). Reparations have to be funded, presumably out of general taxation, and by (3) that's forced labour imposed on people whose own titles might be spotless. If the answer is 'only take from the tainted holdings', then how? Here's the case that breaks me: a mill built in 1790 on enclosed common land, sold six times since, now sitting in a pension fund whose members are mostly nurses and teachers. Who owes what to whom? And if the honest answer is that nobody can trace it any more, haven't I just got redistribution with a history-flavoured excuse, which is what (2) says I can't have?
- (6a) against (2). If I ban kidney sales because a skint seller isn't really consenting, haven't I conceded that background circumstances can make a transfer unfree, and if so why doesn't that sink Chamberlain? If I ban them because some rights just aren't yours to sell in the first place, that seems to sit fine. The two motivations feel seperate to me but I can't get them apart, and I don't know which one I actually believe.
- And my mate reckons (5) and (6b) can't both be true: either inherited holdings are fine or they're tainted, he says. I don't see it — a transfer is a transfer — but he might be on to something and I genuinely can't tell.

What I want is: which pairs are actually in tension, ordered worst first, and for each one what specifically would have to give and what that costs me. Plus which pairs only look like they clash. Don't just explain the three principles at me, I know them, and don't tell me which commitment I should drop — give me the price list and I'll pick.
