I'm writing a term paper for a metaphysics seminar on the identity of indiscernibles, and I've got stuck on a dialectical point that my reading group keeps talking past. Here is how I've reconstructed the dialectic. Black's two-spheres scenario — an otherwise empty universe containing two spheres alike in every qualitative respect, a fixed distance apart — is supposed to refute the Leibnizian principle that no two distinct things can be exactly alike, since the spheres apparently share every property while still being two. The obvious resistance is to insist that they do differ: each one stands in a relation the other does not (being the sphere that the other one is two miles from, or simply being the thing it is), so this was never really a case of indiscernibility. My worry, which my instructor also pressed in office hours, is that if the only difference available has to be spelled out by using the individuals themselves, then the principle being defended has quietly lost whatever content it had.

Two repairs have been floated in the group, and I want a real comparison of them rather than a balanced list of pros and cons. Call the first the haecceitist repair: grant that the spheres share every purely qualitative property, intrinsic and relational alike, but hold that each thing also has a non-qualitative intrinsic property — its haecceity or thisness — so that the restricted principle (things sharing all intrinsic properties are identical) comes out true in every world, Black's included. Call the second the symmetry repair, which I find more attractive: reformulate the principle structurally, so that what it says is that a world contains no non-trivial symmetries, i.e. no permutation of the things in it that preserves every qualitative relation, and understand the principle as one that a given world may satisfy or fail depending on how symmetric that world happens to be.

To pressure the comparison, here are two variants I came up with while procrastinating. Variant One, the swap case: take a Black-style universe in which the two spheres drift toward one another head-on, and compare a history in which they rebound and each returns to the side it came from with a history in which they pass through one another and each continues to the far side. In both histories the overall distribution of sphere-stuff is the same at every instant — two spheres symmetrically placed about the midpoint, approaching and then separating — but there still seems to be a difference about which individual ends up where. Variant Two, the lopsided-signal case: again two intrinsically identical spheres, but this time one of them emits a pulse that the other later absorbs, and not conversely, so the world contains an asymmetric qualitative relation linking them.

Please compare the haecceitist repair and the symmetry repair in the light of these two cases, and take a stand on which is the more defensible and where the weaker one goes wrong, rather than just surveying the options. In particular: does either repair collapse into the other (is the haecceitist repair just the symmetry repair applied to a domain enriched with non-qualitative properties, or the symmetry repair just a world-relative way of saying what the haecceitist says globally)? Which repair, if either, leaves the principle with genuine work to do — something one could actually use in arguing about whether a world's qualitative structure settles which individuals there are — and which merely restates the conclusion it was meant to support? Should the symmetry repair's being contingent on how symmetric a world happens to be count against it, or is that precisely the right result given Variant Two, where the principle seems to hold only because the world happens to contain an asymmetry? And in Variant One, does either repair have the resources to say what the difference between the two histories consists in, or must both concede that there is no difference at all?
