I spent about a decade doing failure analysis for a consumer-products company — mostly drop tests, impact tests, and the arguments afterward about whether the thing we tested was really the thing we shipped — and since retiring I've been reading a little metaphysics on the side, badly. I keep running into the same knot. Everyone I read, and everyone I used to argue with at work, seems happy to say that a thing's being fragile, or soluble, or conductive, or disposed to shatter just amounts to what would happen to it under the right circumstances: fragile means would break if struck, and similarly for the rest. But I have a whole shelf of cases from the lab where that translation goes wrong, and — this is the part I actually care about — it seems to go wrong in several distinguishable ways at once, and I have no vocabulary for any of them. I want the vocabulary because I'd like to be able to explain to an engineer why 'it passed the test' and 'it is safe' can come apart in several different ways, and why only some of those ways are cheating.

Here are the patterns as I've sorted them so far. First: a genuinely fragile ceramic component dropped onto a padded rig. It doesn't break, but nothing about the component changed; the padding absorbed the blow. The interference is entirely external. Second: a genuinely fragile component wired to a monitor that alters the component's internal structure the instant an impact is detected, so that at the moment of the strike it is no longer fragile. Here the object itself changes, and the change is triggered by the test. Third — my actual worry — comes from materials like shear-thickening gels: the material is soft and deformable at rest, but a sharp impact makes it go rigid, and it is the impact itself, with no separate monitor and no added gadget, that produces the change, as a matter of how the material normally behaves. So the very condition that would reveal the deformability is the condition under which the deformability is gone, and yet it is plainly still a soft material: press it slowly and it deforms. It resembles the second pattern, but calling them the same thing feels like a cheat, because in the second case the change was engineered from outside and simply removed a disposition, whereas here the change is the material's own response to being loaded and gives it the opposite of the disposition in question. Fourth, the reverse of all this: a component that is not fragile at all, but which a colleague will smash with a hammer the moment anyone strikes it, so that 'it would shatter if struck' comes out true while the disposition everyone cares about is simply absent.

A colleague with a philosophy minor told me years ago that at least a couple of these patterns already have stock names in the literature — she thought one of them was a short word that also means a police informer — but she couldn't reconstruct the definitions and I was never able to chase it down. So: do these patterns have established names, and if so which name belongs to which pattern, and why? Give me the definitions rather than the labels. In particular, is my third pattern one of the named ones or something else, and what would settle that — which feature should I be looking at: whether the change is intrinsic to the object, whether it is triggered by the stimulus itself rather than by a separate device, whether there is any time gap at all between stimulus and change, whether the disposition is merely hidden rather than removed, whether the response is normal operation rather than an intervention? Tell me which of my four patterns actually damages the naive 'would-M-if-S' analysis and which one is harmless to it, and give me at least one further case that sits right on the boundary between my second and third patterns so I can see where the line is supposed to fall. If the third pattern has no established name, coin one and define it tightly enough that a clear instance of my second pattern falls outside it, and say whether the distinction does any work beyond the analysis of dispositional language — for laws of nature, or causation, or for what we should conclude when a product behaves differently under test than on the rack. One last thing, possibly a separate problem: I don't know where to file one-shot cases like a match, which is disposed to light when struck but cannot be struck twice, so say if that belongs to this same family or is something else entirely.
