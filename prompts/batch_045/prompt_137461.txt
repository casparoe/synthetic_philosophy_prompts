I've got a 90-minute slot in an upper-level metaethics seminar, 14 students, most of them fine with the reasons-first framework and with buck-passing about value, but all of them treating it as obvious that a reason is a reason *for* somebody. I want to loosen that. Can you design an activity where they end up taking seriously the idea that there could be rationales that aren't anyone's — that a consideration can count in favor of something even with no agent around whose reason it is — without me saying any of that up front, and without the label showing up until the debrief? Ideally they build the case themselves and only notice at the end where they've arrived.

Two things I want built in, because they're exactly what I fail to get across when I just lecture at them.

First, I think they collapse two cases: a reason nobody in fact has (unclaimed) and a reason nobody could have (unclaimable — no agents exist, or the content doesn't fit any possible agent's situation, whatever). They hear "no one has it" as obviously meaning "it isn't there," and so they can't even hear the interesting version, which is the unclaimed-but-claimable one. I want the activity to drive that wedge apart in a way they generate rather than one I hand them.

Second, the case type I find most gripping and can never make land: orphaned mandates. A rule or rationale left behind by a practice that no longer exists — a lighthouse whose ships are gone, a checksum in a dead protocol, a monastic rule for a monastery empty for two centuries, a file format nobody can open anymore. Something that seems to still *call for* something while nobody around has the aim that made it a reason. If you can build the activity around them inventing their own orphaned mandates and then stripping features away to see what normative residue survives, great, but do it however works better.

What I need: rules and materials (whiteboard and index cards, that's the room; no money, and ideally no laptops), a rough timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto specific moves in the dialectic. I also want them to come away seeing that this isn't just objectivism about value re-described, since that's definately where half the room will try to file it. And tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — my guess is someone says "that's just a metaphor" and the whole thing deflates, but you tell me — plus be honest about which feature of the idea the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to say out loud myself.

Write it as plain continuous prose, no headings, bullets, bold or tables, just something email-length I can read on my phone on the way in.
