I work on well-being measurement for policy. Right now I am writing the methods appendix for a national survey instrument that a government will use to rank interventions by their expected effect on self-reported well-being, and I keep running into the same problem: I have to say, in operational terms, when a person's own report about how her life is going should be discounted as a distortion rather than counted as evidence. Before I commit the appendix to print I want a consistency check on the package of views I have ended up with. I write them out below in the flat, unordered way I actually hold them; I do not know where the trouble is, and I do not have a favorite to give up.

(a) Take people's own reports at face value by default. The burden of proof is on anyone who says a person's contentment does not track her good, because every alternative I know of lets the measurer's values in through the back door. In particular, the bare availability of a causal story about how a preference was formed — shaped by pressure, by narrowed options, by what everyone around her expected — does not by itself license discounting it. Every preference has a causal history, including mine.

(b) Contentment can be a genuine defect and not merely a report about bad circumstances. Someone who has adjusted her wants downward because her options were unjustly narrowed is not for that reason doing well. Contentment with less because you were denied more is a real loss, and the loss is located partly in the preference itself, not just in the situation.

(c) For the instrument, actual reported well-being is the primary measure. Not a reconstruction of what she would want if she were better informed, not an objective list of goods, not an idealized version of her own valuation. The whole point of asking people is to stop substituting my judgment for theirs.

(d) The one thing I have found that does real work in identifying the cases in (b) is what people do when the option set actually changes. I keep coming back to a ten-year longitudinal panel of a cash-plus-exit program in one district, where a real and non-stigmatizing exit from a domestic arrangement most outside observers would call unjust became available. Three findings: a clear majority of women declined the exit; of the minority who took it, most did not go back and reported being better off than baseline after about two years; and the small group who left and then returned settled back at their old reported level and described the exit as a mistake. I read the second finding as evidence that the prior contentment of the leavers was adaptive, the first as no evidence at all that the stayers were fine, and the third as evidence that the returners had been genuinely fine all along. The asymmetry is deliberate: leaving and not returning counts; declining to leave does not.

(e) A woman who enters a demanding, self-sacrificial caregiving role she initially resented, has no realistic exit for years, and then comes to endorse it wholeheartedly on reflection, is I think genuinely better off for it, and her later endorsement is authoritative rather than a capitulation to be explained away. I notice that the shape of the causal story here is the same as in (b) — pressure, no exit, accommodation — and that this bothers me without my knowing what to do about it.

(f) Whatever test I use must not let me read my own politics off the data. If declining an exit is no evidence of being fine for the woman in the district, it should be no evidence for the associate at a competitive firm who says she is satisfied with her eighty-hour week and declines a reduced-hours track that is genuinely available and not career-ending, or for the voter who supports a policy that predictably cuts services her own household uses. And I do not want to end up classifying most of the population's satisfactions as deformed; I want the test to return "not deformed" often, including in cases like these.

What I want is an audit of the set, not a survey of the literature on adaptive preference. Go through the fifteen pairs, and give me a row for every pair you judge to be in tension, whether the tension is genuine or merely apparent; then one line each on the pairs you judge clean, so I can see what you are not flagging. I expect at least one pair that looks inconsistent and is not, and I want that one identified and dissolved rather than waved through. For each real row I want the mechanism — what specifically one commitment commits me to that the other blocks — a severity, what would have to give, the cheapest revision that fixes it, and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the set. Order the rows by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and tell me which single revision to a single commitment would clean up the most and what it would leave me unable to say. Where a pair's tension is only apparent, say what distinction dissolves it and whether that distinction is one my appendix would have to make explicit. Finally, tell me whether any commitment in the set is doing work its own motivation cannot support — that is, where I am holding a view while quietly relying on a reason for it that one of my other commitments rules out.
