QUESTION

Vega is the night watchman at a warehouse. A standing instruction requires whoever is on duty to lock the warehouse gate at 10 p.m. On the night of June 14 Vega simply forgets; the gate stands open; a passerby walks in and removes the contents of the warehouse. It is natural to say that Vega's not locking the gate caused the loss of the contents.

Two stipulations from the syllabus: the class was told not to treat "omission" and "absence" as interchangeable terms, and told that either of the positions defined in part (c) may be defended. Marks turn on whether arguments are stated correctly and inferences are valid, not on which position is adopted.

(a) Set out the argument for denying that Vega's omission caused the loss, which I have been calling the "no negative relata" argument: the argument that turns on the ontology of events and on what a process or transference conception of causation requires of its relata. [5 points]

(b) Set out the "double prevention" reply to that argument, and the objection that the reply overgenerates causes. Say whether the reply survives the objection, and why. [6 points]

(c) Apply the following two positions to a variant case in which Vega has no instruction, owes no duty, and is not expected by anyone to lock anything, and explain how their verdicts differ:

— The Anchoring View: an omission counts as a cause of an outcome only if the omitted action was required of the agent by a norm that was in force at the time and that the agent had some reason to comply with; where no such norm is in force, the agent's not acting is causally idle no matter what the counterfactuals say.

— The Contrast View: attributions of causation by omission are relativized to a contrast class. "Vega's not locking the gate caused the loss" says that the loss occurred given that he did not lock the gate and would not have occurred given a relevant alternative in which he did; change the contrast class and you change the truth of the claim. On this view the causal relation is three-place — cause, outcome, contrast — and the contrast is supplied by the context of the inquiry, not by any norm.

Also address the variant in which Vega locks the gate as instructed but the passerby has a key and would have entered anyway. [6 points]

(d) Does settling (a) through (c) settle whether Vega is morally responsible for the loss? Explain, distinguishing causal responsibility from moral responsibility. [3 points]

STUDENT'S ANSWER

(a) The no negative relata argument begins from a process or transference conception of causation: a cause operates by transmitting something — energy, momentum, some conserved quantity, a mark — to its effect along a continuous process. Causation is therefore a relation among events, and events are spatiotemporal particulars with the right sort of dynamic structure. An omission is not like that. It is not a particular at all but the non-occurrence of an act, and there is nothing present to do any transmitting. So Vega's not locking the gate cannot serve as a relatum of the causal relation, and the sentence "Vega's omission caused the loss" is not literally true. It remains useful, because the associated counterfactual — had he locked the gate, the contents would not have been taken — is true and does the explanatory and legal work; the surface claim is best read as an abbreviated counterfactual explanation. Two further points. First, this is a case of omission rather than mere absence: Vega is an agent under a standing instruction, and the counterfactual is informative only against that background, whereas the mere absence of, say, a second lock on the gate is a different kind of thing altogether. Second, the law tracks the same asymmetry: in every legal system I am aware of, a failure to act grounds liability only where there was an antecedent duty to act, and never merely because the outcome would not have occurred otherwise.

(b) The double prevention reply concedes the process theorist's ontology but denies her conclusion. The omitted action would have interrupted the process leading to the loss: had Vega locked the gate, the passerby's entry would have been prevented, and the entry is what produced the loss. So the omission is itself a prevention of a prevention — it prevents the entry, which would have prevented the loss — and omissions can be causes after all, without admitting negative relata into the ontology. The standard objection is overgeneration: if preventing a would-be preventer suffices, then everything that could have stopped the entry counts as a cause of the loss, including the Queen's not having been standing in the doorway and my not having parked across the gate, and so on without limit. I think the objection is less serious than it appears. Overgeneration is the mirror image of the difficulty that late preemption poses for simple counterfactual accounts: there, the outcome fails to depend on the cause although the cause is plainly a cause, and the repair is to require that dependence run along a genuine causal chain so that no preempted alternative route is counted. The same repair works here. Once we require the dependence to run along a continuous chain from the omission to the outcome, the spurious double preventers drop out, while Vega's omission is retained because the chain from his failure to the loss is unbroken. Omissions are therefore in no worse shape than ordinary events, and the metaphysics of the case is settled by the counterfactuals together with the chain condition.

(c) The Anchoring View and the Contrast View appear to be rivals but are the same thesis in two vocabularies. To say that the omitted action was required by a norm in force is simply to say that the action belongs to the salient contrast class: what makes "Vega locked the gate" the relevant alternative, rather than "Vega locked the gate and the passerby had a key," is precisely that the former is what he was supposed to do. So the two views agree in the variant. Where Vega has no instruction, owes no duty, and no one expects anything of him, there is no causation by omission: on the Anchoring View because no norm was in force, and on the Contrast View because no contrast is salient. The general moral is that omissive causal claims are not straightforwardly true or false at all; they are assessed relative to the interests and expectations of whoever is asking. That is why legal factfinders are standardly directed to ask whether the defendant's omission made a difference to the outcome rather than whether it caused the outcome.

(d) The metaphysical question and the responsibility question are simply independent, and the metaphysics has nothing to contribute to the ethics. Moral responsibility requires more than causal contribution: it requires a duty, knowledge or reasonable foreseeability, and a fair opportunity to act. Conversely, even if omissions are never causes, a defendant can still be answerable for what he failed to prevent, because liability can be grounded in the duty itself rather than in causation. So nothing in parts (a) through (c) settles whether Vega is morally responsible for the loss; settling that requires the normative and epistemic questions, and Vega forgot, which is a separate matter concerning excuse.
