hey — me and two friends run a tiny reading group on institutional design (we're not academics, we just argue on sundays and put out a zine). We've been stuck on futarchy for about three weeks. The friend who's most into it keeps defending it with the thermostat analogy: voters set the target, i.e. they pick the welfare metric, and the prediction market is the controller that works out which knob-turns actually move the needle, the same way you set 21 degrees and don't care how the boiler manages it. He uses it to argue that futarchy is purely instrumental — there's no politics in the mechanism, it just delivers whatever voters asked for more reliably — so if you hate an outcome you should blame the metric and not the market, and worries about manipulation, inequality of influence, or who gets to trade are really worries about the target variable, not about futarchy itself.

To make this concrete rather than a shouting match, the version we've actually been arguing over isn't quite Hanson's original. It's a tiebreak setup: the legislature shortlists three or four policies, all already legal and all pre-cleared on rights and fiscal grounds; a welfare index is pre-registered up front and measured at a fixed horizon five years after implementation; a conditional market runs for six weeks where people trade claims of the form "the index will be higher if A is adopted than if B is adopted"; and if the winner's implied edge over the runner-up is below a set spread threshold, nothing is adopted and the status quo stays. Stakes are capped, and settlement is in points rather than cash, which get paid out as funding for causes the traders themselves name.

Can you audit the thermostat analogy against that specific setup? I'd like it in these sections:

1. "Mapping" (~200 words plus a small table): what plays the role of the setpoint, the sensor, the error signal, the actuator, the room, the lag, and the person who turns the dial.
2. "What carries over" (~150): the respects in which the analogy is genuinely apt and not just decorative.
3. "Where it breaks" (~400): the disanalogies, and for each one whether it actually bites on this tiebreak version or whether my friend can shrug it off.
4. "What it smuggles in" (~200): the features of thermostats the analogy quietly lends futarchy — automaticity, tight feedback, a measurement nobody can game, a controller with no interests of its own — and which of those the setup lacks.
5. "Verdict" (~250): does the "it's purely instrumental, blame the metric" conclusion survive once the breakages are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it?
6. "Better analogy" (~100): if thermostats are the wrong frame, what everyday mechanism would you put in its place, and what would that frame make visible that the thermostat hid.
