Hey — I'm on a budget advisory committee for our school district (it's a volunteer thing, supposed to be two evenings a month, it has eaten my entire winter) and we are completely deadlocked over one pot of money, roughly 900k of one-time surplus, and I keep hearing the same argument and I genuinely can't tell if it's deep or if it's just "let's split the difference" wearing an economics costume.

The case: one bloc wants the money to expand the selective "gifted and talented" track — excellence, bored kids, merit should be recognised, that sort of thing. The other bloc wants it spent on reading specialists for the lowest-performing elementary grades. And crucially, for that second group it isn't *only* a claim about where money does most good: several of them think the gifted track is itself a bad thing, that it sorts nine-year-olds into a hierarchy, and they'd rather it not exist even if someone else paid for it. So it's not obviously two tastes about the same pot. The guy on our committee who works in finance keeps saying this is textbook gains from trade: two parties, different preferences, each values what it wants more than whatever it gives up, so moving off the status quo is a pure win — apples for oranges, two endowments, everyone walks away better off — and the conclusion he draws is that we should cut the pot roughly in half and stop posturing, and that anyone holding out is leaving value on the table.

Here's why I don't trust that, but I want an actual audit rather than my own hunch. Please give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the two traders, the endowment, the goods, the price, the money, the no-trade point, and the "gains." Then where it breaks, and — the part I care most about — which of those disanalogies actually matter for the job the analogy is being asked to do here, versus which are just decoration. Some things I'm suspicious about, tell me if I'm onto something or barking up the wrong tree: (1) in a market there's a numéraire and you can make side payments, so the efficient outcome can be lopsided and the loser is still compensated; we have no money to move, so "splitting the difference" seems to be secretly doing the job that side payments do, and doing it badly, because halving ignores how much each side actually cares — the bloc that cares twice as much gets the same half. (2) Gains from trade can come from different *tastes* or from different *predictions* about the world, and our disagreement is partly the second kind: will reading specialists actually move test scores, does tracking actually help the tracked kids. If it's the second kind, then "compromising" looks less like trade and more like a bet or a hedge, and I don't see why splitting it is a Pareto improvement rather than a coin flip with a fairness story attached. (3) The market picture treats values like consumer preferences — given, commensurable, satiable, not the kind of thing you argue about — and it seems to import that into a case where one side's position is closer to a veto ("don't build this thing") than to a good they're holding out for. Can a veto be bought off, and is being bought off the same thing as being compensated? Is it even the same kind of thing as losing a vote? (4) I've started calling an alternative "the no-halves rule" in my own notes: nobody gets half of anything, instead you bundle — the equity side gets its reading specialists fully funded, the merit side gets something that isn't the selective track but is still real (enrichment and subject acceleration inside mixed classrooms, say), and you enlarge the pie by killing the $250k standardized test-prep consultant contract that literally no one on the committee wants and that only exists because it got renewed by default in 2019. Is that closer to what the exchange-economy picture actually recommends than halving is, or have I just reinvented logrolling and called it economics? And finally: does the finance guy's conclusion — split it, holding out is irrational — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the whole thing? If markets are the wrong picture, what's a better one for two value systems that have to keep sharing a district after the vote? Don't bother with reading recommendations or citations, I can't get at a library database from here anyway — just reason it out and be concrete about our case.
