I'm giving a forty-minute talk with a twenty-minute question period at a joint session of the statistics department's machine learning theory seminar and the philosophy department's formal epistemology group. I'm a fifth-year PhD student in philosophy, I've been reading the rationalist blogosphere for about fifteen years, and the talk is aimed at the seam between those two worlds. The room will be roughly: twelve ML theorists and statisticians, for whom the no-free-lunch theorems are a technically correct but practically irrelevant curiosity and for whom any whiff of learning is impossible sets off alarms; two visitors from a universal-induction group who regard Solomonoff completeness as the solution to induction and its incomputability as an implementation detail; three formal epistemologists and philosophers of science, who will want to know what any of this adds to Hume and Goodman and who will pounce on the word normative; and a half-dozen rationalist-adjacent graduate students who will reach for embedded agency and Löbian self-trust before anyone else gets a hand up.

The headline of the talk is that the no-free-lunch theorems are Hume's problem of induction with a measure on it. The thesis I'm defending is that the real content of both is a structural condition I call the outsider condition: a predictor can be universally consistent for a class of environments only if it does not itself belong to that class. My argument, which I'd like the objections to target:

1. For any computable predictor p, the anti-p environment — the environment that reads p's next prediction and emits the opposite — is itself computable, with Kolmogorov complexity at most that of p plus a constant. So any computable agent can be maximally fooled, forever, by an environment no more complex than the agent is.
2. Call a class of environments diagonally closed when it contains the anti-p environment for every predictor in it. Nice learnable classes are not diagonally closed: the anti-ERM labelling need not be realizable by the hypothesis class, which is why finite VC dimension buys you a finite-sample guarantee and no contradiction with step 1 arises. The class of all computable environments is diagonally closed, and so is any class rich enough to contain a copy of the predictor.
3. Solomonoff's universal prior evades step 1 only because it is lower semicomputable rather than computable: it sits outside the class over which it is universal. That is the outsider condition, and it is doing all the work. Every computable approximation loses the guarantee, because the diagonal twin of the approximation is cheap to describe and is back in the class.
4. Its guarantee is also the wrong shape for an agent like us. Solomonoff completeness bounds total errors, which says nothing about when the errors arrive; an environment that prints zeros until the largest number nameable by a hundred-bit program and then does whatever your favourite computable learner least expects has low complexity and makes every computable approximation useless for arbitrarily long, which matters enormously if you have a horizon or a discount rate.
5. An embedded agent is inside the class of environments it has to predict, since its own future outputs are part of the environment. For such an agent the class is diagonally closed, so the outsider condition cannot be met by anything physically implementable.
6. So there is no solution, only mitigation, and the mitigation is a choice — of prior, of comparison class, of discount — that evidence cannot settle. I propose to size that residue with a quantity I call exposure: the worst-case expected loss over the next N predictions, relative to an oracle that already knows which member of the class is actual, with the worst case taken over the diagonal closure of the support of your own prior. I concede up front that I have no account of which comparison class one ought to choose; my claim is only that the choice is unavoidable and not eliminable by evidence.

What I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defence of the view. Give me the eight to ten objections I will actually get, each labelled with which subgroup asks it and phrased in that subgroup's idiom — the statisticians should sound like statisticians, with distribution-free, realizable, finite-sample and rate in their mouths, the philosophers should sound like philosophers, and the universal-induction people should sound like people who think I have mistaken an approximation artifact for a philosophical insight. Order them by expected damage, likelihood multiplied by damage, and say explicitly when an objection is unlikely but lethal. For each: a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds, and one sentence on where that reply is soft, circular, or concedes more than I realise. Tell me which two of them are really one objection wearing two vocabularies, so I can answer it once. Give me a one-sentence concession to open on that costs me nothing, tell me which single objection loses the room if I botch it, and give me the one I'm hoping nobody asks.

Two constraints. First, no bibliography, no suggestions of reading, no citations: nobody in this room is going to ask me for references, and padding the sheet with them is wasted space. Assume I know the standard literature. Second, assume your reader knows Solomonoff induction, AIXI, PAC learnability and VC dimension, grue, and the shape of Hume's argument, so don't explain them; and skip the generic objections, since nobody here is going to ask me how I know the sun will rise tomorrow. Where an objection shows that one of my definitions cannot do the work I need — I suspect the statisticians will go after step 2, and I want to know whether diagonal closure as I've stated it can be made to carry steps 4 and 5 — say so bluntly instead of smoothing it over.
