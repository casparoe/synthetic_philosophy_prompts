I'm trying to work out what I actually think about metaphysical grounding and fundamentality by going case by case, and I've ended up with a set of verdicts that I can't fit under a single principle. I'd like you to try. Below are my verdicts; after them is the principle I've been toying with. I want the principle tested against the cases and either repaired or replaced, and I want to know which of my verdicts have to go.

My verdicts:

1. Statue and clay. The fact that the statue exists is grounded in the fact that the clay exists and is arranged thus. Not the reverse. The relation is asymmetric here, and the statue is the derivative item.

2. Singleton. The singleton set containing Socrates exists in virtue of Socrates existing, not the reverse.

3. Overdetermination. Take a true disjunction both of whose disjuncts hold. I want to say its truth is grounded in the first disjunct and separately grounded in the second: two complete grounds, either of which would have sufficed had the other failed. I do not want to say it is grounded in their conjunction. So I'm committed to there being no such thing as the full ground of a fact — only grounds, possibly several, each complete.

4. Negative existentials. The truth that there are no unicorns is not, I think, grounded in the vast totality of particular facts about what does exist, taken one by one. It is grounded in something wholesale: reality's containing nothing of that kind. But I also want to say that wholesale fact is itself derivative rather than fundamental, and I'm unsure what grounds it.

5. Loops are permissible. Let S be the fact that the singleton containing Socrates exists, and M the fact that Socrates is a member of that singleton. I'm drawn to saying that S is grounded in Socrates' existence together with M, while M is grounded in S. More generally, I'm attracted to the idea that a chain of grounding can close into a ring: every fact in the ring grounded, nothing in the ring fundamental, and nothing vicious about that.

6. Nothing grounds itself, not even partially.

7. Linkage facts lie outside the hierarchy. Consider the fact that A grounds B. I want to say this is neither fundamental nor derivative: it is not basic, but it also isn't the sort of item that stands in grounding relations at all. So on my view ungrounded does not entail fundamental — some ungrounded items fall outside the fundamental/derivative divide entirely.

8. Transitivity holds. If A grounds B and B grounds C, then A grounds C, including across arbitrarily long chains.

9. (Least confident.) I'd like to say that the statue is somehow more derivative than the fact that its matter is arranged thus — that derivativeness has depth — while also accepting rings, in which no fact seems to have a well-defined distance from a base.

One clash I can see: (5) and (8) together appear to force S to be partly grounded in itself, contradicting (6). There may be others I haven't spotted.

The principle I've been playing with is roughly this: the grounds of a fact are the items that would have to be specified strictly before it in an ideal, non-repeating specification of reality — one that never presupposes at any stage what it is supposed to specify later. Call this the specification-order principle.

What I'd like from you:

A small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough to be applied mechanically, with the case that motivates each clause. For each candidate, tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, which you would overturn, and what overturning it costs me — in particular whether I lose the ability to say anything substantive about whether reality has a fundamental level.

Then give me your account's verdicts on cases I haven't listed: (a) a ring with a tail, where A grounds B, B grounds C, and C grounds B, with A itself ungrounded — is A fundamental, and is B derivative, and does your answer change if C also grounds A, closing the ring? (b) contrastive cases: is grounding a two-place relation, or does the clay ground the statue's existing rather than its merely being arranged thus, and what does your principle imply about which? (c) necessary truths, and the possibility of a contingent item grounding a necessary one — does your principle require grounds to be modally stronger than what they ground, and if so what happens to the necessary truth that two and two are four? (d) a pruning test: if deleting X from reality would leave Y ungrounded, does that show X grounds Y, and should I want my principle to make grounding track that counterfactual? (e) whether derivativeness admits degrees in a world containing rings.

Finally: on the account you land on, what is the distinction between being ungrounded and being fundamental, and is there anything besides grounding linkages that falls into the gap between them?
