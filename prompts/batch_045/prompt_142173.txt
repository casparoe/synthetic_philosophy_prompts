I'm a retired marine biologist, got into philosophy of biology a couple of years ago, and I've been trying to work out what I actually think about biological function by going case by case with my gut before I read what anyone else says. Here's where I've landed, and I want to know whether there's a principle behind it.

A fish's heart: its function is to pump blood. Yes, obviously. But the thumping noise the heart makes — no function, just a side effect, even though it's useful to me when I'm dissecting and trying to locate the thing.

A nudibranch that steals stinging cells from the hydroids it eats and keeps them in its own skin for defence: I want to say those cells have a function in the slug, even though the slug didn't make them and their whole history is in a cnidarian.

A hermit crab sitting in a whelk shell: I want to say no, the shell has no function, it isn't one of the crab's parts. But I definately can't see what seperates this from the nudibranch case, and that bothers me.

An enzyme from the gut that, in some lineage, ends up being expressed in the eye lens where it just sits there being transparent: if selection has been tinkering with it in its new job for a while, I'm happy to say its function is now lens work, not digestion. If the recruitment happened recently in one population and hasn't been selected on at all, I want to say no — it's merely doing a job.

A whale's pelvic remnant, now anchoring a couple of muscles: not a function, a leftover doing a job. Which I realise is in tension with the lens case unless all the work is being done by "selection has since acted on the new role".

A mutation that was neutral, drifted up to high frequency, and then — because the bay warmed — turned out to keep the whole population alive: no function. It just happens to help. I feel strongly about this.

A lymphocyte receptor thrown together by random somatic shuffling that happens to bind a virus the animal has never encountered: its function is to bind things, maybe even that virus. Which looks exactly like the previous case, and yet I've got opposite verdicts.

Ordinary sperm in a snail: function is to fertilise an egg, even though essentially none of them ever do and each one's actual fate is to die. The weird little non-fertilising parasperm in the same ejaculate: I'm not sure. I lean towards their function being to help the fertile ones along, but I can't say why I'm less confident here than about the heart.

A transmissible facial tumour cell line in Tasmanian devils: the cell lineage has a selection history of its own for getting into new devils. I don't want to call transmission one of the devil's functions, but saying the cell lineage has the function of transmitting seems fine to me. So functions look like they're indexed to some system or level, not to an animal as such.

A dairy cow's udder: function is milk production, even though we did the selecting and it's arguably bad for the cow. A broiler chicken's collapsing legs: no function, that's a side effect of selecting for growth.

My own pacemaker: has a function, someone designed it. A beaver's dam: also has a function, and nobody designed it — so intentions can't be what's doing the work.

Here's the rough principle I've been toying with: a part or trait T of a system S has the function F if and only if (a) T is currently capable of doing F, and (b) T is present because, in the history of S or of the lineage S belongs to, things like T doing things like F is what kept T around — with the artefact cases handled by swapping in "because someone put it there to do F".
