I cover science and ideas for a newspaper and am planning a feature on what I have started calling commitment races: situations in which several parties are each better off being the first to irreversibly surrender an option — ratifying a binding convention, publishing model weights, locking in an exclusive multi-year supply deal — than being the party that has to respond, so that the prize goes less to the best-prepared actor than to the one that first destroys its own freedom to move.

My anchor case is a little unusual: a handful of governments appear to be racing to be the first to ratify a binding treaty on autonomous weapons, and several of my sources say the motive is not the treaty's content but the positional value of setting the default everyone else then has to route around. Two further wrinkles have been on my mind — the holdout premium, the value of being the one party left flexible after the others lock in, and positional decay, the idea that the value of being first collapses for everyone once a critical mass has committed.

What I need is an agenda, not a survey. Given roughly 2,500 words and three months of reporting, with sources who are officials and economists rather than modellers, which three or four open questions about commitment races are most worth building the piece around, and in what order? Defend the ordering: state your criteria — how much an answer would change what a reader should watch for, whether it is tractable with a journalist's tools at all, how neglected it is, whether it is upstream of the others — and how you are trading them off. Then say what observable developments over the next eighteen months would move items up or down, and which standard claims here are overrated or do not follow, especially whether these races are really just races to the bottom with extra steps and whether first-mover advantage is what drives them.
