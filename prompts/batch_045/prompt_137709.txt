I am giving a work-in-progress talk next month on the aggregation problem for Scanlon-style contractualism, and I want a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense of the view.

The thesis I am defending is a *restricted aggregation* view. Standard contractualism is said to be unable to aggregate: on the reasonable-rejectability test, each person's complaint is assessed individually, and no one individual's complaint is made stronger by the fact that many others have the same-sized complaint, so numbers seem to count for nothing. I claim that interpersonal aggregation is legitimate, but only under a condition I call the *shared-agency condition*: numbers may enter the balance among some set of people only when those people are co-authors of a shared activity — joint participants in an enterprise whose point each of them has, in part, made their own — so that a setback to any one of them is also a setback to something the others are, in their own terms, invested in. Where no such relation holds, the contractualist test stays strictly individual and numbers do not enter at all.

My argument is in three steps. First: the reason contractualism refuses to add up complaints across persons is not that small harms are insignificant, but that the standpoint from which complaints are assessed is the standpoint of each person's own reasons, and a stranger's identical loss gives me no additional reason. Second: when people are co-authors of a shared activity, that barrier is dissolved from the inside, because my reasons already include reasons to care about the fate of the thing we are doing together; the relevant standpoint for assessing my complaint is then partly the standpoint of the shared agency, and within that standpoint the losses of the participants can be weighed jointly without anyone being asked to take an alien loss as their own. Third: this yields the right result in the familiar transmitter-room-style case — a very large scattered audience's small losses of pleasure cannot be summed against the severe pain of the one person in the transmitter room, because an audience of strangers is not a shared agency — while still permitting aggregation in cases where it intuitively matters, such as burdens distributed across the members of a joint expedition, a lab, or a crew.

The audience is a departmental work-in-progress session, about twenty-five people, roughly as follows. A senior moral philosopher who is broadly sympathetic to contractualism and who has written on the separateness of persons; she will be friendly but will want to know whether I have quietly reintroduced a holistic entity as the bearer of complaints. Two consequentialists, one a prioritarian who thinks contractualism is a rounding error away from his own view and one a total utilitarian who is genuinely curious; they will press on whether shared-agency aggregation ever actually changes any verdict, or whether it is a bolt-on that saves the intuition in cases where I wanted the aggregative answer anyway. A decision theorist who works on ex ante versus ex post evaluation under risk; he will ask what my view says when the co-authorship relation itself is uncertain, or when people are unsure which of several groups they belong to. And a political philosopher who works on collective action and associative duties; she will ask why the boundaries of morally permitted aggregation should track a contingent social fact about who happens to have signed on to what.

What I want from you is the prep sheet: roughly eight objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how much damage they would do to the view rather than by how likely they are to be asked, each phrased the way that person would phrase it and in their vocabulary, not in mine. For each objection give me a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, and then a blunt note on where that reply is soft, where it is a genuine concession dressed as a reply, and where I am bluffing. I also want to know what I should concede in the first two minutes of the session, before anyone asks anything, so that the sympathetic contractualist in the room does not spend the hour waiting for me to admit it. And I want the one question I am hoping nobody asks: my own candidate is some version of the worry that co-authorship is too easy to manufacture, so that either any crowd can become a shared agency by coordinating and my view collapses back into unrestricted aggregation wherever coordination is possible, or the permissibility of counting numbers turns on a sociological accident. If you think there is a worse question than that one, tell me what it is and why it is worse.

Please do not give me a reading list or citations; I have the literature. Give me the questions and what to say back.
