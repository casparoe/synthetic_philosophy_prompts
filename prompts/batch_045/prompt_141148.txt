I'm a journalist working on a long feature about what can and cannot be recovered about a person's wants by watching what they do — the question that comes up when a hospital tries to learn a senior nurse's real priorities from months of scheduling and charting records, or when a machine learning system tries to infer an operator's goals from the corrections she makes. A researcher I interviewed brushed off my worry that such inferences are underdetermined: underdetermination only bites, she said, when you have almost no data; give the learner enough observed behavior and the space of compatible reward functions collapses to something you can act on. I want to test that claim rather than repeat it, so I'd like you to build me a case that breaks it in a precise direction.

Here is the case I want. Two learners observe *exactly* the same thing — the same trajectories, the same state space and dynamics, the same hand-specified features, the same assumed level of demonstrator imperfection. Learner A is maximum-entropy IRL: a Boltzmann-rational demonstrator, reward linear in the features, fitted so that expected feature counts match the observed ones. Learner B is maximum-margin IRL with an L1 sparsity penalty on the reward weights and a hinge (hence capped) loss on deviations. The case must be built so that A infers a substantial positive weight on some feature while B infers that this feature's weight is essentially zero, and so that the two inferences diverge in a way that matters: a system trained on A's answer would restructure the environment to protect whatever that feature tracks, while a system trained on B's answer would treat those episodes as noise and let them go. That is, the disagreement must be about whether a term belongs in the reward at all, not merely about how confident to be. I've started calling the underlying difficulty the *expensive-seldom problem*: behavior that is costly and infrequent can be read either as a large standing concern that only rarely binds or as an ordinary lapse that occasionally happens, and nothing in the behavior alone seems to settle which. If you think the case I'm asking for is really an instance of that, say so; if you think it is something else, name the real phenomenon.

A few constraints, because I know how these constructions usually cheat. The setting should be ordinary and non-science-fictional — one human demonstrator observed over weeks or months in an institution, with features an observer could actually read off an administrative record, no privileged access to mental states — and the scenario itself should fit in a paragraph. The verdicts must be generated by the frameworks, not stipulated: show enough of the formal structure in each case (the constraint or objective being satisfied, and roughly how the numbers come out) that a reader with quantitative background but no machine learning training could check the reasoning. The disagreement must not be the familiar artifact whereby reward scale and the rationality or temperature parameter are confounded — pin the units by normalizing the weight on the primary feature to one and holding the rationality parameter fixed across both learners — and it must not rest on a degenerate reward (all zeros, constant, or a function of states the demonstrator passes through anyway). The disputed feature should bind at maybe five to twenty percent of decision points but be genuinely costly when it does. Nor may the disagreement be settled by asking the demonstrator: build the case so that self-report is either unavailable or untrustworthy, and say explicitly which route you took and why it isn't a dodge.

After the scenario and the two derivations, I need four things. First, identify the single structural feature of the pair of frameworks that is doing the work; my own hypothesis is that it is the difference between a capped per-deviation loss and a requirement to match observed frequencies, but correct me if I've got it wrong. Second, ablate that feature — give the margin learner a frequency-matching constraint, or cap the maximum-entropy learner's loss — and tell me whether the disagreement collapses or merely shrinks. Third, tell me how a defender of whichever framework comes out looking worse would attack the case, and whether, in your judgment, the attack succeeds. And fourth: consider my interviewee's claim directly. If you multiplied the observed behavior tenfold, would the two answers converge, stay put, or pull further apart? I'd rather publish an honest note about where the inference is genuinely indeterminate than a tidy story that collapses under one question from a skeptical reader.
