I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days, which is where I picked up both the habit of treating expected-utility maximization as the normative gold standard and the habit of distrusting self-report. I now sit on the allocation committee of a small health-and-development fund (roughly $400k a year), and for two years we have ranked grants using subjective-wellbeing accounting: self-reported life-satisfaction points multiplied by the number of people affected, of the kind some happiness-focused funders have been pushing. I've never been comfortable with it. The scale has no fixed unit, and comparing one villager's reported satisfaction with another's looks like exactly the interpersonal comparison of utility that the ordinalist tradition warned us is unscientific.

It occurred to me recently that we might not need introspection at all. If the point of revealed-preference theory is that utility can be reconstructed from choice, and if the von Neumann–Morgenstern theorem gives a cardinal utility function from choices over lotteries — unique up to positive affine transformation — then I can elicit a beneficiary's utility function behaviorally (hypothetical lotteries, certainty equivalents, fit the curve) and then remove the residual freedom by rescaling each person's function to the unit interval, anchoring zero at the worst outcome in that person's relevant menu and one at the best. Call the result a behavioral well-being unit. The output is cardinal, person-specific, behaviorally grounded, and expressed in a common unit, so I can sum across people and compare programs; and nothing in the procedure depends on anyone's introspection or on my guessing what goes on inside anyone's head. I've been using small-stakes lotteries so that the curvature I measure is local, on the theory that this keeps me from merely recording risk aversion.

Two observations make me doubt the scheme. First, the numbers come out strange: under this normalization, someone whose range runs from "nothing changes" to "receives a bed net" has exactly the same zero-to-one span as someone whose range runs from "child survives" to "child dies," so the two can contribute identically to the total. Second, when I ran the procedure on myself over small monetary gambles, the fitted curve came out nearly linear, which would imply I should be close to indifferent between a sure $50,000 raise and a coin flip between nothing and $100,000 — and I am emphatically not.

So where is the mistake, and is it repairable? Is the problem the normalization (and if so, what is the right one?), or is the elicitation itself measuring something other than welfare? I keep being told that Harsanyi already solved the interpersonal-comparison problem, but his aggregation theorem looks to me like it assumes the very comparability I'm trying to measure. Is there a principled version of what I'm attempting, or does the underdetermination go all the way down?
