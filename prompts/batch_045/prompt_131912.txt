I'm a political theory PhD student who has drifted into an interdisciplinary alignment reading group, and I keep watching the same move get made: someone says an aligned system should "serve humanity's values," or "reflect the values of its users," or "aggregate what people actually want," and then the conversation slides straight into defending one aggregation rule or another without anyone first saying what such an account is *for* or what counts as getting it right. I want the specification before the proposals. So: write me the desiderata for a theory of whose values an aligned AI system should serve — what any adequate account must deliver, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, and a verdict on which requirements are non-negotiable and which are really just the preferences of one camp dressed up as constraints on everyone.

Let me fix the purpose, since I take adequacy to be relative to it. I want a theory good enough to be written into the operating spec of a general-purpose assistant deployed by a private firm to a user base spread across many jurisdictions and moral traditions: it has to say whose values go in, by what procedure they are identified, elicited, aggregated, and enforced, and at which point in that pipeline each question is settled. I don't want ideal theory for a single bounded political community, and I don't want anything that assumes we can already read a coherent value system off the model or off the population. My provisional list, which I'd like you to repair, reorganize, or reject items from: (i) the constituency question must be answered separately for the training signal, for deployment-time refusals, and for the system's own statements about what should be done, and a theory that answers it once for all three is hiding something; (ii) it must not collapse serving a person's values, serving their interests, and obeying their instructions — the case I keep using is a user asking for help drafting a phishing email, where the user's values and the target's diverge and "do what the user wants" and "do what serves the user" come apart; (iii) affected third parties have neither exit nor voice while users have exit, and I suspect an adequate theory owes them some procedural substitute, though I can't tell whether that is a genuine desideratum or a substantive morality smuggled in as procedure; (iv) it must be robust to the fact that the constituency is endogenous — who shows up in the user base depends on what the system refuses, so the population being aggregated is partly a fixed point of the policy being chosen, and I want to know whether we should demand existence and uniqueness of that fixed point or robustness across the whole reachable set instead; (v) it must handle double aggregation, over persons and over the system's uncertainty about which moral view is correct, and say whether the order of the two aggregations matters and whether order-invariance should be required; (vi) it must be invariant to redescription of the option space even though the representable option space is itself an artifact of the base model's capabilities, which I take to be a sharper version of the classic menu-sensitivity worries from social choice; (vii) it must be revisable over time without being captured by whoever happened to adopt it first; (viii) it must say something about strategic misrepresentation in elicitation, given that elicitation is gameable and that cleaning up reported values trades against fidelity to them.

Two more things, and they are the parts I care most about. First, flag any pair of desiderata you think cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories — I'd guess the pair involving neutrality among conceptions of the good and any substantive constraint on which value contents may be served, but also the pair involving fidelity to actual values and robustness to gaming, and I'd like your view on whether the list as a whole is satisfiable at all, since if it isn't, that negative result is more useful to me than any proposal. Second, note along the way how much of the neutrality demand is really being carried by an analogy between a deployed model and a coercive state, since a private speaker or association is normally allowed to have a viewpoint, and I want to see what work that analogy is doing before I let it generate requirements. If you have room at the end, a brief scorecard of four families — expected-utility-style aggregation with interpersonal comparisons, a contractualist or public-reason-style constraint, a democratic or participatory account, and a revealed-preference or market-style one — against the list would be helpful, but keep the weight on the criteria rather than on the scoring.
