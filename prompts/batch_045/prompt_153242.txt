I am defending a proposal next week to the technical advisory panel of a multilateral development bank's governance practice, with several of the bank's regional country-office directors also in the room. My thesis: corruption is not a family of act-types — bribes, gifts, nepotism, vote-buying — but a single failure, the substitution of a personal relation for a public reason in the allocation of a state good. I cash this out with the beneficiary-substitution test: an exercise of public power is corrupt just in case the agent's operative reason for the allocation would not survive substituting, for the actual beneficiary, some other member of the relevant public who stands in no such relation to the agent. Which relations count as legitimately reason-giving varies across societies; the structure of the failure does not. I claim three payoffs: clientelism is corruption even where no money changes hands; much routine gift-giving to officials is not corruption, because it asserts a claim to recognition rather than a claim on a particular allocation; and the lawful influence-trading of wealthy democracies — campaign finance, revolving-door appointments, regulatory capture — is corruption in the strict sense and usually the more damaging kind, something the field's standard definition, built around abuse of entrusted power for private gain, cannot say.

The audience: two governance economists who work in principal–agent terms and need cross-country comparable indicators; two criminal-law drafters who need ex ante notice, mens rea, and elements a jury can actually find; a political scientist and an anthropologist of patronage who treat good-governance metrics as a neo-colonial export; and a regional director from an office where vote-brokerage and gift-giving are pervasive, who will want to know what she is meant to tell her staff on Monday.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage each would do, each attributed to the person most likely to raise it and phrased in that person's idiom rather than mine, with a ninety-second reply and a note on where the reply is softest. Tell me what I should concede in the first two minutes, which single reply has to land if the proposal is to survive, and give me the question I am hoping nobody asks.
