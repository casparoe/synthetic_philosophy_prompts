Theres this popular econ-history paperback (lots of exclamation marks, confident tone) with a chapter on the socialist calculation debate, and I cant tell where its being fair and where its quietly changing the subject. Heres roughly what it says, condensed:

"Mises argued in the early 1920s that a socialist economy couldnt do economics. If the state owns the factories and the land, nobody buys or sells them, so thered be no market in them, so thered be no prices for them, and without prices theres no way to weigh one input against another — steel against labour against machine time. His point, though, was really about raw calculating power: working out the cheapest way to produce everything in a whole country means solving millions of simultaneous equations, which no planning board could ever do, even with the best intentions. So central planning is mathematically impossible, not merely difficult. Hayek later made basically the same point with better PR: the relevant facts are scattered across millions of individual heads, so no central board could ever gather them together and add them up. Lange then showed this was all beside the point. Planners dont need to gather anything: they announce prices, tell managers to minimise costs and produce up to the point where price equals marginal cost, then raise prices on goods that sell out and cut them on goods that pile up in warehouses, repeating until nothing is left over. The impossible calculation dissolves into trial and error, and Hayek never actually answered that — by the mid-40s hed more or less quit technical economics for political polemic about serfdom. Which settles it: with modern data and computing power the arithmetic objection is dead, and the surviving case against planning is just a political one about freedom. And that case is awkward anyway, because a giant firm already runs its whole internal supply chain on internally computed shadow prices rather than real markets — which is, in miniature, a planned economy that plainly works."
