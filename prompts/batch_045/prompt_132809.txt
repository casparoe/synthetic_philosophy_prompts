I host a podcast on philosophy of action, and next week's episode is a long conversation with a neuroscientist-philosopher I'll call Dr. Renner. Her view: the Libet-style findings generalize — the slow negative premovement potential recorded over medial frontal cortex in spontaneous movement, together with the fact that a classifier trained on that cortex can predict a binary choice before the subject reports having decided, show that conscious intention never initiates action. Unlike Libet, she also holds that the famous "conscious veto" is no exception: she thinks the veto is itself the outcome of antecedent neural processes, and that in any case it operates only within a narrow window just before the movement, after which cancellation fails.

Draft eight interview questions, each with a one- or two-line follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to make. Press only on the following, in roughly this order:

1. The reinterpretation she must defeat: that the premovement potential is not the footprint of an unconscious decision but the average of stochastic neural activity drifting toward a threshold, so that its apparent onset is an artifact of averaging over trials whose true decision times vary. Assume she answers that the drift rate itself had to be set by something.
2. Whether deciding which of two buttons to press, every few seconds, with nothing at stake, is the same kind of thing as the deliberative, linguistically articulated, consequential intention that attributions of moral responsibility actually track.
3. The veto: whether a last-moment point beyond which cancellation fails shows that conscious will is a causal gate, or only that the motor system has already committed and the subject is reading out a commitment she can no longer revoke.
4. A variant I want to put to her directly: a stop-signal version of the task in which a tone at a randomly chosen delay instructs cancellation. Suppose cancellation succeeds reliably at long delays and fails abruptly below a threshold of roughly two hundred milliseconds, and that on successfully cancelled trials the premovement signal is indistinguishable from that on executed trials until shortly before that threshold. Ask what this pattern would show, and then what pattern would have shown the opposite.
5. Her likely fallback — that the evidence underdetermines the choice between "conscious intention is causally inert" and "conscious intention is a late modulator of ongoing preparation." What observation would break the tie, and is there any such observation she would accept as decisive rather than explaining away?
6. The asymmetry with Libet himself, who argued that conscious will acts through a unified conscious mental field capable of biasing neural outcomes without adding energy. If that escape route is open for the veto, why not for initiation? What makes her epiphenomenalism more parsimonious than his interactionism?
