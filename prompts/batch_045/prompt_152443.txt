I am writing a paper on the boundary conditions of the extended mind thesis, and specifically on a question I cannot get clear about: do the criteria Clark and Chalmers propose locate the boundary of the mind by spatial position, by the reliability of the resource, or by the subject's relation to it? I want a single case in which those three variables can be separated. My purpose fixes what would count as cheating: I want the verdicts to fall out of the theories' own criteria applied to an ordinary-feeling scenario, not to be written into the scenario in theoretical vocabulary, and if the case turns out to show the opposite of what I expect, I want to be told so rather than given a case that flatters my hunch.

The scenario itself: one paragraph, at most about 180 words, realistic, restricted to neurotechnology that exists today or is a straightforward near-term extension of it — no uploads, no telepathy, nothing science-fictional. It must contain two subjects using the same implanted device, interfacing with their own memory systems, for the same task, over the same stretch of time, where the device is in fact equally reliable in both cases and roughly as reliable as unaided biological memory. Everything is held fixed except one variable. That variable is the subject's stance toward the device: one subject trusts it and acts directly on its deliverances without checking, reporting them in the first person as recollections; the other was burned by an early malfunction, now corrected, and treats the deliverances as an informant to be cross-checked against independent sources before acting, so that the caution is baseless and the second subject performs just as well, only more slowly.

The demonstration I want:

State, for each of three views, whether the content carried by the device counts as a belief or mental state of the subject in the trusting case and in the cautious case. Derive each verdict from the view's criterion in two or three sentences; do not assert it. The views are the parity principle of Clark and Chalmers together with the tightening Clark later gave it (reliable availability and typical invocation, easy accessibility, automatic endorsement rather than scrutiny, trust established over time — correct me if I have a condition wrong, and say what you are using instead); the skin-and-skull view, on which the boundary of the mind is the boundary of the organism and cognition is what the brain does; and Adams and Aizawa's objection from the mark of the cognitive, on which a state is cognitive only if its content is non-derived, and artefacts carry only derived content. The design target is a three-way split, with the parity view counting the content as mental in the trusting case but not the cautious one, the skin-and-skull view counting it in both, and the derived-content view in neither — reached by generating those verdicts from the criteria, not by stipulating them.

Then identify which single feature of the case each view is actually responsive to, and ablate: what happens to each of the three verdicts if the device is moved outside the skull and becomes a wearable of otherwise identical function; what happens if the two subjects' attitudes are equalized and nothing else changes; what happens if the device is made subtly unreliable while the trusting subject goes on trusting it, which tells me whether the parity verdict was tracking reliability or endorsement.

Then an honest audit: say which verdict the case is really engineered to produce and why it is not merely stipulated; say which variable, if any, the case fails to isolate; and say whether any of the three views could reasonably decline to return a verdict because the scenario is underdescribed in some respect you can name.

Finally, the two counterattacks I expect, answered properly. First, the extended mind theorist will say that endorsement is not an extra condition bolted onto a functional role but part of what makes a state a belief rather than a consulted source, so the asymmetry is a result rather than a defect, and will add that a subject who has come to distrust her own biological memory after a run of confabulations gets the same verdict by parity — tell me whether that is a reductio of the criteria or a consequence to embrace, and what turns on the answer. Second, the skin-and-skull theorist will retreat to the claim that silicon is not neural tissue, so the implant was never inside the mind in the first place: say whether that retreat is ad hoc, and whether accepting it collapses the second verdict into the third and destroys the three-way split.

If there is room, close with one short paragraph on how a practice-based or enculturation variant of the extended mind, of the sort on which what matters is the learned practices of manipulating a resource rather than the resource itself, would rule on the two subjects, and on whether that version escapes the worry about attitude-dependence that the case is designed to raise.
