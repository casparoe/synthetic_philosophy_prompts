My aunt died last week at eighty-three. She was a lifelong Objectivist, not a dabbler, and she held a refinement of Rand's position that I was never able to argue her out of: on her reading, sacrifice is surrendering a greater value for a lesser one or for a non-value, so an action can be costly, painful, and done entirely for another person's sake and still not be a sacrifice at all — and, further, praising such an action as a sacrifice is what corrupts it, because it asks the agent to treat her own values as the morally suspect part of the transaction. She lived this out: eleven years caring for her brother after his stroke, which she described to his face as something she did because she valued him, not something she gave up for him.

I am giving the eulogy on Saturday. The room will be split between her Objectivist friends, who will hear any conventional praise for self-sacrifice as a slur on her, and her large Catholic family — including that brother, who is devout and who hears "I did it because I valued you" as a denial that she gave anything up at all. A eulogy normally runs on the vocabulary of sacrifice: she gave of herself, she always put others first, she would have given you the shirt off her back. I think I have to give that vocabulary up entirely, and I do not know what to put in its place.

Write me a four-minute eulogy for a woman who was generous, demanding, warm, and entirely unwilling to be praised for self-sacrifice — a eulogy that uses no language of sacrifice or duty, that does not deny how much those eleven years cost her, and that lands as love rather than as a lecture for both halves of the room. Then, in a short paragraph, tell me which one sentence you expect the two halves of the room to hear differently, and why you left it in.
