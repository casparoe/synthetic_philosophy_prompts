I work as a site reliability engineer at a large cloud provider, and I run our internal incident-review course, so the vocabulary I am genuinely fluent in is this one: SLOs and error budgets; capacity versus admission control; quotas, priority classes and load shedding; buffers, caches and hoarding behaviour under contention; correlated failure and single points of failure; failover and alternate supply paths; backpressure and retries; telemetry, alerting, and the difference between a signal existing and somebody being on call for it; control plane versus data plane; rollouts and change management; root cause versus contributing factors; the rule that human error is a starting point for an investigation rather than a conclusion; and the practice of blameless postmortems. I also spend a lot of my time on gray failures, the kind where nothing is down and every dashboard is green but the service has quietly degraded for a subset of users.

My knowledge of the famine literature is roughly podcast-level. I know that Sen argued for an entitlement approach against a food-availability-decline view, that he treats a famine as a breakdown in what people can command rather than in how much food exists, and that he makes a strong claim about famines not occurring in countries with competitive elections and a relatively free press. I know the three standard cases only in outline: Bengal in 1943, Ireland in the late 1840s, and the Great Leap Forward.

What I want from you is a translation, and a residue report.

First: restate the debate about whether famines are natural disasters or political crimes in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Translate the two rival explanations into failure modes I would recognise, and then do the same for each of the three cases, one at a time. What kind of incident was each, in my terms, and which layer failed?

Second: here is my own tentative translation, which I would like tested rather than flattered. I have been describing a famine as a silent brownout with no pager and no owner: aggregate capacity stays close to normal; the failure is in the allocation and admission-control layer; the affected population is a priority class that got demoted, or whose credentials stopped working; the dashboards stayed green because the metric under watch was aggregate supply rather than who could actually acquire how much; and the deaths are the long tail of a degradation rather than the moment of an outage. Bengal looks to me like price-based admission control plus a deliberately disabled failover path plus load shedding that protected one priority class at the expense of another. Ireland looks like a correlated dependency failure on a single input, combined with an access-control policy that kept feeding external consumers while local ones starved. The Great Leap Forward looks like a control plane acting on corrupted telemetry from local nodes, draining local buffers against targets computed from the false numbers, and then disabling the buffers themselves. So: where is this mapping honest, and where does it smuggle in my framework's assumptions? Which case does it handle worst, and is that because the case is different in kind or because my vocabulary bottoms out?

Third: the residue. I suspect three things in the original debate have no counterpart in what I can say, and I want your verdict on each. Culpability: my framework is deliberately blameless, and human error is a starting point rather than a conclusion, whereas political crime is a verdict I have no operator for. Intent: my framework distinguishes planned degradation from incidents, but has no way to mark the difference between shipping a change whose lethal consequences were foreseeable and letting people die, and no notion of a right that an operator is violating. Legitimacy: in my framework the owner of a service legitimately sets its quotas, and the question of who ought to own it is one I cannot ask. For each, tell me whether the gap is a defect in my vocabulary, something reliability engineering genuinely lacks, or a sign that the original claim was less clear than it sounds. I particularly want your view on culpability: in a distributed system with many hands, blame tends to smear across layers, and I cannot tell whether that is a real feature of how famine responsibility works or an artefact of thinking about it as an incident.

Fourth, two loose ends. One: Sen's claim about elections and a free press strikes me as an alerting requirement plus an on-call rotation with something to lose, but an alert that fires and a politician who fears being voted out look like different mechanisms, and I want to know whether translating it as alerting loses the entire point. Two: I have been toying with proposing, for my course, that the relevant service level indicator is the share of the population whose entitlement to food has fallen below the subsistence threshold, with an error budget of exactly zero. The interesting failure, on that framing, is not that nobody measured it but that nobody was on call for it, and in reliability engineering somebody always is. Does the framing survive contact with the three cases, or does it convert a political relationship into an operational one?

Please keep the translation honest: do not quietly replace what the economists and historians are claiming with claims my vocabulary finds easy to make, and flag explicitly wherever you had to fudge something to make it fit.
