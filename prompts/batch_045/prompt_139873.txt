The way it was explained to me: if two people are both honest, both genuinely trying to get at the truth, have no incentive to lie, and have shared all their information, then their credences for the same event have to come out identical; and if they don't, somebody in the room is being irrational. That sounded like exactly the tool for us, so last week we ran an experiment. I put together one briefing packet — same polls, same everything — everybody read it, then we all wrote our numbers on a whiteboard, looked at each other's numbers, wrote new numbers, and repeated that four times. Everyone swore they were being fully honest and weren't holding anything back. The numbers basically didn't move: I went 0.72 to 0.70, Dana went 0.31 to 0.33, and then we kind of trailed off.

So by the theorem, somebody there has to be the irrational one, right? I want to know how to work out who, and how to demonstrate it to them. First, she says the theorem is totally symmetric — she can run the exact same argument and conclude that *I'm* the irrational one — so it can't actually tell either of us anything useful in practice. Second, she says reading the same briefing packet doesn't give us the same prior: the prior is about all the background baggage each of us brings, like how much she trusts local polling versus how much I do, and if those differ then the theorem just has nothing to say about us. I think she's definately wriggling out of it. Who's actually right here, where exactly is my reasoning going wrong (if it is), and what should I do with this — keep announcing numbers, keep betting, or drop the whole theorem as a tool for settling real arguments like ours?
