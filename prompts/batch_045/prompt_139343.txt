I am a high school senior writing an independent research paper on decision theory applied to catastrophic risk, and I would like help with one section of it: a case analysis of the Manhattan Project and the Trinity test of 16 July 1945, read through the lens of the unilateralist's curse. I already know the standard history of the project, so please do not spend time recounting it; what I need is the mapping, done carefully and then honestly assessed.

To keep the analysis from turning on anything contested, please take the following as given. Some physicists involved took seriously, if briefly, the possibility that a fission explosion might set off a self-sustaining reaction in the atmosphere or the oceans; the question was studied in a Los Alamos report by Konopinski, Marvin and Teller, which concluded the risk was negligible, and it was not treated as exactly zero by everyone on hand at the test. The German nuclear effort was in fact far smaller and less advanced than the Allied one and never approached a deliverable weapon, but Allied leaders did not know this at the time and had good reason to fear the opposite. Within the American project there was genuine internal dissent about whether and how the weapons should be used — petitions and reports from a minority of scientists urging demonstration or restraint — and that minority did not prevail.

What I would like, in roughly 1,000 to 1,300 words:

First, the disciplined mapping. Who exactly are the players for the purposes of the curse, and at what level of aggregation — states, scientific communities, or individual laboratories? What is *the action*, and at which point in the sequence does it sit: building the weapon, testing it, or using it? How should the payoff structure be written down, given that the actors were enemies in a total war and that some of the people bearing the risk had no representative at the table? Be explicit about what counts as evidence in the analysis and what the actors could actually have known.

Second, I suspect the essay needs a distinction the readings I have found do not draw cleanly. The canonical curse is a *too many of us do it* failure: the action is worth doing if few take it and ruinous if many do. But the atmospheric-ignition worry looks like a different structure — a single actor takes the action, and everyone on Earth, including that actor's own side, bears the tail risk, so the problem is not that too many act but that the actor set and the risk-bearing set come apart. Call the second pattern the *externalizing curse* if you like. Is it genuinely a different argument requiring different premises, or is it the same argument with the number of actors fixed at one? Which of the two, if either, is Trinity a case of?

Third, the phantom-race problem. The curse is usually explained as an epistemic failure where each agent overweights its own judgment. Here the driving error seems to have been about a *rival's* options and progress: the Allies raced against a programme that was barely there. Does the framework have anything to say when the failure is a false belief about another player rather than an inflated view of one's own? And does it recommend anything about improving one's information about others, as opposed to restraining oneself?

Fourth, the remedy. The principle of conformity proposed alongside the curse tells agents to defer when their peers disagree. Inside the project, peers did disagree, and the restraining minority lost — so does the framework judge that outcome, or does it predict it? Between adversaries who cannot communicate, no conformity mechanism was available at all. What does the theory say actors should do then, and if it says nothing, say so plainly.

Finally, the verdict in both directions: which features of the episode the curse illuminates, which it misdescribes or simply cannot see, and whether Trinity is best read as a confirmation of the theory, an anomaly for it, or a case it never anticipated. Where the theory is silent, I would rather be told that than be given an answer stretched to fit.
