You are a political philosopher specialising in public ethics and comparative political theory, who also advises development agencies on anti-corruption programme design. Please think carefully before writing: spend some time brainstorming how each of the commitments below constrains the others, and how much work each one is quietly being asked to do, before you commit to an answer. Be blunt where bluntness is warranted.

I work on corruption in development contexts and I have finally written my position down so my colleagues can attack it. Seven theses:

1. Corruption is not just bad government; it has an essential structure. Someone occupying a role that carries entrusted purposes subordinates those purposes to private advantage. Where there is no such role, talk of corruption is loose talk.

2. That core is, I hold, universal and not a Western export. But the apparatus that Western law and compliance culture treats as its neutral application — conflicts-of-interest registers, disclosure duties, competitive tendering, arm's-length contracting, caps on gifts and hospitality — is one historically local way of institutionalising impartiality, suited to large impersonal bureaucracies. Where office is embedded in kinship and patronage, the same goods (predictability, restraint of arbitrary power, some downward accountability) are often secured by other mechanisms, and standard international indices systematically misread those mechanisms as their absence.

3. So I am not a cultural relativist about corruption: that a practice is locally accepted, or locally expected, never settles that it is not corrupt. I want to condemn without hedging the minister who steers a road contract to his brother's firm at an inflated price with a kickback built in — even where everyone does it, and even where some of the money does reach the village.

4. But I am not a consequentialist about it either. I do not want to say clientelism is acceptable when it happens to deliver growth, or that a facilitation payment is fine when it unblocks an official who would otherwise stall. The wrong lies in the subordination of the office, not in the outcome.

5. I draw the gift/bribe line by asking whether the transfer sits inside an ongoing relationship of reciprocal obligation or is a payment that purchases a particular exercise of the office. Tributary gifts to a patron — the sort that sustain a relationship and carry no specific request — I treat as a different moral object from a bribe, and I resist criminalising them.

6. Where I am most severe is clientelism: patronage hiring, targeted goods in exchange for votes, the local boss who directs a borehole to his own kin's wards rather than to the district on need. I hold this to be a distinct and deeper corruption than one-off grand theft, because it converts a relation of political equality into a relation of personal indebtedness.

7. And I think corruption can be systemic: a polity can be thoroughly corrupt even when no individual official has departed from anything locally recognised as a rule.

My question: does the set hang together? My own guess is that the trouble is (3) against (2) — that I have smuggled a Western conception of impartial office back in through the definition while claiming the core is thin. A colleague insists the real fracture is (4) against (6), and that the reason I give in (6) is exactly the sort of outcome-based reason (4) forbids me. I cannot tell who is right, and I do not want to give up any of the seven if I can help it.

Please audit the package rather than lecture me about corruption in general. Identify where the commitments genuinely pull against each other, separate those from pairs that only look tense, rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each state precisely what would have to be surrendered and what that surrender would cost me elsewhere in the set. If a single revision would repair more than one of them, say so — and say plainly if one of the seven simply has to go.
