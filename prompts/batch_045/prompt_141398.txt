Last night I had a long argument with a friend who holds that the analytic–synthetic distinction is straightforwardly dead, finished off by Quine's Two Dogmas of Empiricism, and that anyone still helping themselves to it is doing metaphysics with a borrowed vocabulary. I am not convinced. The argument kept circling one kind of case that neither of us could settle, and I want to see it argued out properly.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher working in philosophy of language and metaphilosophy. Before you write, think the dialectic through carefully: decide where each position can legitimately concede and where it must dig in, and only then draft. Assume a reader who already knows the standard moves — holism about confirmation, the collapse of the verification theory of meaning, conventions and implicit definitions in the Carnapian vein, the circularity worries about grounding analyticity in necessity or cognitive synonymy — and who will be bored by a recapitulation of them.

The case is this. A long-running collaborative survey discipline, call it the Baseline Survey, maintains a large and tightly integrated body of claims about a domain. At its founding the community introduced a small class of sentences, charter sentences, by explicit stipulation, and it has a standing practice: when a new measurement, derivation or inference conflicts with a charter sentence, the charter sentence is not given up. Instead the conflicting result is treated as defective — the instrument is recalibrated, the derivation rechecked, the observation declared non-veridical. So when surveyors encounter a large body of water that meets every other criterion the Survey uses for its target kind except that its salinity falls below the open-ocean mean, the official response is to reclassify the water rather than to amend the charter sentence that fixes that threshold. My friend says this is the distinction doing real work: some sentences are held true because of what the words mean, and there is a genuine asymmetry between changing a rule for the use of a term and changing a claim about the world. I think a holist can reproduce the entire practice with no semantic property at all — extreme entrenchment plus institutional convenience.

Write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers, one defending each reading, neither a strawman, who answer each other's actual points rather than restating their positions. Give the holist the strong version of the response — that conventions cannot confer truth, that protected status is a matter of downstream disruption costs, that the idea of changing the subject is not something the holist needs — and let the defender concede something real rather than winning for free: say, that the boundary of the charter class is fuzzy, that stipulation cannot create truth for terms already in meaningful use, or that the distinction must surrender its old epistemological role to keep a modest semantic one. In the last third have them reach a case we could not handle: two charter sentences, introduced in the same way and equally entrenched, turn out to entail contradictory predictions in a newly opened region of the domain, so one of them must be broken. Is breaking it a discovery, a decision, or something the distinction cannot classify?

Keep the dialogue itself under 800 words. Then add a closing note of at most 150 words: where each speaker's strongest point lies, and which contested reading of Quine's target the dialogue depends on — the semantic notion itself, or only the explanatory work the notion was recruited to do. One more thing, and it matters to me: if the case as I have set it up is confused, or if an honest dialogue here cannot avoid flattening one side, say so plainly instead of producing a polite version. I would rather be told the setup misfires than be handed something that only looks balanced.
