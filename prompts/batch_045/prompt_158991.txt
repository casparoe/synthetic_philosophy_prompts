I'm on a tiny committee at my hospital that's supposed to update how we handle psychiatric advance directives — the "if I go manic and start refusing meds, admit me and treat me anyway" kind of document. And the more we talk the more I think the existing practice is quietly committed to something philosophically loaded that nobody on the committee can actually state.

Here's the shape of it as I understand it (in some places these things are legally binding, in others they're just advisory — I don't think that matters for my question, but tell me if it does). A patient with bipolar disorder signs a directive while she's well saying that if she becomes manic and refuses treatment, she wants to be hospitalized and medicated anyway. Months later she's manic, she's refusing, and the team overrides her current refusal in the name of the earlier document. So the earlier self beats the present self. But then take a different case: someone with dementia who years ago signed a refusal of aggressive life-prolonging treatment, and who now, in a fairly lucid moment, says she'd like to keep going and seems to be genuinely enjoying her lunch. A lot of people say the present self wins there, or at least that it's genuinely up for grabs. And in general, a competent patient's refusal right now trumps anything anyone said earlier. So: earlier self wins here, later self wins there, and I can't tell whether there's a principled line (something about who has capacity at the time) or just two habits that grew up separately in different corners of medicine. Also, as far as I can tell, half the time these documents just get ignored by whoever is on call, which makes me wonder whether there's any theory here at all.

What I want first is the reconstruction: what view of a person's will, or of preference, or of what it even means to respect someone, does this pattern presuppose? Is there a substantive theory in it — something like "the will that counts is the one formed under good conditions: informed, not in crisis, not distorted" — or is the practice compatible with a much thinner procedural story where capacity is just a gate and nobody is making any claim about what the person really wants? I'd like the strict presuppositions separated from the stuff the practice merely fits well with, and I'd like to know where it's underdetermined, i.e. where three different theories would all license exactly the same behavior.

Then the part I'm actually stuck on. The idea I keep circling is the alignment-flavored notion of an idealized or extrapolated will: roughly, what you'd want if you knew more, thought faster, and were more the person you wish you were — what people call coherent extrapolated volition. If a hospital took that seriously, what would it do with these two cases? Specifically:

- Which self is the base point for the extrapolation? The woman at 30 signing the document, or the woman at 35 who is currently manic and refusing? If the answer is "it depends on the case," that seems to make the notion useless at exactly the moment we need it.
- Does the manic episode itself count as input to the extrapolation? She has now had experiences, formed projects, changed her view of her own life. Does the extrapolated will get updated with all that, or do we treat the episode as noise to be filtered out before we start?
- Is there even a fact of the matter about where the extrapolation lands, or does it depend on the order in which you imagine her learning things and revising herself? I have a suspicion there isn't, and that the directive is doing more work than any theory of idealized preference can actually justify. If that suspicion is right, say so plainly.
- And concretely: what would a policy written by someone who believed in extrapolated volition look like? Binding instructions or rebuttable presumptions? A "values history" the team actually reads instead of a checkbox form? A duty to try to talk the manic patient round before overriding her? What would have to change in practice, and what would get worse?

I definately don't need citations or a reading list — I can't look anything up from the committee room and I don't want to be sent off to read somebody. Just argue it out. If the honest answer is that the practice is underdetermined and we should stop pretending there's a deep theory behind the asymmetry, I'd rather hear that than a tidy story.
