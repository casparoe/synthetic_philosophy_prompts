I've been asked to give the opening talk, 45 minutes plus half an hour of questions, to the long-term infrastructure and climate adaptation committee of a ministry. My thesis is this: there is no defensible positive pure rate of time preference. Discounting a benefit solely because it arrives later is a prejudice, and it has no more standing than discounting a benefit because it arrives somewhere else.

But I don't want to just assert that, so I've tried to say what I think should replace it. Two moves.

First: I want to split the thing people lump together. There is one discount on the future that I think is legitimate and is not a moral judgement at all, namely the chance that there will be nobody there to recieve the benefit — an actuarial term, like adjusting a treatment by the chance the patient dies first. I've been calling it the survivorship discount. Everything else that looks like time preference I want to reclassify: extinction and civilisational hazard goes in the first bucket; expected growth in future consumption, and opportunity cost of capital, goes in a second bucket that is empirical, contested, and definitely not something you read off a moral intuition.

Second: that second bucket has a property people keep missing, and it's the one bit of my own field I'm confident about. If you do not know the future rate but you have a spread of plausible constant rates, then at long horizons the effective rate doesn't stay at the middle of the spread, it sinks toward the bottom of it — the slowest-decaying component carries almost all the weight, same reason the long-lived tail dominates in a mixture of exponentials. I've since found that economists have a version of this argument, but the people in the room won't have internalised it, and I think it's the single most load-bearing thing I have to say. It means a constant exponential is the wrong functional form for anything past a few decades, and my committee cares about a hundred years and a sea wall.
