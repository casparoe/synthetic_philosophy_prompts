I want a case analysis of one episode through one piece of theory: the fight over how much output Greece would lose under fiscal consolidation, 2010 to 2015, read through the common prior assumption and the agreement results that go with it.

The facts I am working from, so that you need not reconstruct them: Greece entered a first bailout with the IMF, the European Commission and the ECB in 2010 and a second in 2012, both conditional on tax rises and spending cuts. Output fell by roughly a quarter over the crisis, unemployment rose above 25 per cent, and the forecasts embedded in the programmes were repeatedly and badly wrong on the downside. In 2013 the Fund's own staff published work conceding that the fiscal multipliers used to forecast the effects of consolidation had been set too low. Syriza won the January 2015 election on an anti-austerity platform; after six months of negotiation and the closure of the banks, a referendum on 5 July 2015 rejected the creditors' proposals by about 61 to 39; a week later the government accepted a third programme on terms harsher than those the electorate had just refused. Both sides had access to the same outturn data throughout, both published their reasoning, and neither moved much.

What I already know and do not want rehearsed: that the common prior assumption is a modelling device rather than an empirical claim about anyone; that with common priors and common knowledge of posteriors, posteriors must coincide; that the no-trade results put the same machinery to work; and the standard defences, that the prior is a hypothetical point of agreement before any private information, and that once priors are allowed to differ almost any pattern of behaviour can be rationalised. Assume all of that.

What I want is a disciplined mapping. Who are the agents, given that the creditors were three institutions that did not agree perfectly among themselves and one of them, the IMF, arrived at its prior through a modelling framework rather than through evidence; and given that the Greek side changed personnel and mandate in 2015? What is the event space, when what is at issue is not a shock but a structural parameter of the transmission mechanism? What were the information partitions, and were the two sides' posteriors ever actually common knowledge? Then say what the theory predicts for a case so described, and where the description itself is doing work the theory cannot do.

Three places where I suspect the fit is strained, and where I want your verdict on whether the strain lies in the theory or in my way of setting the case up. First, the model-indexed prior: a multiplier is not a state of the world but a feature of the model used to get from policy to outcomes, so the two sides may have had priors over different spaces. Does that make the agreement result silent here, or is it only heterogeneous priors relabelled as heterogeneous models? Second, beliefs against stakes: the agreement results constrain credences, not decisions, and the creditors were pricing Grexit and contagion to other programme countries while the Greek government was pricing domestic welfare and its own mandate. Would credal convergence have settled anything, and how would we tell whether the visible dispute was credal at all? Third, the updating: after the output collapse became common knowledge and the Fund revised its multipliers, the Greek electorate moved further against the programmes rather than towards them, and the creditors did not soften. Is that prior heterogeneity, non-credal factors, or each side tracking a different event? Say which of these the framework can distinguish and which it cannot.

Then run the traffic the other way. Does the episode confirm the common prior assumption, is it an anomaly for it, or is it the kind of case the assumption was never designed to describe — and if the last, what exactly does that excuse it from? Be explicit about where the theory has nothing to say rather than stretching it to speak.

Lead with one sentence stating the bottom line you would defend, then give the analysis. No citations, references or reading lists. Under 900 words.
