hey — I run a small podcast on political philosophy, one guest at a time, long-ish conversations, and next week's guest is Marta Voss, who's just published a book called The Price of Leaving. Her claim as I understand it: the republican tradition — Pettit and the non-domination people — picked the right intuitive target but never gave us a usable test for it, because asking whether some power was arbitrary just turns into another judgement call. Her replacement is that you're dominated to the degree that leaving would cost you: leaving the job, the marriage, the congregation, the country. You're free just insofar as exit is cheap. So domination isn't a status you discover, it's a number, and policywise the interesting stuff is all about lowering the price of leaving rather than about checking whether power tracks your interests.

I need 8 to 10 questions and I want them to actually bite instead of letting her restate the setup. I'm sympathetic to plain old negative liberty (Berlin's version) myself, so I don't want softballs, but I also don't want to spend the hour saying "but what about the kind master who never interferes."

Things I want at least one question each on:

- whether "cost" is meant to include the costs nobody imposed. My rent goes up because of a housing market, so my exit from the city gets expensive, but no agent's power over me has grown. If those count, the exit test looks like it's quietly become a view about distributive luck rather than about power; if they don't, it looks like non-interference with extra steps. I'd like the question to set up that fork, then a follow-up for whichever horn she picks.
- law. Every legal order raises exit costs on purpose — contracts, property, borders, exit taxes. Is having a legal system therefore dominating, or is law special? I suspect she'll say law is fine when it tracks the interests of the people subject to it, and I want the follow-up to point out that tracking is exactly the machinery she said she was replacing.
- whether freedom is a quantity on her view. Republicans make a lot of the claim that freedom is a status — you're either someone's subject or you aren't — and she's turning it into a scalar. Ask what we lose, and specifically what happens to the binary things we care about (citizenship, rights, slavery) if freedom comes in degrees.
- the benevolent dependency. A marriage where leaving would be financially ruinous, and the spouse is a saint who never interferes and never would. Is the wife dominated? Whatever she answers, I want a follow-up pressing whether the answer is driven by the exit cost or by the fact that the husband *could* be awful, since thats the power she says she doesn't need to talk about.
- emigration. If exit cost is the whole story, then open borders plus a relocation grant is the biggest freedom reform on the table, and a closed but very democratic state looks worse than an easy-exit dictatorship. Ask it straight, and give me a follow-up for when she says these cases never come apart in reality.
- voice. Republicans care a lot about contestation, about being able to argue back rather than just walk. If exit is cheap, does voice stop mattering? I want a follow-up for her likely move that cheap exit and real voice tend to come as a package.
- and one closing question about what would actually count against the view — not "what's the strongest objection," but what she'd have to see to give it up.

Also, two habits I'd love help defusing. When a case gets hard she says "that's an implementation detail," and when the scalar-versus-status stuff comes up she says "you're conflating freedom with what freedom is worth." For each of those, one question where the reply is available to her, and then a follow-up that makes it uncomfortable to keep using it.

Shes read everything and shes quick, so no need to define terms for her and please skip any "how did you get into this work" openers. For each question give me one line on what she'll most likely say back, then the follow-up keyed to that.
