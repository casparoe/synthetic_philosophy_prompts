I run a fortnightly reading group that's pretty mixed — mostly philosophy undergrads, two biology people, one compsci strudent, usually around 14 of us — and we get 90 minutes in a room with one big table, paper, index cards, pens and a whiteboard. I'd rather not use screens: last term someone pulled up wikipedia halfway through and the whole session collapsed into people reading instead of doing. The next meeting is on that part of Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach where he invents typogenetics — strands of letters that get translated into enzymes, enzymes that then operate on strands, and strands whose own enzymes bring about copies of themselves — as a way of exhibiting the DNA–Gödel isomorphism. I really don't want to explain this to them, and I especially don't want to announce the analogy up front: I want them to feel the distinction before anybody names it. So nobody should hear the words Gödel, self-reference, quine, code or isomorphism until the debrief.

My half-baked idea is to give them something like a paper-and-cardboard version of the system and let them play mechanised enzyme, without ever telling them what the system is for. Split into three or four teams; each team gets a strip of letters, a one-page rulebook for turning triplets of letters into enzymes and for applying enzymes to strips, and maybe 25 minutes of actual table work. The trick I want them to walk into is that not all self-copying strands are self-copying in the same way. One team's strip should contain an enzyme that just photocopies whatever strip it happens to be sitting on — trivially self-replicating, and completely uninformative, because nothing in the strip carries any information about the strip. A second team's rulebook (not their strip) should contain an entry that names their strip and says to write it out again — self-reference smuggled in from outside, by stipulation. A third team's strip should be the real thing: its enzymes, running blind on the strip, reassemble a strip that codes for those very same enzymes, without the strip's own letter sequence ever having been written down anywhere in the rules. My hope is that the first two teams finish fast and feel like they've won, and that the debrief is where that gets taken apart. But I can't tell whether this three-way is the right one, or whether I'm smuggling in the very lesson I want them to discover, so feel free to fix it.

What I actually need from you is a runnable session plan: invent a small enzyme rule set of your own (much simpler than Hofstadter's, and say so — I'm not trying to reproduce the book's actual enzymes, just to have something mechanical that runs in 25 minutes), the actual strips and enzymes for each team, the materials, any roles worth assigning, and a minute-by-minute timeline including how the strips get handed out so nobody peeks at another team's. Then the debrief: which moment in the activity is supposed to correspond to which part of the idea, what question you'd ask to get them to see that the interesting case is the one where self-reference is achieved through a translation scheme rather than put in by hand, and how you'd handle the two teams who think they won. Please also be straight about what will predictably go wrong — I'm guessing someone will just freehand the answer strip instead of running the enzymes, someone will decide this is a biology lesson, and at least one team will finish in three minutes and start texting — and about what the activity cannot deliver and I'll have to say myself: I assume it can't give them the actual incompleteness result, and it can't show that real cells work this way. Finally, give me one check at the end that would tell me they got the distinction: I was thinking of handing out a fifth strip and asking each team to predict, before running anything, whether it copies itself and what would have to be true for it to, but you may have a better test.
