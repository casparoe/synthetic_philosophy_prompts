I've ended up defending something in arguments with friends that I'm not sure I could actually be talked out of: deplatforming, boycotts, pile-ons and professional ostracism are punishment — real punishment, not just lots of people each exercising their own speech and freedom of association — and that is exactly why they're morally dicey, not because of some seperate worry about chilling speech or about what shaming does to the character of the people doing it.

The bit I lean on to make this precise is a test I made up and have gotten attached to: call it the contempt condition. A collective response to someone is punitive, I say, only if there is a live mechanism by which the target's non-compliance — refusing to apologise in the right register, denying the charge, appealing, going quiet in the wrong way — is itself treated as a further offence and escalated. Plain criticism, avoidance and reputational updating don't do that; you can shrug at them. Punishment, on my account, sets up a structure where there's a right answer about how you're supposed to respond, and you get hurt more if you dont produce it.

I also want to say that punishment without a punisher is coherent: nobody has to be in a position to grant mercy or call it off for this to still be punishment. But I'm not sure I can have both, since my whole reason for caring about the category is that I think punishers owe people proportionality, a hearing, and an end date.

So what would count against this? I want defeaters ranked fatal to merely awkward, including things that have argubly already happened, and I want the ones my side would actually concede rather than just what the critics say. Is the contempt condition doing real work, or is it a sneaky way of rebuilding a state-analogy so that only institutions can punish — which would collapse my view into the expressive/associative rival I'm arguing against? Does the no-punisher move cost me the procedural conclusions I actually care about, or can I keep them by spreading duties across participants in a way that isn't absurd? What about the annoying fact that a lot of high-profile cancellations leave the target more or less fine a year later — counter-example, failed punishment, or neither? And be honest about whether I've defined things so that any hostile reaction aimed at worsening someone counts, in which case I've qualified myself into saying nothing. What I'd personally find most moving is a case that has everything my test asks for and that still obviously isn't punishment.
