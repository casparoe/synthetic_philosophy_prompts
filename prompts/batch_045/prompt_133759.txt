Posting this because it started as a Discord argument and has now eaten two evenings. Somone in a rationalist-adjacent server I've lurked in since about 2014 dropped it, I've tried to steelman it properly and it still seems to go through, and I really don't like where it ends up.

Setup: call "descriptive closure" the thesis that every empirical finding — every observation, measurement, experiment, historical record, whatever — can be stated in vocabulary containing no normative terms. And call a claim normative if it contains "ought", "reason", "should", "justified", "good", "fitting" and the like. Then:

(1) Hume's law: no conclusion containing a normative term follows validly from premises none of which contain one. No ought from an is.

(2) Descriptive closure: all empirical evidence is descriptive.

(3) "S has reason to believe P", "S should have credence 0.7 in P", "S's belief is unjustified" are normative claims — they're about what's correct for S, not about what S actually believes or what caused S to believe it.

(4) So no empirical finding can ever be, or entail, a reason for anyone to believe anything: by (1), any route from evidence to a claim about what someone should believe needs a normative premise somewhere, and by (2) no amount of evidence hands you one.

(5) And you can't just help yourself to a normative premise whenever the argument needs one. If "if D then N" is free for any D and N you happen to like, then (1) is vacuous — you'd have no objection left to the person who gets from "my culture disapproves of X" to "X is wrong", or from "God commands X" to "you ought to X", or from "this maximizes total welfare" to "you should do it".

(6) But epistemic normativity — the whole update-on-evidence, respect-base-rates, don't-double-count, notice-your-information-diet package — is exactly the claim that descriptive facts about your evidence are reasons to change your mind.

(7) So if (4) is right, nothing anyone could ever learn is ever a reason to believe anything. Bayesianism becomes a causal model of how beliefs get revised, not a standard for how they should be. There's no such thing as being epistemically irrational, only being caused to update differently. (It also gives me no reason to accept the argument, which the guy who posted it is completely fine with — he calls himself a quietist about reasons and says "there being no reason to believe it" isn't the same as "it's false".)

I want to keep (1) — I use it constantly, e.g. when someone goes from "these digital systems would be conscious" straight to "so we owe them moral consideration", which looks to me like exactly the move Hume's law is supposed to block. And I really want to keep (6); I've spent a chunk of my adult life thinking of myself as someone who updates. So something in the middle has to go and I can't see what.

My one escape attempt: the normative premise is known a priori, not from observation, so (2) doesn't cover it — "if your evidence supports P, you have some reason to believe P" is a necessary truth you get by reflection, not a finding. But that seems to make (1) toothless in exactly the way (5) describes: every is-ought dispute collapses into a first-order fight about which bridge premises are true, which is a normative fight rather than a logical one. So then I tried constraining bridge premises (they have to be general, necessary, not gerrymandered to the case at hand, knowable by reflection) and noticed two things: that constraint is itself normative, so I haven't stepped outside (1) at all; and people's "a priori" normative intuitions look suspiciously well-predicted by where and when they grew up, which is the kind of thing the debunking arguments I like are built out of.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather keep (1) and (6) than (2) or (3), but tell me if that ordering is backwards. Concretely:

- Is there an equivocation in here — (1) stated as a claim about valid derivation but doing the work of a claim about metaphysical grounding or explanation? If I only grant the logical version, does (4) even follow?
- What does each of the standard ways out actually have to pay: the "belief has a constitutive aim, truth, and that's where the normativity comes from" move; the "reasons are brute irreducible normative facts that don't need to be derived from anything" move; the "epistemic reasons aren't normative in the same sense moral reasons are, so (3) is false" move; and error theory, i.e. bite (7) and say all normative claims are false.
- Whatever you pick, does it leave me able to say anything useful to the person going from "it's conscious" to "we owe it something"? I don't want a theory that saves reasons for belief by making Hume's guillotine too dull to cut with.
