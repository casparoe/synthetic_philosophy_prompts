I am finishing a novel — near-future, comic in register but not a comedy — set in a world where acausal trade has long since stopped being a decision-theorist's thought experiment and become a settled, heavily regulated, thoroughly tedious corner of the financial services industry. Retail investors hold units in funds that transact with counterparties in causally disconnected branches. The transactions are legally binding, audited, reported to a regulator, and in principle unobservable. My protagonist is a junior compliance officer at such a fund, and I want to open her section of the book with a diegetic artifact: the risk-disclosure section of a retail prospectus, headed something like "Important Information About Your Acausal Counterparty Exposure," roughly 800 words, in the flat dutiful plain-English of fund disclosure — the register that explains enormous risks in sentences designed to be skipped.

What I need from it is this. The comedy has to come from the decision theory being right, not from invented silliness. No message, signal, or enforcement mechanism may pass between the parties, and nothing may be transmitted in either direction; yet the document must still discuss settlement, custody, valuation, and counterparty default. The only sanction available is the one the structure actually supplies: that a counterparty who would defect is the sort of counterparty the Fund's models decline to trade with, so defection selects itself out without anyone ever being punished. A decision theorist should not wince; a reader who has never heard of any of this should be able to follow it and should end up feeling, without being told, how strange the instrument is.

Please build it around a few specific clauses I have in mind, which you should feel free to sharpen: a Bounded-Simulation Clause, forbidding the Fund from entering a trade with any counterparty whose decision procedure it cannot model at least as finely as that counterparty models the Fund; a Copy-Weighting Schedule, governing how exposure is allocated among branches that contain different numbers of copies of the Fund's own decision procedure; a duty to report any apparent communication from a counterparty as a suspected fraud event, on the grounds that such communication is impossible; and, unavoidably, a paragraph explaining why the Fund will never publish performance figures. Give the fund a plausible name and a fictitious regulator.

Two further constraints. First, the piece should do novelistic work: a reader should come away with a concrete sense of what this woman does all day, the particular humdrum professional terror of signing off on a trade that cannot, even in principle, be checked. Second, please sustain the deadpan absolutely — no winking, no nudging, no moment where the prose admits that any of this is strange. It should never name a real person, cite or allude to a real text, or imply that the reader ought to look something up.
