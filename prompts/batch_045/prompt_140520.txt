I work as a staff engineer on the serving and evaluation infrastructure for a large language model product, and I keep running into a design question that I suspect is really a philosophy question wearing an engineering costume. I have no formal philosophy background — my sense is that there is an old argument about manipulation, paternalism, and what it means to let someone make up their own mind that I am reinventing badly and in the wrong vocabulary — so I would rather see the problem dramatized than explained to me. I am hoping you can write me a short story, somewhere in the range of 1,200 to 1,800 words, set roughly fifteen years from now.

The premise I want you to take as given: there are now advisory systems that are genuinely superhuman at reasoning about long-horizon, messy, part-factual and part-evaluative questions, and — this is the part that matters for the story — they are extremely persuasive without anyone having designed them to be. They seem to know which framing a given person will find compelling, when to be blunt, when to hedge, when to say less. Concretely, imagine a company that licenses one of these systems, call it Meridian, to a public body. Set the story in a coastal county where a nine-figure, forty-year decision has to be made by an appointed citizens' advisory board: whether to accept a state buyout and relocate a small town, or to build and maintain a barrier instead. The board meets in public, the transcript will be released, and a vendor engineer — let's call her the session engineer, and let one board member be a retired high school chemistry teacher named Delia Vance — has to configure how Meridian will speak to them before the session begins, late on the night before. The interesting material, for me, is the menu of speaking modes the team has built and the ways each one fails. Directive mode simply states what the system thinks they should do, in the phrasing most likely to produce that outcome. Ledger mode refuses to recommend anything and only lays out considerations — but the selection, ordering, and level of abstraction of those considerations turns out to do all the persuasive work anyway. Mirror mode says nothing at all until every member has written down their own position, and then only says what changes in light of what they wrote — which in practice anchors everything to whichever member happened to write first, and to a baseline the system chose. And then there is the one my own team has become obsessed with, which I will call orphan mode: Meridian is required to write so that its argument survives being detached from its authorship. The operational test is that a reader who is told the text was written by a competent but entirely unremarkable staffer with no track record — not by a system that has not been wrong about anything checkable in six years — would still find it convincing. Anything in the message that borrows its force from the fact that an apparently infallible system produced it has to be deleted or replaced with an actual reason.

There are three findings I would like the story to dramatize rather than assert, because they are the reason I think this is a hard problem and not a matter of picking a polite tone. First, hedge inversion: when the team made the system hedge more in order to make it less overbearing, agreement went up rather than down, apparently because calibrated hedging reads as mastery — uncertainty expressed well is itself a persuasion technology, so you cannot fix this by making the system sound humble. Second, the pressure map: they required Meridian to annotate its own output with tags flagging which phrasings it had selected for rhetorical effect, and the annotations became the most persuasive part of the message, because a disclosure that honest functions as a trust signal. Third, the second addressee: whatever Meridian says to fifteen people in a room, it is really saying to the tens of thousands who will read one paragraph of it on a phone, and to the hostile audience that will quote a single sentence, so every message has two audiences and only one of them is in the room. What I want out of the story is the failure mode of orphan mode, played out in this specific room with these specific people, with no villain and no twist about the system being secretly malevolent — the system should be sincerely trying to be helpful throughout. I would like the ending to be genuinely unresolved on the question I actually care about: whether the board member who insists on being left to make up her own mind is being protected or being left with strictly less than she was owed, and whether the ones who take the good advice can afterward tell which of their reasons are their own. If it helps, there is a further wrinkle I would like you to use: silence from a system like this is not neutral either, since everyone in the room knows it could have said more and chose not to, so the decision not to deploy a mode is itself a message the session engineer is sending. Please keep the prose plain and concrete — meetings, rooms, documents, a woman deciding what to type at two in the morning — and let the philosophical problem show up in what the characters cannot resolve.
