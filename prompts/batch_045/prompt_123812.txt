Hi — I'm on the technical advisory group for a mid-sized seafood certification scheme, and I need to draft the memo I'll circulate before our next call. Sorry for the short notice on this.

The situation: our welfare standard was written for finfish, basically with Atlantic salmon in mind. It requires a listed stunning method before slaughter, sets a maximum stocking density, and disqualifies any farm whose cumulative mortality over the cycle exceeds a threshold. Someone on the board has now proposed we extend the same standard to farmed shrimp and prawns, which we currently don't cover at all beyond some vague water-quality language. Our own staff have quietly pointed out the obvious problem: applied literally, essentially no shrimp operation on earth would qualify — there's no practical individual stunning method at that scale, the standard slaughter route is chilling in ice slurry, densities are orders of magnitude higher, and background mortality is high even on well-run farms. So "extend the standard" is in practice a decision about whether shrimp farming gets certified at all, and whether we keep any leverage over it.

Three options are on the table: apply the finfish standard as-is; write a separate shrimp annex with provisional criteria and a three-year sunset clause; or defer decapods entirely until the evidence on their capacity for valenced experience is better than it is.

My half-baked contribution, which I'd like you to develop rather than just agree with: I don't think the real disagreement in our group is about sentience. It's about the unit of aggregation. If welfare cost is counted per individual animal, shrimp look catastrophic per tonne of product — thousands of small animals, months of crowding, and a slaughter method that may itself be unpleasant and slow. If it's counted per animal's *capacity* — how much can be at stake for that individual at any moment, which is plausibly much narrower for a shrimp than for a salmon — the ranking partly flips, and shrimp stop looking like the worst thing in our scheme. And if we aggregate by intensity-times-duration over a lifetime, chronic mild badness in a crowded pond can outweigh a short acute event at killing time, which would point us at density and water quality instead of slaughter method. These are three different standards and they would certify three different sets of farms. So I want the memo to pick a unit, defend it, and be explicit that picking it is doing most of the work.

Things to weigh that I keep going back and forth on: whether a standard that no one can meet is worse than no standard, because it pushes production to uncertified buyers and we lose whatever pressure we have; whether a provisional tier is just a way of feeling good while changing nothing; whether excluding decapods implicitly asserts something about their sentience that we shouldn't be asserting; whether the numbers-per-tonne problem is a reason to care more about shrimp or a reason to admit our framework breaks down at that scale; and whether death itself belongs in a welfare standard or only the experiences leading up to it, since a high-mortality farm might still be fine on a purely hedonic view of what we're measuring. Also: some of us think the honest thing is to weight chronic stress heavily, but chronic stress is much harder to audit than a stocking density figure, so there's a real tradeoff between measuring what matters and measuring what an auditor can actually check on a Tuesday.

Please don't give me any citations or a reading list — I'll drop in the references myself, and I don't want invented ones. No quotes either. Our group is split pretty evenly, and the two people I most need to bring along are the ones who think invertebrate welfare is a distraction from real animal problems and the one who thinks anything short of excluding shrimp farming outright is cowardice, so the losing side needs to be stated in the version its supporters would recognise.

Sections, in this order, roughly 1,800–2,000 words total:

- Bottom line — 150 words: the recommendation, up front, with the sunset or review date attached.
- The decision in front of us — 250 words: what we are actually choosing between, and why the finfish wording makes this a choice about exclusion rather than about criteria.
- Why applying the finfish standard isn't the neutral option — 300 words: what we'd be committing to by doing nothing new.
- The case for a provisional shrimp annex — 400 words: including which unit of aggregation I should adopt and why that one rather than the obvious alternatives.
- The strongest version of waiting for better evidence — 300 words: argue it as well as it can be argued.
- What the annex should actually say — 350 words: concrete criteria an auditor could apply, in the order of priority, with the tradeoff between auditability and accuracy stated honestly.
- How we'd know this failed — 200 words: what we'd see in three years, or sooner, that should make us reverse course.
