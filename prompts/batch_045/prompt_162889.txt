I am a novelist, and I am trying to write a piece of fiction that does conceptual work without degenerating into a lecture. I would be grateful for a draft.

The material I keep circling is periodization: whether divisions such as "the Middle Ages," "the Renaissance," and "the Scientific Revolution" name real joints in the past, or whether they are labels laid over the past by much later writers — useful for museum floors and syllabi, and misleading about how anything felt from inside. Please do not bring in scholarship: no citations, no named historians, no allusions to particular books or debates. The story has to carry the argument by itself, and it should not depend on specialized historical detail. If you set it somewhere real, keep the concrete texture to what any educated reader already knows.

What I actually want dramatized is a third option I have not seen handled in fiction, which I will call the lamplighter problem. Suppose a much later writer declares that a particular stretch of years was a rebirth. That declaration gets taught, painted, built into restorations, and used to decide which buildings are preserved and which are pulled down. Suppose further that the people living in the shadow of those decisions come to understand their own city, their own craft, their own dead, in its terms — and then act differently because of it. In that case the period is neither simply discovered nor simply invented: it has become a cause that acts backward on the years it describes, like a lamplighter lighting the street behind him so that the road he has already walked appears, to anyone looking back along it, to have been lit the whole way. The question the story should force is whether this is a genuine third position, or whether on inspection it collapses back into one of the other two.

Some constraints, which matter to me as craft rather than as pedantry:

Set the story inside the working life of one person or one household — roughly six decades — so that century-scale categories are never visible from where the characters stand. I want the reader to feel the mismatch of scale: the changes the characters themselves notice should be small-grained and datable to a year or a decade (a shift in how a contract is witnessed, in what a workshop is asked for, in how letters are dated, in who is believed about what), while the big periods remain unobservable to them.

Tell it in three movements, separated by two or three very short interstitial "seams" narrated from a later vantage point. The characters must never use period vocabulary; the seams must, and the seams should not agree with each other — the same years should get named differently as the vantage point moves, and the boundaries should slide.

The story should commit to one of the three views and make the strongest available case for it, but the rivals should be present in the texture of the thing, not refuted in dialogue. No character should deliver a seminar, and nothing should be wrapped up with a tidy moral.

Please write between 1,200 and 1,800 words, in formal but plain prose, in American English.
