I am the scientific secretary and non-voting chair of the review panel for a national research council's mid-sized investigator grants. We run three cycles a year. Each cycle is two and a half days, twelve panelists, roughly seventy proposals, and about six million dollars; our ranked list then goes to a council that can and occasionally does send it back. The panelists are senior working researchers who are paid nothing, receive no training in judgement and decision making, and are rotated a third at a time, so I can assume neither continuity nor shared vocabulary, and I cannot add a training day or a second pass over the batch. What I can change is the procedure inside the room and the schedule going in, provided I can defend each change on a single page to a council of non-specialists and to panelists who will resent anything that feels like bureaucracy.

Our current process, in the order it happens: two lead reviewers per proposal read it in full and send me written pre-scores forty-eight hours ahead; the other panelists receive the abstract and a one-page summary of the leads' comments. I open each item with a one-sentence characterisation of where I think it sits. Discussion then runs in a fixed seating order, after which scores are entered sequentially on a shared screen with the running mean visible, and any score diverging by more than 1.5 points from that mean must be accompanied by a typed justification. Proposals are scheduled grouped by host institution, so the panel frequently hears four or five from the same university in a row. One panelist each cycle participates asynchronously and posts written comments that the room reads before anyone speaks. There is no feedback loop: we learn what we got right years later, if at all.

I would like a decision procedure, not an exposition of the theory. What I have in mind has three parts.

First, a real-time diagnostic: an ordered set of questions I can run in about ninety seconds, using only what is observable in the room plus the pre-scores that only I hold, that sorts the current state of a proposal into at least these buckets — genuine convergence on shared evidence; an informational cascade; reputational or conformist herding; structurally mandated deference to the lead reviewers, who are the only people who have read the thing; and spillover from a neighbouring proposal or from the run of items from the same institution. For each question, state what observable would count as a sufficient positive, what that question cannot in principle distinguish from what it is meant to detect, where the procedure simply stops with "no intervention", and where it must hand the decision back to a person because the issue is one of values rather than facts.

Second, a remedial ladder: an ordered menu of interventions from least to most intrusive, each attached to the diagnosis that triggers it, with what it costs in minutes, in goodwill, and in information. The standard literature distinguishes herding that comes from people rationally discounting their own private signals against a public record from herding that comes from the social cost of being seen to be wrong; keep that distinction wherever it changes what I should actually do, and say explicitly where the remedies for the two pull in opposite directions, which one governs when both diagnoses are live, and why.

Third, the ex ante fixes: what I should change before the next cycle, and in what order of priority, given a one-page justification budget and a panel that will not tolerate extra form-filling. Please rule specifically on the deviation-and-justification rule, on speaking order, on whether and when I should speak at all, on the institutional grouping in the schedule, and on the asynchronous panelist whose comments are read first and cannot be cross-examined.

Then please run the procedure on three cases. Case A: the first speaker, a well-known and well-liked figure, gives a proposal a nine and a confident rationale; the next five come in between eight and nine with reasons that are recognisably paraphrases of his; the two lead reviewers' private pre-scores, which I alone hold, were six and four. Tell me what I do in the next five minutes, and in particular whether and how I use the pre-scores. Case B: a proposal that everyone has in fact read, in an area where this particular panel is unusually expert, and which is genuinely in the top decile of the batch — the false-positive control, where I need the procedure to output "leave it alone" and to tell me which observable does that work. Case C: an unfashionable cross-disciplinary proposal from an early-career investigator at an institution nobody has heard of, scheduled immediately after a run of five strong proposals from our flagship university, which the panel is drifting toward rejecting in a way that looks to me like nobody wanting to be the person who championed a soft topic. I think it is good and my instinct is to rescue it. I want the procedure's answer, and I want you to take seriously that the honest answer may be that I should not intervene, or should intervene only through a mechanism that binds me equally when I am wrong: tell me what a discretionary rescue by the chair teaches the panel about how to read my interventions, and what that does to the process over the remaining day and a half.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do. I am aware that we have no ground truth within a cycle, that a correct convergence and a lucky cascade are indistinguishable from the outcome of one meeting, and that nothing in this fixes errors that the whole panel shares because the whole field shares them. Say where the procedure will misfire, which of its steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure, and which parameter — I suspect something like our tolerance for funding risky proposals at the expense of safe ones — is a value choice that the procedure should surface and then leave to the council rather than settle for them.
