Me and my brother-in-law have this argument every Thanksgiving and I never win it properly. He says unelected judges have no business overruling a legislature, that's just nine people in robes substituting their preferences for the people's representatives, and if you don't like a law you vote the bastards out. My go-to is always Brown v. Board, which he grudgingly concedes, but then he says "fine, that's one case in sixty years, and it was a hundred years ago." I want something better than one great example. So I've been going through cases and writing down my actual gut verdicts, and they don't obviousely line up. Help me find the principle, if there is one.

Here's my list. "Fine" = judges were right to strike it down.

1. State legislature requires segregated schools. Court strikes it down. Fine, obviously.

2. Legislature passes a minimum wage / maximum hours law; court strikes it down because the economics is bad and it interferes with freedom of contract (the old Lochner thing). Not fine. That's just judges doing policy.

3. Legislature passes a strict voter ID law after staffers explicitly told them it would disproportionatly disenfranchise a racial minority. Court strikes it down. Fine.

4. The same voter ID law, but this time passed by ballot initiative, 58 to 42. Court strikes it down anyway. Still fine, and I notice I'm not even tempted to say the referendum changes anything here.

5. Legislature bans same-sex marriage. Court strikes it down. Fine.

6. Same ban, but passed by referendum with 62% and turnout was high. Court strikes it down. This one I go back and forth on. Part of me says rights are rights and 62% doesn't matter. Another part says this is exactly the "nine people overrule everybody" case and I hate it. My gut, annoyingly, says: fine but much less fine than 5, and maybe not fine at all.

7. A badly gerrymandered legislature — one party wins 60% of the seats on 45% of the votes — passes a near-total abortion ban, and the court strikes it down citing both the rights violation and the malapportionment. I think fine, and I think the gerrymander genuinely matters to why it's fine.

8. The same gerrymandered legislature passes a minimum wage law, and the court strikes that down too, citing the same malapportionment. Not fine. So the gerrymander is doing work in 7 but not in 8 and I can't say why.

9. Court strikes down a law because the legislature's stated factual basis was simply false — say they banned a medical treatment on the ground that it causes autism, which isn't supported by anything. Fine, I think. But this one bugs me, because structurely it's the same as 2: judges saying "your reasons are bad." The only difference is that here the reasons are factually wrong rather than economically wrong. Is that the whole difference?

10. Court strikes down a statute because it conflicts with a human rights treaty the country signed twenty years ago. Not fine — this feels worse to me than 5 somehow, even though I'm generally pro-international-law. Maybe because the treaty was never voted on either.

11. Court orders a legislature to actually spend money on something — say, accessible school buildings or flood defenses — rather than striking a law down. I lean not fine, it feels like a budget decision. But then why is 1 fine? Segregation cost money to fix too.

12. The one I have no stable verdict on at all: legislature passes a law, court strikes it, legislature re-passes it almost verbatim with better findings, court strikes it again, and this goes on three times over fifteen years, with elections in between that don't change the legislature's mind. My instinct flips depending on the hour.

My tentative principle, which I came up with to beat my brother-in-law, is: judges may overrule a legislature when the people who lose under the law had no realistic shot at stopping it through ordinary politics, and otherwise not. But I can already see it doesn't obviously handle 6 (gay people can vote, they just lost), or 8 vs 7, or 11.

So: test that principle against my verdicts and repair it or replace it. I want the candidate principle stated precisely enough that I can argue with it, with each clause tied to the case that forces it. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts no clean principle can keep — I'd rather drop a verdict than keep a convoluted one. And tell me what your principle predicts for two cases I haven't listed: (a) judges striking down a constitutional amendment that needed a legislative supermajority *and* a referendum to pass, i.e. the strongest democratic pedigree a law can have; (b) judges striking down a legislature's *failure* to regulate something — say, refusing to pass any rules on a new technology that is clearly harming people.
