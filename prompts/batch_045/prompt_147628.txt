I've been messing around with a modified version of that typogenetics toy from Hofstadter -- strands of A/C/G/T read in triplets, translated into enzymes that walk along strands and do things like copy a base, move, delete, cut -- plus two instructions I added myself: SWAP, which exchanges two adjacent bases, and NUM, which writes out, as a fresh strand, the Gödel number of whatever strand the enzyme is currently bound to, under a numbering I fixed once at the start and then never touched again. The soup supplies free bases and the translation machinery, and that's it. Translation is generic: any strand gets translated, nothing has to earn it.

What I'm actually trying to do is figure out what I think "self-replicating" means in there, because I want to write something about whether the DNA/Gödel isomorphism is load-bearing or just a pretty analogy, and my gut verdicts on cases dont line up. Here they are.

1. S translates into an enzyme that builds the complement of S, and that complement, translated in a different frame, builds an enzyme that rebuilds S. Yes, obviously. Paradigm case, no hesitation.

2. A's enzyme copies B; B's enzyme copies A. I want to say neither strand is a self-replicator, but the pair is.

3. D's enzyme copies D, but only once D has been primed by a soup enzyme that nothing encodes -- it's just always floating there, it isn't heritable, nothing maintains it. I still want to say yes, genuine self-replication. Every real replicator leans on chemistry it doesn't encode.

2 and 3 are the pair I can't reconcile. From A's point of view, B's enzyme is just sitting in the soup doing its thing, exactly like the primer in 3. If there's a real difference, I can't say what it is.

4. P's enzyme copies whatever strand it happens to land on, including P, including junk, including truncated junk. P gets copied indefinitely. I want to say no -- or rather, that P isn't the replicator, the soup-with-P-in-it is. But P's presence really does raise the number of copies of P, so I'm not sure what grounds the no.

5. Q's enzyme catalyses the production of more of that same enzyme out of free amino acids and never touches a strand. The enzyme population grows; no strand is ever copied. I go back and forth on this one: part of me says it's crystallization, not replication; part of me says prions, and part of me says the enzyme's structure is still a function of Q's structure, so the information is getting through somehow.

6. R's enzyme copies R but slips one base per generation. For about forty generations the slip lands in degenerate positions, so the enzyme built off each copy is amino-acid-identical to the original, and then it hits a nondegenerate position and the lineage just stops. Per copying event: yes. For the lineage as a whole: I honestly dont know, it feels like yes and no at once.

7. A cloud: roughly ten thousand distinct strands, each of which translates into an enzyme that copies some other member of the set, with high error. No individual text persists for more than a couple of generations, but the distribution over texts is stable and regenerates itself. My gut says yes at the level of the cloud and no at the level of any token -- which I realize is the same move I made in 2, and I don't know whether that's consistency or a habit.

8. V uses NUM: its enzyme writes out the numeral of the strand it's bound to, so V's product is a strand that names V rather than a strand that is V. I want to say yes here, since in the arithmetic image it's just a fixed point of the diagonal. But notice that in case 1 I cheerfully let the copy pass through a complement, i.e. through a different notation. So how much notational distance am I allowed before it becomes self-description rather than self-replication? If the answer is "some but not that much," the thing I'm writing is in worse shape than I thought.

My tentative principle: X is a self-replicator iff (a) X's presence makes copies of X more likely, (b) the causal route from X to the copy runs through something whose structure is a function of X's structure, so the information passes through X rather than merely through X's being there, and (c) whatever else the route depends on is generic -- not itself the product of a code inside the same system. Does it survive the eight? I think (c) is doing all the work in 2 versus 3, and I dont trust it, because "generic" looks like it just means "the stuff I decided to hold fixed."

What I'd like: one principle, or two or three candidates if they genuinely disagree, stated precisely enough that I can run new cases through it, with each clause tied to the case that motivated it, and an honest list of which of my verdicts it can't save and which one you'd overturn. Then its verdicts on three cases I have no intuition about: (i) cooperative replication, where the enzyme copies X only if a second copy of X is already present -- is the replicating unit one token or the group; (ii) X's enzyme destroys every strand that isn't a copy of X and copies nothing at all, pure niche-clearing; (iii) X's enzyme copies a scrambled version of X that happens to translate to the same enzyme, because I made my code heavily degenerate -- is that a copy of X or a different strand with the same phenotype?

And the thing I actually care about: should the verdict be invariant under the isomorphism? If I redescribe case 1 with strands-as-numbers, enzymes-as-proofs and products-as-theorems, should I get the same yes/no out of my principle -- and if the answer is no, what does that tell me about the analogy?
