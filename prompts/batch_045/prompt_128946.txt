I'm putting together a twenty-minute presentation for a small reading group on transcendental arguments and their use against skepticism, and I've been working from a study-guide chapter on the topic that I'm not sure how far to trust. The chapter presents transcendental arguments as Kant's answer to the skeptic: instead of arguing head-on for the existence of the external world, you begin from something the skeptic herself grants — that she has experiences, or that she can formulate and defend a doubt at all — and argue "backwards" to that starting point's necessary conditions; and because the skeptic's own argumentation already presupposes what it denies, she is supposed to refute herself simply by the act of arguing, which the chapter offers as the reason these arguments are thought to be unusually powerful. It goes on to say that since their premises are conditions on any experience or any thought whatsoever, transcendental arguments deliver certainty about the external world — the mind-independent world the skeptic doubted — without needing any empirical evidence, and that they defeat the skeptic on her own terms rather than by accumulating evidence against her. The chapter closes with the objection Stroud is known for, namely that such an argument at best shows that we must believe some proposition in order to go on with a practice, which is perfectly compatible with that proposition being false and with our being mistaken about what our own commitments really are; it then dismisses this objection as nothing more than skepticism restated, hence question-begging against the Kantian, and as having been decisively answered by now.

My own inclination is that the talk of certainty is overblown, but I genuinely can't tell how much of the rest to accept: the "the skeptic refutes herself by arguing" framing in particular struck me as closer to a performative-contradiction style of argument than to what I'd understood transcendental arguments to be, and I suspect the chapter is sliding between two different strategies. To help me get clear, someone in the group proposed sorting transcendental arguments by the kind of support the step from premise to conclusion is supposed to provide: on a constitutive reading, the conclusion is partly constitutive of the doubt or practice in question, so that doubting it would empty the doubt of content; on a presuppositional reading, it is established as a necessary condition on the possibility of the practice independently of what the practice involves; and on an abductive or explanationist reading, it is put forward as the best explanation of the fact that the practice is possible at all. This threefold distinction appears nowhere in the chapter, and I have no idea whether it corresponds to anything philosophers actually distinguish or is something we invented over coffee.

Could you go through the chapter's characterization piece by piece and tell me what a sympathetic but careful defender of transcendental arguments would accept, what they would wince at, and what is straightforwardly wrong? Specifically: is the "the skeptic refutes herself by arguing" gloss a fair account of how these arguments are meant to work, or does it run together two strategies that should be kept apart; is the certainty claim an overstatement of a more modest thesis, and if so, of which one, and does the chapter's reference to the mind-independent world add a further problem given the role transcendental idealism plays in Kant's own use of the argument; and is the dismissal of Stroud fair — does his objection depend on a conflation of its own, or does it survive being told that it begs the question? I'd also like to know whether my group's distinction maps onto any real distinction in the literature, and how the audit comes out differently under each of its three readings: does Stroud's objection bite equally hard against all three, and would any of them license the chapter's talk of certainty? Finally, once the summary is corrected, does anything in it still give us a route to answering the external-world skeptic, or does the corrected version leave the skeptic more or less where she began?
