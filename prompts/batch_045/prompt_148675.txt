Me and my mate Dave had a three-hour argument in the pub last night about where the line between history and pseudohistory actually goes, and I've come away realising I've got a whole pile of verdicts and no principle behind them. Dave's position — which I think is bollocks but I couldn't say why — is that it's purely sociological: pseudohistory is just what the people with jobs in history departments say is pseudohistory, and Fomenko only counts as a crank because he isn't one of them. I don't believe that, because I've got cases going both ways. But I also can't get my own test to work. The test I've been using goes: a claim is pseudohistory if its defenders have a move that converts every apparent refutation into more evidence — forgers, suppressed excavations, the dating is wrong, the mainstream won't look — so there's no state of the evidence that would get them to stop. Call it the no-way-out test. My second idea, which I like less, is about scope: crank theories never stop, they promise to rewrite everything, whereas real revisionism changes one thing and leaves the rest of the furniture where it was. I'm not sure that's more than a vibe, and it may just be a proxy for how big the claim is, which can't be right either.

Here are the verdicts I'm stuck with. (A) Fomenko: as I understand it, roughly everything before the year 1000 is a phantom duplicate of later history, arrived at by running statistical comparisons over king-lists and eclipse records. Obviously crank — and I want to say more than 'false': something about the method is rotten before you even check the answer. (B) Ancient aliens: the pyramids and the Nazca lines needed outside help. Crank, and I also think part of what's wrong with it is moral — it's a refusal to credit people four thousand years ago with being able to organise anything, which makes me suspect demarcation isn't purely epistemic, though I'd rather it were. (C) A visiting professor my girlfriend's department takes the piss out of: the Pictish symbol stones are a complete writing system and the Picts taught literacy to Ireland. Immaculate footnotes, and he does concede individual readings when people push him, but the headline conclusion has never moved in twenty years and every claim happens to land in the direction of national prestige. I call him a crank. (D) A retired surveyor who reckons every parish church in the county sits on a prehistoric grid; when three turn out to be off it, he says those three were rebuilt. Crank. (E) A local amateur who argued in the eighties that our abbey's foundation charter is a twelfth-century forgery. The professionals scoffed, and then a copy in a cartulary turned up with a witness list that can't be earlier than about 1180, so he was right. I want to say it was a real hypothesis all along — but note he was an outsider, he was claiming the documents are fakes, which is exactly the move in my no-way-out test, and the only difference I can find between him and (D) is that he turned out to be right, which is cheating. (F) Two cases from measuring stones: a retired engineering professor who surveyed hundreds of circles and found they all used one unit (the megalithic yard, I think) — crank, and as far as I can tell nobody in archaeology takes it seriously now; versus the passage at Maeshowe lining up with the midwinter sunset — perfectly respectable. Same evidence type, opposite verdicts, and I can't tell you how many coincidences have to pile up before a pattern becomes a pattern. (G) A real professor with a university press book arguing that all the silver in late Anglo-Saxon coinage came from a single Alpine mine. I think it's probably wrong. It is not pseudohistory. (H) One I go back and forth on: my flatmate's mate, a metal detectorist, says the hoard he found was a ritual deposit rather than someone's savings buried in a hurry. Half the time I think that's just I found something lovely and now I want it to be religious; half the time I think it's an ordinary inference from where the stuff was lying. (I) One more: my girlfriend's own thesis, that a whole genre of early medieval saints' lives was written by women. She'd be delighted if it were true and she cares because of a political commitment. I do not think that's crank. But I cannot see what separates it from (C), except that I happen to agree with it.

So: can you give me one principle, or at most two, stated precisely enough that I could apply it cold to a case I haven't seen — with a line on which of my cases motivates each clause, what it says about (H) and (I), and a straight answer about which of my verdicts it forces me to drop? Please also invent one new case yourself and tell me what your principle says about it, ideally the kind of case that shows where it would embarrass me. It can't smuggle in turned out to be true, for the reason in (E): that's no use on a live case and it would make (E) and (D) differ only by luck. And I'd like a sentence or two about what I should have said to Dave when he said it's all just who's in the club — I'm fairly sure (C), (E) and (G) together sink that, but I couldn't argue it properly at the time and he's going to bring it up again on Friday.
