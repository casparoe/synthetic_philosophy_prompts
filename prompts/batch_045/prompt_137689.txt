I work in social epidemiology, and most of my working life is spent fitting age–period–cohort models to repeated cross-sectional data: Lexis diagrams, attained age at exposure, the exact linear dependency between the three time scales, the resulting identification problem and the small set of contrasts that survive it, proxy variables standing in for things I cannot measure, and the standing worry that what I am calling a cohort effect is a period effect wearing a disguise. I follow the philosophical literature on social and human kinds only from a distance — I know roughly what is meant by natural kinds, by social construction, and by the looping or interactive kinds where the classification and the people classified act on each other, but I could not reconstruct the arguments or attribute them correctly.

I would like you to translate, into the vocabulary I actually use, the three positions on whether generations — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z — are real kinds rather than convenient labels:

1. Yes: a birth cohort is a genuine kind, because its members share formative exposures at roughly the same ages and those exposures leave durable cohort-level marks.
2. No: cohort labels are arbitrary cut-points imposed on continuous demographic and cultural change; whatever looks like a cohort finding is age and period underneath, and the boundaries shift whenever a commentator needs them to.
3. The looping option: the labels are real, not by tracking a pre-existing kind but by acting back on the people they sort.

For each, tell me what maps cleanly onto my terms — cohort as an exposure surrogate, critical-period versus accumulation models, the age-by-cohort interaction that is really attained age at a historical moment, administrative birth-year boundaries as measurement error that attenuates contrasts — and what has to be bent to fit.

I also want you to translate, and then criticise, a criterion of my own that I have been using informally. Call a cohort partition *causally load-bearing* for a given outcome when no re-parametrisation of the model that fits the data equally well, and that keeps the age and period profiles within their demographically plausible range, can flatten the cohort profile: that is, when the cohort dimension carries identified non-linear structure that cannot be redistributed onto the other two scales. Is this a genuine constraint on kind-hood, a roundabout way of restating position 1, or something demographers already have a name for and I am reinventing badly?

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. My suspicion is that the mapping fails in a specific and slightly embarrassing place: the claim people care about most — that each successive generation is more anxious, more narcissistic, more risk-averse than the last — is a monotone trend across cohorts, and a linear cohort trend is exactly the piece that the identification problem makes unrecoverable, while the piece that is identified, the curvature or acceleration, has no place in any cultural story anyone tells. If that is right, say so and say what it implies for whether the question has an empirical answer at all. If it is wrong or overstated, correct it. Also tell me what has no counterpart in my vocabulary whatsoever, and whether that residue is a defect of my framework, a sign that the philosophical question was thinner than it sounds, or a sign that the two are asking different questions and neither is defective.

Please commit: do not lay out the options and leave me to choose. Take a definite position on whether generations are real kinds, defend one of the three or a fourth you think is better, and give the reasons you would give if I pushed back. Where you are uncertain, distinguish uncertainty about a technical fact of demography from uncertainty that is really philosophical disagreement, and tell me plainly which it is.
