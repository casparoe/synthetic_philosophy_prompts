I'm writing a final paper for a seminar on collective responsibility, and I've reconstructed my roommate's argument against the moral significance of individual consumption as carefully as I can. The trouble is that it now looks like it travels much further than either of us wants it to.

(1) An act is morally objectionable only if it makes some creature worse off than it would otherwise have been, or otherwise fails to respond properly to something that is bad for that creature. Call this the difference principle. (2) Whether an act makes a creature worse off is settled by the counterfactual: hold everything else fixed, and ask whether that creature would have been better off had the act not been performed. (3) Poultry production is lumpy at every stage: a hatchery sets eggs in incubators of a fixed capacity, a grow-out house is built for a fixed number of birds, and a processor runs a batch once orders accumulate — say it orders in crates of a hundred, so the number of birds bred, confined and killed over a period is the same whether or not any particular shopper buys one more pack. (4) So no individual purchase makes any bird worse off than it would otherwise have been. (5) So no individual purchase is morally objectionable. (6) Nothing in the form of the argument turns on who the shopper is or on what is being bought, so the same holds of every shopper, of a vote in a national election, and of a tank of petrol. (7) But the birds are badly off, and their being badly off is entirely the upshot of individual purchases — there is no other mechanism producing it. So there can be an enormous evil every part of which is produced by acts none of which is objectionable. Note that this is not the harmless-torturer style of case, where each act makes a real but imperceptible difference: here, given the batching, each act's difference is claimed to be nil.

I tried the usual repair, which is to replace the actual-outcome counterfactual with an expected-difference one: since I don't know where in the crate cycle the cumulative total currently sits, there is roughly a one-in-a-hundred chance that my pack is the one that tips the order, and the tip is worth a hundred birds, so my expected difference is about one bird's suffering — a figure that is pleasingly insensitive to the crate size. But two variants make me doubt the repair. First, it makes the reason depend on my ignorance in both directions at once: the logistics manager who has read the day's order book knows she is not pivotal and so, on this account, has no reason at all — she can also, if she prefers, time her purchase to the boundary and thereby cause a hundred deaths rather than an expected one, which is a hundred times worse than what the ignorant shopper does. Second, and worse: run the counterfactual recursively. Each purchase is non-pivotal given the purchases that came before it, and those were non-pivotal given the ones before them, and so on back to the first — yet the flock exists and the whole chain produced it. It seems I can be told, truly, of every link that it made no difference, and of the chain that it made all the difference, and I don't know whether that is a quiet contradiction between (5) and (7) or a perfectly consistent pair of claims that my argument illicitly treats as inconsistent. The repair also seems to exculpate where the answer is obvious: the plant worker's shift quota is fixed by contract and will be met by whoever is on the line, so by (2) no one's stint makes any bird worse off, and by (1) nothing that happens in the slaughterhouse is objectionable.

So: which step do you reject, why does it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? I would like to keep (2) and (3) if I can — the counterfactual test and the empirical claim about lumpiness both strike me as obviously right — and I would rather give up (1), or block the passage from (5) and (6) to (7), than touch either. But I want to know what I would be buying. If I give up the difference principle, what replaces it, does the replacement convict a great deal of ordinary behaviour it shouldn't, and does it still explain why the plant worker's case differs from the shopper's, if it does? If instead the trouble is a quantifier shift or an equivocation inside (2) — on what gets held fixed, or on whether the relevant difference is actual, expected, or something else — I want the step named precisely. And whatever you propose, I would like the three standard sorts of response — the expected-difference consequentialist one, the complicity or participation one, and any group-act or threshold view you find strongest — each priced: what each has to concede, and what each has to explain away. One constraint on the answer: it should say the same thing about the vote and the tank of petrol as it does about the chicken, unless you can tell me in virtue of what those cases come apart.
