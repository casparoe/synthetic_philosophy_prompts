I have been asked to give a fifty-minute guest session on tangled hierarchies and what the strange-loops literature calls the inviolate level. The audience is about twenty first-year students in a general-education writing seminar: bright, but with no logic or mathematics beyond high school. I will have a projector and a large reproduction of Escher's Drawing Hands, and I will not be able to hand anything out.

Two complications. First, they were assigned a short popular video before my session which argues that the self is a "strange loop" and that this is what consciousness consists in; several will arrive already believing that a loop in a hierarchy is supposed to generate a self from below, and that this is the point of the picture. Second, I want to teach this without any formal apparatus: the moment the session turns into a lesson about formal systems or incompleteness, I have lost them.

What I need is a plan for the explanation, not the explanation itself.

1. The order in which the components must be introduced, and what each depends on that earlier steps have already secured, so that for every move I can say why it cannot come earlier. As I currently see it there are at least four things to put in place: what a level is at all (a domain, and a discourse about that domain); what it is for one level to be about another, and for the ordering to forbid a level from taking itself as its subject; what happens when something does apply to itself; and then the tangled case. I may be missing a prerequisite and I may have the dependencies wrong.

2. The example that should carry the session, what it must contain and what it must not, and whether it works better as the object we start from or the object we arrive at. I want students to leave able to keep three things apart: (a) a thing's merely referring to itself; (b) a hierarchy's being tangled, in that something on a lower level reaches back up and acts on the level above it; (c) a tangle's having an inviolate level, which I have been glossing as the level the loop cannot reach or alter, and which is therefore not paralyzed by it. My instinct is that the print gives (b) vividly and only borrows (c) from somewhere outside the frame, so that the session needs a second, smaller example — a sentence, a rule, or a machine — in which the inviolate level is visible inside the system rather than supplied by the viewer. Complicating this: I have also seen it said that the inviolate level lies not above the loop but beneath or behind it, which I cannot square with my own gloss. Tell me which is right and correct my gloss if it is wrong, because where I locate that level on the picture changes the whole shape of the session.

3. The misconceptions to expect, when each will form if I do not intervene, and where in the sequence to head each off. The ones I anticipate are: that a loop in a hierarchy is just circular reasoning and therefore a mistake; that anything referring to itself must be paradoxical, so that the picture depicts a contradiction; that the levels are literally stacked somewhere; that "tangled" means an infinite regress of ever-higher levels; and that the outer level is the real or fundamental one, so the loop is an illusion. I suspect some of these matter less than others I have not thought of, including ones the video will have planted.

4. One thing I should openly refuse to explain at this level, and say so, plus where to draw the line around the consciousness claim, since half the room will think that is what the topic is.

5. A single closing question that would show me they have the distinction between (a), (b) and (c) and not merely the vocabulary.
