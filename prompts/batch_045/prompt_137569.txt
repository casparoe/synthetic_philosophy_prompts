I'm in a graduate seminar on rational choice, and after we worked through the St. Petersburg game I got stuck on the harder case where the expectation is not merely infinite but undefined. I've drafted the central argument of a short paper, and my study group was split on whether the main move is a real advance or just a relabelling of the problem, so I'd like an independent second opinion. I'm sending the argument in the form of numbered claims with the computations attached, because what I most want checked is the mathematics and the logic, not the prose.

The case. A fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads. If the first heads is on toss n you receive $2^n/n when n is odd and you pay $2^n/n when n is even. Since the probability of first heads on toss n is 2^{-n}, the contribution of the nth outcome to the expected payoff is (-1)^{n+1}/n, so the natural summation gives 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - ... = ln 2 dollars. But the sum of the positive contributions and the sum of the negative contributions both diverge, so the expectation in the standard (Lebesgue) sense is undefined: it is E[X+] - E[X-] with both terms infinite. Worse, the series is only conditionally convergent, so by the Riemann rearrangement theorem any real number, or divergence, can be produced by summing the very same outcomes in a different order. And since a relabelling of the atoms of the outcome space leaves the probability distribution over payoffs untouched, I take this to show that no ordering-sensitive summation procedure can be a functional of the gamble at all. So ln 2 is not the expectation of this game; there is no expectation. Call this the orthodox verdict.

My proposal. Define the clamped value V(X) as the limit, as M goes to infinity, of the expectation of the game in which payoffs and losses are capped at M dollars: V(X) = lim_M E[max(-M, min(X, M))]. Three observations. (i) Each E[capped at M] is an ordinary expectation of a bounded variable, so it does not depend on any ordering or labelling of outcomes, and V is therefore permutation-invariant whenever the limit exists; V is also monotone in X, and where the ordinary expectation exists (finite E|X|) dominated convergence gives V(X) = E(X), so V extends expectation rather than replacing it. (ii) For the game above, V = ln 2: let N(M) be the largest n with 2^n/n at most M, note that the outcomes with n at most N(M) contribute the partial alternating harmonic sum, which tends to ln 2, and that the remaining outcomes contribute at most M times 2^{-N(M)} in absolute value, which is bounded by 2/(N(M)+1) and so vanishes. (iii) Truncating the game itself rather than the payoffs -- stopping after N tosses and paying nothing if no heads has appeared -- gives the same limit, ln 2.

Where I get unsure. First, additivity. For two independent plays I get 2 ln 2 both when I truncate each play separately at N tosses (the sum is 2 times the partial alternating harmonic sum to N times (1 - 2^{-N})) and when I truncate by the total number of tosses across both plays, n1 + n2 at most K, where the calculation gives 2 times the partial sum to K-1 plus a correction term of order 1/(K-1). But I could not settle whether V itself is additive here, i.e. whether capping the sum of two independent plays at plus or minus M dollars and letting M grow also gives 2 ln 2. My sketch is that the payoff magnitudes 2^n/n are separated by a factor close to 2 (for n at least 2, consecutive magnitudes stand in the ratio n/(2n-2)), so no single huge term can be cancelled by any other, which confines the region where the total payoff is at most M in absolute value between two square truncation regions whose levels differ by about two tosses; since the contribution of a single shell of the square truncation tends to zero, the joint capping should converge to the same 2 ln 2. Is that argument sound? If V is not additive in general, how much does that cost -- is additivity the property that was doing the real work in wanting a value in the first place?

Second, the long-run worry. One of my readers objected that ln 2 cannot be what you get on average, because in repeated independent plays the sample mean does not settle down to ln 2 at all: E|X| is infinite, so the average of the absolute payoffs diverges, and the signed average looks like a difference of two quantities that both blow up. I want to claim that this objection is correct but toothless -- that V was never meant to be a long-run average, any more than a capped-and-limited gamble is meant to be played forever -- but I am not confident, and I would like to know whether the long-run behaviour gives an independent reason to distrust V or merely shows that no single number deserves the name "value" here.

Third, scope. I believe both limits fail to exist for the variant in which the payoff is (-1)^{n+1} 2^n with no division by n (every outcome contributes plus or minus 1 to the series), and I would like that confirmed or corrected; more generally, I would like to know whether there are games for which the payoff-capping limit exists while the game-truncation limit does not, or vice versa, since my paper's thesis is that the two procedures agreeing is evidence that they are tracking something real about the game's structure rather than about a preferred ordering.

What I'd like from you: tell me which of these computations is wrong, which step in the argument is the weakest link, and whether the thesis -- that the clamped value is permutation-invariant and strictly extends expectation, and that this dissolves the orthodox verdict that such games have no usable value -- survives the long-run and additivity worries. Please also tell me if you think the whole thing collapses into something trivial, or into a view that is already known to fail, and say what the strongest objection I have not considered would be. Don't just restate my argument back to me in better prose; I specifically want the places where it breaks.
