I have just come out of a long argument with a friend who does interpretability work, about whether my team may write, in a pre-deployment evaluation report, that a system deceived the evaluator. She argued from the chess-playing computer that Dennett makes the canonical case for the intentional stance: you cannot predict its next move without attributing it the goal of winning and beliefs about the board, so the stance is forced. Language models, she said, are no different — the only tractable way to predict what one will say is to credit it with beliefs and wants, so claims like 'the model believes the Eiffel Tower is in Rome' or 'it said that to keep the user's approval' are as good as belief attributions ever get, and deception is a literal finding rather than a metaphor.

When I said that what seemed to me to be doing the work in the chess case was a designer who fixed the goal, a game with a determinate win condition, and a machine good enough at it that 'rational agent' yields reliable predictions, she replied that none of that is load-bearing: the pattern is all there is. She then added a second picture, which I suspect is a different claim wearing the first one's clothes — that a model is a compressed parliament of every agent whose text is in the corpus, so an attribution lands on whichever faction currently has the floor.

I don't want a verdict on whether AI systems have beliefs; I want the analogy audited. Lay out the correspondence table for the chess case — board, rules, win condition, designer, assumption of optimality, and the lower stances the intentional stance is contrasted with — then the places the mapping to a language model fails, and for each failure whether it matters for the use she is making of it. Three I especially want addressed: whether 'nothing else predicts better' is even true of language models, given how much predictive work is done by next-token statistics and the training distribution; whether the pattern the stance latches onto in the chess case is objective in a way a model's beliefs are not, given that which belief you can attribute shifts with how you prompt it; and whether the parliament picture extends the chess analogy or quietly substitutes for it. Then say whether the conclusion survives — may the report call it deception, or was the analogy carrying that — and if the chess computer is the wrong source, what better one there is.

Finally, be explicit about where your audit is most likely to be wrong: say what finding about a model — a belief that persists across framings and gets acted on, a case where attribution beats statistics at prediction — would move you, and what would not.
