I lead the discussion sections for a graduate seminar on rational choice, and the lecturer has a habit of handing out a case each week and then telling us, at the end, what he takes the correct choice to be. I have to lead the section on next week's case the day after he reveals his answer, so I would like to work out in advance both which way he will go and what rule he is using. I am not asking what you think is right; I want his verdict, and the rule that generates it.

His verdicts so far, as exactly as I can report them:

Newcomb's problem with a predictor who is right about nine times in ten: take only the opaque box.

The smoking lesion, in the version where a lesion causes both the desire to smoke and the cancer, smoking itself makes no difference to the cancer, and you know that you currently want to smoke: smoke.

The prisoner's dilemma against an exact physical duplicate of yourself, facing the same choice with no possibility of communication: stay silent.

Death in Damascus, with a Death who is right about where you will be nine times in ten: stay in Damascus, on the ground that the odds of survival are the same either way and so the cost of travelling settles it.

Next week's case is one he wrote himself, a variant of the fourth. Death is in Damascus and announces, as before, that he will come for you tomorrow and will be in whichever city you are in. This time, however, he is not predicting you at all: he travels to the city named in a horoscope cast at your birth and locked in his desk, and simply follows it. Nothing you decide now can affect what the horoscope says, and the horoscope exerts no influence on you; the two coincide only because both are consequences of the same facts about your birth. The frequencies, over a hundred cases of your kind, are these: the horoscope names Damascus in seventy; whenever it names Damascus you do in fact stay; of the thirty in which it names Aleppo, you flee in twenty-four and stay in six. You know these frequencies but not what your own horoscope says. Fleeing to Aleppo costs you a small amount, and being alive in Damascus is worth slightly more to you than being alive in Aleppo.

What I want from you:

The rule he seems to be running, stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself to a further case, together with any other rules that fit all four earlier verdicts equally well. I assume there is more than one, and I would rather have all of them than the one you prefer.

What each candidate rule says about the new case, and which single stipulation in it is doing the work, in the sense that changing that stipulation would bring the candidates back into agreement.

Your best guess at his verdict, with your reason for preferring it over the alternatives, and an indication of how confident I should be.

One further variant, ideally a change to a single stipulation of this case, that would separate whichever candidates still agree here, together with what each of them would then say.

Two doubts of my own that I would like settled: whether a rule that insists on probabilities independent of the agent's own act can legitimately use the seventy per cent at all, given that your staying in Damascus is itself evidence about what the horoscope says; and whether the travel cost does any real work for any of the candidate rules, or is decoration.
