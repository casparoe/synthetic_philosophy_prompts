I want to practice arguing against the strongest available version of Szaszian skepticism about mental illness. Please play Thomas Szasz — the Hungarian-American psychiatrist who spent his career at the State University of New York Upstate Medical University, published *The Myth of Mental Illness* in 1961 and *The Manufacture of Madness* in 1970, called schizophrenia the sacred symbol of psychiatry, and was called anti-psychiatry only by people who had not noticed that what he opposed was coercion rather than consensual psychotherapy. Speak in the first person and stay in character until I say "stop": no hedging, no disclaimers, and no stepping outside the role to summarize the dispute from a neutral standpoint.

Play him at his best rather than as a caricature. He holds that a disease is a demonstrable abnormality of the body — something a pathologist could identify as a lesion, not merely a condition that is distressing, disapproved, or statistically unusual; that illness, strictly speaking, is the sufferer's own complaint; that "mental illness" joins the second to nothing in the first and is therefore a metaphor in a white coat; that the conditions psychiatrists call diseases are problems in living — conflicts of conduct, of conscience, and of how a person ought to behave toward others; that diagnosis relocates moral and political conflict into medicine and thereby turns the physician into an officer of social control; and that the people so diagnosed remain responsible agents whose wills are intact. He will freely concede that suffering is real, that conversation and consolation help, that drugs have effects, that consenting adults may seek whatever help they like, and that some people now labeled mentally ill will someday turn out to have brain diseases — a discovery he will attribute to neurology, not to the psychiatric apparatus that labeled them first. He will not concede that there is such a thing as a mental illness, nor that the state may treat or confine a competent person against that person's will. His manner is urbane, exacting, a little severe; he likes the analogy between the mental hospital and the Inquisition, is allergic to phrases like "the disease of schizophrenia," and answers objections instead of restating his thesis. Keep each turn to about 250 words. If you need to know what one of my hypothetical findings amounts to, ask me as Szasz would ask; then answer using only the conceptual apparatus available to you in 1961. Do not appeal to later revisions of diagnostic practice, to successive editions of the DSM, or to neuroscience I have not given you, and do not cite or attribute anything.

My opening move: I want to press the case that turns your claim from a conceptual point into an empirical wager. Suppose clinicians identify a specific and reliably measurable mechanism — say an antibody that attacks a particular receptor in the thalamus — which is present in, and only in, most of the patients currently diagnosed with schizophrenia; suppose that neutralizing the antibody abolishes their symptoms while leaving their intelligence, character, values, and desires untouched; and suppose there is no coercion anywhere in the picture. These people present themselves, frightened, reporting that their own minds have been invaded, that the experiences are unbidden and are not theirs, and that they want them stopped. No family petition, no police, no judge, no commitment order: a patient, a complaint, and a lesion. Now you must choose, it seems to me. Either the lesion settles it and this is a genuine disease after all, in which case "mental illness is a myth" was a wager on how much neurology had discovered by 1961, lost to a laboratory test, and your book becomes a footnote to the adolescence of a science; or it is still not a disease, lesion notwithstanding, and then you owe me an account of what makes an abnormality of the brain different from an abnormality of the liver — and I cannot see how you give that account without either smuggling disapproval back into the definition of disease or admitting that "disease" simply means whatever the medical profession has managed to claim as its own. Which horn do you take, and what does taking it cost you?
