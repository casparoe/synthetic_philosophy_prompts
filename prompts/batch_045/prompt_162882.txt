I've been helping to run a discussion section on scarce-resource allocation — the kind of material that starts from the old lifeboat setup and then gets extended to rich-world obligations toward people in the water — and I keep noticing that my gut verdicts on variants are confident, numerous, and hard to fit together. Before the term ends I would like to write down the principle I am implicitly using, if there is one. I would much rather be told that one of my verdicts has to go than be handed a principle that saves all of them by growing a clause for each case.

Everything below is a variation on one setup: a lifeboat built for nine, ten people aboard, the boat will swamp within twenty minutes unless the load is reduced, and rescue is expected in about three hours.

(a) Ten adults, relevantly similar, nobody volunteers. My verdict: someone may be put over the side; letting all ten drown is not morally required. But it has to be settled by lottery — the strongest person may not simply choose, and I do not think the captain's authority changes anything. I also think the person selected would be within their rights to fight back, which I realize sits badly with my saying the rest may put them over.

(b) One of the ten is the crewman whose negligence sank the ship. No lottery; he goes. And here I do think he may be overpowered if he resists, which I do not think in (a).

(c) One of the ten is unconscious and, on the best clinical assessment available, will die within two hours whatever anyone does. Choosing him is not just permissible but clearly preferable to choosing anyone else; in fact this is the only case where I think a lottery would be wrong.

(d) One of the ten is seventy-two and can statistically expect perhaps ten more years of life against the others' forty or fifty. My verdict: that is not an admissible basis for selecting him — and I hold this even though the thought I keep reaching for in both (c) and (d) is the same thought, that he has less left to lose. The actuarial version, where all we know is that he belongs to a group with lower average life expectancy, I reject even more firmly.

(e) An able-bodied person with no dependants calmly volunteers to go over the side. The offer may be accepted. I also think, less comfortably, that it may be accepted even when the volunteer is the only person aboard who can navigate, so that accepting it probably dooms the other nine.

(f) A different possibility: nobody goes over the side, everyone bails and lashes themselves to the gunwales, and each person then faces roughly a fifteen percent chance of drowning. I prefer this to putting one person over, even though the expected number of deaths is higher.

(g) The same option, but each person faces roughly forty-five percent. Here I would put someone over the side after all — and I cannot say where between fifteen and forty-five the switch happens.

(h) Outside the boat: with room and rations for ten more, swimmers in the water must be hauled aboard. If the boat's own crew recklessly caused the collision, they must take them in even at real cost to themselves, and if there is not room, the culpable crew go over the side before any swimmer is refused. If the boat is genuinely full, refusing swimmers is permissible. But if the crew deliberately keep two places empty and heave the spare rations overboard so that no one can later say there was room, my verdict is that they have not thereby acquired the permission they would have had if the boat really were full.

My own tentative formulation is this: every person aboard has an equal claim to a place; that claim can be defeated by the person's own consent, or by their responsibility for the emergency; and where claims are equal, the selection must be made by a fair procedure rather than by any judgment about whose life is worth more. Please test that against (a) through (h) and repair it where it breaks. I want: a small set of candidate principles stated precisely, with the case that forces each clause; where a candidate and one of my verdicts conflict, a recommendation about which to give up and what giving it up costs me elsewhere; verdicts on cases I have not listed — in particular on the threshold in (g), on the case I keep changing my mind about (one of the ten is a six-year-old child, and I cannot decide whether excluding her is obviously right or is exactly the kind of comparative judgment I rejected in (d)), and on at least one case of your own construction; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts no clean principle can accommodate.
