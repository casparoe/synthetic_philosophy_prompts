I'm in a small philosophy-of-economics reading group — me, one philosopher, and two econ PhD students who mostly roll their eyes at us — and we've now spent three sessions on the old row about whether the realism of a model's assumptions matters, and I genuinely cant tell whether we're disagreeing about anything. Here's the shape of it as I see it. One side, descending from Friedman's 1953 essay on positive economics, says a theory is to be judged only by whether its predictions work; that the assumptions of a good theory will typically be wildly false, because the whole trick is to throw away almost everything to isolate the few forces that dominate for some class of phenomena; and that asking whether firms 'really' maximise is like asking whether a falling body 'really' is a point mass — the interesting theories are the ones whose assumptions are the least realistic. The other side says this turns on an equivocation about what an assumption is, that the famous move where the significance of a theory is supposed to explain the unreality of its assumptions conflates several quite different things, and that predictive success is cheap: a model can track the data for reasons that have nothing to do with its being even approximately right about the world, which matters enormously the moment you use it to do something rather than to retrodict a series.

My working hypothesis is that this is not one dispute but about four, and that it stalls because nobody says which job the model is being used for. I've been playing with a taxonomy of my own, with my own labels so nobody can accuse me of stealing them: (1) negligibility assumptions — the assumption says a factor is small enough to ignore, it's statable as a limit claim (viscosity going to zero, molecular volume going to zero), and there's a well-defined direction in which you could de-idealise and check; (2) scaffolding assumptions — the assumption isn't a claim about the target at all but a device you need in order to have a tractable model space, e.g. a continuum of traders, an infinite horizon, every agent knowing the model; there may be no sensible limit to take, because relaxing it gives you a different kind of object rather than the real system; (3) stand-in assumptions — you put something in that you know is false because you don't know the true mechanism, and here the critics seem plainly right that the model's predictive success is no evidence for the stand-in; (4) domain assumptions — the model is really a conditional, 'in systems where X holds', so the live question is a matching question about whether the actual system is near enough to such a world, not a truth question about X. On top of that, a second hypothesis: both sides quietly test models on within-sample prediction, and the instrumentalist line earns its keep for interpolation but goes silent — or actively misleads — when the model is used to extrapolate to a new regime, or to evaluate a policy that changes the very thing the assumption held fixed. So maybe the real hidden disagreement is about which predictive task is at stake, or maybe it's bedrock, a difference about what theories are even for: saving the phenomena versus latching onto the causes.

So: is this a substantive disagreement, three or four verbal ones under one heading, two camps answering different questions, or a difference about what counts as evidence for a model that nobody has made explicit? Is my four-way taxonomy doing any work, or is it just relabelling the familiar points? I'd like you to run it against four cases I care about and tell me where it breaks: a zero-viscosity / frictionless-plane idealisation in a mechanics derivation; a one-immortal-consumer representative-agent macro model; a continuum-of-agents assumption in a market-clearing model; and a firms-maximise-profits postulate used to predict outside the regime it was fitted in.
