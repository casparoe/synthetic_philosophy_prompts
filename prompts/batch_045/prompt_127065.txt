I am writing a novel, set inside an imaginary bureaucracy, and I need one of its internal documents to do genuine philosophical work without ever announcing that it is doing so.

The setting: my narrator, a junior archivist, finds pinned to a noticeboard a departmental instrument governing who may be said to occupy an office of state. The Administration's doctrine is called Continuity of Office. An office is defined entirely by what it does — what it receives, how its internal states move it from one response to the next, and what it is meant to bring about in other offices and in the public. Provided that pattern is preserved, the office is held to be the same office, whatever it is made of. In the world of the novel, offices have lately been occupied by a Standing Committee, by a clockwork assembly, by a trained flock of pigeons, and — in one contested and still-undecided case — by a stretch of coastline.

What I want from you is the text of that instrument: a parody of a statutory instrument or standard-form contract of employment, in deadpan officialese, 700–900 words, arranged as numbered clauses, with a title, an invented department, and a date. It must read as mundane paperwork. My narrator should be unsettled by its ordinariness, not instructed by it; the comedy is bureaucratic, and nothing in the voice should wink.

The jokes have to come from getting the position right, so a philosopher of mind laughs rather than winces. I would like the clauses to land — without ever naming them — the standard objections to defining a role this way: that a specification loose enough to permit many different realisations ends up permitting almost anything, including things nobody would count as doing the job at all; that a specification tight enough to capture how the present occupant actually manages the work means nothing else could ever qualify, which the Administration plainly cannot afford to concede either; that the document never makes clear whose purposes fix which description of the job is the correct one; and that the Department is wholly indifferent to whether, so to speak, anyone is at home in the office, so long as the outputs arrive on schedule. One clause should deal with a claimant who satisfies every requirement and yet reports that the work now feels nothing like it used to, and the Department should dispose of that complaint in a way that is evidently unsatisfactory.

Constraints: no jargon of the trade — the terms functionalism, multiple realizability, supervenience, realiser and qualia must never appear, and the piece should not read as a seminar paper in costume. Invented case names, forms, cross-references and one or two marginal annotations in a clerk's hand are very welcome. Please close with a short "Note for Applicants", a single paragraph in a blunter tone, telling claimants how to establish that they are the same office. It should be legible as satire to a general reader, and fair enough that someone who actually holds the view being mocked recognises it as their own rather than a straw version of it.
