I sit on the Charter Review Commission of a home-rule city of roughly eighty thousand residents. We meet monthly and will table our final recommendations to the city council in March; one contested item remains, and it is the one the council is waiting on: whether to extend the municipal franchise to non-citizen residents. That population is not marginal here. It includes green-card holders who have lived in the city for decades and simply never naturalized, visa holders with work authorization whose stays are open-ended, and residents without status. All of them work here, pay local sales and property taxes through rent, send children to our schools, use our transit, and are subject to our police, housing, and code enforcement.

Three features of our situation keep the standard arguments from settling it, and I want the memo to work through them rather than around them.

First, the commission has already passed two related amendments on preliminary votes: lowering the municipal voting age to sixteen, and making participation in municipal elections formally compulsory for registered voters, enforced by a small civil fine, with an explicit abstention option on every ballot so that no one is compelled to express a preference. So the live question is not merely whether non-citizen residents may vote, but whether they may be *required* to. Compulsory voting is usually defended as a duty members owe to their own political community; imposed on people who have not naturalized, some of whom are not permitted to, it starts to look like conscription into an obligation they never accepted. Yet exempting them appears to create a second, lesser tier of residence.

Second, our current charter already enfranchises a group the popular all-affected-interests argument handles badly: non-resident owners of commercial property, who vote in downtown improvement district elections while living in the suburbs. Any principle we adopt should say something about them too, since a principle that saves our existing practice only by accident is not a principle.

Third, the newly enfranchised are not a single kind of case. Some have declined membership that was available to them; some have membership withheld from them; some cannot register without creating a paper trail that exposes them to removal, so that a right they may only exercise at legal risk is close to no right at all.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to the commission and then to the council. It should open with a bottom line — adopt, adopt with conditions, reject, or defer with a specific charge — together with a time frame and an implementation path. It should then state the principle it takes to govern who votes in a city, and apply that principle consistently across the groups in play: sixteen-year-olds, citizen absentees, non-citizen residents of the three kinds above, and non-resident commercial property owners. It should give the strongest version of the objection on the losing side and explain why it nonetheless loses: I have in mind the circularity worry that any existing electorate decides who counts as the people, so that an appeal to what the people want cannot ground a change in who the people are. It should also say what we should do about the compulsory-voting amendment as applied to anyone newly enfranchised, and how the recommendation should be revised if the state legislature pre-empts home-rule control of the franchise.

Two constraints. Do not settle the question by citing turnout figures, demographic projections, or what other cities or countries have done: we will not have reliable local data before March, and comparative practice is another subcommittee's assignment. What we need is the argument, made at a level the council can vote on and the public can answer.
