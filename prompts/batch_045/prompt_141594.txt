My reading group is doing a few weeks on classical Indian epistemology and I brought in an argument I've been chewing on for a while, and now I think it's broken but I can't find the break.

Quick setup so you don't have to know the background. In Nyāya, a pramāṇa is a source of pramā — a genuine piece of knowledge, a cognition that gets its object right (seeing the shell as a shell, not seeing the shell as silver). The standard list has four: perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), comparison (upamāna), and testimony (śabda). And there's a big dispute about where a cognition's validity (prāmāṇya) comes from: Nyāya says validity is extrinsic (parataḥ) — a cognition is knowledge because of something other than itself, an excellence (guṇa) in the causal source that produced it — while the Mīmāṃsā school says validity is intrinsic (svataḥ), cognitions are valid on their own, and it's invalidity that needs an outside explanation. Nyāya actually holds that *both* validity and invalidity are extrinsic.

Here's my argument.

(1) For a cognition produced by source S to be knowledge, it isn't enough that the cognition is true and that S produced it. S has to actually be a reliable producer — a real pramāṇa, not a broken one. A damaged eye, a lying witness, a thermometer with a bias: those produce cognitions too.
(2) Nyāya agrees in spirit: prāmāṇya is extrinsic. What makes a cognition knowledge is the excellence of its causal source, not the fact that it just showed up in your head. Validity isn't self-declaring.
(3) So, before a cognition from S can count as knowledge, the excellence of S has to be established — by the subject, or at least in principle. Otherwise a true cognition from a defective source counts as knowledge and the whole distinction between pramā and a lucky guess collapses.
(4) Whatever establishes S's excellence is itself a cognition produced by some further source S′ — normally an inference from S's track record, or from the fact that acting on S's outputs has worked out.
(5) By (2), that cognition needs its own source's excellence established, so S″, and so on. That's the classic infinite regress (anavasthā). No chain ever completes, so (3) is never satisfied, so nothing is ever knowledge.
(6) And note this doesn't just kill inference and testimony. It kills perception too, which Nyāya treats as the most basic pramāṇa of all: my visual cognition of the coffee mug needs the excellence of my eyes established, which needs an inference, which needs its own source established... The baseline case fails along with everything else.
(7) But obviously we do know things, including by perception. So something in (1)–(3) has to go.

The two escapes I've been offered both feel wrong to me.

(8) Escape one: the regress only gets started when there's a live doubt; absent doubt, no certification is demanded, and ordinary cognitions are certified by successful action — you reach for the water and you get water. But the skeptic's whole move is to *make* the doubt live, and "no doubt, no need" seems to make whether you know depend on your temperament: the confident person knows, the anxious one doesn't. That looks like it proves too much in the other direction.
(9) Escape two: go Mīmāṃsā, make validity intrinsic and only invalidity extrinsic. But then the shell-as-silver cognition is as good as knowledge until something corrects it, and the correction is itself a cognition that's valid on its own — so I don't see how error is ever coherently *detected* on that picture, and Nyāya's insistence that invalidity is also extrinsic starts to look like the more honest half of the view.

A friend said this is just the Agrippan regress wearing Indian names and the answer is reliabilism: S can be reliable without the subject ever establishing that it is, and that's all knowledge requires. That feels close, because it makes me suspect (3) is where I smuggled in an epistemic demand — the subject must have checked — inside a premise that sounds like it's only about what *makes* a cognition valid. But if that's the fix, I don't see what the Nyāya versus Mīmāṃsā fight was even about in the first place, since on that reading everyone should just be an externalist and go home.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you? If I have to choose I'd much rather keep (1) and (7) than (3), but I can't see how to drop (3) without letting a stopped clock count as a pramāṇa whenever it happens to be right. Say which premise looked fine and why, and what I'm paying if I give it up.
