Sanity check on something I keep going back and forth on. In Bertrand's chord problem, all three of the textbook answers come from measures on the space of chords that are invariant under exactly the symmetries the problem statement itself hands you — rotations about the centre — and they differ only in the density they put on the distance b from the centre (flat, proportional to b, proportional to 1 over the square root of 1 minus b squared). There are really infinitely many such measures; the "three answers" framing is just an artifact of three nice parametrizations. The invariance reply says: use the full Euclidean group instead, and only one measure extends to a translation-invariant measure on all lines, which forces b uniform and gives a half — but you only get there by conditioning an infinite measure on the event that the line meets the disk.

Now redo the problem on a sphere: a cap of angular radius rho, chords = geodesic arcs with endpoints on the cap's boundary. Here the space of great circles is compact and carries a unique rotation-invariant probability measure, so the invariance reply is at its absolute best — no infinite measure, no dodgy conditioning, just conditionalize the maximally symmetric measure on the great circle's meeting the cap. And the natural procedural answer (two uniform points on the boundary, joined by the geodesic through them) still disagrees with it, even in the small-cap limit where the geometry goes flat agian.

Compare the two readings for me: "symmetry fixes the prior" versus "the question is incomplete until someone hands you an actual randomizer." Does the spherical version adjudicate between them at all? My sense is that it shows the disagreement isn't an artifact of non-compactness or of conditioning infinite measures, but I want the strongest version of each side once neither can gesture at infinity.
