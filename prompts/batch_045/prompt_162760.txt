You are a philosopher of mind and cognitive scientist who also publishes literary short fiction in the tradition of Borges, Lem, and Ted Chiang. You know Hofstadter's strange-loop account of the self well, and you write fiction that takes a philosophical position without ever lecturing the reader about it. Take your time: think through the crux below before you write anything, and do not settle for the first setup that occurs to you.

The crux your story has to dramatize.

Hofstadter's account: in Gödel, Escher, Bach (1979) and especially in I Am a Strange Loop (2007), a self is not an object hidden inside a brain but a pattern that arises when a representational system becomes tangled enough to contain a symbol that stands for the very system that contains it. The symbol he calls the self-symbol — the thing written as I — is peculiar because its referent is not given from outside. The system's own activity of producing and acting on that symbol is what makes the symbol true of something. The loop is self-supporting: the symbol refers to the system, the system is what it is partly in virtue of carrying the symbol, and neither end of the relation is anchored independently of the other. On this view the self is a kind of mirage that is nonetheless real, in the way a vortex is real while no single water molecule is the vortex.

The objection — call it the hollow-I objection — grants all of this and says it is not enough for reference. Since the loop anchors the symbol only from the inside, nothing in the story the system tells picks out one candidate rather than another among the many systems that equally satisfy the loop's own descriptions. Three cases:

1. The silent passenger. Suppose the self-symbol's causal work in a system is entirely downstream of some subsystem that does the deciding, and the self-representations make no difference to what happens. Then the loop is complete and the system says I, but the pattern that does the referring is not the pattern that acts. Which of these is the referent?
2. The branch. A system's self-symbol survives a duplication: two successors inherit the same self-model, the same memories, and the same habit of saying I, and both go on to say I was the one who went through the duplication. There is nothing internal to either successor that breaks the tie, and the loop cannot supply the missing fact of which one the original's I denoted.
3. The successor-in-simulation. A description of the system complete enough to run it, run somewhere else, produces a system that satisfies every internal characterization the loop supplies. If the loop alone fixed the referent, the I in the original and the I in the simulation are the same I — which no one who uses I believes.

The obvious reply is that I is a pure indexical: referents of indexicals are fixed by the act of utterance, so the I denotes whoever is producing this token, and questions about duplication have the same status as questions about which of two speakers said I. The objector's answer is that indexicality does not do the work by itself — an indexical picks out the speaker of the context, and what makes someone the speaker of a context is an external fact, not something the utterance can bootstrap. So the loop needs one small thing it cannot supply from inside: a fact about which system's activity is the one doing the referring.

What I want from you.

Write a short story of roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words that puts the reader inside this dispute and resolves it dramatically. Constraints:

- No exposition dumps. The philosophical content must be carried by incident, scene, and consequence. You may spend at most one short paragraph on anything resembling theory, and preferably none.
- Do not use the technical vocabulary inside the fiction. The words strange loop, self-symbol, indexical, qualia, and the names of any philosophers or works must not appear in the story itself; a reader who knew nothing of the debate should still feel the problem.
- The story must itself contain a genuine self-referential turn, not just characters using the first person. Something in the text must refer back to the text's own act of being produced, or to the reader's act of reading it, in a way that changes what an earlier passage meant — a document that turns out to be part of what it describes, a narration whose provenance is revealed late, a final sentence that can only be understood by returning to the first. Make the loop structural, not decorative.
- Give the machinery a concrete, non-magical failure mode. Whatever system your characters work with should have a diagnosable, mundane pathology — a token that drifts, a copy that inherits more or less than it should, a report that changes what it reports on. No mysticism, no revelation of a hidden soul-substance.
- The ending must take a stand. It should be possible, after the last paragraph, to say whether the story treats the hollow-I objection as answered or as fatal — and that verdict must be delivered by what happens, not by a character summarizing it.
- Avoid the well-worn props: no rogue AI, no murder, no it-was-all-a-simulation reveal, no dead-wife reveal, no last-minute twist that undoes the story's own premises.

Process and format.

First, in three entries of about sixty words each, sketch three different setups — different settings, different characters, different mechanisms — and in one sentence say which you are choosing and why it best carries the crux. Then write the story. Then add an afterword of at most 150 words stating what the ending commits you to and what the strongest thing someone on the other side would say about that commitment.
