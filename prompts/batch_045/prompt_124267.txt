I am putting together a graduate seminar on computational theories of mind for next term, and I have been re-reading von Neumann's short posthumously published book drawn from the Silliman lectures (Yale, 1958) — partly because a colleague in the engineering school told me it was little more than a period piece, and partly because I have come to think it is not. The position I find myself defending is an extension of what I take him to have argued, so let me state it carefully before asking what would break it. As I read him, the nervous system is not simply a slow, noisy digital machine: its elementary operations are sluggish and individually imprecise compared with engineered switching elements, and it compensates by sheer parallelism and redundancy, which is why its computation is broad and shallow per unit time in a way that a serial machine's is not, and why its internal code should not be expected to resemble the language of formal logic. To this I add a generalization of my own, which I have been calling the reliability-deficit principle: whenever a physically realized system must meet a demanding reliability target while its components are too costly — in energy, area, or time — to spend that cost on error-free discrete switching, the system will end up exploiting the substrate's continuous dynamics as computation, not merely as implementation. On this reading, the mixed analog-digital character of the brain is not a biological accident to be engineered away; it is forced on any cognizer built close to physical limits, including future silicon or neuromorphic ones, and a system that is cleanly digital all the way down, with the analog physics doing nothing but realizing discrete states, cannot be the whole story of how cognition is physically done.

What I want from you is an honest map of this position's exposure. Rank, from fatal to merely awkward, what would have to be true for it to be false, and separate what a defender of the view should concede as counting against it from what such a defender would reasonably dismiss as irrelevant or as attacking a strawman. I am aware of the pressure points and want you to press them rather than list them: networks trained and run at very low numerical precision work surprisingly well; digital accelerators are themselves massively parallel; and, most awkwardly, every physical implementation of a digital machine is continuous underneath, which makes me worry that my principle says either something substantive and false or something true and empty. Tell me which empirical findings would discriminate — what does my view predict that a rival, say a view on which the algorithmic level is autonomous and the substrate only supplies speed and energy, does not predict? I would be most moved by either a demonstration that degrading analog precision in a working system degrades cognition no differently than degrading discrete symbol fidelity does, or by a case in which serial, high-precision, purely symbolic computation reproduces the functional profile of cognition at a comparable energy budget. Please also include the developments that have arguably already happened, and give me your candid assessment of whether the principle has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if it has, whether that is a defect for a claim of this kind or just a sign that it belongs to the philosophy of science rather than to neuroscience.

A few practical requests. Write in American English, and do not spend your words on citations, references, or suggestions for further reading; I can chase those down myself and I would rather have the argument. Finally, I want the response to be calibrated: tell me how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one point you are least sure of — especially where you are extrapolating about neuroscience or hardware beyond what is actually established — since I will be presenting some of this to students and would prefer not to be caught out by my own overconfidence.
