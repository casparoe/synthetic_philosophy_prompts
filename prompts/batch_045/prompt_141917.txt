My two flatmates got into aproperly long argument after a news story a few nights ago — I said I'd stay out of it, but its been bugging me since and I actually want to know who was talking sense. The story: an elderly woman died in a fire in a tower block. The valve on the sprinkler riser had been closed by a contractor doing a repair three weeks earlier and never reopened; the council's annual fire inspection had been signed off by an inspector who never actually went into the valve room; and the woman's grandson had been meaning to fit a twenty-quid smoke alarm in her flat for months and never got round to it. Dan (physics PhD, very literal) and Ana (did a philosophy minor, now works in policy) spent about an hour on whether any of those three failures *caused* her death, going round roughly like this:

(1) Dan: none of them caused anything, strictly speaking. What killed her was the fire, in a building whose valve happened to be shut. There is no event of not-reopening-the-valve — no location, no duration, no physical trace — and causation is a relation between happenings in the world. Saying "the contractor killed her" is a blame verdict dressed up as a mechanism; the causal history is fully told by the positive events: the closing three weeks ago, the ignition, the spread of the fire. Once the closed valve is in the history, nothing else needs saying.
(2) Ana: that cheats. The valve's being closed at 3am on the night of the fire isn't a brute fact floating there; it persisted because nobody undid it, and had the contractor reopened it she lives. That's a clean difference-maker, and difference-making is what actual causation amounts to on basically every account there is. Also, you don't get to call "the valve was closed" a state and then stop asking questions — states have to be sustained, and not-sustaining is something an agent can do just as much as acting is.
(3) Dan: then you've got a flood. If bare but-for is enough, the grandson caused her death, the inspector caused her death, whoever failed to make sprinkler retrofits mandatory caused her death, and so did every person who ever thought about buying her an alarm and didn't. A test that returns yes for all of them isn't detecting causation, it's detecting salience, or who we feel like shouting at. Plus a regress: if the valve's staying closed needs an omission to sustain it, does that omission need another omission to sustain it? You're spinning causal structure out of nothing at all.
(4) Ana: the flood is a real problem but it's a problem about *selection*, not about whether omissions can cause. And there's an obvious selector: an omission causes only when the act was due from that agent — contract for the contractor, statute for the inspector, nothing whatsoever for the grandson. That's why the grandson drops out and the other two stay in. On location: omissions are located where and when the action would have been, which is derived but perfectly determinate. And your no-trace argument proves way too much anyway — prevention and double prevention (the airbag that stops the crash from killing you) do causal work all over epidemiology and engineering, and nobody demands a mark on the world for those.
(5) Dan: but look at what your selector is. Whether the contractor's omission caused the death now depends on what his contract says — a causal fact you can change with a paperwork edit. Rewrite the contract and you rewrite the causal history of the fire. If causation by omission has to be read off norms, then it isn't causation at all, it's a normative status wearing causal vocabulary for rhetorical force. I'd rather say his failure explains why he's answerable, not why she died.
(6) Ana: maybe — but here's a version that keeps the selection and drops the norms. Suppose omissions cause only when the agent was *poised* to act: already part-way into the action, set up like a cocked mechanism, with the causal work done by the cancelling of a sequence that had actually started. In the real case the contractor had a job ticket, went down to the basement, got his wrench out, then took a phone call and wandered off. That's why his omission is causal and the grandson's isn't, with no appeal to contracts — the grandson never even got as far as loading the shop's website. And it answers your location worry, because a cancellation happens somewhere and somewhat.
(7) Dan: three things. Poisedness lives in the agent's body and head, so you've made causation depend on someone's motor set, which is worse than depending on a contract, not better. It gets the inspector wrong: he never went near the valve room, so on your view his neglect caused nothing, and I think that's just false. And it doesn't even stop the flood — the grandson had the smoke alarm page open on his phone that week, so by your own criterion he's back in.
(8) Ana: on the inspector I'll just bite the bullet, I think — he caused nothing, he was a fraud, and the fraud and the death are separate facts. And Dan ended by muttering that maybe they just *mean different things* by "cause", which felt like a cop-out to me but I couldn't say why.

Can you go through it move by move and tell me who's right about what — I don't just want an overall winner. Concretely: is the "no event, no location, no trace" argument actually any good, or does the prevention/double-prevention reply defuse it? Does the explosion of candidate omitters force you into a norm-relativised notion of causation, or is there a non-normative way of doing the selection? Is Ana's "poised agent" idea — which I've never seen anywhere and I'm fairly sure she invented on the spot — genuinely an improvement on the duty-based one, or does it just relocate the same problem into the agent's nervous system? And what are both of them missing? I came away with the sense that there were maybe three separate questions tangled together — whether omission claims can be true at all, whether the relation they pick out is causal or merely explanatory, and what the law or the moral accounting should do with them — but I couldn't untangle them myself, and I'd like to know whether Dan's "we just mean different things" is a real diagnosis or an exit.
