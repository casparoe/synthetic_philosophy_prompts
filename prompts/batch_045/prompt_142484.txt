I co-convene a small weekly reading group — half philosophy PhD students, half statistics PhD students — and we have now spent three sessions talking past each other about what a probability claim is a claim about. Neither camp will be moved by a glossary of -isms, so for the next session I want a one-page handout in the form of a decision tree: something that starts from what a person is willing to say about concrete cases and lands them at a named position, so that people get sorted by what they actually commit to rather than by which label they find flattering. I would like your help building the diagnostics. My own suspicion, which you should feel free to overrule, is that the headline objective-versus-subjective split is doing much less work than two other cuts: whether a probability can attach to a single unrepeatable event, and whether two agents sharing all their evidence can nonetheless rationally differ.

The tree has to be built to route all of the following cases, and I want every case to force a branch somewhere: (a) a coin is flipped once in the dark and then both coin and record are destroyed — was there a fact of the matter about the probability of heads before the flip, beyond anyone's information? (b) the one and only atom of a short-lived isotope, created and decayed in a single run of an experiment — can it have a decay probability, and if so in virtue of what? (c) a mint produces coins of unknown and varying bias; you are handed one and must bet on a single toss — is there a determinate probability of heads for you, or only a range of them? (d) a factory produces cubes whose side length is between 1 and 2 centimetres; the question whether the side is under 1.5 cm and the question whether the volume is under 3.375 cubic centimetres are the same question, yet an equal-treatment answer gives 1/2 for one and 1/2 for the other only if you pick the right parameterisation — what does that show? (e) a well-confirmed physical theory: is there a probability that it is approximately true, and is that a different sort of thing from the probability of a measurement outcome? (f) a null hypothesis is tested and rejected at a reported 0.03 — does that number describe the hypothesis, or the procedure that produced the data? (g) two analysts with identical evidence and identical data disagree stubbornly about the probability of next year's recession — is at least one of them being irrational, and in what does the irrationality consist?

Format requirements. Seven or eight branch questions, eight to ten leaves, each branch phrased as a yes/no or a forced choice between two or three concrete claims that a student could answer before knowing any of the labels; no branch question may use the words objective, subjective, frequency, propensity, Bayesian, prior, or frequentist, and no branch may simply ask whether probability is "in the world". Leaves should cover the finite and hypothetical frequentist families, single-case and long-run propensities, the classical or logical-indifference view, coherence-only subjectivism, objective or evidentially constrained Bayesianism, the decision-theoretic derivation of probability from preferences, the chance-plus-credence dualist picture with a chance–credence norm like the Principal Principle, the error-statistical or severity view, and pluralism about the meaning of "probability"; each leaf should carry its name plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. After the tree, please (i) run three of my group's recurring characters through it — the quantum experimentalist who says the atom's decay probability is 1/2 and that is all there is to say, the statistician who says probability statements are about procedures and never about hypotheses, and the cosmologist who says 0.95 is just how confident she is and there is nothing more to be settled — and report which leaves they land on; (ii) flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot really separate, with a note on what further case would separate them; and (iii) list the unoccupied cells in the logical space your cuts define, saying for each whether nobody is there because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply undefended. Please give me the structure and your verdicts rather than a bibliography or further-reading list; we already have one, and it has not stopped the arguing.
