ok so my friend and I keep circling the same thing and I want a spec before we go any further: what would a theory of competence-without-comprehension actually have to deliver to be worth having? I mean the general thing — a process produces a system that can do something, and neither the process, nor anyone who set it up, nor the system itself has any grasp of why it works. Darwin's version is the one where nobody ever understood and the appearance of understanding is an illusion to be explained away; the Turing-flavoured version is weirder and more interesting to me, because there we *do* know what we want (we set the objective) and we deliberately hand the job to a process that won't understand, sometimes because we can't write the solution down as rules in the first place. A theory of this should cover both, and say what's shared and what isn't.

Give me the requirements list, with the case or consideration behind each: which phenomena it has to explain (biological adaptation where the reasons were never represented anywhere; learned systems; the social ones like prices or case law or the scientific method, where the institution knows more than any member), which distinctions it can't collapse, and which results it can't contradict. Two distinctions I care about most: (a) no one understands vs. no one could vs. someone could but hasn't yet — and what test you'd run to tell those apart; (b) what I've started calling borrowed comprehension — most of these processes are swimming in material made by creatures who *did* understand (human text, human-built datasets, hand-engineered architectures and curricula, environments shaped by intentions). Is the resulting competence comprehension-free, or is comprehension just displaced one level out? My attempted test for that is the mute-curriculum case: train two systems on the same task, one on data that includes people's reasoning and one on inputs and outcomes with every trace of reasoning stripped out. If the debt is borrowed, the second should come out badly; if the search was doing the work anyway, it shouldn't matter much. Does that test actually discriminate, or is it a bad experiment?

Also: I want a requirement about comprehension debt — the gap between what a produced system can do and what anyone can say about why — and specifically about servicing that debt with tools that are themselves comprehension-free (using a model to explain a model, automated interpretability). That feels like borrowing more rather than repaying, but I can't say why yet. And a requirement about failure: a theory should tell me *when* this breaks — Goodhart-ish cases, shift, brittle competence, a system that competently appears to understand something it doesn't — because the purpose I care about is deciding whether to trust and deploy a system built this way, not just explaining it after the fact.

Self-application: comprehension is itself a product of comprehension-free processes, so I don't think the theory gets to take comprehension as a primitive ingredient without explaining where it comes from. But I'm not sure that's a real desideratum or just my prejudice. Flag negotiable vs. non-negotiable requirements, and mark any pair you think can't be jointly satisfied — I suspect something like "explain each competence in terms we can fully grasp" vs "cover cases that outrun what we can grasp," and maybe "be fully general across evolution/learning/markets" vs "say something testable." Then, briefly, say which single requirement knocks out the most of the standard ways people talk about this (the selectionist story, the "it's just search" story, the rationalization/skill story where the comprehension comes first and the competence is downstream, whatever else you think belongs on the list). Keep it on the criteria, I can do the scoring myself. And tell me upfront if the whole thing collapses into something I should have asked differently.
