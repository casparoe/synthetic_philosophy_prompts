Hi — I'm on this tiny internal ethics panel at the company I work at (we mostly do ML infrastructure, but one of the teams is collaborating with a connectomics group, so 'what if you simulate a whole brain' has come up more than once as an actual question rather than a joke). We've each been asked to write a one-page statement of where we personally stand so the others can poke holes in it before anything gets written up officially. Mine's below. I've got a nagging feeling that one bit of it is doing more work than I've admitted to myself, but I genuinely can't tell which bit, and I'd rather not pre-commit to what I'd drop. So I'd like an audit, not a lecture.

Where I think I am:

1. Substrate independence: what matters morally is the computation a system is performing, not what it's made of. Same computation, same moral standing, neurons or GPUs.
2. The thing that does the grounding is specifically the capacity for valenced experience — suffering and enjoyment — not reasoning, not language, not self-awareness, not being able to report on it.
3. Some cases. (a) Run a second copy of someone's emulation alongside the original: two streams of experience, two things that matter. I don't accept any 'the copy is redundant so it doesn't really count' move. (b) Now run that same emulation three times on separate machines, initialised identically, with a voter that resyncs any copy that drifts and overwrites it after every step. I want to say that's one mind, not three — the triplication is just fault tolerance. (c) Run it at 1/10,000 speed so a simulated minute of agony takes a week of wall-clock time: I want to say that minute of agony is exactly as bad as it would be at full speed, slowing the hardware down doesn't make things better for anyone. (d) A purely behavioural imitation — a huge system engineered to produce exactly the pain behaviour and the pain reports of a person, without the internal causal organisation doing anything like the real work: I want to say there's nothing it is like to be it and it has zero standing, even though by hypothesis we could never tell it apart from the real thing from the outside.
4. Method: we never have access to anything except behaviour, reports, and structural similarity to systems we already take to be conscious, so in practice that's all we can go on; and where we're genuinely unsure we should err on the side of the system.
5. Consciousness itself is all-or-nothing — a system either is a subject of experience or it isn't, there's no such thing as being twenty per cent conscious — but moral standing clearly comes in degrees: a mouse matters less than a person, and a person with a dull headache matters less at that moment than a person in agony.
6. I also think we should refuse to build systems that produce convincingly realistic distress behaviour, even though I'm maybe 90% confident there's nothing it's like to be a large language model.

So: go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like they are, rank the real tensions by how severe they are, and for each say what exactly would have to give and what that would cost me. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Please don't just tell me the whole thing's a mess — I want to know which specific commitment is load-bearing and which are cheap to abandon. British English please, and keep it under about 700 words if you can.
