I work in public health, mostly on the causal-inference side: potential-outcome notation, directed acyclic graphs, Pearl-style interventions together with the insistence that a causal question is ill-posed until the intervention is specified, Rothman's sufficient-component-cause model on which a cause is a piece of a sufficient cause rather than a lone difference-maker, attributable and etiologic fractions, the prevention paradox on which most cases of a harm come from the many people at small risk rather than the few at high risk, threshold phenomena of the herd-immunity kind, and the usual machinery for when the thing you want isn't identified: bounds, sensitivity analysis, priors over an unknown dose-response curve.

Outside work I'm in a reading group with a political theorist, and we have got stuck on the inefficacy objection to the claim that individuals can contribute to collective harms. My driving to work, or buying one factory-farmed chicken, or voting, does not seem to change any outcome: the emissions and the flooding happen anyway, the bird is already dead, the election is not decided by one vote. And yet the conclusion that it therefore does not matter what I do looks wrong. She has sketched, without citations, three families of reply: an expected-difference reply, on which a tiny chance of being the marginal agent multiplied by a very large harm is not negligible; a participation or fair-share reply, on which what matters is whether you are taking part in a harmful practice or doing your share of it, not whether you made a difference; and replies that reach for some non-counterfactual notion of causal role. My hunch is that my field has already defused this and the philosophers are reinventing a wheel; she says the prevention paradox is a claim about populations and cannot possibly tell me what I ought to do. I want to know which of us is closer, so: restate the whole dispute as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary, and then give me the residue report.

Concretely, please do the following.

1. For the objection itself and for each of the three families of reply, separate what translates cleanly into causal-inference and epidemiological terms, what translates only with distortion, and what cannot be expressed in that vocabulary at all. Name the estimand you think each philosophical claim is implicitly asking for, and say whether it is identified.
2. Flag explicitly anywhere my vocabulary would smuggle in a substantive moral assumption, consequentialism above all, so that calling something a translation would be assuming the answer rather than giving it. This is the part I am least able to check myself.
3. Handle four cases separately, because I suspect they come apart in translation: greenhouse-gas emissions, where the per-unit contribution is continuous and minuscule and the harm is spread thin over very many people; the supermarket chicken, where supply is restocked in discrete batches so there is a real threshold but I have no idea where in the batch I am; voting, where difference-making occurs only in the knife-edge case of an exact tie; and the many-hands case I half remember from a survey course, in which a thousand torturers each turn a dial a thousandth of a turn and none of them makes any perceptible difference to the victim's pain.

For the emissions case, the estimated per-person effect is so small that it is indistinguishable from zero at any achievable sample size: is a confidence interval straddling zero doing any real work for the philosopher, or does it merely restate the puzzle in better notation?
5. Finish with a verdict rather than a survey. Is the residue evidence that my causal vocabulary is blind to something the philosophers are tracking, or evidence that the inefficacy objection is shallower than it sounds? Take a side and say what would change your mind.
