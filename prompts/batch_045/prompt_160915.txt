I sit on the rules committee of a professional body (a chartered institute; I am the youngest member by some distance). We have been asked to decide whether to delete a long-standing entry requirement: that every candidate for full membership must be proposed and seconded by two existing members who have personally observed the candidate's work over at least three years. The rule dates from 1908. The only rationale in the surviving minutes is that it secures the character of the entrant — a phrase several of us think is doing something we would now name differently, and that in practice also worked as a filter on who could be known and sponsored at all. What none of us can tell is whether it is presently doing something that nobody on the committee has articulated.

At our last meeting a senior member circulated what he called Chesterton's fence. The version he sent round runs roughly like this: a man comes across a fence or a gate erected across a road; the modern reformer says brightly that he does not see the use of it and proposes to clear it away; the wiser reply is that if he does not see the use of it, he is certainly not allowed to clear it away, and that he should go away and think, and only when he can come back and say what it is for may he destroy it. He used this to move that the requirement be retained, and to argue that the burden of proof rests permanently with anyone proposing removal: we may delete a rule only once we can state what it was for. A colleague on the other side replied that, taken literally, the same argument would stop us ever adding a rule whose future use we cannot yet state, which she took to be a reductio of the whole way of thinking.

I want an audit of the analogy, not a verdict on the rule. In particular:

Set out the correspondence explicitly (a table is fine): what plays the role of the fence, the road, the person who put it up, the reason it was put up, the passer-by who cannot see the use, the removal, and the putting-back — in the case of a membership rule rather than a fence.

Then tell me which disanalogies matter for the use it is being put to here, and which do not. The ones I keep circling are these. First, a fence is an inert object that goes on standing unattended, whereas the rule is only as alive as the people who keep complying with it — candidates still travel, keep records and solicit sponsors at real cost, year after year. Does that continued costly compliance count as evidence of present function, or can costly compliance persist long after function is gone? Second, a fence has one builder, one purpose and one date, while our rule has been amended three times and has no author. So the instruction to go away and find out why may have no answer to find, and the answer on file may be a rationalisation rather than the operative reason: does the fence picture misdescribe what a reason is in this sort of case? Third, a fence is cheap to rebuild and a road is unchanged by its absence; a deleted requirement, once members have reorganised their practices around its absence, may be expensive or impossible to restore. If that is right the analogy understates rather than overstates the caution it is being used to support, and I want to know how much of the argument turns on it.

There is also a distinction I cannot get straight. One reading of the fence is deference to the intentions of the people who put the thing up. Another, closer to what I take the Burkean version to be, is the thought that an inherited practice can carry more knowledge than any participant can state, and may be doing work of a kind nobody could name — serving as a focal point, or as a sorting signal, rather than producing anything directly. Are these the same argument? Whichever one is doing the real work, say which, and whether it survives in a case where we already know the stated original purpose and find it unattractive.

Finally: does the conclusion survive? If the fence licenses only a temporary and dischargeable burden of proof, tell me concretely what would discharge it here — a pilot, a sunset clause, staged repeal, something else — and how strong the resulting presumption is. If it licenses a permanent one, tell me what prevents it from being a one-way ratchet in which nothing inherited can ever be removed. And if there is a better analogy for this situation than a fence across a road, give it: someone has suggested a piece of inherited code in a large shared codebase with no tests, and I would like to know whether that is an improvement, what it gets right that the fence gets wrong, and what it in turn smuggles in.
