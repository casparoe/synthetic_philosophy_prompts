I chair the Patient Safety Review Board at a teaching hospital, and I also teach one section of a bioethics course for residents each term, so I would like something I can use in both roles. The board meets fortnightly for ninety minutes, reviews four to six incidents per sitting, and works only from the incident record — we have no power to investigate, interview, or order tests, and whatever we write goes into a file that the credentialing committee and, in principle, a plaintiff's lawyer may read. For each clinician involved in an incident we must record two things: a retrospective responsibility rating on a four-point scale (no fault, minor lapse, serious lapse, gross lapse) and, separately, a forward-looking recommendation (no action, additional training, restriction of privileges). Our five members are two physicians, a nurse, a risk manager and a lay member; none of them has studied philosophy, and I need something they can run in about fifteen minutes per case without me translating it. The immediate trigger is that last month we rated two anaesthetists differently for what the record describes as the same departure from the monitoring protocol; the only difference is that one patient turned out to have an undiagnosed condition and died, while the other was extubated without incident. The risk manager argued that the outcome is exactly what we are here to account for; the lay member said it obviously should not count; and we split three ways, which is worse than being wrong.

I have read a little of the philosophical literature on moral luck — Nagel, Williams, the control principle — and my difficulty is that it is very good at telling me why the ordinary way of assigning blame is in trouble and very poor at telling me what to do on Tuesday afternoon. So I want you to turn that literature into a written decision procedure the board could actually adopt. Please make the following non-negotiable: the two outputs must be kept apart, and the procedure must state explicitly which varieties of luck are permitted to move which output and which are barred — luck in how the outcome turned out, luck in the circumstances the agent was placed in (understaffing, a night shift, a broken monitor), luck in the traits and temperament the agent happens to have, and luck in what the agent could reasonably have foreseen or known at the time. I want at least one step to be a blinded re-run: re-present the case to the board with the outcome and the patient's subsequent course redacted, re-rate, and treat any change in the rating as the signature of outcome luck rather than as new information about the agent. I also want the counterfactual comparison class — the same decision, made by a competent peer, in these circumstances, with what was knowable then — fixed by a standing rule agreed in advance, not redrawn case by case, since I can already see members adjusting the comparison class until it yields the answer they want. For each step give me the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer given that we cannot investigate, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to a named person because the question is one of values rather than fact.

Then apply the whole procedure to three cases, showing the ratings and recommendations it produces: the two anaesthetists; a senior surgeon with a decade of unblemished work who is now found to have a mild tremor from early Parkinson's disease and whose technique has visibly deteriorated; and a resident who skipped a checklist item and had an uncomplicated outcome, next to a colleague who skipped the same item in the same week and caused a serious harm. I especially want you to follow the procedure through in the third case even where it yields a result the credentialing committee will refuse to accept, and to say plainly that it will. Close with a short section on what the procedure cannot do: the cases where the record simply cannot settle what the agent knew or believed, the points at which I have to make a judgment call that no checklist can eliminate, and the kinds of incident for which this framework is the wrong tool altogether. Please write it as numbered steps followed by the applications followed by that closing section, in at most 1,000 words total, with no more than 450 words on the procedure itself and no more than 150 on the closing section. Do not give me citations, bibliographies, or suggested reading: I have no library access and cannot follow them up, so if a step depends on a distinction drawn by a particular philosopher, name it in a phrase and use it in the wording of the step rather than pointing me to a source.
