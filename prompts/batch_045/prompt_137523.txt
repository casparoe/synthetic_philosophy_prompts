I write about science and ideas for a newspaper, and for the past few months I have been trying to report accurately on a budgeting reform that several finance ministries and health systems have adopted and that I keep describing badly. The practice is this: every spending bid, whatever department it comes from, is scored in a single unit — one point on the zero-to-ten life-satisfaction question asked in national surveys, sustained by one person for one year, which the practitioners call a wellbeing-adjusted life year. Effects on that unit are estimated by regressing survey responses on circumstances, so that a home-insulation programme, a talking-therapy service, and a hip-replacement backlog all end up as numbers in the same column; the numbers are summed across everyone affected, with no discount or premium for whose they are; the total is divided by cost using a single money-to-wellbeing exchange rate that is the same for every person and every programme; and the bid is approved if its cost per unit falls below a fixed threshold. Alongside this sits a set of local rules I have been picking at: where a policy affects grief, chronic pain or disability, self-reports are overridden by valuations that members of the general public give to written descriptions of those states, on the acknowledged grounds that people living in them adapt and report near-normal satisfaction; gains accruing to people who start from a low baseline are multiplied by a severity uplift, while gains to people with short remaining lifetimes are not; the exchange rate is linear even though the underlying surveys show income buying less wellbeing the more of it someone already has; and for programmes that change who is alive — neonatal care, road safety, a housing policy that affects who moves into a district — benefits are credited to people who would not otherwise have existed, against an average baseline. I also notice that a small reform making a few people better off and nobody worse off is rejected if it costs more per unit than the threshold allows, while a reform that imposes genuine losses on an identifiable minority is approved whenever the summed total clears the bar.

When I described all this on the radio, an economist told me the whole apparatus quietly assumes that one person's wellbeing can be put on the same scale as another's, which is exactly the assumption the old positivist critics said was not a scientific operation at all — choices, they said, reveal an ordering inside a single person, and nothing that can be lined up across heads. That is the part I cannot get straight, and it is not made easier by the fact that the last time an American state tried to rank treatments with a comparable metric, in Oregon at the end of the eighties, it ran into disability-discrimination law within a few years. So I would like the implicit theory reconstructed.

What I need, in terms I can defend in nine hundred words without footnotes — and please do not send me to papers, since I would have no way of obtaining them and would rather have the argument than the references — is this. First, what has to be true about wellbeing, about its measurability and about its comparability across persons, for the scorecard's verdicts to be anything more than administrative convention: does it strictly require a cardinal scale with full interpersonal comparability, or would some weaker structure do the work, and if it does require the strong thing, is the scale being discovered or stipulated, and on whose authority is a stipulated scale binding on the people it ranks? Second, where is the practice philosophically underdetermined: which rival views about value and distribution would license exactly these same numbers, so that I should not claim in print that the scorecard commits its users to summing utility in the classical style? Third, are its parts mutually consistent — the two different sources of the scale, the uniform unit alongside the severity uplift, the linear exchange rate over a curved relationship, the indifference to who receives a unit alongside the stated priority to the worst off, the crediting of benefits to people whose existence depends on the policy — or is the apparatus a coalition of views that would come apart under pressure? And fourth, if the critic is right and no interpersonal comparison is available, what would the institution actually do on Monday morning instead: what allocative procedure would replace the scorecard, what would it gain in honesty, and what would it lose in the consistency and transparency that won the scorecard its adoption in the first place? Throughout, I would find it most useful if you kept separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, since the distinction is the thing my editor will press me on.
