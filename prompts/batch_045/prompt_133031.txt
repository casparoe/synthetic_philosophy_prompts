I am writing a novel and need help with a single scene in it — the scene that carries the argument the book has been circling for three hundred pages. I have the world, the two people, and the philosophical machinery I want the scene to operate; what I need back is the dialogue itself, in finished form.

The setting is the city-state of Terrenne, ten years after a civil war that the government now in power won. Two institutions dominate the peace.

The Bureau of Emphasis is the government's voice. It has abolished official falsehood: every sentence it issues passes verification before it is spoken. What it retains is the choice of which verified facts to publish, in what order, at what hour, and in whose mouth — and the practice of answering an unwelcome question with the least informative true statement available to it. Its defenders call this candour; its critics call it a lie with better lawyers.

The Surplus is the rival institution, and it is not a government organ. For ten years it has maintained an unranked public archive of every verified particular it can collect: casualty lists,procurement receipts, the hour at which a bridge was mined, the text of an order and the names appended to it. Everything in it is searchable by anyone. Nothing in it is edited, arranged, introduced, or interpreted. Its defenders call it the ground; its critics call it a heap.

The two characters meet in the reading room of the Surplus at two in the morning.

Anselm Roy was the Bureau's chief speechwriter for six years and is now its most articulate defender. He holds that politics is the making of a world between people, that a shared world requires selection and shape, that a city cannot inhabit a list, and that the Bureau's discipline is the adult form of that shaping: it tells the truth and still gives Terrenne something to live inside. He thinks the Surplus is not neutrality but abdication, and that in practice it hands interpretation to whoever has the most stamina.

Wren Halloway has spent the same ten years reading the Surplus. She holds that facts are the ground people stand on; that they are contingent, could have been otherwise, resist proof and demonstration, and are more fragile than argument for exactly that reason; that they are destroyed far more reliably by replacement than by contradiction; and that the endpoint of organized falsification is not a city that believes a falsehood but a city that has lost the capacity to take its bearings in the real world at all.

What brings them together: the Surplus has verified and published a signature. Maret Ost, the revered founder of the peace and the author of its founding story, did countersign the order to burn the Kessel district — the act whose denial is the story Terrenne has been living in for a decade. Every year of the peace is built on that denial in some way. At the Convocation in the morning Halloway intends to read the signature aloud. Roy has come to talk her out of it, and he is not a fool, and he does not deny the signature.

The argument should move through the following distinctions, which readers of Arendt will recognize, though no character names her, cites her, or speaks in anyone's vocabulary but their own: the difference between factual truth and the truths of reason or demonstration, and why the former is the more fragile of the two; the claim that the liar remains dependent on the truth, since he must know what he is denying in order to deny it, and that this is why the liar rather than the truth-teller is the man of action, possessed of a freedom the truth-teller lacks — the liar can generate indefinite alternatives while the truth-teller is stuck with what happened; the claim that the capacity to say of what is that it is not is the same capacity that makes a new beginning possible, and that this is what makes lying a standing temptation of political action and not a mere vice of bad men; the difference between the liar and the person who has lost all contact with fact, and why the second is the catastrophe and the first is not; the sense in which factual truth is despotic, admitting no opinion and no disagreement; and the trap in which the professional fabricator ends by believing his own fabrication, a condition distinct from lying and less recoverable from it.

What I want the scene to actually work out, rather than recite:

First, whether the Bureau's practice is lying at all on this accounting, or whether it is something the accounting has no name for. Roy will press that every shared world requires selection and that the Surplus's refusal to rank is itself a selection rule with consequences. Halloway needs a reply that collapses neither into the claim that all selection is lying nor into the claim that only false sentences can lie.

Second, whether a surplus of fact can destroy a common world the way a deficit of fact can. This should be Roy's strongest ground, and he should be allowed to be nearly right on it: an unranked heap of particulars gives a city no story to inhabit. Halloway's strongest ground should be that the ground on which people stand is precisely the facts nobody chose.

Third, what Ost's denial was. Roy should be able to say what it cost the survivors of Kessel without ceasing to believe it was necessary, and should be able to say what kind of thing Halloway would be doing to the city in the morning. Halloway should be able to concede that the Convocation may destroy Terrenne's capacity to act together without giving anyone back the district.

Fourth, and this is the crux I care most about: if everything the Bureau says is literally true, then what is it that the Bureau knows and is denying? Where, in an institution that never utters a falsehood, is the denial physically located — in the sentences, in the selection, in the timing, in the silence? Halloway must locate the lie somewhere, and Roy must be brought to admit that it is located there.

There should be no resolution. I want the two of them to arrive at a question neither can answer and to part with the Convocation six hours away. My hope is that the question turns on self-deception: whether Roy, after ten years of saying things he knows to be shaped, is still in contact with the fact at all, and whether Halloway, after ten years inside other people's testimony, has any world left to stand on besides it.

Two constraints on the writing. No one is a strawman: each character must state the other's position in a form the other would accept before attacking it, and each must make one real concession he or she did not arrive with. And no one is a debater: they are tired, competent officials at two in the morning, speaking in the vocabulary of their institutions and their city. Give me roughly twelve hundred to sixteen hundred words of scene, with sparse stage directions.
