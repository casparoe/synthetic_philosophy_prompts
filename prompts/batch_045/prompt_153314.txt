My sister and I have been going in circles for about two years over what to do about Dad — he's 84, he's had two falls, and she thinks it's time for assisted living and I don't. The genuinely maddening part is that we've seen all the same material: the same geriatrician's report, the same list of incidents, the same numbers on cost, and we've each said out loud, repeatedly, what we think and what we think the other thinks. A friend at work, who does something with auctions, pointed me at Aumann's agreement theorem, and I went off and read about it and then tried using it on my sister, which went about as well as you'd expect. But it set me building the argument below, and I think it ends somewhere I can't accept, and I can't find the bad step.

(1) Aumann's result: if two agents start from a common prior and their posteriors are common knowledge, then their posteriors are identical — people who have genuinely shared everything can't stably disagree.
(2) My sister and I have shared everything relevant and each of us knows where the other stands, so if we still disagree, either one of us is mishandling the evidence or we never had a common prior to begin with.
(3) Priors aren't rationally constrained — Bayesianism will let you start almost anywhere — so a difference in priors isn't itself a defect, and it isn't something either of us could detect, argue about, or repair.
(4) So whenever a disagreement survives full disclosure, the most you can ever conclude is that the priors differ, which is compatible with both parties being perfectly rational and is unfalsifiable from the inside.
(5) But there's a standard reply: prior differences aren't primitive, they're supposed to trace back to differences in what the two people have lived through, and that can be re-modelled as a difference in evidence updated from a single shared starting point — push the disagreement back a stage and the theorem bites again.
(6) So the theorem either bites at every stage, in which case I have to conclude that my sister or I am being irrational or evasive (which I don't believe, and which, applied generally, would convict every long-running dispute in my department of bad faith), or it never bites at all and it's an empty piece of formalism.

The friend who sent me to the theorem says (3) is where it rots; my partner thinks the real work is being done by (2), because knowing what somebody thinks isn't yet the thing the theorem needs. I'd like to keep (1) and the thought that the theorem says something true about disagreement — and I'd really rather not walk away believing my sister is being irrational, since we have to actually decide this before spring. My own attempt at an exit was to say there are two kinds of disagreement: thin ones, where we're carving up the same space of possibilities with different evidence, and thick ones, where we aren't even working with the same possibilities — and that only the thin ones are the theorem's business. But I can't tell whether that's a real distinction or just a fancy way of exempting my own case from the theorem's range.

Which step would you give up, and what does giving it up cost me — do I lose the theorem's bite entirely, or is there a version of it that still says something about two people in my situation?
