I've been defending a view in a paper draft, which I've been calling documentary deference: when a community's living memory of its own past comes apart from what the documentary record says happened, the record gets presumptive authority, and the memory counts as evidence about what happened only once something independent corroborates it — otherwise it's evidence about the community rather than about the past. I also want to add a second, shakier claim: that a memory carried only in ritual or commemoration, with no one left alive who can testify to it, has a weaker claim still, since nobody is really asserting anything anymore.

What would count against this? I'd like the cases, observations, and arguments that should move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to separate what a defender of the view should concede from what she can reasonably dismiss as question-begging. Three things I'm specially unsure about: whether the bit about ritual memory is a genuinely separate claim or just sloppiness on my part; whether I've quietly made the whole thing unfalsifiable by letting 'the record' include anything any institution happened to write down, and if so whether that's fixable or just fatal; and whether there's any case where deference to the record predicts something its rivals don't. For what it's worth, the cases I find hardest to shrug off are the ones where the community is demonstrably wrong about the details but the mistake itself went on to shape what happened next.
