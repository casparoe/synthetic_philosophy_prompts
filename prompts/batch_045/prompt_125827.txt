I'm assembling a set of dialogues on contested points in decision theory, and I'd like you to write one on the Allais paradox and the independence (sure-thing-for-lotteries) axiom. Please write the dialogue in British English throughout — British spellings such as "behaviour", "modelling", "favour", "practise", and sums in pounds.

Set-up. Two colleagues, both thoroughly competent, are working through why the classic Allais pair is or is not a counterexample to expected utility theory. Frame both problems in terms of a single draw from an urn containing a hundred tickets numbered 1 to 100:

Problem 1
- A: £1,000,000 whatever ticket is drawn.
- B: £1,000,000 if the ticket is 1–89; £5,000,000 if it is 90–99; nothing if it is 100.

Problem 2
- C: £1,000,000 if the ticket is 1–11; nothing if it is 12–100.
- D: £5,000,000 if the ticket is 1–10; nothing if it is 11–100.

The pattern to be argued over is A over B together with D over C. Make sure the dialogue gets the structure explicit early on: in Problem 1 the two options agree on tickets 1–89, and in Problem 2 they agree on tickets 12–100; once that shared branch is struck out, *both* problems leave exactly the same residual choice — £1,000,000 for sure against £5,000,000 with probability 10/11 and nothing with probability 1/11. So the pattern is inconsistent with any ranking that ignores a branch on which the options agree.

Characters. Dr Vance defends the independence axiom as a genuine requirement of rationality and not merely a convenient modelling assumption. Dr Kamel defends the Allais pattern: at minimum, he denies that it has been shown to be irrational, and at most he thinks it is the reasonable way to choose. Give them distinct but non-caricatural voices, and let them argue in their own words rather than by appeal to authorities.

Requirements for the argument.

1. Self-contained. Explain any technical notion (expected utility, mixture or compound lottery, reduction, the sure-thing principle, risk aversion) in the characters' own words as they need it. No footnotes, no citations, no bibliographies, no quotation from or allusion to particular published papers or experimental findings that a reader would have to look up. Where a claim about how people actually choose would be doing work, let the characters frame it as a stipulation ("suppose it turned out that…") and argue about what would follow. They may use ordinary labels for well-known positions — expected utility theory, prospect theory, the sure-thing principle — but must not lean on the details of any specific published model.

2. No strawmen, and genuine concessions. Vance must concede that each of the standard arguments for independence carries a load-bearing extra premise: the dominance-style argument assumes that evaluation is separable across branches; the long-run or aggregation argument assumes that repeated independent plays are to be evaluated in the same terms as a single play; the money-pump or exploitability argument is not nearly as clean for independence as it is for transitivity, since an agent who declines to reduce compound lotteries can be hard to pump. Kamel must concede that he owes something positive — either a principled account of why the unused branches of a lottery can matter to the value of the option, or a reason to think that "the one-in-a-hundred chance of nothing has a special sting" is more than a description of a feeling — and he must never fall back on simply repeating that he prefers the sure million.

3. Three probes, in escalating order, each arising because the previous one has not settled things.

(a) The near-certainty variant. Before the ticket is drawn, an independent check voids the whole scheme with probability 0.001, in which case everyone gets nothing; call the resulting options A*, B*, C*, D*. The pattern A over B and D over C is then a ranking of two mixtures that share a common 0.1% chance of nothing, so结构上 nothing normative has changed — but A* is no longer certain. Have them consider what would follow if the pattern dissolved once literal certainty was removed, and what would follow if it survived, and have them disagree about whether either finding settles the normative question, with neither character allowed to treat a hypothetical finding as decisive by fiat.

(b) The staged draw with an optional late choice. Suppose the draw happens in two stages: first it is settled whether the ticket lies in the "settled" group (1–89 in Problem 1, 12–100 in Problem 2), in which case both options pay the same thing, and only if it lies in the "live" group does anything else matter. Before the first stage, the chooser may either commit now or take a late choice: wait until the first stage is resolved and, if the live group came up, choose then. They agree that conditional on the live group, the tempting option is the risky one, and that in the settled group the choice makes no difference to anyone. Vance argues that this settles it — plan now for the only branch in which the choice matters. Kamel's reply cannot be that the branch probabilities depend on his act, because they do not; give him a real alternative instead (refusing reduction of compound lotteries, caring about the shape of the process, or denying that unconditional choice must track conditional choice), have Vance press it hard — for instance by asking what exactly is lost in the settled branch, where the choice changes nothing — and have Kamel answer the further question of whether he would pay something to avoid being offered the late choice.

(c) The hundred ticket-holders, which should be the crux. Instead of one person facing the lottery once, imagine a hundred identifiable people each holding one ticket, so that the risk becomes a fixed pattern of inequality in the final distribution: under A everyone gets £1,000,000; under B, eighty-nine people get £1,000,000, ten get £5,000,000 and one gets nothing; under C, eleven get £1,000,000 and eighty-nine get nothing; under D, ten get £5,000,000 and ninety get nothing. Have one of them spell out in plain terms what the Allais pattern amounts to once there is no uncertainty left — something about the first person left with nothing as against the ninetieth — and then have one of them actually do the arithmetic for a simple, explicitly specified way of scoring final distributions: a symmetric, additively separable score, the sum of some increasing function of each person's amount. State precisely what that rule delivers about the two comparisons and whether any choice of the function can produce the Allais pattern; do not gesture at the result. Then give Kamel his best reply (for example, that his evaluation was never a function of final distributions at all, that it tracks what a choice does relative to what it could have done, or that dividing the outcome among distinct people changes the subject) and let Vance press it — if unused branches may matter, what principled limit is there, and does the licence generalise to comparisons that both of them regard as clearly indefensible?

4. Texture and ending. The two should answer each other's actual points rather than restating their own; at least twice one of them should grant a premise while denying the conclusion. Include at least one place where they discover they do not really disagree after all, and at least one place where the disagreement turns out to be about something other than it first appeared to be — about what the object of evaluation is, say, or about what "rational" is meant to be doing here. End with the disagreement located rather than dissolved: each should be able to say what would move them, and each should acknowledge at least one real cost of their own position.

Format: a prose dialogue of roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words, with the speakers named, minimal stage directions, and no summary, preface or postscript.
