I am a retired evolutionary biologist who has come to philosophy rather late, and I have been invited to open a forty-minute session of our university's artificial-intelligence ethics reading group — mostly computer-science doctoral students, with one or two philosophers present — on the question of which computations, if any, matter morally. I want a plan for how to explain this, not the explanation itself.

My own instinct is that the session has to begin by pulling apart two questions the students will otherwise run together: whether a system's moral standing depends on the computation it implements, and which computation it implements in the first place, given that under a sufficiently ingenious mapping almost any physical system can be said to implement almost anything. After that I want them to feel the duplication case — a simulation snapshotted, paused, restored twice on different hardware, the two copies run in parallel and then re-merged by averaging their states — because that is where I think the distinction between an abstract computation and a concrete causal process stops being academic. But I am not certain this is the best case to build the session around, or that it does not quietly presuppose the answer.

Please reply as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion, in British English:

1. The order in which I should introduce the pieces — the mapping worry, the duplication-and-merge case, and the question of whether sentience admits of degrees — and what each depends on having been said before it.
2. The one example that should carry the whole session, with what it must have and what it must lack; if my merge case is not the right vehicle, say what to use instead and why.
3. The misconceptions this audience will form, in the order they will form them, and the point in the sequence at which I should head each one off. I expect at least these two: that computation is a quantity that can be summed, so that two copies carry twice the moral weight; and that because the boundaries we can draw around a system are arbitrary, there is no fact of the matter about what it is like.
4. One question to put to them at the end that would show they have the distinction rather than the slogan that it all depends on the computation.
5. What I should decline to explain at this level, and how to say so in a sentence; I had assumed the hard problem of consciousness was the obvious candidate, but tell me if something else is the real trap.
