Here is the context. I am a software engineer by trade, about fifteen years of backend and infrastructure work, with no formal philosophy background. I write an internal series of notes for my organisation on the notion of intent in software design: what it means for a module to be *for* something, when code counts as dead, when a workaround has quietly become load-bearing, and what distinguishes a bug from a behaviour someone merely dislikes. In the course of that I started reading about the notion of function in biology, because the same questions seem to arise there in a sharper form, and I have now written down roughly where I have landed. I am giving a talk on this next month and I want the set checked before I embarrass myself.

My commitments, as I would state them:

(a) For evolved biological traits I hold what I have seen called the selection-history view: the function of a trait is what past tokens of that trait were selected for, not what it happens to do now. I hold this mainly because it makes two things work at once. It makes malfunction coherent: a heart that cannot pump is not merely doing something different, it is failing at what it is supposed to do, and I do not see how a view that identifies function with current contribution can say that. And it lets me say that the function of the heart is to circulate blood and not to produce the thumping noise a clinician listens to, even though the noise is a perfectly real effect.

(b) Malfunction is objective, and this is not merely a theoretical nicety. Clinical medicine has to distinguish a lesion from a mere statistical oddity, and I think that distinction has to be grounded in something like (a). A leaking valve is a defect and not a variation, and I take that judgement to be correct rather than conventional.

(c) Function ascriptions earn their keep by being explanatory and predictive: to say that the function of X is to F is to say something that explains why X is still there, that licenses predictions about what the organism will do to maintain, repair or compensate for X, and that tells you what to look for when X breaks.

(d) For code I hold something that sounds like the opposite view, and I hold it confidently. There, a function is for whatever it currently does that the system depends on. A function that nothing calls has no function: it is dead code and should be deleted. A routine written to format logs, which everything now depends on for ordering, is now for ordering. And the case that made me confident: a large part of our current codebase was generated by a language model, so for many modules there is no author intention at all, and yet I have no hesitation in saying that the retry helper is for absorbing transient failures. There is no history to appeal to there, and apparently none is needed.

(e) I also think that calling something a bug is an objective matter and not an expression of taste. If that retry helper swallows a server error and reports success, it is broken, whether or not anyone currently minds, and whether or not the system as a whole still works.

(f) When a part is co-opted for a new use, I want to say that its function changes. In code this is routine: the logging call becomes the ordering mechanism. In biology I want to say the parallel thing, that a structure which the organism's organisation has come to depend on for some new purpose has that new purpose as its function, and not merely as an accident.

(g) A mutation that confers a benefit for the very first time has that function straight away, before any selection has acted on it. If function only attaches after selection has gone to work, then our function talk is blind exactly at the origin of new capacities, which is the part I find most interesting.

(h) A field biologist dissecting a newly discovered species can work out what an organ is for from its current contribution to the organism's survival, without knowing anything about its phylogeny. I take that to be a genuine achievement of function ascription, and I do not think (a) is in conflict with it, though I have been told it is, so I am listing it.

(i) In the evolutionary simulations I write for fun, I want to say that the digital organisms have real functions once selection has acted on them, but that the hand-written ancestor I seed each run with does not have functions in the same sense: it merely does things. I cannot tell whether that asymmetry is a consequence I should accept or a symptom that something above is wrong.
