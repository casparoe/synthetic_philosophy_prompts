I volunteer as a grant reviewer for a tiny effective-giving fund — it's basically eight people in a group chat moving maybe sixty grand a year, so total amateur hour — but we do genuinely try to do expected-value math instead of just funding whatever feels warm. And over the last couple of rounds I've started noticing a pattern in our discussions that I have no word for, and it's starting to feel like the pattern is doing all the work.

Here's the shape of it. Whenever we compare a well-understood intervention against a speculative one, the speculative one wins on EV by orders of magnitude, and it wins *because* it's speculative rather than in spite of it. Concretely: we had one proposal that was boring and well-measured, with a tight confidence interval on its effect — call it "improves some number of lives in the low thousands, pretty certainly." And we had another that was a research-and-policy thing with a long causal story about nudging the very far future, where the upside depends on numbers nobody in the chat has any grip on: how many beings might exist downstream, how good their lives could get, whether our influence persists or washes out. Every time we tried to be rigorous — "okay, but what's the actual probability that whole chain holds?" — the probability came down, and whoever was rooting for that proposal would say something like "fine, but if it does hold the stakes are bigger than we wrote down," and the product just... didn't move. Or it went up. It felt like squeezing a balloon: push on the probability and the payoff swells somewhere else. A friend of mine who's thinking of switching careers to work on a long-shot cause ran the identical argument on herself — one-in-ten-thousand chance of affecting 10^12 future lives — and then admitted to me privately that she invented the 10^12, and that if she'd written 10^9 nothing would have looked compelling, and that nobody can tell her which number is closer to right.

So: is there an established concept this is an instance of? I've seen "Pascal's mugging" and "fanaticism" thrown around in this general neighbourhood, and someone once muttered "cluelessness" at me, but that last one sounds like it's about not being able to predict knock-on effects at all, whereas here the causal story is relatively clean — the trouble is the *size* of the prize, and specifically that the size is unbounded in a way that seems inversely coupled to how well we understand the mechanism, so that EV-maximising systematically selects for whatever we understand least. I also have a half-memory of something about the St. Petersburg game and unbounded expectations, which feels adjacent but not the same thing, since in that puzzle the payoff schedule is handed to you up front and the problem is that nobody would pay much to play. I don't just want a label to paste into our meeting notes — I want to know whether what I've described is one phenomenon or three stuck together, what the nearest established concepts are and exactly where each of them stops fitting, and whether the balloon-squeezing bit is a real structural feature of EV reasoning or just an artefact of how we're doing our spreadsheets. If it really is unnamed, then coin something for it, define it tightly enough that I could tell whether a given case is one, and give me the boundary cases: does it still bite when the payoff is merely astronomically large but bounded (say by how much future there could physically be) rather than literally unbounded, does it work symmetrically on the downside (a tiny chance of enormous harm), and what's predicted when *both* options on the table are equally murky? Please argue it out rather than sending me to read things — I'll be reading your answer on my phone between shifts and I can't chase down references.
