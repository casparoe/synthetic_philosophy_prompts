I have no formal training in philosophy or in psychiatry. I read about this on my own, mostly because I volunteer as a moderator on a peer-support forum for adults who first received a psychiatric diagnosis in middle age, and I keep watching the same argument restart there: one person says the diagnosis finally named something that had been there all along, another says the diagnosis gave them a shape to be unhappy in, and a third says the argument is beside the point because the label gets them accommodations and that is what matters. I encountered the phrase the looping effect somewhere — the idea that people sorted under a description change in response to being sorted, and that the description then changes in response to the people — and it struck me that this might be the hinge the whole argument turns on. I want to build something better than the argument, so I am writing a self-test for the forum wiki.

What I need from you is the instrument and the classificatory scheme behind it: about twelve questions, each a short concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key sorting answer patterns onto the accounts people actually hold, plus the tree the key comes from, each node labeled with the single claim that separates it from its siblings. Roughly eight leaves at most; if you add one, say whether anyone really holds it or whether it is only a cell in the space.

Three constraints on the questions. First, every case must be a self-contained hypothetical — a newly proposed category in an imaginary clinic, a description that becomes available to a person halfway through their life, a category whose members start behaving differently once it exists — so that nothing turns on facts about actual diagnostic manuals, actual disorders, or their history, since my readers (and I) cannot look any of that up reliably. Second, no question may ask whether some category is real, natural, constructed, or useful in so many words, and no answer option may name a position; questions must be about what happens in the case. Third, most of my readers have themselves been diagnosed, so no question's diagnostic value should depend on whether the reader answers from the inside or the outside.

The distinctions I want doing the separating work, several of which I made up because I could not find them named: whether the pattern was there before the description arrived or arrived with it; whether the loop runs mainly through a person's own self-description, through how other people treat them, or through institutions that record and fund them (I suspect these three pull apart but I cannot show it); whether adding a description raises the number of people who have the thing, lowers it, or leaves the number about the same while changing who is counted; and what I call the retirement test — whether anyone has ever left the category, by what route, and whether the category has any built-in way of dissolving. That last one matters to me because of a position I hold and cannot find a name for: that a diagnosis is a wager on a loop, adopted provisionally, whose success is measured by how far it lets you eventually stop using it, including the outcome where the kind disappears. Please tell me whether the space has a cell for that or which existing leaf it collapses into.

I also want the other obvious leaves locatable — the discovery view, the looping-constructivist view, the view that the kind is real but not independent of us, the instrumentalist one, and the skeptical one that says these categories are administrative fictions. Finally: mark the cells of the logical space that are empty and say briefly why (incoherent, unattractive, or simply unoccupied), flag any pair of leaves the instrument cannot actually separate rather than forcing a distinction, and add a short closing note on what the test cannot tell a reader — in particular, whether it can say anything at all about whether their own diagnosis was correct.
