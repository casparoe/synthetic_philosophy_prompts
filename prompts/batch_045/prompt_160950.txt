Most states that come to terms with a large, state-committed wrong settle into the same institutional shape, and I want to know what that shape presupposes.

The criminal part of the settlement insists that guilt is strictly personal: only identifiable individuals can be convicted, and the trials are held long after the fact precisely so that the defendants are named persons rather than a regime. The financial part then treats the polity as a single continuing agent that owes money, and it seems to do so in ways that pull in different directions. Compensation runs to the descendants of victims as well as to victims themselves; it is funded from general taxation, including the taxes of citizens who arrived long after the wrong and of citizens whose own ancestors were among its victims; and it takes the form of an indefinitely renewable series of negotiated settlements and funds rather than a debt with a discharge date. One such polity also offers to restore the citizenship of people descended from those it stripped of citizenship a lifetime earlier. Yet no such settlement has ever been capitalized by a levy on the descendants of perpetrators, or on the churches, universities, firms, and ministries that carried out the wrong and still exist. And where a democracy has compensated those it wrongfully interned in wartime, the payment has gone only to internees alive on the date of the statute, with nothing passing to their children — while the European case pays by descent.

Please reconstruct the implicit theory. In particular:

1. **The persistence of the debtor.** Does the practice strictly require that the present state is numerically the same entity as the wrongdoing state, or is it licensed by something weaker: succession to an estate, the retention of unjustly acquired holdings, or the continuing benefit of institutions that outlived the regime? What does that claim have to do, given that two polities carved out of the same defeated state took opposite lines, one insisting on continuity in order to assume the obligation and the other insisting that it had been a victim and therefore owed nothing?

2. **Does inherited guilt enter at all?** Can the whole package be reconstructed without any notion of transmissible guilt — as the ongoing duty of a collectivity that still holds and administers what was taken, or as the kind of political liability that Jaspers argued every member of a polity bears regardless of what he or she did — and if so, why is the obligation discharged from the treasury and never from members as such?

3. **Why descent?** If eligibility really tracks transmitted harm (lost inheritances, disrupted families, continuing structural disadvantage), then descent is only a proxy, and by the practice's own logic the affluent descendant and the unrelated person equally damaged should be treated alike. Is the descent condition strictly presupposed or merely convenient, and what would a settlement built on transmitted harm rather than transmitted membership look like?

4. **What sort of obligation is the payment?** A debt that can in principle be paid off, an act of corrective justice, an expressive or commemorative act, or an open-ended duty of care? The renegotiated, never-discharged form seems to rule at least one of these out; say which, and what would have to change in the practice if it were replaced by the next-best candidate.

5. **The asymmetry.** The paying side is constituted with no lineage test at all — residence, taxation, subjection to law — while the receiving side is constituted by lineage. Are these two different conceptions of what makes someone a member of a group, and can a single theory of collective responsibility use both without incoherence?

6. **A test case.** Imagine the settlement contained a fixed discharge date after which no further claims lie, and separately an opt-out for current taxpayers who can show that neither they nor their ancestors belonged to the polity at the time of the wrong. My instinct is that defenders of the existing practice would reject the first but ought, on their own principles, to accept the second — and that in fact they would resist both. Is that instinct right, and what does the resistance reveal about which notion of the collective is actually doing the work?

I am interested in the difference between what this pattern strictly presupposes, what it merely fits well with, and where it is underdetermined so that several rival theories would license it equally — not in a survey of who has said what.
