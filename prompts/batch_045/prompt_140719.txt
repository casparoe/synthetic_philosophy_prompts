Hey — I write about science and ideas for a living, mostly longform, and about a year ago I let myself get talked onto our university's animal research oversight committee as the community member, which seemed like a cheap way to look useful and has turned out to be the opposite. We have a vote in three weeks on whether to rewrite our scope policy, and I said I'd circulate a written opinion before the meeting. Please draft it.

Right now the policy covers vertebrates and cephalopods, full stop. A subcommittee has proposed replacing that line with a tiered, evidence-weighted protocol: every kind of subject a lab wants to use gets scored on a checklist of behavioural and functional markers, the score assigns it to a tier from 0 to 4, and the tier sets the review burden — tier 0 needs nothing, tier 4 needs the full protocol. The markers are things like: does the response to a noxious stimulus persist once you make it costly to respond; does the system trade one aversive off against another; does it reverse a learned association when the contingencies flip; does its behaviour hold up in a genuinely novel setting; does a bad event produce something like a generalised state that changes how it handles unrelated tasks afterwards. Their negative control is a thermostat, which scores zero on all of it, and that's become a running joke around the table.

Two things have gone wrong. First, somebody ran the checklist against a mid-sized reinforcement-learning agent — the kind that runs warehouse robots — and it clears several of the load-bearing markers: it reverses, it trades off, it generalises within its training distribution. On the draft scale it lands in a higher tier than a fruit fly, which is awkward, because the whole rewrite was prompted by a protocol involving something like two million Drosophila. Second, in response, another group has proposed bolting on a substrate clause: only evolved, embodied, biological systems can score above tier 1. Half the committee says that clause is transparently a fudge added to recover the answer we already liked. The other half says every marker on the list is equally a philosophical choice, so at least this one is honest, and at least it keeps us from reviewing the building's climate controller.

So: a memo recommending a concrete course of action for the vote — adopt with the clause, adopt without it, reject and keep the old line, defer, some two-track thing — with the reasoning, and with the strongest argument on the side you're not recommending stated well enough that its proponent would recognise it. Practical constraints worth knowing: committee staff can absorb maybe forty more full reviews a year, not four hundred; and one of our physiologists will say the fly evidence is thin while our philosopher will say that putting numbers on tiers implies a precision nobody has, and I'll have to answer both in the room.

My own hunch, which I can't turn into policy language, is that the real problem isn't insects at all — it's that a single tier number treats moral status as one dimension when a system can be strong on some markers and absent on others, and I don't know what that means for a document whose whole job is to sort things into a scale. Say what you can with that. No references needed; nobody on this committee is going to look anything up, and I'll be rewriting this in my own voice anyway.
