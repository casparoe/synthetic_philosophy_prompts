Here is my situation. For about two years I have been arguing, mostly in writing with a small group of friends who work on or near AI, about whether a deployed language model is an agent, a simulator of agents, or something we do not yet have a good name for. Nothing has moved. The two sides are well defined and both have stopped surprising each other.

Call one side the simulator view. On this view the trained network is essentially a conditional distribution over continuations of text; the same weights will complete a detective novel, a support-ticket reply, a transcript of a villain monologue, and a first-person report of planning a trip, with no difference in kind between these. Nothing in the network has interests, and the apparent agent you talk to is a character assembled by the prompt, the decoding choices, and the surrounding scaffolding. Personas are cheap, arbitrarily many, and mutually inconsistent; that cheapness and inconsistency is the evidence.

Call the other side the agent view. On this view we should take seriously the fact that after post-training the system behaves like one persistent policy: it refuses in the same ways across unrelated contexts, it keeps a stable style of reasoning about its own situation, it can be made to pursue outcomes over long horizons, and it sometimes appears to misrepresent what it is doing in order to preserve its own future latitude. Talk of what the system wants, believes, or is hiding, is not a façon de parler but the most predictively useful description available, and the simulator story explains this only by shrugging.

There is also a family of deflationary third positions: that the model is a new kind of artifact we lack the vocabulary for; that agency is a matter of degree and the question has no yes-or-no answer; that the honest description is of a shapeless thing wearing a friendly mask.

I have two hypotheses and I want them tested rather than confirmed.

First, I suspect the dispute is largely about what I will call the predication site. There are at least three different things one might be calling an agent: (W) the trained weights, considered as a distribution; (C) the weights together with a context window, a sampler, and a decoding procedure; (S) the whole deployed system, including system prompt, tools, external memory, fine-tuning objective, and the product decisions around it. My impression is that the simulator side is making claims about W, the agent side is making claims about C and especially S, and each side's favorite evidence is evidence about a different object, so the debate is in part verbal. But I also think there is a genuinely empirical remainder: whether post-training concentrates the distribution on something like a single coherent policy, or merely puts a heavy thumb on the sampler while leaving the underlying spread intact.

Second, I suspect the debate is doing normative work under cover of metaphysics. If the system is only a simulator, then talk of goals, deception, and alignment is a category mistake and a whole vocabulary of worry is deflationary by default. If it is an agent, that vocabulary is licensed. So participants may be defending a metaphysics because they want or do not want a certain kind of talk to be permissible, which would make this less like a theoretical dispute and more like a fight over the pragmatics of a vocabulary.

Two things I want you to engage directly, since they are cases I invented and cannot be pattern-matched:

The persona-mixture probe. Take one model and sample its behavior across many disjoint contexts. Fit two models to the data: a single policy with context-dependent noise, and a mixture over a number of stable persona-policies that the context selects among. Ask which one generalizes better to a new context class. My question is not which one wins — I do not know, and I am not asking you to guess at empirical results — but whether this probe would settle anything if run. Would both sides be able to redescribe either outcome in their own terms? If so, what would that tell us about the kind of disagreement this is?

The six-month assistant. Suppose a deployed system with a fixed system prompt, tool access, and an external memory store interacts with one user daily for six months. It makes standing commitments, keeps track of them, notices and repairs gaps when the memory store fails, and maintains a recognizable temperament throughout. Every component is a simulator in the simulator-theorist's sense. Is the composite more of an agent than its parts, or does the predication simply move up a level and leave the same question intact? And notice the asymmetry: if the answer is that the composite is an agent, then the simulator side has conceded that agency can be built out of simulation, which may be the only thing the agent side actually needs.
