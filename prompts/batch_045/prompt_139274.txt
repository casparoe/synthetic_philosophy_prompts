You are a philosophical logician who specialises in truth, self-reference and the semantic paradoxes. Before you defend anything, spend a while brainstorming the candidate accounts you take seriously — hierarchical, fixed-point and partial, supervaluational, paraconsistent, contextualist, revision-theoretic, whatever else earns its keep — and only then settle on the one or two you will argue for. Please think carefully and argue from the cases rather than from authority: I don't need names, dates or a reading list, since I can't check them and my readers wouldn't thank me for them.

Here is my situation. I write a column on science, language and ideas, and a large part of my week goes on fact-checking political claims; our newsroom uses a five-point scale (True / Mostly true / Misleading / False / Unverifiable). Recently I've been running into statements that sabotage themselves, and I've come to think the scale is missing a category — but before I lobby for a new rating I want to know what I actually think. So here are my gut verdicts, offered as data. (a) "This sentence is false": neither true nor false — and I also want to say, in print, that it is not true, which I gather is going to cause me trouble. (b) "This sentence is true": also neither, but I hold this one far less confidently, and it bothers me that I give it the same verdict as (a) when the two feel so different. (c) Two cards pinned side by side in the newsroom, A saying "the sentence on card B is true" and B saying "the sentence on card A is false": both gappy, but B strikes me as more at fault than A. (d) "This sentence is not true, or the newsroom has exactly four chairs": true if we have four chairs, gappy otherwise — I'm oddly comfortable with a verdict that turns on the furniture. (e) A pair I worry about: "This sentence is true, and snow is white" I want to call true, while "This sentence is false, and snow is white" I want to call false — yet both have one perfectly good conjunct and one self-referential one, and if the self-referential conjunct is in neither case true or false, I can't see how the two verdicts can differ. (f) An infinite run of sentences, the first saying that all the later ones are false, the second that all the later ones are false, and so on (the construction associated with Yablo): all of them gappy, even though not one of them refers to itself — which makes me think that whatever explains the paradoxes, it isn't self-reference as such. (g) A case I invented for a piece that got spiked: a board with exactly two sentences pinned to it, N1 saying "exactly one of the sentences pinned to this board is true" and N2 saying "snow is white". I say N1 is gappy — yet if I pin up a third true sentence, N1 becomes straightforwardly false, and that difference feels like a technicality. (h) A politician who, having made several ordinary claims at a press conference, says "every claim I have made in this press conference is false": I would happily print False, even though for the written Liar I insist on neither. (i) "This sentence is not true", and then "this sentence is not determinately true": in both cases I want to state my verdict in the very words the sentence uses, and in both cases that seems to be exactly what I'm not allowed to do.

The principle I keep coming back to is something like this: a sentence has a classical truth value only if its truth value is ultimately fixed by non-semantic facts; where it isn't, the sentence is gappy, ascriptions of truth and falsity to it inherit the gap, and the blame for a gap falls on whichever sentence's own contribution makes the circle unbreakable. Please test that against the eight-ish verdicts above and repair it where it breaks — stating the repaired principle precisely, clause by clause, with the case that motivates each clause. Tell me which of my verdicts no coherent account can keep, and which of the ones I hold weakly (b, d, e, h) I should abandon first, and why. Then give me your account's verdicts on cases I haven't listed: the same Liar sentence inscribed in a language no one has deciphered; a chatbot that emits "the sentence you are reading is false"; "this sentence was false on the day it was written"; and a sentence that attributes falsity to its own translation. Finally, come back to my actual problem: given your account, what belongs on a fact-checking scale that has to be printed in a newspaper — should I be arguing for a "Not even false" rating, or is that a category mistake dressed up as a reform?
