I'm in a second-year formal epistemology course, and our study group has spent three weeks going in circles about Dutch book arguments. Everyone in the group accepts the basic construction — that a set of betting quotients violating the probability axioms commits you to a package of bets each of which you'd happily accept and which together guarantee a net loss — and everyone also accepts that this is, at most, the beginning of an argument rather than the end of one. Where we fracture is on what the construction is supposed to establish, and my own hypothesis is that we are not really disagreeing about the theorem at all but about which of several different questions each of us is answering with it: some of us hear it as a claim about practical self-defeat, some as a claim about a distinctively epistemic kind of error, some as merely a vivid way of displaying a formal defect. So rather than another round of argument, I'd like a classification instrument: a decision tree that starts from "the sure-loss construction is sound" and finishes at a named position about what it shows.

Concretely, I want roughly eight branch nodes and ten to twelve leaves, with each node phrased as a question my group could answer without knowing which view it leads to — no node should just say "do you accept the Dutch book theorem?" or "is probabilism true?". Please build it from the logical space first (here are the questions, here are the answer combinations they permit), then locate the actual positions in that space, and flag any combination nobody occupies with one sentence on why: incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. The cases I want used as the diagnostic material are the ones we actually fought over, so please build the nodes around them: (a) the vanishing bookie, who offers you the package once, while you sleep, for a loss of one cent you will never notice and never learn about; (b) the charity bookie, whose guaranteed "loss" consists of a compulsory donation to a cause you'd have given that exact amount to anyway, so that money goes down and your utility goes up; (c) the two-sided bookie, who must both buy and sell at whatever prices you quote, versus the one-sided bookie free to pick whichever direction of each bet suits them, together with the follow-up about whether a set of acceptable prices must be convex; (d) the infinite bookie, who needs you to accept countably many bets before the loss materializes; (e) the chance bookie, who only offers bets on events whose objective chances you already know; and (f) the memory-wipe case, where you know in advance that you'll be made to forget something and then offered bets, and are choosing an update rule now. My guess is that (a) and (b) are what separate views that read the bets as constitutive of credence from views that read them as merely diagnostic, but use your own judgment about which distinctions actually cut.

Two more constraints on the output. First, the leaves should include, at minimum: some form of strict probabilism about precise credences; an imprecise or permissive alternative; a view on which the norm is diachronic and constrains updating rules as well as current credences; a view on which it constrains only current credences and says updating is a policy choice rather than a belief; a view that adds a chance-credence requirement on top of coherence; a view that permits non-additive credences and blocks the construction by changing the betting setup; and at least one leaf for the sceptic who thinks the whole style of argument establishes nothing normative. Second, please mark the pairs of leaves your tree genuinely cannot separate — I suspect the pragmatic and the accuracy-based vindications of the same coherence norm may collapse into each other once all six cases are answered, and I'd rather see that admitted than have a fake node invented to split them. Nobody in the group has read the secondary literature, so give each leaf a short descriptive label and put the conventional name in parentheses only where you're confident one exists.
