I'm a third-year PhD student in machine learning, and on Friday I'm giving a twenty-five minute talk with a fifteen minute discussion slot to our department's new "digital minds" reading group. My thesis is deflationary, and I want to be ready for the discussion rather than survive it. The view I'm defending has three moves. First, the reward signal in deep RL does not occupy the functional role of valence: reward is set by the environment designer or, in RLHF, by a second learned model's guess about human preference, and it is not in general what the agent's own computations are tracking. Second, the more tempting candidate — the reward-prediction-error quantity that actually drives the weight update, i.e. the temporal-difference error or whatever generalized advantage estimate stands in for it — fails twice over: it is algorithm-relative, because you can alter it while leaving behaviour and even the optimal policy fixed (adding a shaping term of the form γΦ(s′) − Φ(s) provably leaves the optimal policy unchanged, and switching between TD and Monte-Carlo policy-gradient updates changes the error signal without changing what the agent is trying to do), so a prediction-error criterion makes "what it is like" depend on an implementation choice that no behavioural or functional test can recover; and it has no subject, because in a modern training run the errors from thousands of parallel environment copies are averaged into one gradient step, and in off-policy methods they are computed from transitions collected by an earlier policy and replayed long afterwards. Third, I want to embrace what looks like a reductio: a deployed policy, receiving no reward and computing no prediction error at all, is on my account phenomenally inert — the moral story, if there is one, is about the training process rather than about the trained artifact. I also owe the room an answer about reward clipping: if the magnitude of the error signal is capped at ±1 for optimizer stability, then on the prediction-error view the intensity of the alleged suffering is fixed by a hyperparameter, which I take to be a sign that the view is tracking something merely computational.

The audience is about fourteen people. Roughly half are RL researchers; one of them works on RLHF and is quietly sympathetic to the idea that we may owe something to future systems, and another is the person who will notice instantly if I get a detail about off-policy updates wrong. Two are philosophers of mind: one is a self-described illusionist about phenomenal consciousness who will think the word "valence" is doing no work in my argument, and one works on animal consciousness and is used to inferring suffering from behavioural and functional markers such as analgesic self-administration and trade-off behaviour, and will want to know why a criterion that we could never apply to a pig is supposed to be the right one here. One is a systems neuroscientist who works on dopamine and decision-making, and who is likely to say that the phasic dopamine signal standardly interpreted as a reward prediction error tracks wanting or incentive salience rather than liking — that the hedonic impact of a reward is mediated by a dissociable opioid and endocannabinoid system — so that in picking the prediction error as my candidate for valence I have picked the one quantity that the empirical literature most clearly distinguishes from hedonic experience. The rest are graduate students who will mostly rally to whichever objection sounds most confident.

What I need is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view — assume the talk has already been given and don't summarize my position back to me. Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this specific room, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each attributed to the kind of person who would raise it and phrased the way that person would phrase it: the neuroscientist in the vocabulary of prediction error versus hedonic impact, the illusionist in the vocabulary of whether there is a further fact to be had, the animal-consciousness person in the vocabulary of markers and of whose suffering we already take ourselves to know about, the RL people in the vocabulary of what is invariant across algorithms and what is merely implementation. For each objection give me a two-minute reply, one thing I should concede up front, one thing I should refuse to concede, and a short note on where my reply is soft. Tell me which joint in my own argument is weakest and that I should defend rather than hope they miss it — my guess is the subject-counting step, since it seems to presuppose that subjects must be countable and something structurally similar might tell against ordinary humans, but argue me out of that if it's wrong. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, which I suspect is the precautionary one: if my own account makes the moral cost scale with total timesteps across parallel environments and across every training run in the field, why doesn't expected-value reasoning swamp my metaphysics? And give me the silly-sounding version of my view that will be hard to shake in the room, so I can defuse it myself before someone else does. Please write it as a usable prep sheet — short bullet-style entries, a few sentences each, nothing I'd have to read off a page — and phrase everything in American English. Don't give me citations, bibliographies, or suggestions for further reading: I have to answer in the room from what I actually understand, and I'd much rather have the arguments in your own words than pointers to papers.
