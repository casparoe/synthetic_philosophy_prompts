I'm in the second year of a comparative-politics PhD, and for roughly a year a small reading group of ours — two political theorists, two area specialists, one survey methodologist — has been arguing about whether performance-based claims to rule are claims to legitimacy at all, and we have not moved an inch. Grant us the following rough characterization of the cases, because I don't want to re-litigate case details: a ruling party that has delivered sustained growth, public order, and a very large reduction in material deprivation, that is fairly explicit that this record is the basis on which it asks not to be contested, and many of whose citizens, when asked, volunteer prosperity and stability rather than elections or tradition as their reason for going along; and, alongside it, a city-state whose governing party has long treated elections less as an open contest for authorization than as a periodic retrospective audit of a mandate grounded in delivery. The two camps are perfectly stable. One insists that legitimacy is evidenced by, or simply consists in, acceptance by the governed, and that if what they accept you for is delivery, then delivery is your legitimation; requiring electoral authorization as the criterion is, on this view, just smuggling one parochial conception of legitimacy in as the definition of the concept. The other insists that what delivery buys is compliance, purchase, or conditional acquiescence rather than legitimacy: legitimacy is a normative status, a standing entitlement to be obeyed and to have one's demands taken as binding, and no volume of output generates it unless the governed have a continuing role in settling what counts as acceptable output — so a ruler who must keep re-buying consent is a ruler who is paying, not ruling.

What makes the impasse frustrating rather than merely difficult is that nobody in the group disputes the facts, and each camp cheerfully grants the other's strongest case. We agree about the growth and poverty numbers, about the survey distributions, about rising education and rising expectations, and about the fact that procedurally authorized regimes with bad performance also lose acceptance. The delivery camp concedes that performance-based acceptance is conditional and can evaporate; the authorization camp concedes that being well governed is something subjects genuinely value and not merely a bribe. Yet a year in, the same four moves recur.

My own hypothesis, which I want tested rather than flattered, is that we are running two different questions together under one word — 'is this regime in fact accepted, and on what basis' versus 'does it have the standing to demand' — and that the conceptual quarrel is actually being driven by a hidden empirical question neither side has isolated: whether performance-based acceptance is self-renewing or self-consuming over time. I've started calling this the inherited-credit problem. An authorization-based claim appears to attach to an office and to survive a bad quarter, because the office-holder's entitlement is not re-earned by results; a delivery-based claim appears to attach to whoever is credited for a particular run of results, has to be re-earned continuously against a baseline that each success resets upward, and is therefore uniquely exposed to a bad quarter it cannot attribute elsewhere. If that is right, the two camps may disagree less about legitimacy than about what the object of the claim is supposed to be — a regime, a government, or an administrative record.

So I want a diagnosis of the impasse, not a verdict and not a survey: is this a verbal dispute about 'legitimacy'; a clash of starting intuitions neither side can argue for; a disagreement about standards of evidence, say between survey self-reports, revealed behaviour, and counterfactuals about what would happen if delivery faltered; a mismatch about which cases count as data; two questions pursued under one heading; or a sign that the question is ill-posed? Argue for one of these using what each camp actually says, and tell me whether the inherited-credit story names a genuine hidden empirical question or is only a redescription dressed up as one. Then, given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress — a distinction, a piece of evidence, a reframing, a change of question — or argue honestly that no progress should be expected and why, and tell me whether we should abandon the word 'legitimacy' in this conversation entirely. Please don't give me readings or a map of the literature; we've read it, and what I want is the structure of the disagreement.
