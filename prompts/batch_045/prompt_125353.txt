I co-convene a small reading group that meets in a letterpress studio — half type designers, half philosophers and cognitive scientists. We have spent a term on theories of concepts (the classical definitional view, prototypes, exemplar models) using the alphabet as our running example, and the final session is on the question the term kept circling: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? I want to close with a villanelle, and I want the form itself to carry the argument rather than decorate it.

The reason a villanelle seems right: its two refrains keep returning, and each time they return into a different tercet, among different neighbouring lines. A repeated line is to its appearances what a letterform is to its instances, and the problem of what makes it the same line is the same problem in miniature. So please build the poem so that this is visible.

- Let the first refrain carry the *letter*: whatever makes a glyph an 'a' rather than an 'o', a 'd', a 'q' or a 'g'. I want this to be structural and relational rather than a shape — a closed counter, a bowl, a stem — and above all the fact that an 'a' neither rises nor falls: it is the bowl-and-stem that stays between the baseline and the x-height, which is what distinguishes it from the 'd' that ascends and the 'q' that descends. If you can also get in that it is the slot it fills (a reader shown "c\_t" in a face they know hears "cat"), so much the better.
- Let the second refrain carry the *spirit*: what this 'a' shares with the rest of its alphabet — the angle of stress, the ratio of thick to thin, how the terminals finish, how far the counters open and the spacing loosens as the size drops. The 'a' is where a face declares itself. Keep the traditional rhyme between the two refrains, and let that rhyme survive every change you make to them.
- No refrain may repeat verbatim. Each return should differ from its immediately previous appearance in exactly one word, and the changed word should shift the medium, material or scale rather than the claim — chisel, broad-nib pen, punch, bitmap, seven bars of light, a child's pencil, the opened counters of a caption-size cut, the closed ones of a poster. The constraint is that a reader should still be able to name the line as the same line at the end, and to reconstruct the first version from the last, the way a reader reconstructs an 'a' from a degraded glyph. That is the thesis: invariance is not sameness of parts.
- Somewhere keep apart the two ways a drawing can go wrong — I have been calling them the wrong-letter failure and the wrong-spirit failure. A geometric sans 'a' dropped into a line of old-style serif is read at once and still sounds wrong: the letter is right, the company is not. A blackletter 'a' shown to a reader who does not know the hand is not read as an 'a' at all; and the single-storey shape that is an 'a' in italic is, as Latin alpha, a different letter in the phonetic alphabet. Letter-identity survives changes that style-identity does not, and I would like that asymmetry to be visible in the poem rather than asserted by it.
- In the final quatrain let the two refrains appear in their most diverged forms, adjacent for the first and only time, and resolve nothing between them. The honest ending is that you cannot state the invariant without appealing to the 'a's that have been drawn, and you cannot point to the spirit except as whatever the drawn letters have in common — both at once, with neither giving way.

Form and finish: a strict villanelle, nineteen lines, five tercets and a quatrain, two rhyme sounds. Use real letterform vocabulary — counter, bowl, stem, terminal, aperture, storey, x-height, stress — and use it correctly; a designer should not wince. No proper names of typefaces or of philosophers; kinds of script, materials and writing systems are fine. No preface, no glossary, and no note afterwards explaining what the poem was arguing: someone who has never thought about concepts should be able to read it as a poem, and someone who has should be able to tell which refrain is doing which work.
