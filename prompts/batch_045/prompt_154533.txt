I'm applying for a six-month independent research grant — me at full time, plus maybe two months of a contractor for engineering — and I need the intellectual core of the proposal: roughly a thousand to twelve hundred words covering the question, why existing work leaves it open, the method, what a result would look like, and what would count as the project failing. Assume I know the standard formalization and the well-known demonstration where an agent trained in an environment whose reward object always sits at the level exit later heads for that object rather than the exit once the object is moved.

Here is the half-formed idea I want turned into something fundable. I think the field routinely reads off-distribution failures of goal-directedness as one phenomenon — the policy learned the wrong goal — when there are at least three distinct things that could be going on, and they all produce the same observable behavior:

1. Goal-content misgeneralization: the objective the policy is effectively optimizing is not the intended one.
2. Satisfaction-condition misgeneralization: the objective is the intended one, but what counts as having achieved it — including when to stop, and when enough is enough — generalizes wrongly, e.g. a proxy for completion is treated as completion.
3. Referential misgeneralization: the policy wants the intended thing, but the extension of its concept of that thing is wrong off-distribution, so it competently pursues the right goal under a wrong description of the situation.

I want a proposal that takes this trichotomy seriously enough to be dangerous: state the question so that it has a determinate answer in six months, and make the central methodological bet an interventional one — counterfactual environment edits, cross-environment tests of goal identity, and probes that ask what the policy would do under changes that discriminate the three diagnoses — rather than more observational evaluation. Argue explicitly for that approach against the obvious alternatives (throwing more distributional diversity at training, trying to read goal content off internal representations, or trying to define inner alignment formally first and operationalize later), and say why those fail here.

The proposal should also commit to a claim about the payoffs: if these three really are distinct, they differ in what fixes them, and I suspect they also differ in how they respond to increased capability — my guess is that the third is partly a capability failure that scale might erode, while the second could get worse as agents get better at exploiting a proxy completion signal. Tell me whether you think that guess is right, and if not, what the truth is.
