I used to handle workplace investigations for a mid-sized company, and I've been reading about punishment informally ever since, mostly because of an argument I keep having with a friend in my discussion group. He says that public shaming and deplatforming are just boycotts, that a boycott is never punishment, and that therefore there is nothing here to analyse. I think that is too quick: a boycott withdraws people's custom, whereas what happened in the two cases I keep coming back to withdrew someone's capacity to be heard at all, and their livelihood along with it. So I would like a proper case analysis rather than a verdict.

Here are the two episodes, with the facts as I remember them, since I don't want any of this to turn on details I may have wrong. In December 2013 a PR executive named Justine Sacco, who had a couple of hundred followers, tweeted a joke about AIDS and Africa shortly before boarding a long flight to South Africa; people with very large audiences picked it up, it spread under a hashtag while she was in the air, and by the time she landed she had been denounced worldwide and dismissed by her employer. Five years later, in 2018, several large platforms removed the broadcaster Alex Jones and his programs within days of each other, after years of his asserting that the Sandy Hook shooting was a hoax and of harassment directed at the families of the children killed there; he was afterwards found liable in defamation suits brought by those families. In both cases the outcome was produced by a diffuse crowd and then executed by an institution, and in neither case did anyone announce a sentence.

Please run both through the two accounts of punishment I have managed to piece together: the desert-based one, where the point is that the sanction should fit the wrong and be imposed because it is deserved, and the communicative or expressive one, where punishment is meant to address a wrongdoer as a member of the community, condemn what was done, and leave a way back in. I want the mapping done carefully rather than gestured at: who the punisher is and on what standing; what the harm consists in when both an audience and an income are removed, one harm or two; what would count as having served it, and who would have the authority to say so. I have been working with three rough labels of my own and I would like them tested properly rather than indulged. The authorship gap: each participant's contribution is small enough that nobody is really doing the punishing and nobody can remit it. The expiry problem: there is no sentence length and there is a permanent, searchable record, so the sanction resembles banishment more than a term. Amplification luck: how severe the outcome is tracks how viral the episode went, which has very little to do with the wrong itself. For each, tell me whether it is a defect in an imperfect punishment system or evidence that the wrong category is being applied altogether, and if it is the latter, what the nearest genuine categories are, how each fits, and where each breaks down.

Then turn it around, because I don't want the theory forced to speak where it is silent. Say plainly where it has nothing to say: can a theory of punishment say anything at all about a sanction that no one authored, no one calibrated, and no one can end, or is that simply outside what such a theory was built for? And say what the episodes do to the theory. My sense is that Sacco's case is the one that should embarrass a desert theorist, since nothing about the wrong tracks the outcome, while Jones's case is the one that should embarrass my friend, since there the sanction looks roughly proportionate, was aimed at real and identifiable victims, and came after long notice and many chances to stop. Am I right about which case pressures which view, and does the pair together show that decentralized social sanction has no proportionality mechanism, or only an unreliable one? One last thing: if this kind of sanction can count as punishment at all, what would a defensible version of it require — some procedure, a stated term, somebody with authority to declare it served — and does spelling out those requirements convert it into something the theory already has a name for?
