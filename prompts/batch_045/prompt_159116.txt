I'm working on a piece about whether rationality says anything about how one ought to conduct oneself over time, as opposed to merely about whether one's beliefs and desires cohere at any given moment. I've come to accept what I take to be the standard money-pump argument for a diachronic requirement: that what makes a cyclic or otherwise dynamically incoherent pattern of preferences irrational is precisely the availability of a sequence of trades, each of which the agent prefers at the time, that returns them to where they began but poorer. On this view the pump is not a vivid illustration of a wrongness established elsewhere; it is supposed to be what constitutes the wrongness, and rationality is thereby a genuine requirement across time rather than synchronic coherence plus means–end efficiency.

Tell me what would count against this. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to say which of them a defender of the view should concede rather than dismiss. In particular: does the resolute chooser — someone who settles on a plan in advance and then simply executes it — escape, or does that only relocate the requirement from occurrent preferences to plans? Does the argument need an exploiter who knows one's preferences and can frame offers, so that the verdict becomes a claim about vulnerability to one's environment rather than about rationality? Does it over-generate, condemning ordinary discounting of the future, or the refusal to pay to avoid being manipulated, in a way that should embarrass it? Does the analogy with Dutch-book arguments help or hurt, given that those are often read as representation theorems that merely display the costs of incoherence rather than constitute it? And is there anything the view predicts about actual choosers that a purely synchronic account of rationality would not?

Two further things. First, I suspect much of the argument's force depends on where cycles come from: a cycle generated by thresholds or rough equality, where each step moves to something the agent himself counts as no worse, may not be a loss in the relevant sense at all. Second, I would be most moved by an argument showing that the pump only bites against agents who have already accepted a quite demanding norm of self-governance. Please also tell me honestly whether the view has been qualified into something unfalsifiable, and whether that would matter. Write in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, as you would in a letter.
