"In the late 1970s two philosophers cooked up a little puzzle that finished off causal decision theory. You're in Damascus, you run into Death, and he tells you he's coming for you tomorrow. You can stay put, or you can buy a ticket to Aleppo. Death has never once been wrong about where somebody was going to be — he isn't guessing, he's seen how tomorrow turns out — so he is already in whichever city you end up in. Causal decision theory says your choice can't change where Death is, since he's already there, so it treats the two options as equally good and tells you it doesn't matter which you pick: heads you stay, tails you fly. Evidential decision theory says buy the ticket. Staying put would be evidence that Death is in Damascus, whereas fleeing is evidence he's somewhere else, and evidential decision theory cares about the news your action brings, not about what it causes. So the evidentialist gets on the plane and lives, the causalist shrugs and dies — and that, along with Newcomb's problem, which is the same case with boxes instead of cities, is why hardly anyone defends causal decision theory anymore."
