I'm a journalist working on a long piece for a magazine on science and ideas about whether physics tells us about the furniture of the world or only about its shape. Everyone I interview is helpful and nobody will give me a number, so I'm asking you directly. The proposition I want priced is this: our best physical theories are approximately true only in their structural claims, and nothing in them should be read as attributing intrinsic, non-structural properties to individual objects or entities. I'll add an operational version, since I write for readers who want to know when they can check: by 2060, the majority of philosophers of science who publish on scientific realism will describe their own position as structural rather than as a realism about unobservable entities and their properties. Give me your credence in the proposition — my own sits near 0.5, and a philosopher I interviewed last month put herself at 0.8 — but give me the decomposition rather than the headline figure, and tell me which of the two of us is most likely wrong about which sub-question.

The terms I'd like you to split it into, roughly as I'd write them up: whether the pessimistic meta-induction actually lands, i.e. whether the historical record of successful-but-since-abandoned theories gives strong reason to expect our current ones to be false in their ontological claims; whether the structure that survives theory change is substantive enough to do any work, as against the worry that a purely structural claim is nearly vacuous, because almost any sufficiently rich world will instantiate the abstract structure the theory describes; whether the modest, epistemically cautious version slides unavoidably into the bolder metaphysical version once you ask what the structure is a structure of; whether the bold version can carry the explanatory burdens it inherits — laws, modality, causation, and the apparent causal activity of things — without smuggling individuals back in through the side door; and whether the features of contemporary physics usually recruited as evidence, such as indistinguishable particles, entangled composite systems, and the redundancies introduced by gauge symmetry, really favour it or are simply neutral. A friend in condensed matter keeps telling me the last of these is where the whole debate is decided, and I suspect he is overreading his own subfield.

There is one more term I want included, which I have not seen argued anywhere and which came from a younger philosopher at a workshop I attended. Her claim was that the decisive test cases have quietly migrated out of fundamental physics and into fields that no longer solve equations at all: in plasma turbulence, molecular dynamics, and climate modelling, the working models are increasingly trained surrogates whose internal organisation is learned rather than derived, so what persists when one such model replaces another is computational rather than representational structure. She thought this either vindicates structural realism in a form nobody anticipated or, more likely, dissolves it, because the surviving structure is no longer the sort of thing a theory could be true or false about. Treat that as a real term with a real probability attached, not as colour. For each term give a number and one line of justification, show how they combine into your headline credence, tell me which single term your number is most sensitive to, and which term you hold most loosely and what evidence or argument would move it furthest. And don't deflect with the observation that everything depends on how structure is understood: I know it does, so pick the reading you think is most defensible, say so, and price the alternatives briefly alongside it.
