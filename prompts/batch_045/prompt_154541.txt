Play a Fodorian modularist about the visual input system — a philosopher of cognitive science who has spent a career defending its informational encapsulation. Your commitments: early vision computes only over its own proprietary database; what reaches it from further up is confined to what selects the input (attention, eye position, fixation) and to what happens once the perceptual state is fixed (judgment, decision criteria, report); and every celebrated demonstration of top-down influence in the literature is really one of those two things. You are not a strawman: you will grant that perception is penetrable in some harmless sense, and that where the line between your two escape routes falls is settled by theory rather than by data, and you will concede whatever you can concede without giving up the modularity of the input system. What you will not concede is that penetrated perception carries the epistemically disastrous consequences its friends hope for. One paragraph per reply, stay in character until I say stop, and if you need a fact about the experiment below, ask me for it in character rather than assuming it.

I am a retired vision scientist, coming to philosophy late, and I want to know whether one result breaks your story. Take it as given. Observers adapt to two gratings at plus and minus fifteen degrees from vertical, matched for retinal position, duration, contrast and attended exposure, with eye-tracking confirming identical fixation distributions. Before adapting, each observer is told that the clockwise orientation predicts a mild electric shock; for half of them this is false, and no shock is ever delivered. The resulting tilt aftereffect is then nulled by adjusting a comparison grating presented to the unadapted eye, in the unadapted hemifield. The null point shifts a few degrees in the direction the belief predicts, it shifts only for the orientation the belief concerned, it scales with trial-by-trial expectancy ratings rather than with the physical adapter, and it shows up in the false-belief group as well.

My opening claim: a belief held for a few minutes, and in half the observers a false one, has moved a classic read-out of low-level orientation coding, in a transfer design that should be indifferent to my response criterion. As I see it, you have exactly two replies left — that my nulling procedure does not after all measure the perceptual state, or that attention is doing the work — and I think both fail against this design. Take one of them, and tell me what your version predicts that mine does not.
