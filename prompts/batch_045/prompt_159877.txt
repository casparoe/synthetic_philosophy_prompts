Six weeks ago my co-founder and I shut down the company we had spent four years and most of our savings on, and the post-mortem has turned into a somewhat unwanted education in escalation of commitment. As penance, the incubator that housed us has asked us to run a forty-five minute session for about fifteen first-time founders on why intelligent people keep paying into failing projects. So the topic is not academic for me right now — but I want the session to be conceptual rather than confessional, and I'd rather you did not coach me about my own case. Treat me as an instructor preparing a session.

You are a philosopher of action and decision theorist who has also taught judgment and decision making to managers, and who has thought hard about where the rational-choice story and the psychological story come apart. Please think carefully and brainstorm before you commit: I would like, first, two or three candidate orderings for the session with the one thing each of them loses, and only then the ordering you recommend and why.

The plan I want has these parts.

One: the dependency structure. What has to be in place before what? In particular, I want the bookkeeping point (money already spent is gone and cannot be recovered) kept visibly distinct from the forward-looking point (whether the remaining cost is worth paying for the remaining chance), and I want to know which of the two has to be secured first and whether securing the first makes the second harder to teach. I also want escalation separated from neighbouring things it gets confused with: plain risk-seeking in the domain of losses, the option value of genuinely continuing, consistency as a virtue, and the sunk-cost slogan itself.

Two: the carrying example. I was going to use the dollar auction — a dollar sold to the highest bidder on the rule that the runner-up also pays what they bid — but I have two worries. First, it is a two-player game with a fixed prize and a locally rational next step, whereas the phenomenon I am trying to get at is single-agent: the person you are bidding against is, in some sense, your own earlier self. I want you to say whether that mismatch is fatal or whether it can be turned into the thing that makes the example work, and how. Second, I have been toying with two modifications and would like you to pick between them or reject both. In the bridge-round variant, at any point a bidder may bring in a backer who covers half of the next increment in exchange for half of the eventual prize. In the growing-pot variant, each bid adds to the prize rather than merely chasing it. Tell me what the carrying example must have and must lack if it is to do the work here — I suspect it must have a step that is locally rational, so nobody dismisses the escalator as a fool, and it must lack any pre-declared budget or fixed number of rounds, so the audience cannot lean on a rule instead of seeing the trap. Correct me if that is wrong.

Three: the misconception map, in the order these founders will actually form them, and where in the sequence each should be headed off. My candidate list, which you should cut down, re-order and add to: that the moral is simply ignore sunk costs, which they will over-apply and use to abandon projects whose remaining costs really are worth paying — the mirror-image error, and the one I most want prevented; that escalation is irrational by definition, which sits badly with each step of the auction being defensible; that perseverance is constitutive of being a founder, so that any anti-escalation message will be heard as an attack on their identity and defended against rather than examined; and that the fix is a pre-committed stop-loss, when the difficulty is that the threshold you would need to write down depends on information you only get later. There is also a notion I have started calling the completion gradient: the pull to continue seems to grow with the proportion of the project already finished, even while the absolute remaining cost is growing too. Tell me where that sits — a separate mechanism, a redescription of sunk cost, or a confusion of fraction-completed with probability of success — and whether it should be named out loud at this level or left implicit.

Four: the check. Give me one question to end on whose answer distinguishes someone who has the structure from someone who has absorbed a slogan, and tell me what a confidently wrong answer to it will sound like.

Five: what I should openly refuse to explain in forty-five minutes to this audience, and say so from the podium. I have in mind the formal apparatus of backward induction and subgame perfection, the shape of a prospect-theoretic value function, the self-justification and self-presentation literature, and above all any general rule for knowing when to quit — but tell me if I am cutting in the wrong place.
