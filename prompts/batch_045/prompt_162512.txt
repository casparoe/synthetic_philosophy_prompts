Ive been asked to do a forty-minute on-stage interview with a philosopher at a one-day thing next month, and I could use some help with the questions.

The background: I retired a few years ago from about thirty years in agrometeorology — satellite rainfall estimates, crop-yield modelling, drought early-warning indices, that sort of work — and roughly a year ago I started going to a philosophy reading group at the local library, which is how I ended up saying yes to this. The guest defends a strong version of the view that famines are never natural disasters, always political crimes: not "sometimes political as well", and not "man-made" in the flat sense in which everything is. As I understand her, she defines famine as a collapse of entitlements rather than as mass starvation or as a shortfall in food availability, argues that whenever that happens some agent with both the authority and the capacity to prevent it failed to prevent it, and insists that "crime" rather than "tragedy" or "grave injustice" is the right word. I find the argument attractive and I want to press on it rather than let her restate it.

I want eight to ten questions, and for each one a follow-up keyed to what you'd guess she'll answer, because she is quick and I am not. Nothing soft please — no "what first drew you to this topic" — assume the room has read her. The specific things I want covered:

Her definition is doing an awful lot of work: if famine just *is* entitlement failure then "no famine is natural" starts to look true by stipulation. Give me a question that asks what a famine would have to look like for her to concede it was natural, and a follow-up for when she says the definition is warranted by its explanatory power rather than being a gerrymander.

One question each on Bengal in 1943, on Ireland in the 1840s, and on the Great Leap Forward — and they shouldn't be the same question with different dates stapled on. My understanding (correct me if Ive got it wrong) is that Bengal was a boom-time famine where availability didn't collapse but prices ran away from landless labourers' wages; that in Ireland the blight really was a natural event while food kept leaving the country; and that in China grain kept being procured out of badly hit provinces while output collapsed and the centre was fed inflated reports. So: what's the Bengal question where the pressure is "if nobody intended this and markets did the damage, where does the crime come in"; what's the Ireland question where she has to say how much causal weight she gives the pathogen as against the fact that the crop was a genetically near-uniform monoculture — my hunch is that the vulnerability was engineered and only the organism was natural, and I want to know whether that's the general shape of her argument; and what's the China question about whether suppressing the information is the crime, or whether her view quietly becomes strict liability for whoever starves under your rule.

Her best-known claim is the one about famines not occurring in functioning democracies with a relatively free press. Give me a question on whether the mechanism is really electoral accountability or whether democracy is only a proxy for administrative capacity and money, and a follow-up for when she answers that it's about rulers who want re-election: what does her view say about a democracy whose voters don't care about the starving minority?

One question on the word "crime" itself — defendants, mens rea, limitation periods, states not being persons — asking what "crime" buys her that "culpable injustice" doesn't, and a follow-up for when she says the word is deliberately doing political work.

And one on my own hobby-horse, which is the bit I actually care about. In my old job the identical meteorological drought on either side of a border would produce famine on one side and very little on the other. That is her point and I don't want to just hand it to her. What I genuinely wonder is: now that a drought can be partly attributed to anthropogenic emissions, and those emissions trace back to identifiable emitters who are not the state that failed to protect anyone, does her natural-versus-political contrast collapse, does it get stronger, or does it just relocate the defendant? Put that as a question, with a follow-up for whichever way she jumps.

If there's an objection I haven't thought of that people in the literature actually press — something about famine versus chronic endemic malnutrition, or about how much hindsight her preventability judgements are allowed — add it in.
