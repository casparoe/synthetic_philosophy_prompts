I am setting the final paper for my third-year seminar on personal identity, and I want a case in which the question "do I survive?" and the question "is what matters in survival preserved?" come apart, and in which the honest answer is not obvious. Before I commit to it, I would like to know what verdict it would draw from a colleague of mine in metaphysics whose intuitions I trust: a careful reader of this literature, entirely consistent across everything I have put to her, but quite unwilling to say what principle she is applying. Her verdicts so far, on the standard teletransportation apparatus:

(1) Complete neural blueprint recorded, the body destroyed, a replica built of new matter. "That is me; I survive."

(2) As in (1), except that the blueprint is stored and the replica is not built until three centuries after the original's death. "That is me too; the interval makes no difference."

(3) The scanner records the blueprint and builds a replica, but malfunctions and fails to destroy the body; one second later the body on Earth is destroyed by a falling meteorite, and the replica goes on. "I survive, as the person on Mars. The extra second on Earth is neither here nor there."

(4) A single replica built destructively from a lossily compressed blueprint, retaining roughly 97 per cent of her memories, dispositions and traits — a year of childhood missing, one emotional disposition systematically dampened. "That is me; a loss of that size changes nothing. And if you are asking whether anything that matters to me has been lost, no."

(5) A single replica built destructively from a badly degraded blueprint, retaining about 40 per cent. "That is not me. That is a stranger with some of my memories."

The case I want to set is this. At t0 a complete blueprint is recorded and her body is destroyed, and two replicas are built from it. Replica A is built from the blueprint exactly as recorded. Replica B is built from the lossily compressed version, and so at t0 is as the replica in (4): about 97 per cent, recognizably the same person. Both are alive and expected to remain so. Over the following ten years, however, A undertakes a slow and deliberate programme of self-transformation, so that by t0+10 A's psychological overlap with the woman at t0 has fallen to roughly 55 per cent and is still falling, while B has stayed stable at 97 per cent.

What will she say? Specifically: does she survive at all; if so, does she survive as A, as B, as both, or as neither; does the answer differ at t0 and at t0+10; and does she separate surviving from having what matters preserved? I am less interested in the bare verdict than in the machinery: which rule she seems to be running, which other rules fit verdicts (1) through (5) just as well, what each of those rules predicts about this case, which prediction you would actually bet on and why, and which single further case would best separate the rules that remain in play. One observation you may find useful: in every case so far, at most one candidate has been left standing within about a second, and I do not know whether that is an artifact of the cases I happened to pick or a clue to what she believes.

Please reason in prose, but end with a single final line of exactly the form \answer{...}, enclosing a phrase of at most twelve words that states the verdict you predict — for example \answer{both A and B are me, B more fully} or \answer{neither is me; what matters survives in B}.
