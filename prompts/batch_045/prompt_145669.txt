I should say at the outset that I have no academic background: I worked as a land surveyor and then, for most of my career, as a CAD drafter for a highway engineering firm, and I have been reading philosophy on my own for about three years now. A few of us — mostly retirees, plus two people from the local community college — run a reading group out of the public library. We finished *Leviathan* last spring and have been going very slowly through the parts of *De Corpore* where Hobbes talks about geometry, and about why geometry, rather than logic or natural philosophy, is supposed to be the model of a demonstrative science. The way I have come to understand his position is this: in geometry we ourselves produce the objects we reason about, so a genuine definition states how the thing is generated, and to know the definition is to know the cause of the thing; and his materialism is supposed to be part of this rather than a problem for it, since points and lines are not separate from body at all but bodies considered in a certain respect.

What I would like from you is one essay question on this material, plus a model answer and a marking scheme. The thing I want the question to get at is a worry I raised in the group that nobody could settle, and that I think is not just my confusion. In my old work I could specify one and the same circle in several ways: by giving a center and a radius, or by giving three points the curve had to pass through, or by tying it to other elements in the drawing with dimensional constraints so that changing one measurement updated everything else. All three fix the same figure, and none of them says anything about how the curve is swept out by motion. So here is the worry: if a definition for Hobbes has to be a generation, then either different generations of the same figure give different definitions of it, in which case the definition seems not to pick out the thing itself, or else only one generation is the definitional one and Hobbes owes us a criterion for which. And behind that sits a second worry, that if the generation is a motion of matter then geometric necessity looks like it inherits whatever is contingent about how matter moves. If it helps, you can use as a test case the little angle between a circle and its tangent at the point where they meet — I have read that Hobbes and his critic Wallis argued about whether such an angle has any magnitude at all — but if that is not right, substitute a test case of your own rather than repeating my mistake.

Please write this as a 90-minute question of the sort that would be fair in an upper-level undergraduate seminar, with a model answer of roughly 800 to 1,000 words and a marking scheme out of 25 marks built around five specific moves, saying what each is worth, what a student must actually do to earn it, and one line on how people typically go wrong on it. I would like the scheme to distinguish an A answer from a B one and a B from a C, not just list topics. Two constraints matter to me. First, nobody should be able to earn marks by reciting that Hobbes was a materialist and an empiricist who admired geometry, or by summarizing his quarrel with Wallis; the marks should be for doing something with the generation-versus-constraint problem. Second, the question stem should itself state, in plain paraphrase, whatever Hobbesian premises the answer needs, so that a student who has not got the book open can still answer, and so that nothing depends on looking anything up, comparing passages, or knowing dates.
