I am putting together the diagnostic handout for the first session of a graduate seminar on karma considered as a theory of moral desert: not as a mechanism of rebirth and not as folk retribution, but as the claim that a wrongful act leaves a residual burden that belongs in the first instance to the agent who acted, and that it is borne because it is owed rather than merely because bearing it does some good. The students arrive holding very different package deals, and they can all say the word karma without agreeing on anything. I want an instrument that sorts them by what they are committed to about cases.

Please build it as a decision tree rather than as prose. Start the tree from a single seed commitment that nearly everyone in the room will initially accept, something like: a wrongful act leaves a remainder that has to be answered for, and the answering should fall on the agent rather than on whoever happens to be nearest. Do not start from a menu of schools or traditions; start from that seed and let the branches do the work.

The branching nodes should be the claims on which views in this space actually come apart. I want at least these six to appear: whether the remainder is triggered by the agent's intention or by the act's actual consequences; whether the remainder is best modeled as a quantity that can be accumulated, drawn down, and balanced against credit, or as a qualitative alteration of the agent's character and dispositions; whether the desert at issue is only a matter of fittingness, or whether it additionally licenses or requires someone to exact the fruit; whether the fruit is justified by what the agent deserves or by what its imposition brings about afterwards, such as reform, restraint, or the reassurance of others; whether the remainder can be assumed or discharged by a third party, or inherited by a family, a community, or a lineage; and what has to remain true of the agent at the time the fruit matures, given that people change their aims, their character, and their memories. If you think one further node belongs, add at most one and say why.

Each node must be phrased as a concrete case plus a forced choice, such that a student can answer it without knowing what the branch leads to. Among the cases, I want at least these five used: an agent who acts from a wholly benevolent motive but whose act foreseeably causes great suffering; an agent who causes great suffering through an act that was, at the time, neither careless nor wrongful; a third party who knowingly volunteers to carry someone else's remainder; an agent who, between act and fruit, ceases to share any of the desires, values, or character of the person who acted; and an agent whose subsequent condition is far worse than any proportionality between act and consequence would license.

Lay out first the branching questions and the space of answer-combinations they formally permit, noting where one answer prunes another by entailment. Then place the occupied positions in that space as leaves, six to nine of them. Give each leaf a short descriptive label, one sentence of characterization, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Where a combination that the questions allow seems to have no defender, mark it unoccupied and say briefly whether that is because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any two leaves your tree cannot separate rather than pretending the last branch distinguishes them. Finally, tell me which single node does the most sorting work and why.

Two constraints on presentation. A student with no Sanskrit and no exposure to primary sources must be able to answer every node from the case as written, so any technical term should be glossed in half a line, no leaf should be identified only by the name of a tradition, and any attribution to a historical school should be flagged as a rough placement rather than asserted. And the instrument should not ask anyone which view they find appealing; if a student who answers honestly ends up at a leaf they would not have chosen in advance, the handout has done its job.
