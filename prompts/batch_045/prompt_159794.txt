THE QUESTION (Question 3, 25 points)

A two-person society, consisting of Lewd and Prude, must decide what to do with a single copy of a novel. There are exactly three feasible social states:

x — Lewd reads the novel, Prude does not
y — Prude reads the novel, Lewd does not
z — neither reads it

Their preferences over these states are:

Lewd: y ≻ x ≻ z
Prude: z ≻ y ≻ x

A social decision function assigns to each admissible profile of individual preference orderings a complete social preference relation R over the three states, and society chooses from any feasible subset S those elements of S that are R-maximal. Assume that R must always admit at least one maximal element in every non-empty feasible subset (equivalently, that the strict part of R is acyclic). Say that an individual i is decisive over the unordered pair {a, b} if, for every admissible profile in which i prefers a to b, society strictly prefers a to b. Consider three conditions on such a rule:

U (unrestricted domain): every logically possible pair of orderings of x, y, z by Lewd and Prude is admissible.

P (Pareto): if both individuals strictly prefer a to b, then society strictly prefers a to b.

L (minimal liberalism): there are at least two individuals such that each is decisive over at least one pair of alternatives differing only in that individual's own private conduct. In this example the only such pairs are {x, z}, which differs only in whether Lewd reads, and {y, z}, which differs only in whether Prude reads; so L requires that Lewd be decisive over {x, z} and Prude decisive over {y, z}.

(a) [12 points] Show that no social decision function satisfies U, P and L simultaneously.
(b) [8 points] Say briefly what the result does and does not establish. In particular, does it show that respect for individual rights, or liberalism as a political doctrine, is incoherent?
(c) [5 points] Indicate one way the conflict could be avoided: which of U, P or L would you restrict, and why?

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER

(a) This is the impossibility result known as Sen's liberal paradox. Minimal liberalism says that a person should be decisive over some pair of alternatives differing only in that person's own private conduct; more precisely, the condition requires only that at least one person be decisive over at least one such pair. That is what makes it so weak, and it follows that even a single recognised private right is already enough to clash with the Pareto principle.

Now take the stated profile. The pair {x, z} differs only in whether Lewd reads, so Lewd is decisive over it; Lewd prefers x to z, hence society must rank x above z. The pair {y, z} differs only in whether Prude reads, so Prude is decisive over it; Prude prefers z to y, hence society must rank z above y. Finally, both Lewd and Prude prefer y to x, so the Pareto condition requires society to rank y above x. Putting the three together gives x above z, z above y, and y above x: a cycle. A social decision function has to yield maximal elements in every feasible subset, and in the set {x, y, z} a cycle leaves no maximal element. So no rule of the kind described can satisfy U, P and L together.

The result is best understood as a variant of Arrow's impossibility theorem. Sen in effect replaces Arrow's non-dictatorship condition with a liberalism condition, and the cycle above shows that any rule meeting his three conditions must violate independence of irrelevant alternatives; that violation is what drives Arrow's conclusion that the rule is dictatorial.

(b) What the argument establishes is that one particular formal reconstruction of liberal rights — as unconditional pairwise decisiveness over one's own personal affairs — cannot be combined with the Pareto principle and an unrestricted domain. It does not show that liberalism as a political doctrine is incoherent or that rights must be given up; the lesson concerns that axiomatisation, and leaves open which of the three conditions ought to be relaxed. It is also worth noticing that the conflict here depends on the fact that each person's ranking is meddlesome, in the sense that it turns partly on what the other person reads. If Lewd and Prude cared only about their own reading, Lewd would be indifferent between y and z and Prude indifferent between x and z; then Lewd's right over {x, z} gives x above z, Prude's right over {y, z} gives z above y, and Pareto gives x above y — a perfectly consistent ranking, with no cycle.

(c) The cheapest escape is therefore to restrict the domain of admissible profiles, excluding preferences of the meddlesome kind, or, equivalently, to decline to apply the Pareto principle to preferences that turn on other people's private conduct. What will not work is simply to give rights lexical priority over the Pareto principle in the pairs they govern, because the Pareto ranking that creates the difficulty concerns the pair {x, y}, over which neither Lewd nor Prude holds any right.
