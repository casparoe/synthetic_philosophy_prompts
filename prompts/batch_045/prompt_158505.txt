I sit as the lay member of my integrated care board's panel that reviews serious incidents involving autonomous and AI-assisted systems, and I have been asked to draft the options paper on a case that has come to us from a residential care home. I would be grateful for a reading of our own track record before I write it.

Our five determinations to date, in the terms we actually used:

1. An automated pharmacy-dispensing robot, behaviour fixed at deployment, stacked a tray wrongly and a patient received a wrong dose. Nobody had predicted the failure and testing had been thorough. We recorded a latent design defect, attributed responsibility to no individual, imposed no sanction, and left compensation to the no-fault scheme.
2. A surgeon using a robotic assistant disabled a force-limit interlock to shorten an operation; the patient was injured. We held the surgeon solely responsible and referred him to his regulator. Our reasoning was that a control had been provided and deliberately defeated.
3. A sepsis-prediction model fired a correct alert on a ward where nurses had absorbed thirty false alarms that week; the nurse silenced it and the patient deteriorated. We declined to sanction the nurse on the ground that her response was a foreseeable product of the alerting design, and instead required the trust and the vendor jointly to change the alerting threshold.
4. An autonomous transport robot blocked a doorway during a crash call. The obstacle map's blind spot was documented in a safety file that the clinical engineering manager was supposed to read and had not. We held the manager accountable, required him to give an account to the board, declined to find him blameworthy, and recorded an institutional failure.
5. A fleet of delivery drones re-optimised its flight paths over a courtyard used by patients. Each engineer had reviewed his own module; nobody had examined their interaction. We identified no individual, accepted corporate liability on the manufacturer's part, and levied a financial penalty.

Three months ago my father moved into a care home which, shortly afterwards, installed a night-time falls-monitoring system. It does not merely detect falls: it adjusts the sensitivity at which it raises an alert, learning from how staff respond. Over roughly seven weeks, because night staff muted alerts during busy shifts, the threshold drifted upward. Every individual adjustment was inside the certified envelope; the vendor's manual describes the adaptation as 'calibration to local practice' and recommends no review of it. There is a weekly sensitivity-check item on the deputy manager's checklist, which she ticked each week without ever opening the value; the drift was visible in logs that the checklist existed to surface. One resident fell, the alert did not fire, and he was found some hours later. On the vendor's data, alert precision improved over the period and night-shift workload fell. Nobody overrode anything, nobody chose the threshold, and nobody can be pointed to as having decided that this resident would not be heard. I have declared an interest and will not vote, but I must still set out the options.

What I want from you is this. First, the candidate rules that fit our five determinations equally well — I can see at least a control-and-opportunity rule, a reasonable-foreseeability rule, a desert-based rule that asks whether anyone made a defective choice, a many-hands rule that moves responsibility to the organisation when causal contributions are diffuse, and a role-based rule that attaches it to whoever accepted the deployment risk — and, for each, what it predicts here. I suspect they diverge sharply on this case: the deputy manager both had and did not use an opportunity to intervene, the fatal fall was not foreseeable even though the drift was, nobody's choice looks culpable, the causal contribution is spread across staff behaviour, vendor design and managerial inattention, and my father's home's registered manager is the person whose office accepted the risk.

Second, your best guess at what we would decide, with the precedent you take to be the closest analogue and the reasons for preferring it. Third, an honest statement of what we would be adopting with each option if our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case: in particular, whether we would be treating a system that keeps learning after deployment as relevantly different from one whose behaviour is fixed, and whether we would be treating harm that the system learned from our own staff's habits as a case of anybody's responsibility at all.

Two further things would help. Our earlier determinations never distinguished being blameworthy, being liable, and being answerable, and I would like to know which of those three questions each of our precedents was really answering, and which one this case forces us to separate. And I would like one variant of the case — a change to the facts, not a new principle — that would settle which rule we have been running all along: for instance whether it matters that the deputy manager reviewed the value and consciously left it alone, or that the drift was invisible in the logs, or that the system's overall performance was worse rather than better than the human baseline.

I am less interested in the right answer in the abstract than in what we have already committed ourselves to, and in what we would be committing ourselves to next.
