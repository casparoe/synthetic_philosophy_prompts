I am writing the framing chapter for an edited volume at the intersection of philosophy of biology, astrobiology, and synthetic biology, and I do not want to open it by parading the familiar candidate definitions and scoring them. I want the specification first: what would a definition of life have to be able to do in order to count as adequate?

The purpose, since adequacy is relative to it. I need the definition to do two jobs, and I strongly suspect they are not the same job. The first is to tell a sample-return team what kind of finding would justify announcing a discovery, and what would justify staying silent. The second is to tell an oversight body for synthetic biology which artifacts fall inside its remit and which are merely complicated chemistry. If those two purposes pull the requirements in different directions, that divergence is itself one of the results I want, so please say so explicitly rather than splitting the difference.

What I want from you is a list of requirements, each accompanied by the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a taste: which phenomena the definition must cover, which verdicts it must be able to return, which distinctions it must respect, which findings from chemistry, thermodynamics, and evolutionary theory it must not contradict. For each, say whether it is non-negotiable or merely the preference of one camp, and identify which requirement does the most exclusionary work against the theories actually on offer. Where some subset cannot be jointly satisfied, give the argument for the incompatibility and say which item you would drop and why.

The case base, so that the requirements are anchored rather than abstract. Here is my working inventory; add to it any case that discriminates between definitions my list does not separate. Viruses and viroids, which evolve and have lineages but no metabolism, with the complication that the largest of them carry genes for translation and blur into the cellular. Prions: heritable, selectable, sequence-free, no nucleic acid at all. Transposons and other selfish genetic elements that reproduce inside a genome while being parts of one. Organisms of zero fitness: mules, sterile workers, a neutered animal. Everything at the temporal edges: spores, desiccated tardigrades, centuries-old seeds, permafrost microbes revived after geological time, cryopreserved embryos, a vitrified organ, a cell line that has outlived its donor by decades and whose karyotype is no longer that of the donor species. The part-whole tangle: a cultured cell, a gamete, an organoid, a siphonophore colony, a eusocial colony considered as a unit, a mycelium with no clear individual boundary, a cellular slime mold aggregating. Things that maintain themselves, grow, and propagate without being organisms on any view: flames, growing crystals, convection cells, autocatalytic sets, storms. Software: a computer worm, an evolved agent in a simulated physics, a program running on hardware it cannot build. Engineered and dissociated entities: a cell whose genome was chemically synthesized and booted up inside a recipient stripped of its own DNA; protocells with compartment and metabolism but no heredity; hereditary systems with heredity but no metabolism; frog cells reassembled into motile constructs that replicate by pushing other cells into piles; organisms engineered with recoded genomes and kill switches so that they cannot exchange genes and have no evolutionary future; chirality-reversed organisms that would metabolize and evolve while sharing no molecular code with anything on Earth.

And one case I have constructed precisely because no existing discussion has had to take a stand on it. A culture is derived from a subsurface sample returned from an icy moon. It is maintained in a solvent that is not water, at temperatures where Earth biochemistry does not operate, on a mineral substrate. Across two hundred transfers it shows heritable variation, competition for surface, and directional change: the composition of the mineral face shifts in ways that persist and that improve retention of the face under the transfer regime. There is no membrane, no polymer, no genome, no identifiable individual, no discrete generation. If there is a unit of selection it is the composition of a surface. I want to know what a definition must be able to say about this, what would count as evidence on either side, and which of your requirements a definition fails the moment it cannot return a verdict here.

Distinctions I expect a definition to keep apart rather than collapse: being alive versus being an organism; being alive versus being a living individual; being alive versus being part of something alive; being alive versus being capable of becoming alive; being alive versus having interests or a moral status; being alive versus having a mind, a goal, or agency; and the definition's own content versus the operational markers a lab would use to detect it.

Constraints from outside the case base. No vitalism: the definition must be continuous with chemistry and physics rather than carving life off as a separate substance or force. Every organism we know descends from a single origin, so we have one independent instance, and a definition checked only against it cannot claim Generality without argument. Any definition that builds in carbon, water, or nucleic acid must answer the parochialism objection directly. Far-from-equilibrium self-maintenance is presumably necessary but plainly not sufficient, since flames and convection cells have it. Evolution by selection is a property of populations over time, not of a token organism at an instant, which threatens any definition that makes life a property of individuals and then has to verdict on the mule and the spore. And the definition must not be circular with respect to our instruments: a definition of the form life is whatever our current assay portfolio flags would tell the sample-return team nothing it did not already know, while a definition no instrument could ever apply is useless to them.

Tensions I want adjudicated rather than smoothed over. Generality across unknown substrates versus discriminating power against flames, crystals, and software: I suspect you cannot maximize both, and I want to see where the trade-off bites. A sharp boundary, which the oversight body needs, versus fidelity to what may be a genuinely graded phenomenon. Verdicts at the level of individuals versus verdicts at the level of lineages, given individuals without lineages and lineages without individuals. Descriptive neutrality versus the temptation to gerrymander the boundary so that embryos, organoids, or artificial systems land on the side one already wants for ethical reasons; I would treat that temptation as a vice, but tell me if you disagree. There is also the possibility of a relational definition, on which a thing is alive only with respect to some larger system or process; I would like to see how far that goes, what it costs, and whether it is a real option or a way of changing the subject.

Close with a short scorecard, no more than a sentence or two each, for the main contenders as you see them: the astrobiology working definition in the vicinity of a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution, autopoietic accounts, negative-entropy and far-from-equilibrium accounts, accounts that start from Darwinian populations rather than from organisms, and any others you think belong on the list. Keep the weight on the criteria and on the cases; the scorecard is for orientation only. And give me your view on the last question: if the requirements really are jointly unsatisfiable, does that show that life is not the kind of thing that has a single correct definition, and if so, what replaces the project?

One instruction about form. Write as if you had no library and no connection: reason from the cases, the distinctions, and the constraints I have given, and do not spend your words on who said what. A table for the requirements is welcome, prose for the incompatibilities. I would rather have eight argued requirements than twenty listed ones.
