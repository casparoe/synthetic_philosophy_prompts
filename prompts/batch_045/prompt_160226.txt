Do not settle on a diagnosis immediately: first spend some time brainstorming — list the moves the argument below could be making, the places it could be cheating, and the views that would each have to pay for rejecting a given step — and only then commit to the step you would reject and to what rejecting it costs.

I am preparing a seminar presentation on split-brain cases and the unity of consciousness, and I have built the following argument. Every step looked fine to me when I wrote it down, and I do not like where it ends.

(1) Criterion: two conscious states belong to the same subject if and only if they are mutually accessible — accessible in the sense that either can, on its own, guide what that subject says or does.

(2) In a patient whose callosum has been sectioned, a stimulus presented to one hemisphere produces a conscious state that guides the responses controlled by that hemisphere, and neither guides nor is available to the responses controlled by the other; and conversely for a stimulus presented to the other side.

(3) So such a patient, in the standard testing situation, contains conscious states that are not mutually accessible, and therefore contains two subjects rather than one.

(4) That conclusion is not an artifact of only one hemisphere normally having speech. Imagine fitting the patient with a dual-channel speech prosthesis: cortical implants driving two independent synthesizers, calibrated so that each reports only what the hemisphere it is wired to receives. Nothing about mutual accessibility changes, and the prediction is two simultaneous, conflicting, first-personal reports issuing from one organism.

(5) Now apply (1) outside the laboratory. In an intact human, cortical and subcortical systems are locally integrated, take their own inputs, control their own outputs, and exchange only narrow and lossy signals with the rest; even the two hemispheres of an intact person are partly independent, and callosal transfer is partial rather than total. So if (1) is right, intact humans either already contain more than one subject, or the number of subjects in a person depends on where we set an arbitrary threshold for how much mutual accessibility is enough.

(6) Sharpen that. Imagine the callosum sectioned a bundle at a time across many sessions, with testing after each. Accessibility degrades continuously, while the number of subjects, if the criterion is right, must jump from one to two at some point. So either there is a fact of the matter about which bundle did it, or how many subjects there are can be indeterminate.

(7) I am not willing to accept either a multiplication of subjects in ordinary people or an indeterminate subject count. So one of (1) to (3) has to go.

Tell me which step you reject and why it looked sound. If you reject or repair (1), say exactly what extra condition you are adding — a global workspace, self-representation, a single locus of practical agency, first-personal reference, or something else — and then say whether (3) survives once that condition is in place; I want to know whether every such repair quietly collapses the split-brain case back into a single subject. And if the argument needs a premise I have not stated, state it. If it comes to a choice, I would rather keep (3), in some interesting sense of 'two', than swallow (5) and (6), but I want to know what keeping it costs me.
