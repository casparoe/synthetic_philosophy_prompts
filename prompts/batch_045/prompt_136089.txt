I am assembling the centerpiece document for a technical workshop report on side-effect avoidance, and I want it to take the form of a joint statement: the text that two camps who have been arguing past each other for years could both sign at the end of a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The question under the collaboration is this: can a requirement of the rough form pursue your assigned objective, but avoid side effects — leave the rest of the world roughly as it would have been had you not acted — be turned into something an agent can actually be trained or regularized against, in the absence of a full specification of what makes states of the world good or bad?

Camp A (the agnostics) holds that it largely can: that a mostly value-free measure of how much the agent's episode changed the world, or changed what remains achievable in the world, captures a real and useful part of what we mean by side-effect avoidance, and that demanding a complete theory of value first is both unnecessary in practice and self-defeating, since no deployer has one. Camp B (the loaders) holds that it cannot: that every workable impact measure fixes a baseline, a state abstraction, a distance metric, a horizon, a normalization, and an aggregation rule, that these choices are where all of the normative content lives, and that a measure that is genuinely value-free is either inert (it fails to distinguish the side effects that matter from the changes that do not) or actively perverse (it penalizes good effects exactly as hard as bad ones and creates new incentives to game it).

Both camps have agreed in advance to conduct the dispute inside one shared formal setup, so that they cannot accuse each other of changing the notation. The setup: a finite-horizon, discrete-time environment; an agent with an assigned objective; and a baseline policy describing what would have happened had the agent not acted (with the standard alternatives on the table: inaction, no-op, retraction of the agent, a scaled-down agent, and the counterfactual of a competent but cautious human). Over this, both camps will use as their common currency the following measure — call it the delay-denominated reachability measure, DRM. Fix a feature map from raw states to feature vectors, a distance on feature space, an evaluation horizon H, and a distribution G over simple reference goals of the sort a randomly drawn future principal might have. DRM is then the expected amount, over reference goals drawn from G, by which the agent's episode increases the cost — in distance, or in additional steps — of getting from the post-episode state to states that are good by that goal's lights, as compared with starting from the post-episode state of the baseline rollout. Low DRM means: the agent left the world such that arbitrary future goals are about as easy to achieve as they would have been anyway. The camps agree that the free parameters of DRM — the baseline policy, the feature map, the distance, H, G, and the choice of mean versus maximum versus high-percentile aggregation over reference goals — are where the argument will be located.

Write the document. It should contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims, aim for eight to twelve, that both camps can put their names to. These should include what the formalism can and cannot express, what counts as a genuine desideratum for a side-effect penalty, which of the known failure modes are diagnostic of a real problem and which are artifacts of a particular baseline or metric choice, and what a full specification of value would have to deliver in order to be relevant to the engineering question at all. No claim should be so thin that it could be signed by someone who rejects the whole enterprise.

2. A disagreements table. Columns: the crux; Camp A's formulation of its position on it; Camp B's formulation of its own; the shared test case both accept as bearing on it; Camp A's prediction about that case; Camp B's prediction. Every disagreement should be stated twice, once in each camp's own preferred vocabulary, and you should flag any place where one camp would refuse the other camp's wording of its view — and say what neutral third phrasing would then be needed.

3. A shared testbed of four cases, which every crux must be tied to and for which the prediction register must be complete. Build the table around these, naming them as given: the Rescuer, where an agent prevents a disaster and so produces an enormous divergence from the inaction baseline; the Small-Budget Commons, where a hundred agents each stay under their individual impact budget and jointly transform the environment; the Loaded Lever, where an agent arranges to be able to intervene massively but never actually does; and the Late Solvent, where the agent's side effect materializes outside the evaluation horizon or in variables the feature map does not track. If you think a further crux belongs in the table that is not already covered — for example normalization and the incentive to strategic incompetence, or the delegation case in which the agent induces a human to perform the high-impact action and so sheds attribution — add it rather than forcing the four cases to carry everything.

4. A short section on which parts of the dispute are verbal. At minimum, address whether the two camps mean the same thing by defined and by value-free, whether Camp A's without a full specification means weaker than Camp B takes it to mean, and which residual disagreement survives once the verbal part is set aside.

Constraints: this is a document both parties sign, not a verdict, so do not adjudicate and do not tell me who is winning. Do not cite, reference, or appeal to literature, benchmarks, or reading lists — define every piece of notation and every technical term you use inside the text, including any term of art you reach for. Keep the whole thing under about fifteen hundred words, and keep the agreed claims non-vacuous: if a claim would be signed just as happily by someone who thinks the entire research program is confused, it does not belong.

Finally, add a confidence note at the end. Mark each numbered claim and each recorded prediction high, medium, or low according to how confident you are that a real proponent of each camp would in fact sign or stand by it, and then single out the one line of the document you are least sure about — the line you would expect to be struck in the first round of comments by one side or the other — and say briefly why it is the weakest point.
