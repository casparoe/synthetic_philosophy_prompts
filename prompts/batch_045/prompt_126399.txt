I spent some thirty-five years as a nucleic-acid chemist before I retired, and I have lately been sitting in on a philosophy of biology reading group at my old department. The group has grown fond of one analogy, and before I decide whether it is doing any real work I would like it audited properly.

The analogy, as our convenor states it, is that the genome stands to the cell as a Gödel numbering stands to a formal system. A strand of DNA, he says, is at once a passive string that gets copied and an encoded description that, once translated, specifies the very enzymes that copy it — the gene for the replicase is replicated by its own product. Replication is therefore not mere chemistry but the material realisation of the same strange loop that lets a formal system contain a sentence about itself, and Hofstadter's toy system of letter-strands and enzymes from his 1979 book is offered as the cleaned-up version of it. The conclusion he draws — and this is the part I actually care about — is that genuine self-reproduction requires a code-mediated architecture: a symbolic vehicle, a translation scheme, and a description of the machinery that reads the description. Crystals, micelles and prions, on this account, only grow or propagate; they lack the loop, so nothing there deserves the name of replication in the full sense. He then extends it: because what does the work is the symbolic architecture rather than the substrate, digital realisations of life and mind are unproblematic in principle, and an analog or merely chemical system is not really alive.

My own attempt to locate what is wrong runs as follows, and I would like to know how much of it survives contact with the literature. I distinguish two ways a structure can be self-involved, which I have started calling the cast and the recipe. In cast mode the strand serves as its own mold: base complementarity produces a copy the way a plaster cast or an advancing crystal face does, and no code, description or interpretation is needed. In recipe mode the sequence is translated into machinery that acts back on sequences. Only the second looks even remotely semantic, and I suspect the analogy earns its plausibility by sliding between the two. I also notice that the loop is not where the analogy puts it: no gene copies itself, and the closure holds, if at all, for the whole cell rather than for the strand — DNA outside a cell is just a polymer. And I keep returning to a technical worry. A Gödel numbering has to be injective, and its decoding has to be representable inside the system, for the diagonal construction to go through at all; the genetic code is many-to-one, and translation lands in a different alphabet from the one it starts in. Relatedly, von Neumann's own self-reproducing machine needs a separate copier precisely because a description cannot carry instructions for building an equally long description tape.

Please do the following.

First, lay out the mapping explicitly, as a table: every element of the formal side (numbering, well-formed formula, proof predicate, negation, the diagonal lemma, the undecidable sentence, the interpretation under which the sentence is true) against its supposed molecular counterpart, with rows where no counterpart exists left visibly empty.

Second, sort the disanalogies into those that are load-bearing for the conclusion my convenor draws and those that are merely decorative: which ones, if repaired, would let the analogy carry his conclusion about replication requiring a code, and which would not.

Third, tell me what the isomorphism predicts that biology does not obviously deliver. If the mapping were sound, one would expect a limitation theorem on the biological side — some analogue of there being truths the system cannot reach. Say what that analogue would have to be, whether anything like it is true of cells or of evolving lineages, and what its absence tells us about the mapping.

Fourth, use the prion as a stress test. Here is heritable, self-propagating structure with no nucleic acid, no encoding and no translation: is that replication or growth on the analogy's own terms, and is the answer it forces one we should accept?

Fifth, say what a better analogy would be, if there is one — von Neumann's constructor–description–copier with its double use of the description, a quine in a programming language, or something drawn from autopoiesis and the closure of constraints — and, for each candidate, what it preserves of the original comparison and what it costs.

Finally, tell me plainly whether my cast/recipe distinction is genuinely my own or whether I have simply rediscovered something von Neumann already built into his architecture, and whether that discovery helps my case or hurts it.

I would be grateful if you answered in British English.
