My position, for the record: I think there is a fact of the matter here; I have not decided which story is true, and I shall argue for the fact-of-the-matter claim before I argue for either side of it.

Opening move. Take the phenomenon as it is standardly reported: a dot of one colour is flashed briefly at one location, then, after a gap, a dot of another colour is flashed at a second location; with suitable spacing and timing, subjects report a single dot travelling between the locations whose colour changes abruptly partway along the illusory path — that is, at a time before anything in the second colour has been shown. The Orwellian story says the experience itself was innocent and only the memory of it was rewritten once the second flash arrived; the Stalinesque story says nothing innocent ever occurred, and that the experience-as-of-a-colour-change was manufactured once the second flash arrived and then passed off in memory as having happened earlier.

I grant you the usual point at once, and more than the usual point: no probe settles it. Not a speeded report, not a continuous slider or a pursuit eye movement, not even the design where the second flash is dropped at random and a cue is jittered across the gap — every such measure is itself an event in time, its own results are revisable, and probing looks less like reading a content off than like fixing one. So I offer you no clever measurement.

Here instead is a dilemma, and my claim is that you must take a horn.

(A) The two stories differ in nothing at all: not in report, not in subsequent behaviour, and not in the causal or computational role of the intermediate states between the flashes. Then 'there is no fact of the matter' is not a discovery about the timing of experience; it is a proposal about what we are entitled to say, and the Orwellian/Stalinesque dispute is not dissolved but relocated — it becomes a dispute about which posits earn their keep in the best causal account of the subject's performance, and you owe me a criterion for earning one's keep that is not a difference in what the posit predicts.

(B) They do differ in the causal-explanatory role of the intermediate states: on one story some state occurring before the second flash does the work an experience-of-the-dot-changing-colour would do, and on the other no such state occurs until later. Then there is a fact of the matter, in principle accessible to neuroscience, about which obtains — and your thesis collapses into the empirical, possibly true, claim that no pre-second-flash state has the profile in question.

If you reach for a third option — the difference is real, but neither causal nor empirically decidable — then say what it consists in, and say how you can know there is one while also holding that content is fixed only relative to a probe.

My bet is that you must take (B), because your view is supposed to tell us something substantive about how the brain gets this job done. And once you do, I get to ask what profile you require of that intermediate state — and to argue that your answer is either question-begging or hands me back the distinction you say was never there.
