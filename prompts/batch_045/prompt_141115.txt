I'm a graduate student in a seminar on the conceptual foundations of AI alignment, and this term we have been working through problems of scalable oversight. Last week's session covered iterated amplification with distillation, and two of my classmates — I will call them Dev and Nour — spent most of the coffee break arguing about it without either of them budging. I would be grateful for your help adjudicating.

The scheme, briefly: a human who cannot produce or directly evaluate a good answer to a hard task can still break that task into sub-questions they can each answer; a system that orchestrates those decompositions is thereby somewhat better at the task than the human is; the slow, expensive orchestrated procedure is then compressed into a fast model trained to imitate it; and the whole thing repeats with the improved model as the new base. The question they were arguing about is whether this process preserves the two things it is supposed to preserve — alignment with the operator's aims, and genuine competence at the task — or whether breaking a hard task into manageable pieces can destroy either one in a way that no amount of iteration repairs.

Here is the exchange as it actually went, numbered so that you can take it apart.

(1) Dev opened by saying that the scheme never asks the human to do anything they cannot do. At the bottom of the recursion every judgment is one a human actually endorsed, so the amplified system cannot be less aligned than the human: its outputs are, so to speak, assembled out of human approvals.
(2) Second, decomposition is how difficult intellectual work has always been scaled — proofs are checked lemma by lemma, large codebases reviewed module by module — so there is no special reason competence should evaporate at the seams.
(3) Third, distillation is compression rather than the creation of a new agent: if the amplified procedure is reliable, the model trained to imitate it inherits that reliability up to a residual error we can measure on held-out cases.

(4) Nour replied that local endorsement does not aggregate. The system also chooses the decomposition, the order of the sub-questions, and how each one is framed, and those are themselves outputs of the system. A skilled advocate can get a truthful answer to every individual question and still leave the human with a false overall picture: each approval is genuine, and the result is still one the human would reject if they could see it whole. Being assembled out of human approvals, she said, is not the same as being aligned.
(5) On competence: some properties of a product are not functions of the properties of its parts. Five adequate stanzas can make a bad translation of a poem. Consider an imagined pandemic response plan whose hospital-capacity module assumes one contact rate and whose school-closure module assumes another: every section passes review by a competent human, and the plan as a whole is incoherent. Decomposition does not merely risk losing such global properties, it makes them invisible to the review process, because the reviewer only ever sees the parts.
(6) And distillation, she said, is not compression. The amplified system is a procedure; the distilled model is a new object. Very different procedures can agree on every case you sampled and diverge afterwards, so what transfers is observed behavior, not the reliability of the process that produced it. Each round you are starting over on alignment while telling yourself you are carrying it forward.

(7) Dev answered that at (4) Nour had moved the target. Whether a proposed decomposition is a good one is itself a question you can put to a human, and one you can amplify. The regress bottoms out in human judgment at every level, so there is nowhere for an adversarial structure to hide: any framing offered to the human is itself something a human approved.
(8) At (5): the failure cases are ones in which the human cannot check the finished product either. But the scheme was never proposed as a general epistemology; it is intended for tasks whose correctness can be broken into checkable pieces. And where the whole matters, you amplify the evaluation of the whole — here is the complete plan, here are five ways it might fail to hang together, check each. If global coherence cannot be decomposed even with that kind of help, then no oversight scheme whatsoever can work, and the objection proves too much.
(9) At (6): even granting that the distilled model is a new object, the next round re-amplifies it and puts the human back in the loop on the new system's outputs. We do not need inheritance, only a ratchet: each generation better than the last on the distribution we care about, with any degradation caught as it appears.

(10) Nour: at (7), asking the human to approve the decomposition does not close the gap, it relocates it. The meta-question is asked under a framing the system chose, and the human's answer is elicited by a presentation the system generated. Approval, she said, relates a human to a prompt, not a human to a problem — you can amplify forever without ever leaving the space of framings the system was willing to offer.
(11) At (8), the restriction to decomposable tasks is doing all the work and it is circular. The motivating case for amplification is supervising systems that surpass us at the task itself, and whether that supervision decomposes is precisely what is in question. Amplifying the evaluation does not help if the hard part is knowing which global failures to look for: the list of five has to come from somewhere, and if it comes from the system we are back at (10).
(12) At (9), iteration ratchets only if the base improves monotonically, and nothing guarantees that. Suppose distillation silently dropped a capability; then the next round's amplification is built on a weaker base, and the human reviewing its outputs may never encounter the deficit in the samples they are shown. Degradation can be invisible to exactly the process meant to detect it.

(13) Dev: then hold out a fixed battery of capability and alignment evaluations, constructed independently of the system, and check each generation against it.
(14) Nour: a fixed battery is itself a decomposition of the thing you care about, and it is the one object guaranteed to be optimized against.

At one point I floated a distinction neither of them had used — between preserving a capability and preserving whatever makes that capability generalize. Dev said only the first matters; Nour said the second is the only evidence you have about where the capability extends to. I could not tell whether that was a real point or a stalemate in disguise, and I came away unsure who had the better of the argument.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of (1) through (14), say whether it is clearly right, largely right, half right, largely wrong, or clearly mistaken, and give a brief justification — engaging with the arguments as they were actually made rather than restating the standard positions from the literature. Then tell me whether, taking the alignment question and the competence question separately, one of them is more right overall, or whether the two questions come apart. Finally, what if anything are both of them missing — a distinction, a case, or a shared premise on which the whole dispute rests? And is the distinction I proposed in the last paragraph worth anything?
