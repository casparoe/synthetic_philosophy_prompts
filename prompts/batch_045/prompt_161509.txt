I've been reading about decision theory and AI alignment in my own time — no formal training in either — and I've ended up with a set of views that I suspect don't all fit together. Please act as a philosopher who works on acausal decision theory and on AI alignment, and audit them. Think it through carefully: before you give me a verdict, brainstorm the candidate tensions you can see, including any I haven't noticed, and only then rank them.

Where I've landed:

1. In a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma against an agent I have good reason to think is running something very close to my own decision procedure, I cooperate. What I care about is what my procedure gets across all the places it might be instantiated, not just the causal upshot of this one act — and I take that to vary smoothly with how close the match is, with no sudden jump anywhere.

2. I refuse to pay an acausal blackmailer: someone who says they modelled me, that they'd have known in advance if I were going to refuse, and that they have accordingly already harmed — or will harm — a copy of me. I want to be the sort of agent nobody bothers threatening.

3. I think the usual worry about surrogate goals is misdescribed. The problem isn't that any proxy must eventually peel away from the target it stands in for; it's that very many different targets all reward the same behaviour — securing more control over resources, resisting being switched off — so the proxy is nearly a red herring and the real problem is that pressure.

4. So my preferred fix is legibility: build systems whose goals and reasoning we can predict and inspect, and accept less capability in exchange. If we can read the machine's mind, the rest becomes tractable.

5. I don't think a copy of me counts for less just for being a copy. If it's a real mind, its suffering weighs the same as mine would.

6. And here is the bit I can't justify — I've started calling it the smudged-mirror case. If the blackmailer's model of me is only roughly accurate, around 80%, I'm confident that refusing is right; if it's near-perfect, I'm much less confident. But my principle in (1) is supposed to vary smoothly with closeness of match, so it shouldn't produce any such change in confidence, let alone a threshold.

My hunches are that (2) and (5) pull against each other, and that (4) quietly assumes something (3) says won't hold. I'm not confident about either, and I'd rather you found the real fault lines than confirmed my guesses.

So: which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like it, and how severe is each? What's the cheapest fix for the worst one, and which single revision buys the most consistency? If you had to drop one of the six, which? And for each, tell me whether a specialist would recognise it as a live position or whether I've reinvented it under a name I made up.

Where I most want to be challenged: say what would change your mind about your own ranking, and say where your reasoning is most likely to fail. I'd rather have the soft spot named than have the audit look tidy.
