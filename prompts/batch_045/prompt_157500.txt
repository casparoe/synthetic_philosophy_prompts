I keep running into one configuration in the way people describe themselves, and I want to know whether there is an established concept for it — and, more than that, whether the way I am carving it is the right carving, since a bare label would not tell me what I want to know.

Two instances. A friend says, without any apparent discomfort, that she is simply not a mathematical person. Nothing here looks like a lie: the incapacity is real, well documented in her own history, and expensive to reverse. But her treating it as settled is also part of what keeps it settled — it organizes what she attempts, what she practises, and what she notices about herself — and she then cites the resulting incapacity as the justification for treating it as settled. Second: the member of a family who is the calm one. The role is assigned from outside, then taken up, then experienced as a temperament discovered rather than acquired; and it becomes harder to be angry without ceasing to be that person.

The general shape: someone avows a trait as a plain fact about themselves; the avowal is true; and the avowal is among the things that make and keep it true, so that the fact cited as the ground of the avowal is in part its product. It is not quite a self-fulfilling prophecy, because what the avowal brings about is not some further event but the very fact it reports. And my sense is that it is not bad faith in the Sartrean sense either — there is no falsehood and no ignorance of one's own freedom, and the person seems to be taking up a posture rather than hiding from one — but I want to know whether that sense is correct, or whether the absence of a lie is itself the most refined form of the thing.

Does this configuration have a name of its own? If the nearest established concepts fit it only partially, what exactly are the respects in which it differs from each, and which fits best? I would also like the edges pinned down: what changes when the trait has a firm organic basis, when the avowal is socially enforced rather than chosen, or when the person avows it unwillingly; and is there any way to distinguish, from the inside, a trait genuinely sustained by its own avowal from one that is fixed and merely avowed?
