I'm in a seminar on the ethics and economics of markets, and I keep getting stuck on one feature of rent regulation that we haven't discussed. In many rent-regulated systems the protected tenancy is heritable but not saleable: a family member already living in the home can succeed to it and go on paying the controlled rent, but the sitting tenant cannot sell the right to occupy at that rent to anyone else, and landlords are barred from taking payment to hand a regulated tenancy over. Yet the gap between the controlled rent and the market rent is plainly worth money — a tenant who would happily move somewhere smaller is, in effect, sitting on an asset she is not allowed to cash in.

What view of the tenant's interest does that combination presuppose? It seems to treat occupancy as something like a personal status attached to a household rather than as property — but then why is the interest transmissible at all, and why to a co-resident specifically rather than to whoever the tenant chooses? Suppose a city made the regulated tenancy freely transferable, so that the tenant could sell the right to renew at the controlled rent to any buyer at any price. What theory of the tenant's interest, and of what rent control is for, would that rule express, and what would it lose? And is the actual rule — heritable but inalienable — strictly committed to one story about that interest, or is it also compatible with views that treat rent control as a subsidy to households? Please answer in no more than 400 words.
