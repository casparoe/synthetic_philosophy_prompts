After thirty-odd years in experimental geophysics, and three years of reading philosophy seriously since I retired, I have been trying to set down in a short manuscript—written mostly for my own benefit, though I hope it will eventually be readable by someone who knows the literature—exactly where I have landed on decision theory. The position I find myself holding has roughly these parts. (1) The core idea I am convinced by is the updateless or functional one: that the object of evaluation is not the causal upshot of the act I perform now, given what I happen to know now, but the output of whatever procedure I am running, assessed as if I were settling once and for all what that procedure should return in every situation in which it might be run. (2) Accordingly I one-box in Newcomb's problem, and I also one-box in the transparent variant where the boxes are glass and I can see whether the million is sitting there. (3) I pay the driver in Parfit's hitchhiker. (4) I refuse to pay in the XOR blackmail case, that is, the case where the letter reaches me exactly when one or the other but not both of the following holds: I have a painful disease, or I will pay. (5) I smoke in the smoking lesion, where a gene produces both the lesion and the taste for cigarettes but nothing I decide now changes whether I have the gene. (6) I want none of this to rest on anything metaphysically exotic: I am a thoroughgoing determinist about the physical world, I think there is no room for a special sense in which I could have done otherwise, and I suspect that the talk of logical counterfactuals and of algorithmic similarity that surrounds these theories is ordinary reasoning about what a reliable predictor would have recorded, dressed up in unfortunate vocabulary. (7) Connected to this, I take seriously the objection that there is no principled cutoff between a predictor who simulates me in fine detail, one who runs a coarse psychological model of me, and one who has merely read a long questionnaire I once filled in—and I do not think a theory of rationality should turn on where along that spectrum a line gets drawn. (8) Finally, I am not prepared to give up ordinary responsiveness to evidence: if the radio says the bridge is out I turn the car around, and I regard any theory that recommends avoiding free information as a non-starter, whatever else it gets right.

What I cannot do, and what I would like from you, is locate the fault lines myself. I have one inchoate worry to the effect that (6) is doing less work than I assume—that if the predictor's reliability is only a correlation, I owe an explanation of why the smoking lesion comes out differently from Newcomb's problem, and I am not certain I have one that does not quietly reinstate the machinery I said I distrusted—and a quite different worry about whether the transparent variant in (2) is even a case my principles can reach, since a perfect predictor should never let me see an empty box in the first place. I do not trust either worry. Please treat the set as the object of scrutiny: tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look it, rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than listing them as equal, and for each one say what exactly would have to be given up and what that would cost me. In particular I want to know whether the least expensive repair is to abandon the deflationary story in (6), to retract one or another of the case verdicts, to accept some primitive notion of subjunctive dependence on my own procedure that I would rather not accept, or to make some distinction I have not drawn. Where an item of mine is simply orthogonal to the rest, or where the real pressure comes from something I have not listed at all, say that too. I would rather be told that one of the eight is incoherent on its own than be given a tidy reconciliation of all of them.
