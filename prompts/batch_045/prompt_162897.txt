I am in the lower sixth and I have been asked to run a twenty-five-minute taster session on the question "Is the brain a computer?" for two Year 10 forms. The pupils are fourteen and fifteen, mixed attainment, none of them has studied philosophy, and a fair number will be in the room mainly because it is warmer than the playground. I have a whiteboard, no projector and no handouts, and I want to take a show of hands on the question at the beginning and again at the end.

I am not asking you to explain computationalism to me. I have read enough to follow the arguments, and I would rather do the rest of the reading myself. What I need is a plan for explaining it to them, and specifically the parts of a plan that only make sense if you know how this particular audience will mishear it. So:

1. The order, and why. What has to be true of the word "computer" before they can so much as use it in the question, and how do I get them there without opening on a definition? More importantly, should the distinction between "we can describe it as computing" and "calling it a computer explains something about it" be established before or after the carrying example? Give me the dependency, not just the sequence.

2. The one case that carries the session. What must it have, and what must it lack? And why not the two that occur to me first: a laptop compared part by part with a brain, and a calculator doing what a pupil does in an arithmetic test?

3. The misconception map. Here is what I expect: (a) it is just a very fast calculator, with transistors standing in for neurons; (b) it cannot be, because brains are wet, analogue, and have no central clock or stored programme; (c) our class uses an AI assistant for homework, and several pupils will either say it understands them, so software can understand, or say it obviously does not, so computation can never yield understanding; (d) it is all physics and chemistry either way, so the word computer is doing no work; (e) if it were a computer we could copy someone onto a laptop and they would be the same person. Which of these are the same confusion in different clothes? Which is a genuine objection I should not defuse? At exactly what point in the sequence should each be headed off, and which one should I actively invite because the session needs it to get off the ground?

4. Something of my own. I have been playing with a test: a thing is a computer in the interesting sense only if you can hold the hardware fixed, change the programme, and get systematically different behaviour out of the same stuff. A rock or a thunderstorm fails, because you cannot vary the computational description without varying the physics as well. Does this help fifteen-year-olds, or is it a philosopher's distinction in disguise? Where would it go? And here is what I actually want to know: does it smuggle in the conclusion, given that whether the brain has anything separable playing the role of a programme is close to the thing being argued about? If it does, tell me how to present it honestly, and whether the honest version is still usable at this level.

5. The check. One question — not "so, is the brain a computer?" — that shows me whether they have grasped the structure of the disagreement rather than a slogan. Say what a satisfactory answer would have to contain and what a plausible-but-wrong answer would look like.

6. What to leave out, and to say out loud that I am leaving out. Name the two things I should refuse to go into, and the sentence to use when somebody asks. My guesses are the full Chinese Room thought experiment and the machinery of Turing machines, but tell me if I am wrong.

7. The ending. I want them to leave with the question open rather than with my verdict on it, and I suspect that is produced by how the sequence is built rather than by anything I say in the last minute. Tell me how the ordering should be arranged so that an open ending is the natural result.

Please write in British English, and keep the whole plan to roughly 600–800 words: I would rather have the ordering argued than illustrated with material I could have found myself.
