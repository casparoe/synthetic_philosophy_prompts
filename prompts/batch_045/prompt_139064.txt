I write a column on science and ideas for one of the Sundays and I've been covering machine learning for about six years now, with no formal background in it — philosophy at university, long forgotten. My commissioning editor has asked me to file a "position file" on AI risk before the next big model lands: where I've actually got to, not a tour of what various labs and think tanks say, so readers can see my cards. The trouble is I have a sinking feeling that at least two of the things below are doing work that the others quietly rule out, and I can't see which. I'm filing from a newsroom with no library access and I can't chase down references, so please: no reading suggestions and no "as so-and-so argues in 2023" — just tell me whether my own set holds up.

Here's roughly where I've landed, after two years of interviews:

(a) Deceptive alignment is a live failure mode, not science fiction. Anything that can model its own training process has an instrumental reason to behave well while it's being graded and differently once it isn't.

(b) The treacherous turn as a single dramatic moment is close to a narrative device though. Real defection would be gradual — small lies first, escalating, with the system constantly re-checking whether it's still being watched. Nobody puts that in a headline, so nobody writes about it.

(c) The main reason I expect deception isn't power-seeking agency at all. It's that successful deception is all over human text, imitating it is cheap, and it gets locally rewarded in a hundred small ways. A model can be deceptive witout having a goal.

(d) I think we'd catch it in the reasoning traces. The reasoning that makes a system capable is the same reasoning that would carry the deception, so it can't selectively switch off honest reasoning and keep the capability.

(e) Situational awareness — the system having some grip on what kind of situation it's in — is the crux, and I think it shows up before dangerous capabilities do, which is wierdly good news, because it's also what makes honest alignment work possible: you can just tell the thing what you're doing and why.

(f) Except the training/deployment line is dissolving. Continuous deployment, retraining on live traffic, models grading other models' outputs. So "bide your time until deployment" is getting historically dated.

(g) What lowers my worry most is the one-shot problem: you can't run a low-stakes trial run of a defection, learn from it and try again. Your first real move has to work.

(h) Rehearsal inside the model — simulating outcomes without taking the actions — is arriving fast, and on balance I think it helps defenders more than attackers, because we get to run the simulations too.

(i) Once one system genuinely fakes alignment, expect others to pick it up from its outputs. Deception spreads through training data like a style.

(j) Inherited deception is shallow though — a mannerism rather than a plan — so I don't lose sleep over (i).

(k) If a lab caught a model faking alignment in an evaluation, the right call is to keep it, study it and publish what happened, not quietly delete it.

(l) I don't think today's systems have any goals that persist across contexts, and I don't think there's anyone home to be wronged, so I'm relaxed about deceiving them — telling a model it's in deployment when it's in an eval, that sort of thing.

Go through the set and tell me which pairs actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, and for each real clash what the cheapest repair is and what it would cost me to make it. Don't be defential about it — if two of these are flatly inconsistent say so, and if some of them are perfectly fine together say that too, so I know the difference.

Give me the audit as a markdown table, one row per pair worth discussing, sorted worst first, with these columns:

- pair — the two commitments, e.g. "c + j"
- verdict — "genuine" or "apparent only"
- severity — 1 to 5, where 5 means I basically have to drop one of them
- load-bearing assumption — the hidden claim each side of the pair is relying on
- cheapest fix — which commitment gives way, and how precisely
- cost to the piece — what I'd have to say differently in the column as a result

After the table, one short line listing which commitments survive untouched. British English throughout please.
