I'm in the first year of a psychology degree and I've been reading a popular-science chapter on language and thought that my flatmate, who reads linguistics, dismisses as Whorfian propaganda. I'd like to know what I should actually trust before I argue with her. As I noted the chapter down, it claims the following: that Sapir and Whorf jointly set out the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis, the thesis that your language determines what you can think, so that speakers of different languages inhabit incommensurable mental worlds; that Whorf established this by showing Hopi has no words, tenses or constructions referring to time, from which it follows that the Hopi have no concept of time at all; that the notorious case of the Inuit, with their dozens upon dozens of words for snow, lets them perceive distinctions in snow that English speakers simply cannot see; that Russian speakers, who have two basic colour terms where English has one, are measurably faster at telling two blues apart, which shows that the number of colour words in your language fixes how many colours you literally see; and that because Spanish marks the word for bridge as masculine and German marks it as feminine, the two groups were found to describe bridges with adjectives fitting their grammatical gender, proving grammar reaches down into perception itself. My flatmate also said, rather vaguely, that later fieldwork turned up plenty of temporal expressions in Hopi, that the snow-word count was inflated, and that someone failed to repeat the bridge study — but I can't tell whether any of that is right, and I don't want to take her word for it either.

The chapter then moves to a case nobody can look up, because the author gives no reference. He describes "Kavari", supposedly spoken by a few thousand highland farmers, with two features: every past-tense statement must carry an obligatory marker saying whether the speaker saw the event, was told it, or inferred it; and there is no grammaticalised future tense, later events being expressed with a construction that literally means something like "setting out towards". The author reports a study in which Kavari speakers and speakers of the regional lingua franca, matched for years of schooling, were compared on two tasks: the Kavari speakers did better on a source-memory test (remembering whether they had seen something or only been told it), and when a delay-discounting task described future rewards using the "setting out towards" phrasing rather than a plain paraphrase, they chose more patiently. From this he concludes that grammar builds cognitive machinery that speakers of other languages simply lack, and that if English-speakers were taught to mark evidentiality and to talk about the future "Kavari-style", they would become markedly less likely to pass on things they had merely heard, and markedly less prone to credit-card debt.

His last section is the bit I find most dubious: he says Whorf's real vindication has arrived in the data centre, since a large language model has nothing but words and the statistical patterns among them and can nonetheless reason about physics and write verse, which "proves that a system's whole cognition is shaped by the linguistic patterns it is immersed in, and that there is no thinking apart from language". My flatmate said that is either trivially true or a category error, possibly both. Could you go through the chapter's claims one at a time and tell me what a working psycholinguist would accept, what is a defensible simplification, what quietly conflates two claims the literature keeps apart, and what is just wrong — and whether the practical conclusions he draws follow from whatever survives? I'd be grateful if you'd also say whether my flatmate's three doubts are fair, how much weight a study like the Kavari one, as I've described it, could really bear (given the obvious worries about causation running the other way, culture, schooling, contact with the lingua franca, and how the tasks were phrased), and what sort of further evidence would actually move the question. If you can put a rough number on it — what fraction of the chapter's central claims you would personally defend, with your reasoning — that would help me most of all.
