I should say at the outset that I am not a philosopher. I spent thirty-five years as a neurophysiologist, mostly recording from motion-selective neurons in extrastriate visual cortex, and since retiring I have been auditing a philosophy of mind seminar at a nearby university. Next month I have been asked to give a forty-minute paper to that group, and I would like to arrive with a proper Q&A prep sheet rather than my usual habit of improvising and then discovering on the walk home what I should have said.

The thesis I am defending is a deliberately deflated version of the language of thought hypothesis, which I call the addressing thesis. Roughly: what is worth salvaging from that tradition is not the claim that there is a neural syntax, or that Mentalese is innate, or that all cognition is computation over sentences, but the claim that there is exactly one representational format we know of in which an arbitrarily large space of structured states can be addressed, evaluated, and recombined at a cost that does not scale with the size of that space — a format with discrete constituents, role–filler structure, and token-level addressability. My argument has four steps: (1) any system that must score a combinatorial space of candidate states, in order to select among them, must have a way of picking out individual candidates and their parts; (2) picking out is not the same as similarity — a vector that is close to many candidates is not a way of addressing any one of them — so similarity-based formats cannot discharge this function in general; (3) so such a system needs constituent structure with addressable tokens; and (4) this is corroborated by a test I have been running on trained sequence models, which I call the recombination-transfer test: instead of holding out surface strings, one holds out an entire role–filling pattern while leaving its parts well represented in training, and then measures whether error at test tracks a measure of binding novelty rather than string edit distance or token frequency. My provisional result is that it tracks binding novelty, and I want to claim this as a partial vindication of the addressing thesis and a partial explanation of why purely similarity-based systems degrade off distribution.

The room will be about twenty people, and I can tell you its composition fairly precisely. Roughly half are cognitive science graduate students and two postdocs from a machine learning lab who work on interpretability: they think about representation in terms of features, directions in activation space, and circuits, they are entirely comfortable with distribution shift as an explanatory notion, they have no patience for the debates of the 1970s and 1980s, and they will be the ones who actually press me on the details of my test — held-out splits, baselines, whether my binding-novelty measure was fitted after I saw the results. There is one senior philosopher of mind who holds that ascriptions of representational content in cognitive science are instrumental, that there is no principled line between a system that genuinely represents and one we merely find it convenient to describe as if it did, and who is the only person in the room who has read the classical literature carefully. There are two people of a predictive-processing and dynamical-systems stripe who think that talking about internal sentences is a caricature of what nervous tissue does. And there is one developmental psychologist who will want to know what any of this implies for infants and for non-human animals, which I currently propose to answer by saying that nothing in the thesis settles it and then changing the subject.

What I would like from you is a prep sheet, not a defence of my view. Give me the eight or nine objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each phrased the way the relevant subgroup would phrase it rather than in philosopher's jargon — the interpretability people in their own vocabulary, the dynamical people in theirs, the senior philosopher in his. For each: a reply I could give in about two minutes, and a note on where that reply is soft or evasive. Tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes, before anyone has to drag it out of me. Tell me which single reply has to land if the overall thesis is to survive the session, and why. And name the weakest point in my own argument — I suspect it is the step from needs addressable structure to has constituents with semantic values, but I may be wrong about that — and tell me how to defend it rather than hope nobody notices. Include the question I am privately hoping nobody asks, which I think is some version of what would have to happen for you to give this up.

Finally, and this matters to me: be committed. Do not hand me a menu of options with the costs of each and leave me to choose. Rank the objections, say which of them you take to have real force and which are largely verbal or reftable, say plainly whether step (3) or step (4) is where my argument breaks, and tell me how much of the thesis I should cut if I want it to survive this particular room. If you think I am wrong about something, say so directly and give your reasons; I would rather be corrected before the talk than during it.
