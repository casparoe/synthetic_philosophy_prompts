I've got a 90-minute slot with my third-year value theory seminar — 18 students, they've already done the repugnant conclusion last week so I really don't want another population case — and I want them to *feel* the pull of a spectrum argument for thinking "better than" might not be transitive.

The case I had in mind (steal it, or swap it for something better, but it needs the same shape): audio mastering and the loudness war. You start with a raw live tape that has tons of dynamic range but not much punch. Each pass through the chain adds a little loudness and immediacy and costs a little dynamic range. At each step, if you just A/B the two versions side by side, the new one sounds better, or at least not worse. Run enough passes and you end up with a crushed, lifeless brick of a master that everyone agrees is worse than the raw tape you started from — and no single pass was the one that ruined it.

What I need is a runnable activity, not a handout. They should build the chain themselves, or at least authorize each link one at a time without being able to see the whole staircase, and they should end up having *done* something that only gets named afterwards: either they've collectively signed off on a cycle of "better than" judgments, or, better, they've been pumped around it with something small at stake. No lecture up front, and they shouldn't hear "transitive," "intransitive," "spectrum," "money pump," or even "better than" as a technical term until the debrief.

So: rules, materials (I've got index cards, a whiteboard, and I can scrounge poker chips or raffle tickets), a payoff scheme that doesn't involve real money (bonus participation points or a coffee-voucher raffle is fine), a timeline for the 90 minutes, and the debrief.

The debrief has to map moments in the room onto the three exits I want them to walk out able to state, because I don't want them to just conclude that value is weird:
- maybe "better than" really is intransitive, and the spectrum just reveals it;
- maybe each local judgment was fine and the mistake is thinking they add up — value is holistic, there are organic unities, so the package of maximum punch plus zero dynamics is bad even though every increment looked like an improvement;
- maybe the local judgments were never really "better" at all, just "I can't tell the difference" or "not noticeably worse," with imperfect discrimination doing all the work.

Tell me which moment of the activity is supposed to make each of those three feel like a live option, and where in the 90 minutes each one lands.

Also: tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and what to say or do in the moment. I definately know two already — someone will look ahead down the staircase and refuse step 4, and half the room will say it's just the heap/sorites again and switch off. Give me a line for the second one especially, because the shape is similar but the payoff is different and I don't want to loose them. And assume the usual seminar problems too: one loud student anchors every vote, someone tries to hoard their card, the trading stalls in the middle.

Last thing: be honest about what the game can't do. The chain is rigged by me, so they can always say I built the intransitivity into the cards rather than discovering it in the concept; and I suspect the activity can't really reproduce the thought that intransitivity would be a *rational* defect in the judgments rather than just a curiosity of my descriptions. Say plainly which bits I'll have to supply out loud at the end because no arrangement of rules or incentives could show them.
