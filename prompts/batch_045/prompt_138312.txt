I teach a graduate seminar on epistemic paradoxes, and my own verdicts on the lottery and the preface refuse to line up. I would like the principle they add up to, if there is one.

In a ten-thousand-ticket lottery with exactly one winner, asked about ticket number one the day before the draw, I deny that I know it will lose, and I would refuse to assert it. Yet in my own four-hundred-page monograph, which contains roughly two thousand substantive assertions, I take myself to know each one, and I also write the standard preface disclaimer that the book certainly contains further errors; both attitudes seem to me impeccable. If I pare the book down to two claims, each of which I rate at about 0.9 with independent chances of error, the disclaimer still seems permissible but I feel the strain, and with a single claim it is plainly incoherent, even though nothing about the arithmetic of the case has changed shape. Consider next a reference database of a hundred thousand entries, each individually warranted, plus a query function that returns their conjunction: I say the addition of the aggregation function makes no individual entry one whit less assertible, and that a system may be composed wholly of warranted parts while the one sentence it is disposed to output is certainly false. What I cannot settle is the diagnostic panel: a thousand assays, each 99.9 percent accurate, one positive result returned, and no other evidence about the patient. My instinct says I know nothing, but the structure looks exactly like the monograph.

My tentative principle is that support is adequate for knowledge and assertion only when it is claim-local — an argument, a check, a measurement directed at that claim — and never when it is merely a base rate over a set some member of which is certain to fail; and that where support is claim-local, the resulting attitudes need not be closed under known conjunction. Test this against the cases, repair it where it breaks, and say honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon. Note that it seems to entail that the author of a second edition who has corrected forty located errors may no longer assert that errors remain, since at that point the disclaimer is pure base rate, which I find hard to accept. Finally, tell me what the principle predicts for a case I have not listed: a lottery in which the draw may yet be cancelled, so that no ticket wins.
