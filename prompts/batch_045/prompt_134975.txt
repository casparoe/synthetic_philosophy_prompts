I'm giving a philosophy-department colloquium in three weeks, and the paper defends this claim: Solomonoff induction does not vindicate Occam's razor for any agent like us, and the obvious repair — moving from plain Kolmogorov complexity to a time-bounded complexity, Levin-style Kt or the speed prior family — converts the razor from an a priori result into an empirical hypothesis about this world. Three steps: (i) the convergence theorem is asymptotic, and the machine-relative constant in its bound can swamp any volume of evidence we will in fact ever get, so the usual "UTM choice washes out" reply buys nothing at our sample sizes; (ii) the specific 2-to-the-minus-K weighting is not itself delivered by the framework — dominance is a property of an entire class of universal priors, and nothing in the setup singles Solomonoff's out as the one; (iii) the time-bounded variants restore computability, but they make predictive success depend on a time bound, and getting that bound right is a contingent claim about the causal grain of the world, not something argument gives you. I call that contingent claim the modest-computation hypothesis, and my conclusion is that the razor is warranted the way a well-confirmed empirical generalization is, not the way a lemma is.

The room will be mostly mainstream epistemologists who treat simplicity as one theoretical virtue among several and are politely skeptical of formalism; one Bayesian statistician who will want to know how this differs from minimum-description-length model selection; one formal epistemologist who has actually worked through the convergence proof and thinks step (ii) is straightforwardly confused; and a philosopher of science who holds that simplicity is interest-relative all the way down.
