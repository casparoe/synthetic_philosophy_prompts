Me and my roommate got into a genuinely long fight after a Casablanca rewatch last night — like, three hours, popcorn gone, the whole thing — and I want someone to actually settle which of us is reading the movie wrong. Here's the fight. He's been on a Rand kick, and he says the film is basically a two-hour argument for altruism in Rand's technical sense: the view that the beneficiary of your action, not the value it has to you, is what makes it good, and that the virtuous act is the one where you surrender a greater value for a lesser one (or for a non-value). On that reading Rick's move at the airstrip is the textbook case — he hands over the thing he cares about most so someone else can keep his work and his wife, and the movie frames exactly this as the moment he stops being a cynic and becomes a good man. And it's worse than one scene: the film's whole moral geometry is against the guy who says he doesn't stick his neck out for anybody, and for the guy who lives for a cause bigger than himself. So, he says, if you take Rand's critique seriously you have to say the movie is corrupt at the level of its ending — the redemption arc is the corruption.

I pushed back with two things and I'm not sure either works. First: Rand's definition is supposed to be about sacrifice, and sacrifice means you actually lose something net. But maybe Rick isn't doing that at all. He'd been a person with real convictions before the movie starts — running guns against fascists, fighting in Spain — and the version of him we meet in the cafe is a guy who's buried his own values under a pose of neutrality. On that reading giving Ilsa up isn't a loss, it's him getting himself back: an investment, or a re-ranking, where what he recovers (being the kind of man who acts on what he thinks matters) is worth more to him than what he gives up. My roomate says that's just a denial that the loss is real and the movie clearly means it to hurt. Second, I floated a category I basically made up on the spot and want to know if it's real or if I'm smuggling in altruism with a nicer name: what I called declarative renunciation — giving something up where the point isn't to transfer value to another person (so it isn't altruism by the beneficiary test) and isn't to buy something back for yourself (so it isn't trade or investment), but to make the actual rank of your own values visible and binding, to yourself and to anyone watching. Renault tossing away his Vichy perks at the end felt like the small version of this to me, and the hill-of-beans speech felt like the big one.

So: take all three framings seriously and tell me where each one gets a grip on the film and where the film resists it. Which scenes would you point at as evidence for each — I'd especially want your take on the La Marseillaise scene, Ilsa's own earlier renunciation at the train station, and whether Laszlo functions as a moral ideal or as somebody the movie quietly uses. Is my declarative-renunciation thing a genuine fourth option in the moral taxonomy, or is it altruism with the beneficiary bracket quietly moved? And if the honest critic has to say the movie really is committed to something Rand would call altruism, say that plainly rather than rescuing it — I can accept losing the argument, I just want to lose it for the right reasons. Don't worry about quotations or citations, I know the film well enough; I just want the reading. End with a last line that gives me the bottom line on which reading the film actually supports, in the form: Verdict: <one sentence, naming which of the readings you're going with, or saying that the film defeats all of them>.
