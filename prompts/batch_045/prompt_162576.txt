I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on rationality and reasons — about fifteen students, one ninety-minute session, no phones, and no real money (we play for raffle tickets or chocolate). Last year I ran a simple trust game in the same slot and it collapsed into a lesson about reputation in repeated interaction, which is not what I'm after, so I want something tighter. The session should make students feel the force of Parfit's hitchhiker: a stranded traveller is offered a lift on condition of a promise to pay on arrival; once in town the promise costs more to keep than to break; both parties can see this in advance, so no lift is offered and both lose everything. More exactly, I want them to feel the force of the step that people usually skip past: the disposition that would solve the hitchhiker's problem has to survive his knowing, at the moment of arrival, that his reason to pay has lapsed. Binding yourself with a resolution or a device may not be enough, because at that later moment you are fully informed and free, and a resolution that tracks your reasons will release you. What seems to be needed is that it can be rational, earlier, to bring it about that you are later moved by something that is not a response to your reasons at all — and I want that to land as a problem they have walked into, not as a thesis I announce.

So design me the activity. Constraints on the mechanics. Each hitchhiker-driver pair must meet once, anonymously, and never interact again, so that reputation, reciprocity and future consequences cannot do any of the work; tell me how you'd actually enforce that (ID numbers, sealed envelopes, pairing procedure, and how the debrief talks about aggregate outcomes without exposing who did what). The second-stage choice has to be genuinely against the payer's interest in a way that guilt, friendship, or the hope of approval from me cannot offset — no transfers to a person in the room, and calibrate the stakes so that paying hurts a little but nobody is tempted to grandstand. There must be no external enforcement available: no escrow, no contracts, no photos, no apps. But rather than banning these by fiat, which students read as cheating, I'd rather you build in one or two deliberately weak substitutes they can reach for themselves — an unenforceable public pledge, a deposit that only partly binds, a promise repeated out loud — so that they discover the cheap fixes don't close the gap and are pushed toward the thought that the only thing that would work is being a certain kind of person. Ideally there is also a moment in which the driver has to judge credibility rather than being told it, and a moment in which each hitchhiker, before deciding, writes down in one sentence why she is about to decide as she is: those sentences are the raw material for the debrief. No lecture up front, and they shouldn't hear the words Parfit, hitchhiker, self-interest theory, or rational irrationality until afterwards.

What I need on paper: rules, materials, a payoff scheme, and a minute-by-minute timeline. Then a debrief that maps specific moments in the room onto specific parts of the structure — the round where almost nobody gets a lift, the student who says she'd pay because she said she would, the driver who says he trusted her because she looked like the type, the student who says he'd sign something binding if he could — and that then separates the three things the activity will tend to blur: fixing the payoffs or the institutions, sticking to a plan you made earlier even though at the later point sticking to it is worse for you, and changing your motives so that at the later point you aren't weighing reasons at all. Tell me which of the three the activity actually produced, and how to get from where the students are to the third. Then be honest about what will predictably go wrong: students paying anyway because the stakes are trivial and they feel watched; students dissolving the case with contract-and-reputation talk and refusing to engage; drivers all refusing at once so there is no second stage to debrief; the student who insists the promise itself gives him a reason, which turns the exercise into ordinary promise-keeping; and especially the student who says she'd just pay because she'd feel bad — I want a way of showing her that the feeling she is reporting is precisely the disposition under discussion, without making it sound like a trick. Finally, say plainly which feature of the case the activity cannot reproduce: my worry is that it distributes one person's conflict across two roles and two moments, so students take it to be a puzzle about other people's credibility rather than about their own future selves, and that a participant who decides to be the sort of person who pays is still deciding, which is exactly the kind of control the disturbing claim says you have to give up. Tell me what the facilitator has to supply in words at the end, and what to cut if I ever have to run it in fifty minutes instead of ninety.
