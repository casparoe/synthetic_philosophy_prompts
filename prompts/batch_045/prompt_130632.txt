In the reading group I go to, mostly engineers and one biologist, we have been working through a book that lays out a four-rung ladder of kinds of minds: Darwinian creatures, whose design is fixed by selection acting over generations; Skinnerian creatures, which can be reshaped within a single lifetime by reward and punishment; Popperian creatures, which can try out candidate actions in their heads and let the bad candidates fail before anything irreversible happens to the animal itself; and Gregorian creatures, which get most of their intelligence from thinking tools, such as words, numerals, diagrams and routines of argument, that other creatures built and handed down.

One member leans on an argument I cannot evaluate. He says all four rungs are the same algorithm, generate-and-test, running on a different substrate each time: the gene pool, the individual lifetime, the internal model, the shared tool-kit. From that he concludes there is no principled line anywhere on the ladder, and in particular none between our minds and a neural network trained on human text. He calls that a fifth rung, a Scriptorian creature: one whose candidate hypotheses arrive already written, by minds that had them tested by living, and which therefore has the same kind of mind we do, only with a shorter biography. When I objected that a network does not die when its guesses are wrong, he said neither does a Popperian creature, and that this is exactly what makes it Popperian.

Please audit the analogy. What maps onto what, meaning what plays the role of generation, of the test, of retention and of heredity at each rung, and where does the mapping break? Which features of selection on replicators does the generate-and-test reading quietly carry into a lifetime, into a head, into a culture, into a training run, that those targets may not actually have? Does the test mean the same thing when nothing dies? Does it matter whether the generator sits inside the creature or outside it? Is there anything in foresight, or in having a stake in the outcome, that generate-and-test language cannot express? Then tell me whether his conclusion about the fifth rung survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing the work all along. Keep clear throughout which claims are settled among people who work on this and which are genuinely contested, and flag it whenever a contested view is being stated as if it were the consensus. I would rather read this as continuous prose, with no headings, bullets, bold text or tables, since I read on my phone.
