The U.S. Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act of 2008 (GINA) bars the use of genetic information in health insurance and employment: it prohibits group health plans and health insurers from denying coverage to a healthy individual or charging that person higher premiums based solely on a genetic predisposition to developing a disease in the future, and it bars employers from using genetic information in hiring, firing, job placement, or promotion. The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission’s fact sheet on Title II of GINA defines the protected category by saying:

“Genetic information means:
Information about an individual's genetic tests;
Information about the genetic test of a family member;
Family medical history;
Requests for and receipt of genetic services by an individual or a family member; and
Genetic information about a fetus carried by an individual or family member or of an embryo legally held by an individual or family member using assisted reproductive technology.”

Several features of this practice puzzle me. First, a relative’s diagnosis—family medical history—counts as genetic information about an untested individual, even though no one has read that individual’s DNA; the pattern could reflect shared environment as much as shared genes. Second, merely requesting or receiving genetic services is protected, even if no test result is ever produced or is negative. Third, the health-insurance protection is framed around a healthy individual and a future predisposition; a disease that has already manifested is not, on the usual summary, protected as mere genetic information. Fourth, employers may not request, require, or purchase genetic information about applicants or employees, but they may acquire it from commercially and publicly available sources—newspapers, books, magazines, and public websites—while still being told that there are no exceptions to the prohibition on using genetic information to make employment decisions. The EEOC’s illustration is that an employer may not reassign an employee out of a job it believes is too stressful merely because it learned of the employee’s family medical history of heart disease.

In a neighboring area, synthetic biologists have treated DNA as something that can be written and read. In 2008 the full set of Mycoplasma genitalium genes was constructed in the laboratory with watermarks added to identify the genome as synthetic; by 2010 the complete genome of a Mycoplasma mycoides subspecies was synthesized from a computer record and transplanted into a cell of Mycoplasma capricolum whose own DNA had been removed, and the recipient cell then lived and replicated according to the imported genome. The inserted watermark sequences are non-biological identifiers: stretches of DNA added so that later sequencing can reveal that the molecule was human-made.

Both practices take DNA, or “genetic information,” to be philosophically loaded. The legal practice protects a person against discrimination on the basis of a relative’s diagnosis, a request for a test, or a predisposition to future disease, without anyone having read a molecular sequence; the laboratory practice treats a molecule synthesized from a computer file as the controller of a cell’s traits and inserts arbitrary identifying tags into it.

What I want to know is what philosophical commitments about genetic information, and about whether DNA is a code, these practices presuppose. Reconstruct the implicit theory of genetic information at work in GINA and in the synthetic-genome practice. Does GINA strictly presuppose that DNA is a code, a program, or a text—something with semantic content, authorial origin, or a read/write distinction—or would a thinner view, on which genes are mere causal difference-makers or hereditary information is just one statistical predictor among many, license the same rules? Does the watermark practice show that DNA is semantically a code, or only that its sequence can bear arbitrary syntactic or identifying information that cells copy and sequencing later recovers?

Please distinguish what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely fit well with, and say where they are philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories of genetic information would license them. Handle the apparent inconsistencies explicitly: why is family medical history treated as the individual’s genetic information even without a DNA test, why is a request for services protected, why is future predisposition protected more readily than manifested disease, and why is acquisition from public sources permitted while use in employment decisions is not? Then tell me what would have to change in the practice if the presupposed view were replaced—for example, if we stopped treating DNA as a code and treated it as only one causal factor among many, or if we instead treated genetic information as fully semantic and code-like.

Finally, make the answer epistemically self-aware: say what would change your mind. Identify which argument or consideration would lead you to abandon or significantly revise your reconstruction of what the practices strictly presuppose, and say where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Distinguish what you would defend strongly from what is speculative, indicate your confidence in the main claims, and tell me what would raise or lower that confidence. I do not want a merely assertive interpretation; I want to know where your reading of the practices is most vulnerable and what would count against it.
