I'm a political philosopher, and for the past eighteen months I've sat as one of five members — the only one without a legal or audit background — on the Integrity Panel of an international development foundation that funds small infrastructure and livelihood projects in a dozen countries, several of them in West Africa and South Asia, as well as in two English cities. Our founding instrument defines our remit in the familiar terms of the abuse of entrusted power for private gain, but nearly every case that reaches us is one in which no money has changed hands, or in which money or gifts have moved in ways that are locally entirely unremarkable. I've been trying to reconstruct the rule we are actually running, because I suspect the definition we cite does very little work. Our verdicts so far: a procurement officer who placed his brother-in-law's firm on a shortlist from which it then won a contract at a market rate for work that was well done — he declared the relationship and took no part in the scoring — corrupt; a district nurse who accepted small customary cash payments from patients' families after treatment had finished, which everyone in the district expected, which she could not have refused without giving offence, and which made no difference to whom she saw or how quickly — not corrupt; a municipal water engineer who sent two council workers and a council vehicle on a Saturday to repair a privately built borehole serving his own village — corrupt; a village water committee that presented the headman with a goat and a bottle of whisky before he authorised a well, as local custom requires, where the authorisation would plainly have been given anyway — not corrupt; and a mayor who steered a small public works programme preferentially towards firms in the wards that had voted for him, who gained nothing personally and made no secret of it — corrupt.

The case on Monday's agenda is this. A regional grants panel funded by our foundation awarded four grants from a published rubric, scrupulously applied, and the four highest-scoring applications included three from one recently arrived refugee community. In the months before applications opened, one panel member — herself a member of that community and the unpaid chair of its mutual-aid society — ran free workshops on putting together a strong application, using her own time but our premises and our branding, and advertised them only through the mutual-aid society's own messaging groups, so that in practice only people from that community attended. She disclosed her position, never voted on any application from the society or from anyone she had coached, no money or gift ever moved, the scoring was clean, and no unsuccessful applicant has complained — although the unsuccessful applicants outside that community did not know the workshops existed. Our auditors have flagged it, and I have to vote.

I can get one formulation to generate all five verdicts — something on the lines of office resources or discretion deployed for the advantage of a section rather than for the purposes of the office, irrespective of whether anyone consents or anything is monetised — and I can also get a rival formulation in terms of benefits distributed by connection rather than by publicly announced and impartially applied criteria to generate them, though I genuinely cannot tell whether that second rule reaches upstream of the formal decision or stops at the allocation itself. What I'd be grateful for is a recommendation rather than a survey: what should we rule here if we want to be consistent with ourselves, which of our five precedents is the closest analog and why, which of the competing rules that fit our track record predict what, which plausible rules the track record actually rules out, and — if our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case — what principle we would be adopting with each of the available rulings and how far that principle would then commit us elsewhere, since I am also worried that the verdicts above smuggle in a distinctly Western ideal of impersonal administration. Please also name the further case that would best discriminate between the rules you think are live, tell me which consideration or further fact would move you to the opposite ruling, and say where you think your reasoning is most likely to break down; flag any empirical assumption you are relying on that I could actually check before Monday. Something in the region of 700 to 900 words would be ideal.
