I'm in a first-year molecular biology module and our lecturer says "DNA is the blueprint of life" constantly — last week he used it to argue that once you've got an organism's genome sequence you've got, in principle, everything you need to build or predict that organism. That feels like a huge leap, but I can't say where it goes wrong. A friend on the course says the problem isn't the word "blueprint" so much as the slogan lumping together several different relations DNA could have to the adult organism: being reliably correlated with it, being a cause of it, being a representation or description of it, and — her own addition — what she calls a "context-dependent read-out", where DNA is a resource that the machinery already in the cell or egg uses differently depending on what else is present (read in a different frame, spliced differently, and so on), so that nothing in the sequence by itself settles what you end up with. All I really have to go on is the first-year version of the code (it's "degenerate", several codons per amino acid, that sort of thing).

Can you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims "DNA is a blueprint" — and the cousins "DNA is a code" and "DNA is a program" — could be making, from the true-but-trivial ones through the substantive and defensible ones to the ones that are just false, and tell me which of them, if any, would actually get my lecturer from the slogan to his conclusion?

One more thing: I gather people who work on this argue about whether genetic "information" talk is literal or just a useful metaphor, but I can't tell what's settled and what's still live. Please mark clearly which claims are consensus among people who actually work on this and which are contested, and flag it when you're giving me your own read rather than the standard one.
