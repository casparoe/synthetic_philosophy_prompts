I teach philosophy of law in a philosophy department, and this term I have been sitting in on my law school's faculty colloquium as preparation for a graduate seminar on statutory interpretation that I am co-teaching next spring. At the last colloquium, a senior colleague in administrative law was asked whether a court faced with a provision whose literal application to a novel technology seems to defeat the provision's evident point may consult that point. He answered: "Look — we are all textualists now. Nobody thinks Congress has an intention anymore, so there is nothing to consult." He said it in the tone one uses to close a question rather than to answer it. A second colleague, more or less an open purposivist, later threw back the older line: "the letter killeth, but the spirit giveth life." Both were received as having said something decisive, and I do not think either had.

The hypothetical I use in my seminar: a 1993 consumer-protection statute provides that "no person shall transmit, or cause to be transmitted, an unsolicited commercial message to a facsimile machine." The defendant runs a marketing firm that sends unsolicited advertisements over telephone lines to numbers at which the recipient's "fax" is a cloud service that converts the transmission into a PDF delivered to an email inbox and prints nothing. The plaintiff says the point of the provision was to stop advertisers from shifting the costs of unsolicited advertising onto recipients; the defendant says that what he transmits to is not a facsimile machine and never was one.

What I want from you is a disambiguation of both lines, dense enough to hand to graduate students. Please do not give me a reading list, a survey of the literature, or citations to secondary sources; I want the distinctions themselves.

For "We are all textualists now" (usually credited to Justice Kagan), set out the distinct precise claims the sentence could be making — as different from one another as you can make them, from the trivial to the radical. So far as I can tell these should include at least: a descriptive claim about what courts in fact do; a priority claim about where inquiry begins and what outranks what; a semantic claim about what the content of a statute consists in; an ontological claim about whether a multimember legislature can have intentions, or beliefs, or a point, at all; a legitimacy claim about what may count as law given the process by which law is made; a claim about the shifting extension of the word "textualism" itself; and an eliminative claim to the effect that purpose is never part of the law. Add any reading I have missed, including any that turns on what counts as "the text" (a single provision, a whole act, a corpus of related acts) rather than on what to do with it.

For each reading, give: the precise thesis; whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, a substantive but contested one, or straightforwardly false — and, where the claim is empirical, whether it is even plausible; and, most importantly, whether that reading, if true, would actually entitle my colleague to close the question the way he did. My suspicion is that the readings that are most defensible are ones a purposivist could cheerfully accept, and that the readings which would do the work he wanted are undefended. Tell me whether that is right.

Then give a shorter version of the same treatment for the second line. There I expect the interesting dividing lines to be about whether "the spirit" is an independent moral entity that judges enforce, a constituent of what the words mean, a tie-breaker consulted only when the words give out, or merely a name for the contextual information a competent reader would bring to any utterance; and about what "killeth" is supposed to consist in — a false result, a result the enacting legislature would have rejected, or a result no speaker would have intended to produce.

Then say whether there is any pair, one reading from each menu, such that both could be true without contradiction, or whether every reading strong enough to be interesting on one side contradicts every such reading on the other. If there is such a pair, tell me whether that shows the textualism/purposivism dispute to be partly verbal, and if there is not, locate the real disagreement. Finally, run the readings that survive against my facsimile hypothetical and say which of them, applied honestly, would actually resolve it — or why several of them fail to resolve it at all.

Format: begin with a single sentence giving your bottom line — which reading my colleague would have to mean for his move to work, whether that reading survives, and whether the two slogans are in the end compatible — then give the menus as tables (reading / precise content / verdict / whether it supports the use), then the application to the hypothetical, with no preamble and no restatement of my question. Under a thousand words if you can manage it.
