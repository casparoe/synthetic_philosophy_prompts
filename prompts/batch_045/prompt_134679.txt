I want a short story — roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words, in American English — that does for the question of when a legend becomes history what good conceptual fiction does for a concept: makes the difficulty something the reader feels in a character's situation rather than a thesis asserted in narration.

Use this setup, which is my own invention, so don't go looking for a source. The city's Archive keeps the founding narrative in two registers. There is the Vulgate: one fixed, beautiful, ritually recited account of how the city was founded, identical every year. And there is the Spoil: unsorted receipts, local variant tales, letters, contradictory king-lists, graffiti, boundary stones. By law the Spoil may never be edited into agreement with the Vulgate. The archivists' professional rule is that a detail of the founding counts as historical only if it also turns up independently in the Spoil — the thought being that material nobody shaped to fit the epic is the only material capable of corroborating it. Their second rule is the embarrassment heuristic: a detail that flatters the city is suspect; a detail that shames it is more likely genuine.

The complication the story has to live inside: the Spoil has been curated too. Someone has been planting corroboration, or quietly destroying it, and it's the independence assumption that fails — not the documents themselves. Meanwhile the protagonist owes a one-word verdict to a body that will act on it (a funding council, a priesthood, a Senate committee): did the founding happen, yes or no.

I've read the standard framing — the motte of something like this happened and the bailey of it happened as told — and I don't want it restated in the prose. I want three things to land through the plot instead. First, that confirmation of the vague claim does almost no work on the specific one, and that people keep spending it as though it does. Second, that the two rules can point in opposite directions on the same artifact: a forgery can be engineered to embarrass, and a genuine record can survive precisely because it flattered someone powerful enough to preserve it. Third, that the absence of a record is evidence only relative to a background model of how much of that kind of record should have survived, and that this model — not the past — is what the characters are really fighting about when they think they're fighting about the past.

Give me one scene built around a specific mundane document — a grain tally, a shipping ledger, a boundary stone — that moves the protagonist to a conclusion she can't fully justify to her colleagues. End with the verdict delivered and its consequences, not with an ironic shrug or an it-was-somewhere-in-between. Characters, a place, stakes; no fable with a moral stapled on, and no Socratic dialogue.
