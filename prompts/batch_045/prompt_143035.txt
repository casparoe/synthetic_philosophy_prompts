I work as a backend engineer, and a large part of my week is incident review: when something breaks, we write up what happened, and we deliberately avoid naming a person to blame — the working test is whether another engineer, with the same dashboards, the same runbook and the same level of sleep, would plausibly have done the same thing. A friend of mine doing a history PhD got annoyed with me for extending this to the July Crisis of 1914, and in the course of the argument she mentioned Fritz Fischer, the German historian who argued in the early 1960s that Imperial Germany deliberately brought on a European war to secure great-power status, and the enormous West German fight that followed. I've since been pulled into a volunteer side project — a small interactive web explainer, aimed at curious general readers and high school teachers with no history or philosophy training — and I want its centrepiece to be a decision tree that takes a reader from "a world war started in the summer of 1914" to a named position on who, if anyone, is to blame for it.

What I want from you is the tree itself, not an essay about the historiography and not a reading list. Concretely: around eight to twelve branch nodes, each phrased as a question someone could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and six to eight leaves, each a named position with one sentence on its least comfortable commitment. I've sketched the distinctions I'd use as nodes and I'd like you to keep, drop or reorder them as you think fit: was the big war something the decision-makers wanted, or merely something they knowingly risked; were its scale and its industrial character foreseeable from where they stood, and does that change the blame; does a great power's military mobilization count as an independent cause of the war or as a foreseeable response to someone else's; does backing an ally with an open-ended promise make you responsible for what that ally then chooses to do; and can a state, as opposed to the handful of people who actually decided, be the thing we blame at all. Please also build the thing from the logical space rather than from a list of names: sketch the combinations of answers the nodes allow, place the actual positions in that space, and tell me about the cells nobody occupies — is each of them incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored — and about any two positions your tree cannot actually separate, rather than papering over the collapse. One more thing I care about: please keep apart causal contribution (whose action made the difference), moral culpability (whose action was blameworthy), and liability to repair or pay, since in my day job those come apart constantly — the team whose deploy caused the outage is often not the team that owns the fix — and I suspect they come apart here too. If the tree can only sort along one of the three, say which and why.

Two last requests. First, on confidence: for each node, mark whether you're reasonably confident that this distinction is actually doing work in how people who study 1914 disagree, versus your own reconstruction from the logic of the question, and flag anywhere you're guessing or where you'd expect a historian to object that a node is a fake choice. Second, push back plainly rather than being helpful: if a decision tree is the wrong instrument here — if, say, the positions differ by emphasis and temperament rather than by answers to yes-or-no questions, or if "who is to blame" turns out not to be a well-formed question when the subject is a state and the events are over a century old, or if my incident-review vocabulary quietly imports an assumption that makes one position look better than it is — tell me that instead of producing the tree anyway. I would much rather hear that the centrepiece doesn't work and get one paragraph on what would work better.
