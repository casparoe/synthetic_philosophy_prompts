Hi. I'm 71, retired after 35 years in experimental particle physics (mostly calorimetry and trigger work), and for the last two years I've been sitting in on a philosophy department reading group because I got tired of hearing people in my old field use words like "risk" and "expected cost" as if they meant something obvious. I'm used to numbers that come with error bars and to asking where a number came from: in my lab we used to say that a systematic you can't bound isn't a number, it's a hope. That habit has now gotten me into trouble, because I've been trying to write down what I actually think about tiny probabilities attached to enormous stakes, and my gut verdicts on individual cases won't line up with any single rule I can state.

Please act as a professional philosopher who works on decision theory and on this specific cluster of puzzles. Take your time, start by brainstorming several candidate principles before you commit to one, and don't just tell me what the textbooks say. I don't want citations, reading lists, or names of papers — I have no library access and I wouldn't be able to check them anyway. Please answer in American English. Here are my verdicts, going purely on gut:

- Sure $100, or a coin flip for $220: I take the coin flip.
- Sure $1M, or a coin flip for $3M: I take the million. (I know, that's just diminishing marginal utility and not the interesting part.)
- $10k to an outfit with maybe a 1-in-100,000 shot at preventing something that would kill everyone alive: yes, obviously, and I'd do it again tomorrow.
- $2M to cut in half a 1-in-a-billion-per-century extinction risk that comes out of a physical model that has been tested against data and didn't know anyone was going to make a decision on it: yes.
- $2M to rescue four named, identifiable people from certain death: no, and I feel lousy about it. Note that this is the same expected number of lives as the previous case, and a certain rescue feels like it should count for *more* than a one-in-a-billion shot at the same number, so something here is off.
- The Pascal's mugger setup: a guy in a coffee shop says hand over $5 or he'll use powers from outside the matrix to inflict 10^30 units of suffering: no.
- Same guy escalates — 10^50, then 10^100, then "and I'd do it to 10^30 people each": still no, and my confidence doesn't budge at all. That worries me. If I really believed that any nonzero probability times a big enough stake dominates, I ought to crack somewhere as the number climbs, and I don't.
- A $1 ticket on a 1-in-10^12 chance of saving 10^15 lives: my honest reaction is "meh," and I decline. Offer me a portfolio of 10,000 such bets on independent events and I say yes. Offer me those same 10,000 bets one at a time over ten years and I say no every single time — which I realize is the same decision as the portfolio. I cant tell whether the packaging is allowed to matter.

The pattern I half-believe when I read my own list is that what moves me isn't the size of the stake at all, but the *provenance* of the tiny number: in the cases I say yes to, the number fell out of a model that was tested against data and that had no idea anyone would ever make a decision on it, whereas in the mugger cases the number was picked by someone who looked at my rule, found the number that breaks it, and then named a bigger one. I've started calling that "adversarially selected stakes" and I want to discount those to zero. But I can't tell whether that's a real distinction or just a way of being stubborn with better vocabulary, and it doesn't obviously say anything about the portfolio case. Someone in the reading group said the label for all this is "fanaticism" and that the standard fix is a bounded utility function, but that strikes me as crude in its own way — it seems to make you weirdly calm about large losses that are certain.

The case I actually want your judgment on, and the one I go back and forth on every week: an organization emails me a detailed plan to build server farms that will, they claim, run simulations containing 10^30 suffering minds unless I pay them $5,000. Unlike the guy in the coffee shop, I can check the engineering myself — it's just servers, the physics is fine, the power and cost figures are in the right ballpark, and their code passes an independent audit. The only part I cannot check is whether they would really switch it on. So the mechanism is verified and only the intent is testimony, and yes, the 10^30 was chosen to defeat whatever rule I'm running. What is the principle that best captures my verdicts, stated precisely enough that I can apply it myself, with each clause tied to the verdict that motivates it? What does it say about this last case? Which of my verdicts does it refuse to accommodate, and which one would you overturn? And what single further case would best discriminate between your top two candidate rules?
