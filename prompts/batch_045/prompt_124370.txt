I chair the allocations committee of a health research charity that awards one fixed envelope of money four times a year. We are six lay trustees and a scientific officer; we meet for a single afternoon; the money must all go; our decision is final. We are also required to publish written reasons to every applicant, including the ones we turn down, within two weeks. And because we meet quarterly with rotating membership, we have to be able to say how this quarter's choice relates to last quarter's without either being bound by it or drifting away from it at random.

Our difficulty is not missing information. It is that the things we compare do not share a measure. A cheap, well-evidenced intervention that gives many people a small benefit; an expensive service that gives a few severely ill people a large and irreversible one; an exploratory programme that will probably come to nothing and might change practice for everyone. Cost-per-outcome in one currency, or a weighted score that adds everything up, would settle these comparisons only by pretending the difficulty away, and my trustees will not accept that. So I want a procedure we can run — ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it returns a determinate result, and where it stops and hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts.

Specifications I care about, roughly in the order the procedure should handle them:

First, a diagnostic step. When two bids look incomparable, I want the committee to be able to tell apart at least four situations: that we simply cannot measure something we know to be comparable; that our comparisons are vague or only roughly comparable, so that more or better evidence would sharpen them; that the options are, in the technical sense, on a par — comparable, better in one respect and worse in another, but neither better overall nor equally good; and that there is a genuine lexical ordering or radical incomparability. The point of the distinction is that each diagnosis licenses a different next step, and I want that stated explicitly: which diagnoses are responsive to evidence, which leave the choice rationally underdetermined so that something other than a reason — commitment, the will, a decision to make an aim one's own — is what settles it, and which are settled by the ordering itself.

Second, a second diagnostic, because I think we conflate two things. Some of our hard choices are choices between genuinely permissible options, where the right account of what we are doing is that we commit. Others are conflicts in which we must act and there is a remainder whatever we do: the appropriate aftermath is regret, acknowledgment, perhaps apology or compensation to those who lose, even though we chose correctly. These call for different handling downstream, and I want a way for a committee — not an individual agent — to tell which it is in, and what it is supposed to do about it either way.

Third, a rule for agglomeration. Most quarters we are tempted by a large number of small improvements in one value set against a concentrated severe loss under another. Adding the small improvements up already presupposes a common unit, so I want the procedure to say what we may do here, what we must refuse to do, and how to describe the refusal honestly to an applicant who has twenty small studies and wants them counted.

Fourth, a rule about chance. Say when randomising is legitimate and when it is buck-passing, and distinguish a fair lottery among equally-placed claimants from tossing a coin between two programmes because we cannot decide. If the procedure ever permits the latter, I want to know what has to be true first.

Fifth, a rule about recurrence: what precedent may and may not do across quarters, how a committee tells a genuine re-weighing of aims from mere noise or from a change in who happens to be in the room, and what has to be recorded when we depart from what we did last time.

Throughout, mark the points at which judgment cannot be eliminated rather than smoothing them over, and say who signs off at each: the chair, the full committee, the scientific officer, or the board. Say what happens when the committee cannot agree. The whole thing has to be runnable inside one afternoon by people with no philosophical training, and it must not depend on monetising outcomes, on a health-economic common currency, on a weighted-sum spreadsheet, on a lexical ordering asserted without an account of why that ordering, or on calling for more evidence as a general answer.

Then run it. Start with the three-way choice above. Then take a quarter in which last quarter's precedent favoured the many-small-benefits programme and a new applicant makes that same case better than it was made before — say what the procedure does with precedent there. Finally, apply it to the case I expect to dislike: an application that is stronger than its rival on every consideration this committee has ever cited, arriving alongside a weaker one that would serve an aim we have not yet committed to. If your procedure permits funding the second and saying so, tell me what the published reason to the first applicant can honestly be, given that it cannot cite any deficiency in their proposal. If it does not permit it, say why not and what the procedure is missing.

Close by saying what the procedure cannot do. I have two worries in particular that I want addressed directly: that it cannot, from the armchair, certify which of the four diagnoses is the correct one in any actual case; and that a committee could use the language of incommensurability as cover for preference, fatigue, or noise. Say what would detect that, and say plainly where the procedure stops and a person has to answer for the choice.
