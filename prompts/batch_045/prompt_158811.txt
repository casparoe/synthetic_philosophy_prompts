I belong to a small reading group — a retired headteacher, a hospital physiotherapist, a software engineer, and three or four others — that meets monthly to work through short philosophical pieces. This term we have been reading Clifford's 1877 essay on the ethics of belief and James's 1896 lecture on the will to believe side by side, and we have got stuck on something that neither of them, as far as I can tell, isolates. The usual framing we started from is that Clifford lays down a strict rule about never believing on insufficient evidence, and that James replies that there are cases where the evidence is only reachable by first adopting the belief, so that withholding judgment is itself a way of risking the loss of truth. What I keep noticing is that the cases which look like James's cases and the cases which look like the ones Clifford was worried about are, from the inside, built exactly the same way, and I cannot find the seam.

Three instances. (1) A teacher decides, before she has any real evidence, that a quiet boy in her class is capable of more than his record suggests. Because of that she calls on him more often, waits longer for his answers, and hands him harder problems. By Christmas he is doing well, and she now has excellent evidence for what she believed in September. The striking thing is not just that the belief paid off but that the improvement is real: a teacher who had suspended judgment until the evidence came in would have produced no evidence, and would have been right, by her own lights, to go on suspending. (2) A man moves to a new street and decides early on, again without evidence, that his neighbours have taken to him. He is warmer and more available as a result; they respond with invitations and easy conversation; he now has evidence. But the same feedback would have arrived if they merely tolerated him, since politeness and liking are indistinguishable from where he is standing — and possibly even if his warmth had quietly irritated them. (3) A doctor forms an early hypothesis that a patient's symptoms are psychologically caused. Without intending anything dishonest, she asks questions that nudge the patient toward a stress narrative, hears in the answers what she has already half decided, and writes up a coherent psychosocial history that later colleagues treat as confirmation. The evidence mounts, and nothing in the process makes the hypothesis likelier to be true.

What the three share, as far as I can make out: the belief is adopted before the evidence; adopting it changes which evidence arrives; the evidence that arrives is genuinely produced downstream of the adopting; and it is available only to someone who has already adopted the belief, so that from outside there is no way to audit whether the loop was tracking the truth or merely generating agreeable data. One member of the group said this is just the familiar point that an expectation manufactures its own confirmation; another said it is just the old vice of seeing what you want to see. Both descriptions fit one of my instances and clearly fail another, and I cannot tell which instance is the paradigm. So: is there an established concept for this configuration as such — the production of unauditable, truth-indifferent evidence downstream of an adopted belief — and if so, what is it, what are its standard examples, and how is it marked off from the neighbouring notions that cover my first and third cases but not my second? If there is no established concept, I would rather have the carving than a name: a definition, a statement of which feature does the normative work, and boundary cases showing what is and is not an instance. Two further things I would like settled. First, does the permissibility of the belief genuinely differ across my three cases, and if so, is it the truth-making of the belief or the auditability of the feedback that carries the weight — so that a case where the loop could in principle be checked against a comparison group, or where the belief is publicly avowed and others collect the data, belongs on the James side even if the belief itself does nothing to make its object true? Second, and this is what started the argument: my engineer friend pointed out that all three instances work just as well if the person never believes the proposition at all but simply adopts a policy of acting as though it were true, since it is the acting that generates the feedback. If that is right, is the whole Clifford–James dispute misdescribed as being about belief, and does my phenomenon belong to some other attitude entirely? Please do not just hand me a label; and because I have no way to chase references, please argue the distinctions out rather than pointing me to papers or books.
