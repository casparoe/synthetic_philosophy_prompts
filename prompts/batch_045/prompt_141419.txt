I have come to philosophy late, after a working life spent on colonial marine invertebrates — siphonophores and their relations, where the question "is this one organism or many?" has a technical answer that settles nothing of interest — and I now sit, unpaid, on a small panel that advises our regional health board on which of the treatments it has been asked to fund it should actually pay for. The seminar I have been auditing on distributive ethics has convinced me that nearly every dispute in the room turns on one contested idea: that persons are separate, and that this separateness constrains what may be done to some for the sake of others. No one, however, will tell me what would settle the question of whether they have got the constraint right. So I would like the specification instead. Before I read another paper, tell me what an account of the moral significance of the separateness of persons would have to deliver in order to count as adequate — adequate, that is, for my purpose: a view that a committee such as mine could genuinely use to rank competing claims, under uncertainty, and without always knowing in advance which named individuals will end up benefiting.

Give me the desiderata as a list, each with the case or consideration behind it, and be explicit about what a theory is supposed to explain rather than merely assert. In particular I want to know: what separateness is meant to consist in once one stops helping oneself to a metaphysically tidy boundary between one person and the next — my animals offer nested candidate units all the way up, and the fission and duplication cases I have met in the literature seem to me to press the same worry about what the things being counted actually are, and whether the ethical objection can survive a deflationary metaphysics of persons; what the theory must say about the very different case of one person bearing a great deal across a whole life and the case of many people each bearing a little; about compensation that arrives after the loss and lands on someone other than the person who suffered it; about choosing when the identities of those helped are unknown and only a distribution over them is known; about claims that arise only on condition that a particular person is brought into existence; and about collectives — hospitals, trade unions, families — that deliberate and act as one while their members each feel their own pains. I also want the distinctions the theory must respect and not quietly collapse: between counting as one and counting for much, between being permitted in prospect and being justified after the fact, between being answerable to someone and improving things for them, between refusing to aggregate and aggregating badly.

Two things matter most to me. First, sort the requirements into the non-negotiable and the merely sectarian: which follow from the job the theory has to do for an institution like mine, and which are preferences of one camp dressed up as constraints — I suspect, for instance, that the demand that the view be fully impartial and the demand that it never weigh one person's loss against another's gain are not on the same footing, and I should like to be told if I am wrong. Second, tell me if some subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied: if the list is itself the result, and a committee must then decide which demand to abandon, I want the argument for the incompatibility and not merely the assertion of it. I should also value your view on which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates — my own guess is that it is whatever requirement forces a theory to answer the person who loses, but the seminar has not yet persuaded me either way. Finish, if you would, with a brief scorecard, no more than a paragraph each, for the aggregative consequentialist view, the prioritarian, the Nozickian side-constraint view, the Scanlonian contractualist one, the equal-chance view that declines to count numbers at all, and the ex ante expected-utility view associated with the impartial spectator behind a veil; I shall not hold you to the scoring, since it is the criteria I came for. Kindly write in British English.
