I'm in a moral psychology seminar this term and our professor has spent like four weeks giving verdicts on whether particular cases count as genuine self-deception, and I definately think she's running some rule but I can't pin it down. Her verdicts so far:

1. Classic jealous-partner case: she finds the texts and the hotel receipt, explains each one away, ends up sincerely convinced he's faithful, keeps checking his phone, has nightmares about it. Yes, paradigm case. (In lecture she said the old puzzle about how one person can believe p and not-p is a pseudo-problem, but she still says things like some part of her knows.)

2. Patient given a vague, gentle prognosis, told they'll just keep an eye on it: believes she's basically fine, never asks a follow-up question, never googles anything. No. Her reason was that the doctor's vagueness, not her own wants, is doing all the causal work, though she added that if the patient had skipped the follow-up scan out of fear, that would be much closer.

3. Lab head whose research program is visibly failing: sends drafts only to supportive friends, and when a hostile review comes back she files it as the reviewer not understanding the method. Yes.

4. Guy who believes a dumb conspiracy theory because his feed is full of it: no stake either way, would be equally content either way, has never seen the counter-evidence. No, no desire doing any work.

5. Woman convinced a colleague is sabotaging her, and he actually is. No: not deceived, just anxious.

6. Department chair who sincerely believes he opposed a hire purely on merit grounds, having sat through meetings that made the real motive plain and discounted them. Yes, and she added that the object can be a motive or an attitude, not just a proposition.

7. Guy who wants to believe his band is good: sends the demo only to people who'll praise it, and reinterprets the one lukewarm response as jealousy. Yes, and she said the straight-versus-twisted distinction doesn't matter for whether it counts.

8. Patient who believes she'll beat the odds, where the belief genuinely helps her stick with treatment. Yes, and she said whether it's good for her is irrelevant to the classification.

What I've noticed is that every yes case seems to have both (a) her taking in some counter-evidence and handling it badly because of the desire, and (b) her steering which evidence reaches her in the first place — and every no case seems to have neither. Which is annoying, because it means I can't tell which one she thinks is doing the work.

My case for next week: a fourth-year, call her M., who suspects her supervisor thinks her project is dead in the water and wants her to change topics. She has never opened one of his messages directly — she routes them through a chatbot assistant she configured herself to summarise them in the most encouraging way that's still accurate and to drop the critical bits; she's muted the lab Slack; she's asked her labmates not to repeat anything he says about her; and she set it up in the first place because a friend with a similar setup said it made her whole semester bearable. She now firmly believes he thinks it's going fine and is just busy. She sleeps fine, no nightmares, doesn't snoop, and there is nothing she is currently reinterpreting or discounting, because no counter-evidence has ever reached her. If you asked her why she built it she'd say she doesn't care whether he thinks it's going badly, she just can't deal with that this semester. She would also shut down a friend who tried to tell her.

So what's she going to say about M.? I care less about the yes or no than about which rule she's actually running — does the desire have to distort how you take in evidence you already have, or is steering the sample enough on its own, or is it a third thing I haven't thought of (maybe something about the counterfactual, that she'd resist being told, or about her motive being not-wanting-to-be-hurt rather than wanting the proposition to be true)? Does it matter that the distortion lives in a tool she built rather than in her own reasoning? Give me your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, and tell me which variant of the case would actually separate the candidate rules: I was thinking the version where the department rolled out the summarising tool for everybody and she just never switched it off, the version where she starts having nightmares and creeping on her supervisor's calendar, the version where she built the whole setup two years ago before she had any suspicion at all, and the version where if you pressed her she'd admit she does want to believe he thinks it's fine. If you think her verdicts genuinely underdetermine it, say so and say what she'd be committing herself to with each answer.
