A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether the functional-decision-theoretic verdicts in the two familiar cases survive when the predictor's model of you is only coarse-grained, and we each came away convinced the other had missed the real point. We agree about the textbook versions, so set those aside: hand over the $100 in the counterfactual mugging even though the coin has already come up tails, and be a non-payer in XOR blackmail, where the demand is $1,000, roughly one householder in a thousand has the termites, they cost $1,000,000 if unnoticed but only $50,000 once you know about them, and the letter is sent iff exactly one of two things holds — you have the termites, you would pay.

Our variant: the predictor's simulation of you is reliable but blurred. It represents the shape of the situation and not the particular magnitudes, filling the missing numbers with generic defaults of its own, and the heads-branch payout, or the sending of the letter, is keyed to what that blurred model says.

(1) He said the coarsening is irrelevant in principle: the model is still a function of you, so you should still choose as if you were choosing the output the model tracks — pay in the mugging, refuse the demand, exactly as before.

(2) I said the model was asked a different question, namely whether to hand over some money of unspecified size, and that the entire content of my actual decision sits in the blank it filled in; so there is no fact about what my algorithm outputs on the coarse question that is settled by what I do now, and FDT's verdict depends on how I extend my policy to questions I was never asked.

(3) He replied that the honest way to state it is as a noisy correlation: comparing policies ex ante, paying wins whenever 10,000p − 100 > 10,000(1 − p), where p is the chance the model's verdict matches my actual policy — that is, whenever p > 0.505 — and in the blackmail case he put the threshold at roughly a third, since being misclassified as a payer while you refuse costs you only the lost information rather than the demand itself. Both thresholds, he said, are easily cleared by a reliable-but-blurred model, so the verdicts stand.

(4) I said that this quietly replaces subjunctive dependence with evidential correlation, so the verdict now floats with an empirical fact about the predictor's accuracy instead of tracking the decision problem; and that it is unstable, because a person disposed to hand over a small sum to a trustworthy stranger may be exactly a person disposed to refuse a demand of unknown and possibly enormous size, so a model that substitutes its own magnitudes can end up systematically inverted relative to my actual policy — and FDT's verdict then flips for a reason internal to the predictor rather than to me.

(5) He said inverted correlation is still correlation: FDT is answerable to the actual dependence structure, and if the model inverts, not paying genuinely does better. That is a feature, not a bug — I was describing a theory that responds to how the world is and complaining that it does.

(6) I said that on that picture the counsel has collapsed into find out how the predictor models you and maximize against that, and I no longer see what the counterfactual machinery is contributing: what looked like a claim about which algorithm to instantiate is really a claim about which predictor to hope for. He said that is just what any decision theory looks like from the inside, and we left it there.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justifying each rating and checking the arithmetic wherever there is any, then say who is more right overall and what both of us are missing — in particular whether a blurred predictor is best handled by carrying uncertainty over what the model does, by restricting attention to policies the model can represent, or in some other way.
