so you have the material right, here's roughly what we settled on, and please get the shape of it right rather than name-dropping:

in 1956 Oxford decided to give Truman an honorary doctorate, and Anscombe, then a research fellow at Somerville, fought it in public — a short pamphlet, letters, the works. her objection wasn't pacifism and it wasn't a cost-benefit calculation coming out the other way. it was that deliberately killing people who are not liable to be attacked — non-combatants, "innocent" in the old legal sense, meaning not the sort of person you may target, not sinless — as a means to your end is murder, and the ban on murder has no exception for good consequences. so the Hiroshima and Nagasaki deaths arent, on her telling, a foreseen side-effect of hitting a military target that she's fussily refusing to weigh against the gains: they were the point of the thing, the deaths were what was supposed to force surrender, which is exactly why the usual move of appealing to foresight-without-intent can't help Truman here. she also thought the "necessity" was partly manufactured — it only looked unavoidable because the Allies had insisted on unconditional surrender. one defence she pauses on is that Truman only signed a piece of paper and didn't kill anybody himself. please don't give me a poem where she's just the woman who thought dropping the bomb was sad.

what i want formally: a villanelle, 19 lines, and i want the two refrains to do the arguing. keep one of them byte-identical every time it returns — same words, no drift, no improving it — and let that one be the prohibition. let the other be the statesman's reason, and let it shift slightly on each return, so it starts in the official register (the war was shortened, something like that) and each time it comes back it loses a euphemism, until by the last stanza it says the thing plainly — that the deaths were what was being aimed at. then the closing two lines set the unchanging line and the stripped-down line side by side. the thought i want the form to carry, and tell me in the notes if you think it's wrong about her, is that the honest description already contains the verdict: once you can say what was actually being done, there isn't a further open question about whether the numbers justified it.
