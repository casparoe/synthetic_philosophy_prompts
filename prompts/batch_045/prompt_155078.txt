hey — i'm in a foundations of QM seminar this term and we just did the quantum suicide setup (spin measurement wired to a gun, one outcome kills you, run it again), and i run a little satire column for the undergrad physics society (mostly fake syllabi, fake grant rejection letters, that kind of thing). I want to do a mock product FAQ page for a startup that sells a home quantum-immortality kit. Around 800 words, numbered FAQ entries, deadpan corporate-support voice, like a real help page.

A few hard requirements so it doesnt just become generic "haha many worlds" filler:

- The company never, anywhere, says whether collapse happens. Every sentence has to be technically defensible on a no-collapse picture and still function as marketing. That tension is where the jokes should come from.
- Two pricing tiers: a premium "branch-count" plan, where survival is guaranteed because there is always a branch in which you make it through all fifty trials of a standard session, and a much cheaper "amplitude-weighted" plan. The sales copy pushes the expensive one; the fine print quietly concedes that amplitude/measure is what a rational customer should actually track, which makes the premium tier worth nothing and the cheap one the honest one. Nobody in the marketing half is allowed to say "measure" or "amplitude" out loud — let it leak into the legal half only.
- One entry has to handle the redundancy objection: if the no-collapse picture is true, then ordinary risks — crossing the road, a bad arrhythmia — already branch you, so what is the device even for? The company's answer should be a real answer, not a dodge, and it should still be phrased as a reason to buy.
- One entry on decoherence: the kit requires your alive/dead states to stay in superposition rather than being effectively settled by the environment, so there's an untested "coherence restoration" step with a window of a few tens of milliseconds per trial. Treat this as a minor firmware-grade inconvenience, not as a problem anyone at the company has worries about.
- One entry on why no testimonial can be evidence: the only customers able to post a review are the ones in branches where they survived, so the review section is a survivorship-selected sample in which everyone gives five stars. Include at least one invented five-star review from a satisfied customer whose reasoning is completely airtight and completely useless.
- One entry, if you can fit it, on the death benefit: the payout goes to your family in the branches where you died, and the page has to explain why you cannot claim it in the branch where you survived. Let that entry be the one place where the view's real awkwardness about whether you should care about the other branches shows through, while still sounding like paperwork.
- Include one short quote from an outside critic (a former company physicist, a rival lab, whoever) objecting on measure grounds, with a company reply that is technically true and completely fails as reassurance.

Nobody in the piece should say "this is only a thought experiment" or otherwise wink at the reader. Stay deadpan and let people notice on their own that the product is either redundant (if the physics is right) or just a suicide machine (if it isn't). Half the physics society has taken the seminar and will check whether you got the first-person/third-person probability distinction right; the other half are first-years who just want to laugh, so no unexplained jargon dumps. And dont cite or link anything — i'll add footnotes myself if i want them.
