I'm a novelist, and the book I'm finishing is narrated in part by a woman who runs a small livestock-welfare charity; over the last year I've found myself putting a compressed version of my own views about eating animals into her mouth, and before the thing goes to copy-edit I'd like to know whether those views actually hang together, or whether I've made her into a hypocrite without noticing. Roughly, here is where I've landed. First: what matters morally is a being's capacity to suffer; species membership does no work of its own, and intelligence matters only as evidence about that capacity, so I don't think a chimpanzee's bad day counts for more than a chicken's merely because the chimpanzee is cleverer. Second: given that I am going to eat some animal products, I should eat beef rather than chicken, because it takes vastly more birds than cattle to supply the same weight of meat, and the lives of intensively kept broilers and laying hens are far worse than those of grazing cattle; my rule of thumb has become that the larger the animal, the better. Third: but I take the crop-cultivation argument seriously — ploughing, harvesting and pest control kill field animals, rodents and birds and enormous numbers of invertebrates, and per calorie some staple crops plausibly kill more of them than beef production does, so I think the vegan who waves these deaths aside as merely natural or accidental is helping herself to something. Fourth: at the same time I don't accept that we have any duty to help wild animals as such — nature is not our responsibility, there is a real difference between harms we bring about and harms we merely fail to prevent, and I am sceptical of intervening in ecosystems we barely understand. Fifth: I buy higher-welfare meat and support cage-type reforms even though I think the humane-washing worry — that they make meat morally comfortable and push consumption up — is sometimes correct, but I reject the more-happy-animals argument; I don't think bringing extra farmed animals into existence is a benefit even if their lives would be good and their deaths painless, and I would rather there were fewer farmed animals in the world. Sixth: I discount invertebrates heavily, at something like a hundredth of a bird or less, though I take this to be mostly uncertainty about whether they are conscious at all rather than a verdict that they matter less if they are.

What I want is an audit rather than a lecture. Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look as though they are — I can see that the sixth sits oddly with the first and I suspect the fifth sits oddly with the second, but I'm less sure about my own case than I was when I started writing, and I don't know which of them I'd give up. Rank the tensions by severity, and for each real one say precisely what has to give and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set; then tell me which single revision would clean up the most at once. I'd also like your confidence marked explicitly: for each tension, say whether it turns on a conceptual clash or on a contested empirical claim about land use, feed conversion, demand elasticities or invertebrate sentience, and how sure you are. And if the problem isn't tension at all — if one of my commitments is simply confused, or if my beef-versus-chicken comparison doesn't survive putting everything I say I care about into the same ledger — please say that plainly instead of being polite and building a tidy structure on top of it.
