Write a dialogue between two scholars of ancient Greek ethics who disagree about how Plato's defense of supervised communal drunkenness in the Laws is meant to work. Both accept the same description of the practice: wine is prescribed, at controlled symposia run by a sober symposiarch, as a way of producing the passions — boldness, fearlessness, shameless appetite — that a well-educated person must learn to resist, so that resistance gets practiced under the conditions in which it is hardest to sustain. They disagree about what the exercise is really for, and I want them to argue about three questions.

First, the diagnostic question: does drunkenness uncover a character the drinker already has, or manufacture a temporary one that exists only in the cup? Have them test this with a variant case. Suppose that instead of wine there existed a drug that dissolves every trace of shame and fear while leaving the intellect perfectly clear and unimpaired. One scholar should argue that this clear-headed drug would be the better instrument and that Plato ought to have preferred it; the other should argue that it would be useless, on the ground that what must be trained is self-command at exactly the point where calculation itself has grown weak — and should press the first to say why a failure brought on by cognitive impairment counts against a person's character at all.

Second, the pedagogical question: whether the wine-rite is a permanent instrument of education, needed even in a city whose citizens had the best possible early training, or a prosthesis that only second-best cities require, because their citizens were habituated late and imperfectly.

Third, the consistency question: whether a philosopher who holds that the rational part of the soul must rule, and who compares the soul to a puppet pulled by cords of pleasure, pain, fear and anticipation against a soft golden cord of calculation, can coherently prescribe the deliberate induction of a state in which that cord goes slack.

Give both positions equal weight: each speaker should respond to the strongest version of the other's objection rather than a simplified one, each should concede at least one genuine point, and each should close by naming the single claim they refuse to give up. Keep the dialogue free of citations and bibliographic references; the argument should stand on its own.
