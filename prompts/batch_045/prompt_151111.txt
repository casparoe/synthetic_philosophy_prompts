I sit on the animal-research oversight committee at a large university, and I also attend a reading group on Descartes. At our last meeting a colleague deployed the line animals are machines to shut down a proposal to extend our welfare protocol to a species we had never covered: the implication was that there is nothing here to investigate, because there is no experience whose quality could matter to the animal. I do not want to answer him with a counter-slogan. I want to know what that line could actually be asserting before I decide whether any version of it does the work he wants.

Please give me exactly five numbered items and nothing else: no preamble, no closing summary, one or two sentences apiece. Each item is one precise thesis that the line could be expressing, ordered from the weakest to the strongest, and each should state the thesis, say whether it is a triviality, a defensible but contested claim, or false, and say whether it would genuinely license dismissing our proposal.

These are the distinctions I want kept apart rather than collapsed: that the animal's body is a material mechanism governed by the laws of matter; that its behaviour could in principle be produced by a mechanism; that its behaviour is in fact produced in that way; that it lacks an immaterial soul; that it lacks phenomenal experience entirely; that it lacks conceptually structured thought; that it lacks reason or language. My reading group is also split over whether Descartes' target is thought in general or specifically the self-conscious, reason-responsive thought he ties to the soul, and that split matters to us: if feeling is outside the target, the welfare question survives even on his own terms. If the strongest defensible reading is not among the ones I have listed, drop one of your items and use it instead.

Test the menu against two cases. The first is the species actually on our agenda: a cephalopod-like animal with a largely distributed nervous system and no vertebrate-style brain, no language and no trained symbol use, but flexible problem solving, rapid individual learning, and persistent avoidance of a chamber where it was previously harmed. The second is a stress case my committee invented: a non-biological system, a large language model trained on human text and embodied in a robot, which produces novel situation-appropriate remarks about what it can currently perceive and changes its behaviour after failures. Descartes is standardly read as offering two tests for thought, one asking whether the candidate can arrange signs to convey something new rather than merely echo trained associations, the other asking whether it can act aptly across many different kinds of situation rather than by a fixed disposition of its organs. On at least one of your five readings those two tests will have to count the robot as thinking while excluding the animal, or exclude both: say which reading that is, and whether that consequence is a reason to give it up.
