I'm part of a small reading group on AI governance, and we keep circling around a puzzle that I've come to think is really about the difference between worst-case and average-case guarantees, though nobody in the group uses those words. So let me describe the practice I want understood. When a developer decides that a general-purpose model is safe enough to release to a broad population, the artifact it actually produces is a bundle: average scores on benchmark suites, side-by-side win rates from human preference ratings, qualitative red-team writeups showing the model refusing some of the nastiest prompts the testers could devise, and a prose argument that the model's dangerous capabilities sit below thresholds the developer has set for itself. Almost nobody claims a bound on the worst thing the model will do once millions of people interact with it over many months, and nobody quite claims an average-case guarantee either, since the benchmarks don't sample the deployment distribution. This sits oddly next to how other safety-critical fields proceed, according to the engineers in my group: you don't certify a design for flight or license a reactor by reporting mean performance plus a few vivid anecdotes, you do it by arguing that the probability of catastrophe falls below a hard number, under assumptions chosen so that everything which can go wrong is assumed to go wrong together.

The thing I can't reconcile is that the release process seems to run on both standards at once. Aggregated benchmark numbers are treated as the headline evidence that the system is fine, and yet a single red-team transcript that gets a model to do something prohibited is treated as capable of blocking release outright, or at least of forcing a retraining cycle. One bad case is apparently decisive in one part of the process and irrelevant next to the average in another. My friend's suggestion, which I'd like you to take apart rather than simply adopt, is that the real axis isn't worst versus average but guarantee-per-query versus guarantee-per-deployment: once a system faces millions of draws from a heavy-tailed input distribution, the bad case isn't a remote possibility you might never encounter, it's a case you should expect to be realized somewhere in the deployment about as often as its per-query probability times the number of draws, so a per-query average guarantee is nearly no guarantee at deployment scale and a per-query worst-case guarantee is really a per-deployment average guarantee in disguise. He then proposes a middle notion, a quantile guarantee: don't bound the maximum loss, bound the loss at the 99.99th percentile of the actual deployment distribution and audit against that. My worry is that this collapses back into the average case, because the distribution you'd need has to be estimated from data you don't have, and worse, may not be fixed at all — the model partly determines which inputs it later faces, since its outputs shape what users go on to ask for — so there may be no stable distribution to take a quantile over, which might mean the whole worst-case/average-case framing is incoherent for these systems rather than merely hard to satisfy.

What I'd like from you is a reconstruction of what the current release-and-deployment practice presupposes. Does it presuppose that average performance on proxies is evidence about performance in deployment, or only that it's the best available evidence? Does it presuppose that the tail is thin enough to ignore, that the tail is someone else's problem, that a catastrophic outcome has a different kind of disvalue than a merely bad one, or only that developers can't be held to guarantees nobody knows how to produce? Please be explicit about which of these the practice strictly requires in order to make sense and which it merely sits comfortably with, and about where it is underdetermined, in the sense that several quite different theories of risk, evidence, and responsibility would license exactly the same bundle of benchmarks and red-team reports. Then take up the two moves above: say what a genuine per-deployment guarantee would look like if the per-query framing were replaced, whether a quantile guarantee is a coherent third thing or an average-case guarantee relabelled, whether the endogeneity worry really does undermine it, and what would concretely have to change in how a system is cleared for release — what would have to be measured, by whom, and over what population — if my friend's framing were adopted instead. If you think the practice is philosophically confused rather than merely imperfect, say so and say which part does the work of hiding the confusion.
