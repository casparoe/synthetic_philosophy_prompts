Here's the setup. He's not being lazy — he's using the analogy to argue for things. Three conclusions, in ascending order of how much they annoy me. (i) Information-talk in biology is literal, not a convenience: genes really do specify, prescribe, carry messages about what to build, and that's what makes them explanatory in a way that cytoplasmic or maternal factors aren't. (ii) Everything else in and around the cell — methylation marks, the maternal cytoplasm, the cytoskeletal layout of the egg, the womb — is at best annotation on the text, or a setting on the machine, not information in its own right; so development and inheritance are, in the last instance, about the sequence. In particular I want you to chew on these, which are the things I keep circling:

The reader is inside the text. The codon-to-amino-acid assignment is implemented by synthetases that are themselves proteins encoded in the very DNA being read, and the tRNAs and ribosome are too. So the codebook is written in the language it decodes, and the text can't be read without machinery the text itself specifies. There's nothing like that in ordinary writing — the reader isn't encoded in the text, and the codebook isn't composed in the code it translates. Does that regress sink the analogy or just relocate it?

Template versus convention. In replication the base pairing is chemical complementarity — nothing conventional there. In translation the mapping looks arbitrary, and the old "frozen accident" line is supposed to explain why it's this mapping and not another, with the near-universality of the code across life taken as evidence. But isn't that universality just evidence of common descent, i.e. of shared machinery, rather than of a convention anyone could in principle have agreed to differently? What does that do to the claim that arbitrariness is the mark of a code?

My favourite, which I made up and can't get anyone to engage with: build a fully enantiomeric cell — mirror-image sugars in the backbone, mirror polymerase, mirror ribosome, mirror synthetases, mirror tRNAs, everything — and let it run on its own mirror DNA. Does it read "the same code"? If the mapping travels with the machinery rather than with the string, what happens to the idea that the sequence has content of its own? And the converse case: a synthetic sequence sitting in a tube that no organism has the machinery to read. Compare an undeciphered inscription in an unknown script, which we'd still want to call meaningful, partly because someone wrote it. Biology has no author.
