I co-teach PHIL 347 (Rules, Law, and Self-Reference) with Dana, and a third of the course grade rides on a semester-long, Nomic-style game the students run: a set of mutable rules amendable by majority, a handful of entrenched rules amendable only unanimously, a rotating student adjudicator whose rulings on points of order can be appealed, and — this is the bit I added after last year's edition turned into a pure majoritarian steamroller — a hard cap of three proposal actions per player for the whole term, so that agenda-setting is genuinely scarce and coalitions have to decide what to spend themselves on. I sit on our department's Experiential Learning Committee, the body that signed off on running the game for credit and that doubles as the appeal route when a dispute affects grades, and we now have exactly such a dispute: two students have appealed the week-7 adjudicator's ruling upholding something called Rule 41, and we vote Tuesday morning. I need a memo I can circulate Monday night, so please draft it.

The four clauses that matter are these. Entrenched Rule 108: "Any mutable rule may be amended or repealed by a majority vote of all players." Entrenched Rule 110: "No rule adopted by majority may purport to amend or repeal an entrenched rule; such a rule is void, unless the offending portion can be severed, in which case only that portion is void." Mutable Rule 112: "A proposal is adopted if it receives a majority of the votes cast." And then, in week 6, a coalition passed this by 12–8 under Rule 112: "Rule 41. Notwithstanding any other rule, no proposal is adopted unless it receives two thirds of the votes cast. This rule may be amended or repealed only by a unanimous vote of all players, and any rule to the contrary is void." In week 7 a rival bloc's redistributive scoring proposal failed 11–9 — a majority, but not two thirds — and that is what got appealed. Note that Rule 41 does two separable things: it raises the adoption threshold for everyone's future proposals (I've been calling this other-entrenchment), and it shields itself from ordinary majority repeal (self-entrenchment). Dana's line is that the whole thing is void in toto, since a mutable rule passed by majority cannot touch the operation of entrenched Rule 108, and that the clean reading of 110 makes severability unavailable where the self-entrenchment clause is the rule's whole point. Marco's line is that this is precisely what the unit on constituent power is for, that a majority which can never bind itself isn't a constituent power at all, and that voiding 41 retroactively rewrites the record of week 7 in a way we can't undo. The reliance wrinkle is real: three players who still had proposal slots left say they deliberately sat on them in weeks 7 and 8 because they believed nothing could clear two thirds, and if we now strike 41 they've lost irreplaceable actions they would have spent.

I'm leaning toward something messier than either of them — roughly, that the threshold clause is validly enacted (it just amends mutable Rule 112, which is exactly what majorities are for) while the self-entrenchment clause is the part that overreaches, with some kind of conditional validation or re-ratification requirement to settle whether the two-thirds bar survives — but I can't shake Marco's point about constituent power, and I also can't tell whether my instinct to sever is just squeamishness about voiding a rule that eleven people are currently living under. What I want from you is the memo itself, addressed to the committee: recommend one course of action, in the imperative, with a time frame that fits inside a semester that ends in five weeks; say what happens to the week-7 vote and to the appellants' proposal; say whether your remedy reaches back or only forward; and propose whatever standing default we should announce for the rest of the term so the same fight doesn't recur every session (I've been toying with a sunset, under which any self-entrenching clause lapses unless re-ratified at its own threshold within two sessions, but say if that's incoherent). Give Marco's strongest argument the honest treatment it deserves rather than a footnote, and don't hide behind the observation that this is only a game — I know it's only a game, and the grading consequences are still real. End the memo with a "Verdict:" line giving the bottom line in one sentence, and then a final line reading \answer{...} containing your recommendation in twelve words or fewer.
