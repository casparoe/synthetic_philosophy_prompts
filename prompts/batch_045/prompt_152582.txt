hey — you're a Nietzsche specialist and a working poet at once, so please think hard about the form before you start typing verse.

Context: I'm TAing a grad seminar and we close next week on Nietzsche's 1874 essay on history (second of the Untimely Meditations — monumental, antiquarian, critical). I want to end the session with a poem instead of my usual summary handout.

What I want: a sestina, proper — six stanzas of six plus the three-line tornada, standard retrograde rotation. Suggested end-words: LIFE, DEED, PEAK, ROOT, JUDGE, FORGET (swap at most two if you've got better ones, but keep them easy to end a line on).

The structural bit I actually care about: stanzas 1–2 spoken in the monumental register, 3–4 antiquarian, 5–6 critical. Since the form drags the same six words back in a new order each time, each word has to return carrying a different sense depending on who's speaking — PEAK as the great attainable height vs. PEAK as the summit we're downstream of, ROOT as piously preserved origin vs. root to be dug up and severed, and so on. The form should be making the point that the past is never discarded, only rearranged and reinterpreted.

Before the poem, give me a short brainstorm — a few sentences, not a bulleted essay — on how you're mapping the three modes onto the six stanzas and where in the rotation each word flips meaning.

Accuracy checks a specialist in the room should be able to pass: monumental is the history of what was great plus the belief that it can be great again, and it degenerates into myth and into using past greatness to block anything new; antiquarian is the preservative, pious we-are-the-product-of-our-forefathers stance, degenerating into mummification and into venerating everything old just because it's old; critical is judging and, where necessary, condemning and annihilating the past, degenerating into the fantasy that we could hand ourselves a past of our own choosing. All three are supposed to serve life and the capacity to act, none is self-sufficient, and a life also needs forgetting / the unhistorical.

Niche thing from my own work: I argue for a fourth stance Nietzsche only half reaches — counter-monumental remembrance, where you keep someone permanently in the critical register, refusing both the monument and the piety but also refusing to be done with them. Picture a department that keeps a compromised founder's name on the building while never once saying anything celebratory about him. I'd like the tornada to leave room for that stance rather than resolving the other three into a moral.

Final constraint: the last line should step outside all three modes — toward forgetting, or the horizon — not sum them up. No explanation after the poem, no footnotes; I'll do the framing out loud. Typoes are fine, but the rotation had better be correct.
