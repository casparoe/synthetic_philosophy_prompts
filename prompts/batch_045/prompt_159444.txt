I'm drafting a short methods note on the class of heuristic arguments that work by breaking a contested conclusion into a conjunction of uncertain claims, attaching a rough probability to each, and multiplying — the shape familiar from order-of-magnitude estimates of the prevalence of extraterrestrial intelligence, from anthropics-style trisections, and from the various we-should-already-have-seen-it-by-now arguments. What interests me is not any particular instance but the house rule such arguments almost always invoke: the factors are assumed independent, not because anyone can defend that, but on the grounds that independence is the least committal thing to assume when nothing is known about the dependence. Call the denial of that rationale product skepticism: in the actual run of published arguments of this shape, multiplying the author's marginals under acknowledged, unmodeled dependence yields a number that is systematically too high — because individual marginals are overconfident, because the list of necessary conjuncts is almost never complete, and because those two effects dominate whatever dependence structure pushes the other way. My credence in product skepticism, as I have stated it, is about 0.7. A co-author sits at 0.4 and argues the reverse: that residual dependence among conjuncts is overwhelmingly positive (shared causes, a shared background theory, a single author whose reliability is common to every step, conjuncts that are partly restatements of one another), that under positive association the true conjunction lies in the upper part of the coherent range between the product and the smallest marginal, and hence that the convention is conservative if it is biased at all.

Give me your own number, but spend most of the answer on the decomposition: separate the sub-questions, give each a rough probability with a one-line justification, then combine them and say which term the result is most sensitive to. At minimum I want you to weigh (i) whether unmodeled dependence in real conjunctive arguments is predominantly positive association, or whether competitive, trade-off, or conservation-style structure among the factors is common enough to matter; (ii) whether the dominant error term is the dependence structure at all, rather than miscalibration of the marginals or the omission of further necessary conjuncts; (iii) whether there is a defensible formalization of presume independence that is not independence — I have been toying with two, treating the k conjuncts as exchangeable draws from an urn with unknown base rate, so that the estimate becomes a rule-of-succession product rather than p to the k, and positing an explicit latent common-mode reliability variable over the whole argument in the style of reliability engineering, with the conjunctive probability taken as an expectation over that variable — and whether either of them changes the direction of the bias; (iv) whether the relevant notion is independence of the events in the world or independence of my evidence for them, and which way a shared evidence base actually pushes the product; and (v) whether the question has stakes at all, given that these arguments are usually read as good to an order of magnitude, so that a modest systematic bias disappears into the width of the interval. Finally, tell me what would settle it: what comparison or measurement would move your number the most, and which of the two of us at 0.7 and 0.4 you think has located the disagreement in the wrong place.
