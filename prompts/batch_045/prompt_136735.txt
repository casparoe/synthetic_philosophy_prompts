The question I set:

Q. In Lewis Carroll's dialogue, Achilles tries to get the Tortoise to accept B from the premises A and "if A then B". The Tortoise accepts both but won't infer. So Achilles gets him to accept a further premise, Z: "if A and if-A-then-B, then B". The Tortoise accepts that too — and still won't infer B. And so on.

(a) Explain, in your own terms, why the regress keeps going however many premises Achilles adds. [6 marks]

(b) Someone in the seminar — call her the Hasty Enthusiast — proposes a fix. Add the following, as a rule:

(C) From a set of premises Γ, together with a premise saying that some rule R permits inferring φ from Γ, you may infer φ.

The Enthusiast says C is self-applying, so the regress stops at the second order: you never need a further rule telling you to use C. Assess this. In particular, does it make a difference whether C is added as a rule (something the Tortoise is willing to use) or as a further premise (something he merely accepts as true)? [8 marks]

(c) Does any of this show that deduction cannot be justified? Distinguish that question from whatever it is the dialogue does show. [6 marks]

The answer I need graded:

(a) The basic problem is that adding premises never tells you to do anything. Achilles offers A and A→B; the Tortoise accepts both and sits there. So Achilles writes down Z, i.e. (A ∧ (A→B)) → B, and gets the Tortoise to accept that as well. But the Tortoise can still refuse to infer B, and he can do that without contradicting himself: there is a valuation on which A, A→B and Z are all true while B is false, so his position is perfectly consistent — he just isn't closed under modus ponens. The same thing happens at every later stage. For Z you can always write Z′, namely ((A ∧ (A→B) ∧ Z) → B), and the Tortoise will happily accept Z′ and still not infer B. So the regress is infinite, and each new premise is just one more sentence in the object language. Nothing about accepting a sentence — not even a necessarily true one — amounts to having a licence to infer. That's the distinction between implication and inference: A and A→B imply B whether or not anyone ever infers it, since implication is a relation between propositions, whereas inference is something an agent does, and doing it requires a rule rather than another premise. Carroll's Tortoise accepts propositions but no rules, and that's why he never moves.

(b) I think the Enthusiast is right, as long as C is added as a rule rather than as a premise. The original regress is vicious because every step leans on a further step: you need Z to get from A and A→B to B, then Z′ to get from A, A→B and Z to B, and so on without end. C is different, because it is self-applying. It licenses inferences from premises about what rules permit, and it doesn't need any further rule to tell you to apply it, since applying it is itself an instance of it. So if the Tortoise is willing to use C, the regress stops after a single step: he accepts the premise "modus ponens permits inferring B from A and A→B", invokes C, and reaches B. If C is instead written down as a mere premise, it does no good whatsoever: the Tortoise will accept "C permits inferring φ from Γ" and then ask what entitles him to move to φ, which is just the original difficulty one level up. So the real moral is that Carroll's puzzle is solved by moving from premises to rules, and C is the minimal rule that does the job.

(c) None of this, though, shows that deduction cannot be justified. Carroll's regress is a point about the structure of inference: rules can't be replaced by premises, because accepting-that isn't the same thing as following a rule. That's a constitutive claim about what inferring even is, not an epistemological claim about whether our rules are any good. The question whether deduction is justified is the question whether our rules are truth-preserving and whether we have reason to think they are, and that is a seperate project. You might argue that modus ponens is justified because it never takes you from true premises to a false conclusion, or you might argue, more quietly, that the demand for a justification of deduction is confused in the first place because any argument for it would already have to use deduction. What Carroll shows is only that you cannot answer the justificatory demand by piling up premises; it doesn't tell you that the demand can't be met at all.
