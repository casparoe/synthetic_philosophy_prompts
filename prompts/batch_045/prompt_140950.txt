Two friends of mine — one a cosmologist, one a philosopher of science — got into an argument over dinner after a department colloquium, and they asked me to referee. I could not do it, and I would like it adjudicated properly rather than settled by assertion.

The setup they had agreed on: two rival hypotheses about the total number of human-level organisms that will ever exist — call them H_sparse (10^11) and H_teeming (10^24). Working cosmology assigns them roughly equal prior probability, and no observation available at our epoch could distinguish them. The disputed question is what, if anything, the bare fact of your own existence, together with your position in the human sequence, entitles you to conclude. One of them reasons from what I take to be the self-indication assumption, the other from the self-sampling assumption, though neither used those labels at the time.

(1) Iris: I should treat my own existence as evidence. Conditional on H_teeming there are 10^13 times as many slots for a creature with my evidence to occupy, so the likelihood of there being someone with my evidence is 10^13 times higher under H_teeming, and my posterior on H_teeming is therefore as close to one as makes no difference. She knows this is the presumptuous-philosopher argument and bites the bullet: selection effects are real evidence everywhere else in science, so if physicists will not let an armchair argument overturn a laboratory prior, they owe an error theory for why anthropic selection is the one kind of selection that cannot move probabilities.

(2) Tomas: Do not treat your existence as a datum about how many observers there are. Treat yourself as a random sample drawn from the observers who actually exist in your reference class; then your existence has probability one under either hypothesis and generates no boost at all. What carries information is where in the sequence you land: finding yourself roughly the hundred-billionth human is vastly more likely if the total is 10^11 than if it is 10^24, since the chance of landing in the first 10^11 out of 10^24 is 10^-13. So the update runs the other way, to near-certainty that humanity ends within a few generations. He grants that this has the shape of the doomsday argument and says he accepts the consequence.

(3) Iris: Then both rules generate cosmology from the armchair, so the objection cannot be that one of them does. The real difference is that SSA's shift is only as good as its reference class, and the reference class is a free parameter: set it to humans with my birth rank and the doomsday effect evaporates; set it to all terrestrial beings with inner lives and the number changes again. SIA needs no such parameter — it needs only the possible observers, and the ratio of their numbers is fixed by the hypotheses themselves, not by a stipulation.

(4) Tomas: SIA's boost is just as parameter-dependent, only the parameter is better hidden, because SIA must decide what counts as one observer. His case: two hypotheses about a far-future civilization, equal on ordinary evidence and introspectively indistinguishable from the inside. Under H_fused, 10^9 human-like organisms are wired together so that at any moment there is exactly one unified stream of consciousness. Under H_separate, 10^3 organisms each sustain their own separate stream. If the unit counted is organisms, SIA's boost favors H_fused by 10^6 to one — a teeming world of bodies. If the unit counted is subjects of experience, it favors H_separate by 10^3 to one — a tiny world with many minds. So SIA's famous conclusion is not that the world is big; it is that there are many of whatever we choose to count, and the choice is ours.

(5) Iris: Not ours — the hypotheses'. What gets counted is whatever the hypothesis itself posits as the bearer of a perspective, the thing whose evidence you are now occupying: one hive subject under H_fused, a thousand separate subjects under H_separate. Once that is fixed the count is objectively determined, and SIA's verdict answers a real question rather than restating a stipulation: how many perspectives of the kind I now enjoy does this hypothesis contain? And in the cosmological case, where both hypotheses posit one perspective per organism, SIA's teeming-world verdict survives untouched.

(6) Tomas: Then the presumptuous philosopher's boast was hollow. If SIA only ever says expect many perspectives, it says nothing about the size of the world except where the competing hypotheses agree about how many perspectives a body contains; as soon as the metaphysics of the subject is itself in dispute — fusion, fission, persons versus person-moments — SIA's verdict swings on a question it has no resources to settle. And SSA at least has a miniature in which it is plainly right: you are assigned a room in a hotel of either ten or ten thousand rooms, all occupied, and finding yourself in room seven is straightforward evidence for the small hotel. SIA's boost cancels that inference exactly, since a bigger hotel means more guests and hence a proportionally larger chance that there is a you at all; so SIA must maintain that an inference everyone accepts in miniature is invalid.

(7) Iris: The hotel comes with a known assignment mechanism, and that mechanism is what licenses the room-number inference; cosmology supplies none, and it is precisely the absence of a mechanism that forces the prior to be spread over possible rather than merely actual observers. In the hotel you also know in advance that the hotel has guests and how many, so that you are a guest is not news; in cosmology the number of observers is the unknown quantity, and there is no fixed list to sample from.

(8) Tomas: The sampling in SSA is not a causal mechanism but the formal expression of indexical uncertainty, which needs no mechanism for the same reason that not knowing what time it is needs none. And in the fused case SSA returns no verdict at all, because every subject in the relevant class has exactly the evidence you have: one member under H_fused, a thousand under H_separate, likelihood one either way. That is not a defect but modesty — a rule that manufactured a 10^3 to one verdict between two hypotheses that are indistinguishable from the inside would be inventing evidence, and SIA's verdict there is just the presumptuous inference applied to minds instead of worlds.

Please adjudicate the exchange rather than the two views in the abstract. For each of the eight moves, say whether its central claim is clearly right, partly right, or clearly wrong, and why: whether the inference actually goes through once its key term is disambiguated, whether the move answers the point that was actually made rather than a neighbouring one, and where a move quietly shifts the burden. Then say who is more right overall, and identify anything both of them are assuming that they should not be. A compact table of the eight ratings would help. Finish with a single final line in exactly one of these forms: Verdict: Iris, Verdict: Tomas, or Verdict: neither.
