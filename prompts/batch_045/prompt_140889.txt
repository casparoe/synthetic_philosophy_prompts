Two people on my team had a proper row in the office kitchen about our appointment-reminder pilot, and they've asked me to settle it, which I definately can't — I lose the thread somewhere around the third thing either of them said.

Setup: an NHS trust texts reminders to some outpatients before their appointment. The pilot randomised within each age band but at deliberately different rates — about 1 in 5 under-65s were randomised to get a text, against 4 in 5 of the over-65s. Numbers (attended on the day):

- under 65: no text 80% (3200/4000); text 85% (850/1000)
- 65 and over: no text 60% (600/1000); text 65% (2600/4000)
- pooled: no text 76% (3800/5000); text 69% (3450/5000)

Then someone split each band by whether the patient's registered address is urban or rural. The urban/rural mix is about the same in the text and no-text groups inside each band (roughly 70% urban among under-65s, 40% among over-65s), and the gaps come out as: under-65 urban +8, under-65 rural −2, 65+ urban +14, 65+ rural −1. The rural cells are the thin ones — the under-65 rural cell is about 1500 appointments, with only ~300 of them texted.

The exchange, more or less as it happened:

(1) Mina: the headline figures are the figures. 76% without a text, 69% with. We're making attendance worse, so we stop.
(2) Rob: that's textbook Simpson's paradox. Texts went mostly to over-65s, who attend less anyway, so the pooled comparison is comparing different kinds of patient. Hold age fixed and the text is +5 points in both bands. And the principle you're relying on — if it's better in every subgroup it's better overall, the sure-thing principle — just isn't true once the group sizes are lopsided.
(3) Mina: that's exactly the move I don't trust. Once you let yourself slice, you can slice till you like the answer — and here's the proof, split by urban/rural and two of the four cells flip to harmful. So the "adjusted" number is just whatever you chose to adjust for.
(4) Rob: not arbitrary, no. Age is the variable that drove who got a text; rurality wasn't in the assignment at all. Adjusting for the thing that actually created the imbalance is principled. And those cells are thin — both rural flips are within a couple of points of zero, which is what noise looks like.
(5) Mina: if it's noise then your +5 is noise too — you're keeping the cuts that agree with you and writing off the ones that don't. Also both flips are rural, which is a pattern, not scatter.
(6) Rob: even if rurality mattered, we don't pick who to text by postcode, we pick by age, so the band numbers are the only ones we can act on.

Could you adjudicate move by move — who's right about what at each step, maybe rating each move from clearly wrong to clearly right — rather than just declaring an overall winner? And say what, if anything, they're both missing. Oh, and write it in British English please (organise, analyse, that sort of thing).
