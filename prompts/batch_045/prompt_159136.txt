I'm putting together a problem set for a second-year epistemology seminar on the KK principle and luminosity, and I need one purpose-built case rather than another variant of the fake-barn or brain-in-a-vat material. A colleague suggested I just use the familiar graded series (the subject who can't tell whether she is cold, tired, or seeing red as the light shifts), but my students always balk at those: they read the subject as poor at discriminating adjacent cases and so as failing through some kind of carelessness, which lets them dismiss the argument without engaging it. So here is what I want from you.

Design a single realistic case — a laboratory, an airport, a hospital, a train station, an office; no demons, no brains in vats, no deceivers, no science fiction, no lotteries or purely statistical evidence, and nothing that turns on a proposition nobody could know — in which a subject S knows some ordinary proposition p and fails to know that S knows p, with all of the following holding at once. (1) The first-order knowledge has to be comfortable rather than borderline: p is true, S's belief is not Gettiered or merely luckily true, and there is a wide margin between S's actual situation and the nearest situation in which the belief would be false; all the interest must come from the second-order step. (2) The second-order failure must be structural, not a performance failure: S actually asks herself whether she knows p, is attending carefully, is not tired, distracted, self-deceiving, or lazily assuming, is not ignoring a defeater, and is not subject to misleading higher-order evidence about her own reliability; the practical stakes attached to acting must be the same whether the question is posed as whether p or as whether she knows that p. (3) The failure must be generated by whatever really does the work in the margin-for-error case against luminosity — the shape of the similarity structure over a graded series, the fact that the margin for knowledge is wider than the margin for truth, or whatever you think it actually is — and not by any of the cheap routes: no contextualist shift in the standards between the two questions, no pragmatic encroachment, no skeptical hypothesis, no retreat to the claim that S only ever had true belief at the first order. (4) It has to be presentable to students in a single short paragraph.

After the scenario, give me the demonstration. State p, S's first-order grounds, and the nearest few alternatives; then say what (a) a safety-theoretic account, (b) a margin-for-error, anti-luminosity style account, and (c) a knowledge-first or competence-theoretic friend of KK would each say about the first-order claim and about the second-order claim, and why. Then the diagnostics I actually need for the handout: name the single feature of the case doing the work, say where in the relevant series S sits and why that location rather than another is what makes the verdicts come apart, and show what happens if you move S one step further from the boundary — does KK hold there, and if so why doesn't that collapse the case entirely? Finally, write the strongest paragraph a defender of KK could use to attack your case, not a strawman; if the best available reply is that the argument only refutes the actual-knowledge reading of KK and leaves the weaker in-a-position-to-know reading intact, say so plainly and say whether that weaker reading survives what you have built. One thing would defeat the purpose: stipulating the verdicts, as in a subject who simply knows p but somehow cannot tell that she does. The verdicts have to fall out of the theories, or the case is unusable.
