Compose a sestina on the distinction between indicative and counterfactual conditionals, in which the form itself carries the philosophical point and the semantics is rendered accurately enough that a philosopher of language could read it without wincing.

Form. Six sestets followed by a three-line envoi. The six end-words are *if*, *would*, *match*, *lit*, *world*, *similar*. Rotate them by the standard rule: each stanza takes the previous stanza's end-words in the order last, first, fifth, second, fourth, third — so a stanza closing 1-2-3-4-5-6 is followed by one closing 6-1-5-2-4-3 — with the result that no arrangement of these words recurs until the envoi. The words should be identical at every appearance; inflect or compound them only where English makes strict repetition impossible.

Content, stanza by stanza. In the first two stanzas, set out the minimal pair: the same antecedent and the same consequent, differing only in mood, and differing in what the speaker is committed to. The indicative *if Oswald did not shoot Kennedy, then someone else did* can be asserted by someone who has no idea who fired, and it can be followed by an avowal of that ignorance; the counterfactual *if Oswald had not shot Kennedy, then someone else would have* concedes, in the very act of being asserted, that its antecedent is false. Make the reader feel that the difference is one of commitment to a body of evidence, not one of decoration.

In the middle stanzas, run the sequence in which each further detail added to the antecedent flips the consequent: a struck match lights; a struck match in a room without oxygen, or choked with vapour, does not; add the oxygen back and it lights again. Nothing is retracted and no contradiction is incurred, because what shifts is which world counts as the relevantly closest one. Let the migration of *would* across these stanzas do the argumentative work: the word that carries the counterfactual never occupies the same line-position twice, and no arrangement ever returns, so the reader should finish these stanzas convinced that there is no fixed destination called the nearest world — and register, too, that there may be no single closest world at all, that *similar* is not allowed to mean *identical*, and that the ordering is not a fixed metric but one the antecedent itself helps to move.

In the envoi, bring all six end-words back and place an indicative and a counterfactual with the same antecedent side by side. Let it be clear that they answer to different standards of correctness — the one to conditional probability, the probability of the consequent given the antecedent, with no selection of worlds at all; the other to comparative similarity among worlds — and that the fact that both are written out of the same six words cannot bridge them. The closing line should hold *if* and *would* inside a single clause while leaving it open which of the two standards governs it.

Avoid rendering the distinction as merely one of tense or grammar: past-tense indicatives and present-tense counterfactuals are enough to show that morphology does not track the split. Avoid letting the poem settle the question by quietly unifying the two under one semantics; if unification is gestured at, the cost should be visible. After the poem, add three or four sentences of prose identifying which lines carry which of these moves and why the envoi ends where it does.
