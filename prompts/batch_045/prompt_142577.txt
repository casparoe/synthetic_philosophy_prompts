Compose a satirical white paper in the manner of a constitutional-reform pamphlet — the sort of document a well-funded think tank circulates to journalists a few weeks before a referendum — which proposes to carry the logic of government by lottery to its conclusion. Target length: 900 to 1,100 words. Structure: a title, a short preamble, at least five numbered sections with subheadings, one small mock table, and a closing implementation timetable.

The setting and the machinery are invented, so the piece must define them for a reader who has never heard of the literature. The country in question has already replaced its upper chamber with a body called the People's Sample, chosen by stratified lot from the adult population and rotated every eleven weeks; it is now debating a "Second Lot" reform. The pamphlet is issued by the Office of the Lot, a permanent secretariat now in its fortieth year, and defends the following devices of its own devising:

The Sealed Verdict. Members' votes on each question are recorded on arrival, before any briefing, expert evidence, or debate. Deliberation continues for the full eleven weeks but cannot alter the outcome, on the ground that a member who changes their mind ceases to stand for the stratum that was drawn, and a body that no longer matches the population has lost the only property that made its verdict authoritative.

Sample drift. A technical term for the measured divergence between a member's post-deliberation opinions and the opinions of their cell in the population at large, monitored weekly, with any member exceeding the threshold re-randomized — discharged and replaced by a fresh draw from the same cell.

The Reversion Lot. A decision of the Sample that subsequently proves unworkable or unpopular is not treated as a mistaken decision but as evidence of a defective draw: the decision is void, the question returns to the pool, and the body is thereby never wrong, only mis-sampled.

The Question Lot. Since power over the agenda would be the one remaining form of rule by some over others, the questions themselves are drawn by lot from a pool drafted by the secretariat, which holds no power because it only decides what is asked.

Shadow members. Because the chance of selection must be equal for all residents, including those who cannot serve, each such category is assigned an adult proxy drawn from the same stratum, who deliberates, speaks, and does not vote.

Requirements for the voice and the comedy. The tone is deadpan throughout: the author is entirely in earnest, never signals that anything is amiss, and closes without a wink. Every absurdity must be an exaggeration of a real commitment of the sortition position — descriptive representativeness as the source of legitimacy, rotation and short terms, the epistemic case from aggregation over many imperfect judges, the claim that removing elections removes faction, ambition, and corruption, the reliance on facilitation and expert briefing, and the claim that lot-selected officials are trustworthy precisely because they are ordinary — so that a democratic theorist recognises the piece as fair rather than a caricature. Compelled service should be handled as the purest form of consent, with a footnote on refusal rates in the strata least able to afford to serve and the Office's remedy of weighting the fine. Include one passage in which the author explicitly acknowledges the worry that deliberation and representativeness pull in opposite directions, and dispatches it by doubling down rather than by retreating. The standard objections — who sets the agenda, whether a transformed sample is still representative, whether a few hundred people are enough for the aggregation argument to do any work, whether the unelected staff hold the real power — must never be stated as objections; they should be legible only through the apparatus. The timetable's final line should quietly dispense with elections without ever using a word like abolish or end.

Do not quote or paraphrase any real text, do not cite real authors, and invent any epigraph, consultation response, or footnote reference the piece needs. The result should be funny to someone who works on democratic theory and comprehensible to someone who does not.
