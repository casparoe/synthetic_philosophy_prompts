I'm writing a term paper for a formal epistemology seminar on whether the no-free-lunch theorems have anything genuinely new to say about Hume's problem of induction, and I've been going in circles on it for about two weeks now — my draft deadline is Sunday, and every time I think I've settled on a verdict, one of my study-group partners pokes a hole in it. So I'd like you to write the essay I should be writing: not a survey of a debate, but a single sustained argument that comes down somewhere and defends that landing.

Here is where I've gotten to. The group's working line is that the NFL theorems are just Hume with linear algebra: in the optimization case (Wolpert and Macready's 1997 results) and in the earlier machine-learning version, if you average uniformly over the space of possible objective functions or target concepts, every algorithm does exactly as well as every other — in the optimization setting, no better than blind search. Hume, on this reading, says the uniformity of nature can't be non-circularly justified; the NFL theorems say the same thing but make the price exact: with no structural assumption about your problem, your expected advantage over chance is precisely zero, and a simplicity-weighted prior over data streams (Solomonoff-style) is what buys back a convergence-to-the-truth guarantee for computable sources. I was happy with that. Then a friend of mine, over a very long discussion after a departmental talk, sketched a case that I think breaks the equivalence, and I can't tell whether it's a real counterexample or a parlor trick. Call it the Delphic mirror. An agent faces an infinite binary stream. Before each bit is revealed, the agent forms a forecast — a genuine probability p that the next bit is 1 — and the device then emits the outcome to which the agent assigned no more than half its credence (emitting 1 when the agent is exactly at 0.5). Assume the mirror keys on whatever the agent would actually be willing to stake money on, so announcing a decoy 0.5 while privately betting on 1 isn't available. Then: at any step where the agent has a definite opinion, it is wrong; the best long-run accuracy attainable is one half, achieved only by never having an opinion at all; and this holds no matter how much data has accumulated or how clever the forecasting rule is. Yet the mirror is about as simple and deterministic a specification as one could ask for, and the entire interaction history is computable if the agent is. My friend's conclusion, which I half believe: "That's not Hume's problem at all — Hume never had to worry about the world reading your beliefs."

The essay should take up roughly these threads, weaving them into one argument rather than answering them as a list. First, does the mirror show that the load-bearing presupposition behind both the NFL moral and the Solomonoff-style escape hatch is not structural simplicity in the world but the world's causal indifference to your credences — a Cartesian separation of predictor from predicted, which is exactly what the fixed-objective-function framing of the theorems builds in? Second, is there a real obstruction here or only an apparent one: is the truth in a reflexive setting a fixed point of the agent's own forecasting rule, so that any convergence to better-than-chance accuracy destroys itself, and could a prior over a richer hypothesis space — hypotheses that are functions from the agent's own policy to streams, rather than streams — recover a dominance theorem, and at what cost in complexity or in content? Third, if the right response is simply to add belief-independence to one's prior, does that assumption beg the question in the way Hume accused the uniformity principle of begging it, or is it a different and milder kind of circularity, something closer to a constitutive precondition of there being a prediction problem at all? And fourth, some realism: the mirror is contrived, but belief-tracking environments are not obviously exotic — markets, adversarial classifiers, published forecasts that feed back into the thing forecast — so should any of this move a working inquirer's confidence in induction, or is the mirror a curiosity that leaves ordinary inductive practice exactly where it was? (If you think it matters, you might also consider whether an agent could coherently profit by deliberately inducing in itself a credence it knows to be inaccurate.)

Please write about 1,200 to 1,500 words, in American English, and in continuous prose with minimal headings. Don't cite or cite-check anything: no bibliography, no papers, no named authors beyond the ones I've already mentioned — argue from the setup as I've given it, and define any technical term you use. Weigh the strongest version of the position you end up rejecting, and then commit: say plainly whether the NFL theorems sharpen Hume's problem, merely restate it in a more precise vocabulary, or are largely orthogonal to it because the deeper difficulty lies elsewhere, and finish by naming the one consideration that would most seriously make you change your mind.
