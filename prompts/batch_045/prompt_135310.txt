Hi — I'm on a tiny patient-advocacy committee and we have to take a position next month on whether to support adding a proposed new psychiatric diagnosis (it's for adolescents who have odd perceptual experiences and suspiciousness that don't yet meet the bar for psychosis — the idea is to catch people early; I think the working name is something like attenuated psychosis syndrome, but I might be misremembering that). One of our members circulated an essay arguing against it, and the part I can't evaluate is the philosophy. Her summary of Ian Hacking goes like this:

"Hacking's looping effects are basically the old self-fulfilling prophecy point from labeling theory: once you stick a psychiatric label on someone, they start acting in ways that confirm it, so the label stops telling you anything about what's really going on underneath. These are what Hacking called interactive kinds — the people classified can become aware of the classification and change, so the target moves — which is why they can never be natural kinds on the model of gold or a disease gene, which are indifferent to how we describe them. The loop gets going as soon as patients themselves hear the diagnosis. Since the people being classified keep changing, these categories are really just a snapshot of whatever a given culture currently finds deviant, which is also why the diagnostic manual keeps getting rewritten: each edition is a new fashion. And if a classification is interactive in Hacking's sense, the only honest thing to do is drop it."

My instinct is that this is doing more work than Hacking would want — the "drop it" line in particular feels like it's smuggling in a conclusion, and I've heard that Hacking was careful about global claims that something is "socially constructed" — but I can't tell which sentences are the load-bearing ones and which are just rhetoric. So: could you go through that paragraph claim by claim and tell me what someone who works on Hacking would accept, what they'd wince at, and what they'd flatly reject? Especially: is the label-as-self-fulfilling-prophecy gloss fair, or does it lose something; does awareness on the part of the diagnosed person actually have to be part of the loop; and is the interactive/indifferent contrast being drawn right?

Two more things, if you can. First, does the "drop it" conclusion follow from whatever's true in that paragraph — or is there a version of the looping worry that a defender of the new diagnosis could accept and still support it? And second: another committee member says that even if the essay oversells it, the looping worry is genuinely worse for a category defined by subthreshold experiences in teenagers than for something like a dementia diagnosis, and that that difference survives correcting the essay. Is there anything to that, and if so, what exactly is the difference supposed to consist in?
