I retired nine years ago from a career in radiation metrology — thirty-odd years spent calibrating scintillation counters and arguing with health physicists about whether a given reading really meant anything — and on Tuesday mornings four of us, all former laboratory people, meet to work slowly through philosophy. This term we have reached formal epistemology, and in particular the problem of learning from evidence you are not certain of: the rule associated with Jeffrey, on which a shift in your confidence across a partition of evidential propositions is propagated to everything else by holding your conditional probabilities fixed on the cells of that partition. I am the one in the group who sets the problems, because I used to set the practicals, and I have become dissatisfied with what I have managed to produce so far.

The difficulty is that every account of this rule that we can find says the same three things: state the rigidity condition, write down the weighted average of the old conditional probabilities with the new cell probabilities as weights, and remark that the rule degenerates into ordinary conditioning when the new probabilities are zero or one. My colleagues can now recite that, and I do not think it shows that they understand anything. What I want instead is a question whose crux is this: the rule takes as input only the old probabilities, the partition, and the new marginal probabilities on that partition, so it is blind to what *produced* the shift — yet whether rigidity holds seems to depend on exactly that. Take a contaminated-sample hypothesis H with prior 0.5, a monitor reading that is positive with probability 0.8 if H and 0.2 if not, and suppose the agent's confidence that the reading was positive moves from 0.5 to 0.9. In one variant this happens because the display was read in a badly lit counting room, so the agent has a genuine but inconclusive sensory state; in another it happens because the agent reads in the logbook that the run was made at the high-gain setting, which is itself more common when samples are hot. The weighted average gives 0.74 in both cases. I want the question to force a student to say whether that shared answer is a defect of the rule or a sign that rigidity is meant to be precisely the statement that the shift carries no further information, what the agent is certain of in each variant, and whether the standard pragmatic arguments for conditioning carry over. I have also been toying with a repair of my own — hold rigidity only relative to a stated cause C that the agent is certain of, so that the rule takes an extra argument — and I would like the question to make someone tell me whether that collapses into plain conditioning or is a genuine generalization, and whether the minimum-information style generalization of the rule helps at all with the source-blindness worry.

So: please write me a problem-set question for my group, in three or four labelled parts, answerable in about an hour and marked out of 20, together with a model answer of roughly 700 words and a marking scheme that gives the marks per part, names the specific moves that separate a first-class answer from a merely competent one, and gives a one-line note for each part on the mistake that should cost marks. Use the contamination example above, or replace the figures with cleaner ones if you prefer, but then keep the question and the model answer consistent with each other and check the arithmetic; we are ex-laboratory people and we will check it too. One firm constraint: nothing in the question or the model answer may depend on a library or a database. No citations, no page references, no attributed slogans — where a position matters, describe the argument in a sentence rather than naming the person attached to it, since none of us could look any of it up.
