I write novels, and a long stretch of my most recent book takes place inside a sleeping character's head: whole chapters of it, narrated in the present tense, in the same free indirect style I use for her waking hours. While I was writing them I went looking for the philosophy of dreaming and found the two positions I take it you know — Malcolm's, on which the grammar of *I dreamt that…* leaves no room for an experience undergone in sleep, since the waking report, or the impression of memory, is the primitive and nothing can correct it; and Dennett's, on which the dream is not something lived through in the night but something assembled at the moment of waking, a cassette recorded while I slept and played only when I stirred. I finished the book, so I can live with either of those. What I cannot live with is the argument a friend built out of them, which seems to me to prove far more than either of its authors wanted. I have written it out as carefully as I can:

(1) For it to be true that S underwent an experience with content C at time t, there must be some possible difference between its being true and its being false — something that would settle the matter, in principle if not in practice.

(2) The only thing that settles what was dreamt is the sleeper's waking account of it: nothing about her brain, her eyes, her body, the length of the interval, or the stage of sleep confirms or corrects a particular dream-content, and during the night there is no one at home to consult.

(3) That account is not a copy of something stored. It is composed on the spot — shaped by an audience, by a genre of telling, by mood, forgetfulness, and every ordinary art of story. Composition makes its object rather than finds it. (This is the only premise I have any professional stake in: it is what writing feels like from the inside.)

(4) So there is no fact of the matter, antecedent to and independent of the telling, about what was dreamt: no determinate content, no determinate duration, no determinate hour.

(5) An experience with no determinate content, duration, or time is not an experience; there is no such thing. So dreaming is not an experience, and the dream comes into being, if at all, when it is told.

(6) None of this turned on sleep as such. What did the work was this pair of claims: that the sole criterion for what an experience was is the subject's later account of it, and that the later account is composed rather than transcribed.

(7) And that pair holds of waking no less than of sleep. Take any stretch of waking experience that leaves no public trace and admits no concurrent check — a drive along a road so familiar that nothing survives of it, ten minutes at a window, an ache nobody saw and you never mentioned, a taste to which you gave no attention. What it was like would be settled only by what you would later say; and what you would later say is composition, by (3).

(8) So there is no fact of the matter about what any of those were like either, and so, by (5), they were not experiences. Every experience that is never narrated is not an experience: the inner lives of infants and of animals, and every unmentioned minute of my own day, come out blank. And — this is the part that stops me — the interior of a character, which I compose in sentences written years after the events they describe, then differs from the interior of the person reading about her only in being honestly invented.

One move I have already tried and failed with: people in a sleep laboratory now claim to reconstruct dream content from brain scans, which looks like a counterexample to (2). My friend replies that such a decoder is trained on the very reports whose authority is in question, and that in any case it says nothing about when in the night the content occurred, or how long it seemed to last, or whether it had any temporal order at all. I cannot tell whether that reply is good, and I would like it priced along with the rest.

I feel that something slipped between (4) and (5), or else between (6) and (7), but every time I try to name it the argument reassembles. So: which step or inference do you reject, and why did that step look sound? Then price the alternatives — for each of the other places one might cut, state the view you are left holding and what it commits you to about dreams, about un-narrated waking experience, and about the relation between a report and what it reports. Where Malcolm's and Dennett's own versions of the anti-experience view differ, say which of them survives your diagnosis and which does not. If I must choose, I would rather keep (3) than (1), and rather keep (2) than (5): I can accept that my dream chapters are made rather than found, but not that my reader's unmentioned morning was nothing. Do not flatten this into agreement with me; tell me what my preference costs.
