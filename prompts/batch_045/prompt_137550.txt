so I finally wrote down where I've landed on the drowning-child stuff, because a friend who is very hard-nosed about this is coming over sunday and I'd rather find out myself whether my package falls apart than have him find out. Here it is, roughly:

(1) I accept the argument: neither distance nor the fact that millions of other people are also in a position to help makes a difference to whether I'm required to act, and I'm required to hand over a substantial share of what I have to effective charities.

(2) My duty isn't a share of some collective job. If literally nobody else gave, I'd still owe what I owe. But I also think the "why am I the one carrying the free riders" complaint has something real in it and I don't want to have to say it's just resentment dressed up.

(3) I resist the "you might as well have pushed her in" framing. I think the killing/letting-die distinction is real, and I want it to show up both in how much blame I deserve and in what I actually owe.

(4) I reject the demandingness objection. "If that's right I'd have to give until it really hurts" is not by itself a reason the requirement goes away.

(5) Same pond on my commute every morning, different kid each time, costs me a ruined pair of shoes and ten minutes: I think on morning two hundred my obligation is the same as on morning one, and I'm happy to count expected lives rather than identified ones.

(6) I dont think the argument requires maximizing. It requires preventing something very bad when I can do it without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, so my kid's music lessons and flying out to see my brother are defintely fine.

(7) But I also think there's a point at which a person is allowed to stop and just live a life, and that this isn't only weakness or an excuse — that a life with no pointless projects in it isn't merely harder but worse, and that that counts for something.

I honestly dont know which of these I'd give up, and I don't want to be told to be more consistent in general. Audit the set: which pairs actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, what has to give in each case. Give me five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no intro and no sign-off: (1) the worst tension, (2) the next worst, (3) one pair that only looks like a tension and why it dissolves, (4) the single revision that cleans up the most, (5) what that revision costs me — the thing I'd end up having to believe that I'd rather not. Naming a position or an -ism is fine, but don't point me at a reading list or cite papers; I've got no library access out here.
