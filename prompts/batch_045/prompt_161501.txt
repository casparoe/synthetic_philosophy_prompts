I do a long-form interview podcast for the science-and-ideas desk at my magazine — smart general listeners, about 45 minutes of usable tape per episode — and I'm recording one next week on how historians explain ordinary people who end up as perpetrators of mass killing. The guest is a historian who's spent her career on Holocaust perpetrator studies and who defends what I'd call the division-of-labor synthesis: situational and small-group mechanisms (peer pressure, deference to authority, not wanting to let your mates down, careerism) do the causal work in explaining *whether* men participated, while the specifically German cultural story — the eliminationist-antisemitism thesis from Goldhagen's Hitler's Willing Executioners — explains the content, the target and the gratuitous cruelty; so Browning's Ordinary Men and Goldhagen aren't really rivals, they're answering different questions. She is very good and very quick at two moves: whenever I push on whether the two halves actually fit together she says it's a false dichotomy, they're different levels of analysis, and whenever the conversation drifts toward blame she says she's doing causal explanation, not moral assessment. I want questions that make both of those moves progressively less available to her, not a list where she can just restate the synthesis in a fresh idiom each time.

Here's where I keep getting stuck in the reading, and I'd like the questions aimed at these. (a) The spread: at one of the first massacres the commander offered anyone who didn't feel up to it the chance to step out, and fewer than a dozen of roughly 500 took it — and on later occasions men who asked to be excused mostly had their task reassigned to someone else, so refusing didn't reduce the killing, it just moved it onto a mate. So for me the puzzle isn't why they started, it's why almost nobody took the exit ramp when the exit was free and kept being offered across months; give me a question that forces her to say whether her mechanism predicts that, plus the follow-up for when she answers with habituation or escalating commitment. (b) The minority who did refuse or evade: if she explains them by biography or temperament she's brought character back in exactly where the variation lives, and if she explains them by weak ties to the group then the situational story seems to explain everything and the cultural half does no work at all — I want the question that makes her choose, and then the follow-up keyed to whichever one she picks. (c) The evidence base: postwar trial testimony comes from men trying to stay out of prison, and I can't see how you discount that testimony when it says they were only following orders and credit it when the same men describe Jews in exterminationist language — ask her for the rule she'd actually apply, with a follow-up for when she says something about corroboration. (d) Her favourite word is ordinary: does ordinary do any causal work in her account, or only the moral work of telling listeners these men were like us? My listeners always want to ask whether they'd have done it, so I want one question that pins down whether her thesis licenses any answer to that at all, and if it doesn't, why a non-specialist should care how the two explanations are divided up. I also want her pinned to a number at some point: of a randomly drawn cohort of middle-aged German men in 1942, what share does her model say would have ended up pulling a trigger — and I want one question about what finding in the archives would make her give the synthesis up.

Give me eight to ten questions in the order I should ask them, each phrased so a listener who hasn't read either book can follow it, with the follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give rather than a generic tell us more. After each, add a line on what you expect her answer to be and where that answer is soft. Flag the two questions I should spend my time on if we're running long, and end with something better than what are you working on next — last episode finished on that and it was defintely the weakest two minutes we've ever aired.
