I sit on the procurement working group of a large hospital trust — I'm the token philosopher; the others are a dietician, an accountant and a supply-chain manager — and we are trying to write something defensible about the animal products and animal-protein alternatives we buy. I keep running into the same shape of rule, and I want to know what it presupposes, not what its defenders say it presupposes. Here is the shape. If a researcher wants to keep an octopus, EU rules in force since 2013 require her to justify the work under the same directive that covers mice; if she keeps a million fruit flies, she needs nothing at all. The UK statute of 2022 recognises all vertebrates and, in addition, octopuses and lobsters as sentient, but says nothing about how a lobster is killed or a shrimp is farmed; our seafood buyer tells me the industry norm in hatcheries is still to cut or crush the eyestalks of broodstock females to induce spawning. Black soldier fly larvae and crickets are now grown by the tonne for feed and for protein powder, killed by freezing, drying or grinding, with no paperwork at any point, although one supplier's brochure describes its own method as humane. A mouse in a lab needs an ethics protocol; the same species in our kitchens gets a glue trap. And at the far end, nobody at all — not even the supplier with the humane brochure — thinks our estates department's building-management system has anything to say for itself, though it does register and respond to being too cold, and though I have watched a colleague apologise to a robot vacuum.

Our consultant has proposed that we stop drawing a line and instead adopt what he calls a graded schedule: for each input we buy, estimate the probability that the animals have valenced experiences, multiply by some measure of how much experience a creature of that kind has in a life and in a death, and let the resulting number drive how much we are willing to pay to avoid or improve it. This sounds to me like a quite different theory of what is going on from the one the existing rules embody, but I can't tell whether the rules strictly presuppose a threshold — that sentience and moral standing are all-or-nothing, and that only our evidence about them is uncertain — or whether they are equally compatible with a view on which status really is continuous and the line is just administrative, drawn where evidence is strong and compliance is cheap. Maybe they presuppose only something about precaution under uncertainty, or only something about tractability: insects are uncountable, shrimp are cheap, and no one's constituents care. Maybe the octopus-versus-fruit-fly boundary rests on a substantive condition — embodiment, integrated nociception, motivational trade-off, learning, something like a unified point of view — that also explains why the thermostat is excluded, and I would like to know whether that condition can be stated without sliding either into a bare appeal to neural substrate or into a functionalism that admits the thermostat by a very small, non-zero degree.

So I would like the implicit theory reconstructed, and then pulled apart. What does the existing pattern strictly require us to believe about sentience, moral status and evidence, and what does it merely sit comfortably with? Where is it underdetermined, in the sense that several quite different theories — threshold views, graded-status views plus an administrative cut-off, precautionary views, cost-based views — would all generate the same rules? Which rival theories does it quietly rule out, for instance any view on which the sheer number of insects can make up for the thinness of each insect's experience? Is it coherent as it stands, given that the same jurisdiction can require an ethics protocol for an octopus in a laboratory and none for an octopus in a restaurant, or for a mouse in a lab and none for a mouse in a storeroom — or is that only an inconsistency if you already think the practice is tracking sentience rather than something else? And finally, if we replaced the presupposed view with the consultant's graded one, what in our actual purchasing practice would have to change: would we be obliged to pay more for insects killed one way rather than another, to stop buying shrimp from hatcheries that ablate, to treat a trillion insects as a genuine moral sum, or to take seriously the residual claim of the building-management system? I'd rather be told which of those follow and which are the reductio that shows the graded view needs a stopping place.
