hey — I TA a small undergrad seminar on philosophy of mind, and next week's session is on whether pain is bad in virtue of how it feels. We keep circling the same clinical picture: someone who can tell you exactly where a stimulus is, how strong it is, whether it's burning or stabbing, but reports that it doesn't bother them — they have the pain, they just aren't suffering from it, and (I was talking to a friend in med school about this and I might be misremembering) they'll sometimes still pull the hand away even while saying it's fine. The dissociation between the sensory side — location, intensity, quality — and the affective side — how much it sucks, wanting it to stop — is what I want fiction about, because my students glaze over when I put it on the whiteboard but argue for an hour once there's an actual person in the room.

So: write me a short story, 1200–1800 words, that dramatizes that split. Setting seed if you want it (change it if you've got something better): a hospital-based psychophysics lab in the dead of winter, overnight shift, narrator is the research coordinator who runs the rating battery on participants — the two 0-to-10 scales, one for intensity, one for unpleasantness — and sits with people while the stimulator does its thing. Low-tech only: a plausible drug, a nerve block, a questionnaire, a consent form. No brain-reading gadgets.

Here's the part I actually want, and I haven't seen it done anywhere so please don't reach for the usual material: invent the mirror image of the familiar case. Give me a second character whose sensory side is basically gone — can't localize it, can't say whether it's burning or cramping, intensity ratings that are noise and don't track the stimulus at all, and a nerve block that abolishes the incoming signal changes nothing — but who is flattened by the unpleasantness: pleading, terrified of the next episode, life coming apart, and the episodes get set off by things that aren't noxious at all, a warm draft from a vent or someone reading a sentence out loud. Then build the scene that does the work: both patients are offered the same intervention, something that peels the two dimensions apart (say, a drug that wipes out the sensory signal and leaves the affective one completely untouched — or whatever you prefer), and their reactions invert what you'd predict. The one who reports pain without minding it refuses treatment, because there's nothing wrong with them. The one with no sensory information begs for it, even though by the protocol it cannot touch the thing that's wrecking their life. And somebody has to decide whether to give it. Feel free to split the affective side further if it helps — felt unpleasantness versus the urge to make it stop versus the retrospective judgment that the episode was bad — and if you can make those come apart in your second character without it reading like a philosophy diagram, do it.

Some constraints, because most fiction on this topic goes wrong in predictable ways. Nobody explains the theory: no character giving a mini-lecture, no tidy last paragraph that resolves anything — the argument should live in what people do, refuse, and ask for. I'd like to finish genuinely unsure which of the two I'd rather be, and ideally my verdict flips once on the way there: I start out thinking the first patient got off easy and end up not so sure. Skip the war-story material I've read a hundred times — wounded soldiers on the battlefield who don't ask for morphine, the inverted-spectrum setup, anything like that. No sage-like patient and no saintly sufferer; make them both annoying, specific, and a bit irrational, with jobs, a bad night, and an unglamorous reason for being in the study (the money? a court-ordered assessment? insomnia?). Then, in 200 words at most after the story: tell me which dimension your story ends up making the badness of pain track, and one line on how someone who thinks the badness lives in the other dimension would accuse you of rigging the characters. I'll use that as discussion bait.
