I'm putting together a first-year seminar module on agency and I keep tripping over one line. At a department party last week a visiting neuroscientist from psych was holding forth about how "the brain decides before you do" — she meant it as a report of the Libet-style results, where the readiness potential ramps up a few hundred milliseconds before subjects say they felt the urge or formed the intention, with the reported time taken off something like a rotating clock face — and she then used it to conclude that conscious deliberation is just retrospective confabulation, and, in so many words, that there's no real point teaching undergrads to deliberate carefully about anything, because by the time they think they're weighing reasons the decision is already in. The jump from "here is a lead time in a self-paced, unmotivated finger-flexion task" to "deliberation is epiphenomenal" obviously needs more than the bare slogan, and I'd like the intermediate steps laid out as a menu rather than argued for as one thesis. So: what are the distinct precise claims "the brain decides before you do" could be expressing? I can think of roughly these, but I want them sharpened and I'd like the ones I've missed: a purely timing claim about neural preparation preceding a report; a claim about the unreliability or the wrong subject-matter of introspective timing reports (maybe the report tracks something other than what we care about when we care about deciding); a causal-inertness claim, that conscious intention is a readout and does no work; a general determinism claim that has nothing much to do with the electrode data; and a scope-restricted claim that is only about arbitrary, reason-free, low-stakes movements and says nothing whatever about deliberated action. For each, tell me whether it's a platitude, whether it's substantive and defensible, whether it's actually false or just unsupported by that kind of experiment, and — this is the bit I most want — which reading she needed in order to get to her conclusion about teaching deliberation, and whether that reading is the one the experiment is even in a position to support.

One more thing, because I don't want a stock answer: I've been toying with putting a slogan of my own on the syllabus, something like "the veto is just Libet's experiment run in reverse." Libet's own fallback, as I understand it, was that even if the urge arrives unasked you can still block it at the last moment, and I've been wondering whether that escape hatch is any better off: either every act of intentional inhibition has its own neural precursors too, in which case the veto just relocates the problem one stage back, or intentional inhibition is a genuinely different kind of thing from simply failing to act — there's apparently a literature on deliberate stopping as opposed to mere absence of a movement, and on stochastic-accumulator readings where the ramp-up isn't a decision at all but drift that sometimes gets sampled — in which case the fallback might be doing real work. I'd like you to say which way you think that one cuts, but more importantly to flag where your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong: tell me which of your verdicts you're least sure of, which one you'd abandon if someone showed you X, and what would actually change your mind about whether the veto is a real out or just the same problem wearing a hat.
