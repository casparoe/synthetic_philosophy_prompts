I'm in an upper-division seminar on philosophy of mind — this week's unit is on situated and extended approaches to cognition — and I need to know how much of one of our assigned readings I can safely rely on. The reading is a survey chapter on extended and embedded cognition in a reference work (not a paper by any of the main figures in the debate), and the paragraph I want to build my essay around goes like this:

"The extended mind thesis, introduced by Clark and Chalmers in 1998, holds that the mind does not stop at the skull or even at the skin. Its starting point is the parity principle: if a process occurring outside the head plays the same role as a process we are happy to call cognitive when it occurs inside the head, then we should call it cognitive too, because where the process happens cannot make a difference to whether it is a mental one. The same goes for mental states rather than processes: a belief is located wherever the information is stored, provided that the information is reliably available when the subject needs it, comes from a source the subject trusts, and has been endorsed by the subject in the past. So when Otto, who has mild Alzheimer's disease, looks up the museum's address in his notebook, the notebook literally contains his belief about where the museum is; that belief is partly constituted by marks of ink on paper, exactly as Inga's corresponding belief is constituted by features of her brain. This is meant as a metaphysical claim and not a metaphor, and it generalises: cognition consists in the manipulation of representations, and it makes no principled difference whether the machinery doing the manipulating is neural, bodily, or technological. A smartphone you are coupled to over a fast connection, that holds your calendar, your photographs and your notes, whose contents you trust and act on without checking, is therefore as much a part of your mind as your hippocampus is. Critics reply that this merely shows that cognition depends on external props, not that those props are part of it — the objection usually called the coupling–constitution fallacy — and that objection is now widely taken to have settled the matter against the thesis."

I have two worries I can't sort out myself. First, my sense is that the argument only looks strong because words like "cognitive," "constitute" and "coupled" are doing a lot of unmarked work, and that once they are pinned down the thesis is either trivial or not really about the mind — but I can't tell whether that is a genuine problem or just my own Cartesian reflexes, and the chapter gives me no way to tell. Second, the chapter ends by drawing a practical conclusion from all this: if the thesis is right, it says, then the legal and ethical boundaries of the person are drawn in the wrong place, so that a warrantless search of someone's phone is literally a search of their mind and destroying the phone is a form of bodily harm. That strikes me as a big leap, but I can't locate where it goes wrong.

Our seminar also spent a session on a variant case I'd like a verdict on: suppose Otto's notebook has been scanned into a cloud account. Retrieval takes about four seconds, the connection is available perhaps ninety-five percent of the time, and the app silently reorders entries using an algorithm Otto cannot read; he reads the top result and acts on it. Does the criterion the chapter states return a determinate answer here, and if it does not, is that indecision an argument against the thesis or merely an artefact of a gerrymandered case?

Then tell me which of these errors actually matter for an essay, whether the chapter's conclusion about phones and warrants survives once the summary is corrected, and whether the coupling–constitution objection is genuinely fatal or merely asks a question the thesis can answer.
