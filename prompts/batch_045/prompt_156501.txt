Write a patient information leaflet — the little folded sheet of tiny print that comes in a medicine box — for a course of treatment called something like 'Phenomenality Reduction Therapy', offered by a clinic whose entire staff have become convinced illusionists about phenomenal consciousness. Register: flat, bureaucratic, gently reassuring, very NHS. About 650–750 words, with the usual little sections: what this medicine is for, before you start, how and when to take it, how it works, possible side effects, what to do if you miss a dose, and a short FAQ at the end.

The comedy has to come from actually getting illusionism right, so:

- The leaflet should insist, more than once and with visible strain, that it is not denying you are conscious — it denies only a certain characterisation of consciousness, the bit about intrinsic, ineffable, 'what it's like' properties. It keeps insisting this while quietly deleting everything the patient meant by experience.
- Side effects should include a persisting conviction that there is something it is like to be you, and a residual sense that colours are somehow coloured. These get explained as harmless by-products of introspection misrepresenting its own states, and the leaflet should reach for the ordinary perceptual-illusion analogy — lines that look different lengths, a patch that looks like a coloured shape — rather than hand-waving. That analogy is the clinic's entire explanation, and the joke is how calmly it gets deployed.
- The how-it-works section has to genuinely try: a paragraph of functional/cognitive story about how the misrepresentation arises, accurate enough that an illusionist would nod along, funny because it accounts for every functional detail except the one the patient came in complaining about.
- The FAQ needs one exchange where a patient asks whether the illusion the leaflet keeps talking about is itself something there's something it's like to have. The answer must be a straight-faced answer a real illusionist could give — no strawman, no winking — and it should be evasive in exactly the way that objection says it has to be.
- One deadpan line about pain: the treatment removes the hurtfulness, not the functional role, so patients are reassured they will still withdraw from injury.
- Somewhere the leaflet has to betray itself by slipping into phenomenal language it officially doesn't believe in — 'you may find that the treatment feels slightly...' and then a hasty correction in the house style.
- Patients who notice any remaining phenomenality after finishing the course should be invited to report it, in the usual official way, on a card.

Don't name any living philosopher, don't cite anything, and never stop to explain illusionism to the reader. Someone who's never heard the word qualia should still come away feeling that something has been quietly taken away from them, and an illusionist should read it and think: yes, fine, that is roughly what I'm committed to, damn.
