I maintain a small open-source puzzle game built out of letter-string analogy items: the player is shown a source pair such as abc → abd and is asked to produce the corresponding result for a new string. I am rewriting the answer-checker, and two of my longest-running contributors have been going back and forth in the issue thread about how to grade answers that do not match the rule I had in mind. I have to merge one of their two pull requests and I genuinely cannot tell who is right.

The three items they keep returning to: for "abc → abd, ijk → ?" the intended answer is ijl (replace the last letter by its successor), but a minority of players answer ijd; for "abc → abd, mrrjjj → ?" the intended answer is mrrkkk, but a large minority answer mrrjjjj (add a fourth j); for "abc → abd, mrrzzz → ?" they cannot agree on anything, because z has no successor and players produce mrrzzzz, mrraaa, and mrryyy in roughly equal numbers.

(1) Mira: all of these are one phenomenon. The change a player carries over from abc → abd can be described in more than one way, and in the source pair the descriptions coincide: the successor of c just is the letter d, and one more of the last thing could mean either the next letter or another copy of it. Since the source pair does not determine which description a player is running, none of these answers should be marked wrong.

(2) Owen: underdetermination by the source pair cannot be the test, or every answer is right. What separates mrrjjjj from ijd is local pressure: mrrjjj arrives in runs of repeated letters, which invites reading the change as one more of the same. Nothing about ijk invites reading successor of k as the letter d. A re-description that nothing in the target calls for is simply a different rule.

(3) Mira: pressure is not a property of the string, it is a property of a player. Plenty of players see jjj and still answer mrrkkk, and plenty see ijk and reach for d because d is sitting right there in the source. You are laundering your own sense of what is salient into a fact about the item. And your distinction makes a checkable prediction that fails: if ijd were merely copying the letter, the same players should answer xyd to "abc → abd, xyz → ?", but they do not — they answer xya or wyz.

(4) Owen: that check assumes what is at issue. If concepts really are this fluid, a player should be able to run one description on one item and another on the next; cross-item inconsistency is what your view predicts, so it cannot count as evidence against mine. There is also a design argument: if a re-description counts whenever it is available, apply the same change has no content left and the checker can never reject anything. Counting re-descriptions only where the target resists the obvious reading at least gives us a criterion.

(5) Mira: which is exactly where your criterion collapses, because mrrzzz resists the obvious reading completely. Pressure there is total, so by your own rule mrrzzzz, mrraaa, and mrryyy are all correct. The criterion is not doing any work; you have just moved the discretion into the word pressure.

(6) Owen: then items where the source rule fails outright should be pulled from the game rather than graded permissively.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: at each step say who has the better of it and why, and identify the point where the real disagreement actually lies. Also say what both of them may be missing about what it means for two answers to come from the same rule. I should mention that I am an engineer with no philosophy background, so I would rather have concrete reasoning than a tour of the literature.
