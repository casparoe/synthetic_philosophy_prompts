Here's the passage, more or less as it appears:

Radical interpretation is Davidson's name for the problem a field linguist faces: she turns up among people whose language she doesn't know and has to work out what their sentences mean. Her only evidence is which sentences they hold true, and when — she can't peek into their thoughts or intentions. To get anywhere she has to apply the principle of charity, which says: interpret them so they come out mostly rational and mostly right about their world. That's really just the golden rule dressed up in technical language — be generous to the people you're trying to understand. Once you impose charity, you can pair every sentence with the conditions under which it's true, and since for Davidson a theory of meaning just is a theory of truth conditions, that's the whole job done: to know what a sentence means is to know when it's true. The catch is that endlessly many different pairings fit the evidence equally well, so meaning is indeterminate — there's no fact of the matter about what anyone means, which is Quine's indeterminacy of translation all over again. Late on, Davidson added something about "triangulation": the interpreter, the speaker, and some object in the world they're both reacting to have to form a kind of triangle. Nice image, but it's basically a footnote — the real work had already been done by the principle of charity back in the seventies.
