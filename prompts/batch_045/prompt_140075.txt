Hi — please answer as a political philosopher who does paid consulting for city governments on commemoration and symbolic-reparation questions, and take your time: before you commit to anything I'd find it genuinely useful if you brainstorm out loud for a bit about what principles could even be in play here, and then lay out a short table of each candidate rule and what it predicts about my case. Don't sit on the fence at the end — I need a recommendation, not a survey.

I've just been appointed to my city's Naming and Memorials Commission and I've spent the weekend reading through about a decade of its written decisions, and I honestly cannot work out what rule it thinks its applying. The track record: (1) an equestrian statue of a Confederate cavalry general on Courthouse Square, put up in 1911 by a Confederate memorial association, was removed 5–2 and put in storage (not destroyed); (2) a street named for a merchant who made his fortune in the slave trade and later endowed the city's first public library was kept 4–3, on the stated ground that the name has become "purely a navigation aid" and nobody in this city knows or cares who he was, with a marker rejected as "odd"; (3) a statue of a Progressive-era reform mayor who built the parks and the sewer system but who also authored the 1916 residential segregation ordinance and testified for a state eugenics bill was kept 6–1 with a new interpretive plaque; (4) an elementary school named for a prominent sterilization advocate was renamed 7–0 after a local educator, reasoning that a school is a place children are compelled to attend and are asked to look up to a name, which is different from a statue you can walk past; (5) a statue of a segregation-era governor was taken off the capitol grounds and relocated to the county history museum with interpretive material, 4–3 (dissenters wanted it gone, one wanted it left alone); (6) a bust of a labor leader who was tried and acquitted in a factory bombing that killed two night watchmen, honored for organizing the eight-hour-day strike, was kept with no plaque, 6–1, on the grounds that what he is commemmorated for is seperable from what he is condemned for; (7) a 1925 "Pioneer Mother" monument with no named individuals, celebrating settlement, was removed 4–3 because it "commemorates a process of dispossession as though it were a finished achievement" and names no one whose record can be weighed; (8) a 1961 bronze plaque honoring the city's founders that listed only white men was neither removed nor replaced but reworded by consensus; and (9) the commission has twice declined to touch the city's own name (it's named for a man who bought and sold enslaved people), saying that's above its pay grade.

Next month's docket is this: a bronze bust, installed in 1981 in the lobby of the central public library, of Councilwoman Ora Bell Kendrick — the first Black woman elected to the city council, who led the fights to desegregate the schools and to desegregate public housing. In 1977–79 she was also the loudest local backer of the state habitual-offender law, publicly defended the police chief through the scandal in which three men's confessions were later found to have been coerced (two of them were exonerated after eleven years; she never apologized and said at the time that the department "knows who the real criminals are"), and she spoke at a rally against the 1978 gay-rights ordinance in language about protecting children. The bust was paid for in small donations collected by neighborhood clubs and installed over the objection of the council of the day, which had refused city funds for it. It is the only piece of public sculpture in the city depicting any Black person, and the only one depicting a woman who isn't an allegorical figure; school groups get their photos taken with it and the library's teen reading prize is named after her — and her family has said privately that if the bust comes down they'll pull the prize money. There's a removal petition from a coalition of a local gay-rights group and the families of two of the exonerated men; a counter-petition to keep it from her old neighborhood association (majority Black) and the local branch of a national civil-rights organization; the library staff want it kept but moved out of the lobby into the local-history room; and one commissioner has floated relocating it to the history museum along the lines of the governor precedent.

So: what should we do if we want to be consistent with ourselves, and which of those nine is the closest analog and why? Give me the two or three rules that fit the whole earlier track record but come apart on Kendrick, say what each predicts, and tell me which you'd go with and why. If you think our precedents genuinely underdetermine it, say so plainly and tell me what principle we'd be committing ourselves to with each of the live options (leave it, leave it with a plaque, move it within the library, move it to the museum, remove it). Also: does it matter morally, and not just politically, that she's a Black woman and that the city's public sculpture is otherwise all white men — i.e. is there a real principle about who gets to be commemmorated at all, or is that just a tiebreaker we're dressing up? Should we discount her family's funding threat entirely? If your answer is some version of keep-and-contextualize, tell me roughly what a plaque should say. And finally, name one further case you'd put on the docket, if you could, to tell the leading rules apart.
