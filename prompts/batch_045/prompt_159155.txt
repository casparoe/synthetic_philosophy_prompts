I spend time around a literature that I have come to think is stuck in a way that nobody inside it seems willing to name, and I want a diagnosis of the dispute rather than a verdict in it.

Here is the dispute, as concretely as I can state it. One side holds that certain non-empirical considerations can do genuine evidential work for a theory that makes no testable predictions at accessible energies: that a large, well-funded, highly competent community has searched hard for decades and found no workable alternative to the framework; that the framework goes on to explain things it was not built to explain; and that theories sharing these structural features have, historically, usually gone on to be empirically vindicated. Call these the search argument, the coherence argument, and the track-record argument. The other side replies that the search argument is an argument from ignorance about the power of a search nobody designed as a test; that the coherence argument cites evidence the community already had and so cannot confirm in the way it is being used to; that the track-record argument runs on a reference class with no known base rate, assembled after the fact around the successes; and that the practice as a whole is better explained by the sociology of a field that controls its own hiring and its own seminar invitations than by the epistemology of evidence. A third group says the whole thing is a category mistake, because the object in question is a framework or a research program rather than a theory, and there is nothing there of the right logical shape to be confirmed or disconfirmed in the first place.

As far as I can tell, nothing has moved in fifteen years. Every argument on both sides is in print, everyone has read everyone, and the same three replies get produced in the same order every time a new version appears. So what kind of disagreement is this?

My hypothesis, which I would like tested rather than flattered, is that there is no single question here and that one word is doing the hiding: the sides may be arguing about different outputs. One reading is that the defenders are making a claim about what a research community is permitted to *invest in* — what it may rationally allocate graduate students, telescope time, and careers to under uncertainty — while the critics are making a claim about what a person is permitted to *believe*, or to assert as supported. On that reading the two sides mostly agree on the facts and disagree about which question the word 'confirmation' is being used to ask, and the track-record argument is really an argument about expected research payoff rather than about truth.

The competing hypothesis I cannot rule out is that the disagreement is substantive and empirical at its core: that it is, in the end, a base-rate dispute about what belongs in the reference class, and that this is a real question with a real answer which happens to be unanswerable in practice, because the sample is a handful of cases drawn after the fact from a population that cannot be specified without already taking a side.

The device I have been using to try to separate these hypotheses is an imaginary retrospective audit. Suppose a foundation funded a large, careful, methodologically neutral study of the history of physics which identified every episode in which a research program leaned on arguments of these three kinds, coded only what was knowable at the time, and then recorded what happened next — vindication, abandonment, or continued suspension. Suppose, further, that both sides accept the study's findings as accurate. My question is what would happen at that point. My guess is that the argument would not end, because each side would reject the other's coding of which episodes count, and the fight would relocate to the construction of the reference class — which would suggest that the base-rate reading is closer to the truth, and that the disagreement is a genuine empirical one trapped inside an unanswerable methodology. But I am not confident, and I can imagine the opposite result: that both sides would accept the findings, draw opposite conclusions from them, and reveal that they were never asking the same question.

I'd like you to do three things. First, say which of these diagnoses is right, or give a better one, and argue for it by engaging what each side actually says rather than by surveying the literature — I have read the surveys. Second, tell me whether the audit thought experiment is a good probe: does it isolate what I think it isolates, and if not, what device would? Third, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a piece of history, a change of question, a decision to stop asking — and be honest if the right answer is that no progress should be expected and the debate will simply be abandoned rather than settled.
