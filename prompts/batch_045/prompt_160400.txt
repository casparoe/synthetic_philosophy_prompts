hey — I'm the student rep on my university's Predictive Analytics Oversight Board and I have to write up a recommendation for next tuesday's meeting, so I'd like a draft of the memo I can circulate to the board beforehand. Background: the provost's office wants to roll out Northgate, a vendor model that flags first-years as at risk of dropping out so advisors can reach out first — priority holds on tutoring slots, emergency grants, that kind of thing. The vendor's fairness audit is really an interpretability audit: they trained a sparse autoencoder on the model's middle-layer activations and report that (a) no feature in the dictionary corresponds to race, gender or neighbourhood, (b) a first-generation/commuter feature and a housing-instability feature do most of the predictive work, and (c) if you clamp the handful of features that look demographically loaded, the predictions barely move. A friend in the ML lab re-ran the same pipeline with a dictionary four times bigger and got a feature that fires almost entirely on first names that are statistically associated with race; the vendor's reply is that this is a degenerate split, an artefact of choosing a finer-grained decomposition rather than a discovery, and that features are just a coordinate system we impose on activations, so asking which ones are really there is confused. Half the board reads (a)-(c) as proof the model isn't using protected attributes; the other half says the audit can't distinguish the model doesn't represent race from we didn't find the feature that does, in the basis we happened to look in.

I'm taking a seminar right now on representation in cognitive science and neural networks, and its honestly made me useless in these meetings, because I keep thinking the whole audit rests on a question nobody on the board has answered: what would make a feature in a network a representation of something at all, rather than a useful fiction for steering its behaviour. The view I keep coming back to is that no race feature in the dictionary is evidence about the dictionary, not about the model, unless the feature has been shown to do the kind of work we expect a representation to do — support counterfactuals, generalise to new inputs, survive a change of basis. So instead of the vibe-based arguements I've been pushing a concrete test on the board: the substitution test. Take pairs of student records the model scores differently, swap only the tokens the candidate feature fires on — name, zip code, high-school code — and see whether the score gap travels with the swapped tokens or stays with the surrounding context; if it travels only in some contexts the feature isn't carrying the information, and if it travels only under one decomposition then you've learned something about the decomposition. I also want the memo to be honest about something that cuts against me: even a confirmed race-coded feature wouldn't be automatically disqualifying here, since the intervention gives a flagged student more resources rather than denying them something — which also means the model is deliberately tracking disadvantage is not a defence the vendor gets to just assert, because we still don't know which students get flagged as risky for the wrong reasons.

So: draft the memo. Recommend one concrete course of action on the rollout — approve, approve with conditions, delay pending further tests, or decline — with a time frame and who has to do what by when, rather than just restating the audit. Take the vendor's anti-realist line seriously: state the strongest version of features are only a useful decomposition and the substitution test only tells you about behaviour, not about what the model is representing, and say why it still loses (or say why it doesn't, if you think it wins). And be explicit about confidence: which step is load-bearing, where the reasoning is most likely to fail, and what specific result — from the bigger-dictionary replication, from the substitution test, from somewhere else — would flip the recommendation. Format: exactly six numbered items, one or two sentences each, no introduction and no conclusion, with the bottom line as item 1.
