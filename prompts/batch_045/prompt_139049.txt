I'm in the final year of a PhD in infectious-disease modelling — I fit transmission models to surveillance data and then have to stand up in front of people who control animal-health policy and argue that one model is better supported by the data than another — so I have more than an academic stake in the argument below. My flatmate, who works in philosophy of science, has been chipping away at me for months with it. His claim is that no body of evidence, however large, could ever favour one empirically adequate theory over another, and hence that what my field calls model selection is really aesthetics plus career incentives. Every step looks unobjectionable to me when taken on its own, and the conclusion is one I cannot accept either professionally or in ordinary life. Here is the argument as he finally wrote it out for me on the back of a takeaway menu:

(1) Take any theory T that fits our current evidence E. For any claim C you like about some as-yet-unobserved case, define T* as: T holds for every case in E, and in every case not yet observed, C holds instead. T* fits E exactly as well as T; T* is logically incompatible with T whenever C contradicts what T says about the unobserved case; and there is no limit on how many such C you can pick, so for any E there are arbitrarily many mutually incompatible theories each fitting E perfectly. (This is just the grue-style manoeuvre generalised: emeralds are green in all observed cases and blue thereafter, gravity is attractive until 1 January 2100 and repulsive afterwards, and so on.)
(2) If a body of evidence E is compatible with arbitrarily many mutually incompatible theories, all of which fit E equally well, then E gives no reason whatsoever to prefer T to any of them. As far as the evidence goes the theories are symmetric, and any further tie-breaker — simplicity, elegance, unification, the fact that T is the one that occurred to us first — is a fact about us rather than a reason to think T is true.
(3) The construction in (1) is not peculiar to the evidence we happen to have now. For any body of evidence whatever, present or future, the same manoeuvre can be run again on it: however much we have observed, there are rivals that fit all of it and diverge everywhere else. So at no point, and after no amount of evidence, does anything give us a reason to prefer T to its rivals.
(4) Therefore no claim about anything unobserved is ever justified by evidence — not 'this bridge will hold', not 'the sun will rise tomorrow', and not 'this model will forecast next quarter's case numbers better than that one'.

I have a vague sense that something is being smuggled between (1) and (3) — that (1) shows something about each body of evidence taken separately while (3) needs a rival that somehow survives every body of evidence we could ever get — but when I put this to him he says the gap is easily closed, either by iterating the manoeuvre or by pointing to theories that really are empirically equivalent come what may (Euclidean geometry plus a universal distorting force, or the hypothesis that the world sprang into existence five minutes ago complete with my data spreadsheets). I don't know whether that repair works, and I don't know what it would cost me if it does.

So: which step of the argument do you reject, and why does the step you reject look so much like it is fine? Please price the alternatives honestly — in particular, if you reject (2), tell me what it is about simplicity or prior probability that could make it a guide to truth rather than a psychological convenience, and whether that guide is itself answerable to evidence; and if you reject the move from (1) to (3), tell me whether the repaired argument (the one resting on globally, permanently empirically equivalent rivals) would then prove too much, since it seems to bear on observable matters like tables and other minds and not merely on electrons. If I have to choose, I would much rather keep the claim that my evidence can favour one model over another than keep the argument that says it cannot. Please answer in British English, and be explicit about how confident you are in the diagnosis you give: say which step your own account is most likely to be wrong about, and what argument or consideration would change your mind.
