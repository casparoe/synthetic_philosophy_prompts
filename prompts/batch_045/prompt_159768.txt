i work on a tiny team that maintains an internal scheduling system for a home-care provider — it hands out a limited pool of carer visits each week to about 4,000 clients. i've been arguing about its design with a friend who does ML infra and we just go in circles, so i thought i'd ask people who think about this kind of thing properly. (i studied engineering, not philosophy, so please don't assume i know the jargon — my friend keeps using words like "deontological" and i'm not convinced they're doing any work.)

roughly, here's the setup. at the core there's an optimiser that maximises a predicted-wellbeing score summed over all the clients; it's a model's estimate of how much each visit helps, and it will cheerfully trade one client's big loss against a lot of small gains elsewhere. layered on top is a list of what we call red lines that the optimiser is simply not allowed to cross: never cancel a booked visit with less than 24 hours' notice, never tell a relative anything about a client's condition without that client's own consent, never roster a carer for more than 60 hours in a week. if a red line blocks the best-scoring plan, the system takes the best plan that respects it and writes a "blocked" event to a log. two details seem important to me. first, the red lines weren't handed down from anywhere — a couple of years ago we ran a survey asking service users and carers how bad it would be if each of those things happened, took the median badness rating for each, and promoted the worst-rated ones to hard constraints. so they were born as welfare estimates and are now treated as non-negotiable. second, at our quarterly review a board looks at every blocked event alongside a number the system computes for it: how much predicted wellbeing was "left on the table" by obeying the red line. if a red line keeps costing a lot, the board talks about dropping or weakening it. but being blocked never counts against the system day to day.

what i can't get strait is whether this thing is actually committed to rules that bind independently of the numbers, or whether it's just maximising plus some machinery that happens to look like principles. does treating a constraint as a hard trump at runtime really presuppose that some considerations aren't to be weighed against aggregate benefit, or could a thoroughgoing consequentialist — the indirect or rule-based sort, or one who thinks the decision procedure should be insulated from case-by-case calculation — have designed exactly this? is the quarterly "left on the table" figure in tension with the runtime behaviour, or is it just bookkeeping? does it matter that the red lines came out of a wellbeing survey — does that make the whole architecture consequentialist all the way down, or can a constraint acquire authority of its own after the fact? i'd also really like to know which parts are genuinely underdetermined, i.e. which bits several rival theories would all happily license, and what specifically would have to change in the system if we swapped the implicit view for either (a) straight act-by-act maximising, or (b) constraints that are absolute in the sense that no amount of lost wellbeing could ever justify relaxing them.

please don't give me a reading list or citations, i can't follow up on any of that. a few hundred words is plenty. end with a one-sentence line starting "Bottom line:" and then a final line that is just \answer{...} containing one short phrase naming what the architecture as a whole actually presupposes — something like "side-constraints", "thresholds", or "consequentialist all the way down", or your own label if none of those fit.
