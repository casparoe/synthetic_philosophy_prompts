so I run a little reading group on tech-and-politics stuff and we've completely stalled out. Half of us think the "artifacts have politics" line is onto something real and half think its a cute metaphor for "people have politics and sometimes use artifacts," and every example anybody brings gets dissolved the same way: you name a political feature of the thing, and the other side names the committee vote, the budget line, the procurement spec, or the social meaning that put it there. I want a case that resists that move, and I want it *built* rather than stipulated.

Two views, so we dont talk past each other. Call the first **Inherence**: the material and technical properties of a device can themselves institute a distribution of power or authority — who can be called to account, who has to defer, who gets to say no — and they can do this independently of what anybody intended, who is operating it, or what anyone in the story believes about it. Call the second **Attribution**: a device is political only in virtue of the social relations around it — designers' intentions, the groups that have a stake in it, the meanings they attach to it, the rules and training, what people actually do with it — and every material feature the first view points at is just another choice some person made, so the politics is always downstream of people and could in principle be undone by changing people rather than things.

What I'm asking for:

A realistic scenario, one paragraph, present day, a hospital or a depot or a benefits office or a school. No sci fi, and no machine learning: if there's a rule inside the artifact it should be simple enough to write on an index card and known in full to everyone in the story.

In it, two variants of the same artifact. Same function, same purchase price, same running cost, same designers, same procurement story, adopted by the same organization under the same written rules, operated by the same people with the same decent non-ideological intentions, and — the part that has to be genuinely held fixed — the same stakeholder groups with the same attributions and the same latitude to reinterpret the thing. Everything an Attribution story knows how to cite is identical across the variants.

They differ in exactly one material respect, and the respect is this: *what the artifact makes checkable, by the people it acts on, using ordinary means.* Same actions, same outcomes, same error rate, same rule in the box. What differs is only what comes out of it and in what form. And nobody may have chosen that difference for political reasons — it has to fall out of something boring, like a substituted part, a battery-life requirement, a legacy interface, a printer that can't do line-level detail.

The kicker: despite identical outcomes, the two variants institute different relations of authority — who can be held to account, who must defer to expertise, who can refuse — and they do it without anybody in the story changing how they behave, and in a way that survives the disempowered group's best attempt to reinterpret or route around the thing.

Then show that it works, and be honest where it doesn't. Where Inherence and Attribution agree (both should end up saying: yes, political, and the same variant is the bad one) and where they split (what would have to change to unmake the politics). Then the dissociation, spelled out: hold the material difference fixed and let all the social stuff vary — new rules, retraining, new management, users reinterpreting it — and the politics stays; put the material difference back and the politics goes with nothing else moving. Then name the single feature doing the work, and say what happens if you remove it: does the case quietly collapse back into "a manager used a tool to do what he already wanted to do"?

Then the best Attribution-side attack. The strongest one I can come up with is that "what it makes checkable" is itself a design choice with a social history, so you haven't escaped the committee after all; the runner-up is that the authority relation only exists because some background institution stands ready to act on the output, so the institution is doing the work and the artifact is a relay. Say which of those actually lands, whether the case can be patched against it, and what the patch costs.

Give me your real verdict at the end, not a survey of the options: does a case like this show Inherence doing work Attribution can't absorb, or is the whole research program of "political artifacts" resting on a trick? Commit to one and defend it. Prose only though — no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables, just write it out like an email.
