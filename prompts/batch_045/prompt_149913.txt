I am a retired hydrologist. Thirty years of flood-frequency work for a state water agency, and then, because the county asked for a volunteer with some technical training, four years as the technical member of our five-person board of adjustment. I came to philosophy late and by accident — I started reading in the evenings after a bad season at the board table, looking for a name for a difficulty I had met for decades in my own field and had assumed was somebody else's problem. I gather it has one: the reference class problem. I want your help turning whatever is genuinely defensible in that literature into a procedure my board can run.

What we do. We hear about thirty-five applications a year, in monthly evening sessions of five or six, roughly fifteen minutes each. An applicant wants to build, rebuild, or place fill on a parcel inside the mapped floodplain; we grant or deny, usually with conditions. Our findings go out in writing within ten days and are appealable to a state board that asks only whether the record contains substantial evidence for what we did. The stakes are asymmetric and I feel both sides: when we deny, a household loses the use of a lot it owns, and sometimes most of what it owns; when we are permissive often enough, the county's standing in the federal flood insurance program is in question and every resident pays for our slackness. The other four members are a realtor, a general contractor, an attorney and a retired schoolteacher. Anything I bring them has to fit on one page, in plain words, and survive a reviewer who is not a hydrologist.

What we have, and nothing more: the effective flood insurance study and its maps; the applicant's site plan and elevation certificate; a county permit file reaching back about forty years; a state-maintained flood insurance claim file; and my own memory. No new modelling, no consultants, no sampling. Fifteen minutes.

The question, in our terms. For one parcel, and one question — what is the annual chance that a structure built as proposed suffers flood damage above the threshold that matters — which class of comparable cases do we put that parcel in, and how do we show the work?

Three complications have come up at our table in the past year, and I want the procedure to take a position on each rather than to slide past them.

Routing attributes. Some candidate attributes are not features of the parcel but features of the path the application took to reach us: that it was filed after the last remapping; that the applicant appealed a staff denial rather than accepting it; that the elevation certificate was commissioned by the applicant's own engineer; that claims appear in the file only for insured structures and only above a deductible. My instinct is that these are admissible as evidence about the quality of our data but not as evidence about the hazard, and that a class defined partly by one of them is not measuring what we want to measure. Give me a test that separates the two uses, and tell me if the instinct is wrong.

Mitigation and the time index. Applicants propose to elevate, to fit flood openings, to fill. If we grant with those conditions, the finished structure belongs to the class of elevated structures, and that class's frequency is what we care about afterwards. But if we use it to decide whether to grant, we are classifying the parcel by a feature that exists only because we said yes — and the class of structures that were elevated is not a random sample of structures, since it is made up disproportionately of ones whose owners had already been flooded or who are cautious in ways that travel with maintenance, insurance and income. I suspect the honest answer is that there are two questions and they must be kept apart: the risk if built as proposed, and the risk if built as proposed with our conditions imposed. Confirm or correct that, and give me the rule.

Who proposes the class. Applicants' engineers have learned to submit their own. Last month one submitted the claim history of parcels within two hundred metres of a fire hydrant — not a flood variable by any mechanism I can name, but in our data it separates cleanly, because hydrants sit on the older streets near the river. We need a rule about who proposes, who carries the burden of justifying an attribute, and what we do when the applicant's class and ours both clear whatever relevance test you give us and disagree.

Here is my draft, which I do not trust. Please repair it rather than replacing it, and tell me which steps are inert and which are doing work I have not noticed.

1. List the attributes anyone might think relevant.
2. Keep an attribute only if there is a physical reason it should matter and the county data show a difference significant at five per cent.
3. Narrow by the surviving attributes, one at a time.
4. Stop when the class falls below twenty-five cases, or when further narrowing moves the rate by less than the width of its ninety per cent interval.
5. Read the annual rate off that class. If the class is empty, fall back to the mapped zone rate.

One defect I can see myself: the order in which the surviving attributes are applied changes where we stop and therefore what number we report, so two members following my steps in good faith reach different answers. I assume there are others. The two tests in step two cannot both be met when data are thin, which is precisely when we argue. Step five takes a rate from a claims file one time and from a hydraulic model the next, as if those were the same kind of object. And nothing in my draft says what counts as one member of the class — a structure, a parcel, a claim, a flood event, a household — which matters more than it sounds when twelve units sit on one parcel and one flood damages all of them.

My own field has a standard move for a neighbouring version of this problem, and I want to know how far it licenses us and where it stops. In regional flood-frequency work, when a single gauge record is too short to estimate a rare flood, one pools station-years across several gauges judged to lie in a hydrologically homogeneous region and scales each record by an index flood for its site, so that a short record borrows strength from its neighbours; the judgement of homogeneity is itself tested against scatter within the region. That is what I did for a living. Does it give my board any warrant at all to borrow cases from a neighbouring county or from the state file when the narrow class is empty here, and what would we have to check before doing it?

One last worry, which I would like addressed on its own and not folded into the steps. Everything we know about flooding in this county comes from one hydrologic and hydraulic model, one rainfall-frequency dataset, and one gauge record. Narrowing makes our answers look sharper, but it cannot shrink the error those three inputs share, because every class we can build is drawn from the same modelled world. Tell me plainly how much of the apparent precision of a narrow class is real, and how a board should behave once it understands that.

Please spend no words on the literature, on names, or on citations. I have read the standard moves — the rule of the narrowest class with usable statistics, the argument that the difficulty does not disappear when probabilities are treated as personal degrees of belief, the requirement that evidence about the outcome itself be kept out of the class — and I have no library here. I need the procedure, and I need to be told where it fails.

What I want, in this order:

1. "Scope and assumptions" — what question the procedure answers, what it takes as given, what it declines to answer. About 150 words.
2. "The procedure" — the steps, in language I can put on a one-page sheet for the board, with an explicit stopping rule, an explicit default, and a statement of what counts as one member of a class. About 700 words.
3. "Rulings on the three complications" — routing attributes; mitigation and the time index; who proposes and who bears the burden. About 400 words.
4. "Verdict on my draft" — which steps survive, which are inert, which are actively misleading, including the ordering problem I found and whatever else you find, and a repair for each. About 350 words.
5. "Three applications" — run the repaired procedure on the following, in this order, and give me the number, the refusal, or the hand-off that comes out. Flag any figure you had to invent.
   (a) Single-family house on a riverine lot in a mapped zone with base flood elevations; proposed finished floor one and a half feet above the base flood elevation. Staff's class: all parcels in the same zone within five hundred metres of the channel, about 380 parcels, twenty-two recorded claims over forty years. The applicant's engineer proposes instead: parcels on this street built after 1995 with no claims on record, nine parcels, zero claims — a class he selected after looking at the claims file.
   (b) A twelve-unit manufactured home park on one parcel, inside the same zone. County data for manufactured homes in the floodplain: fourteen units over forty years, three claims. The state file holds roughly sixteen hundred manufactured-home claims, from a region with different soils, rainfall and topography, and with a different inspection regime.
   (c) The one I expect to dislike. A widow's application to rebuild, on the existing footprint, in a lakeside subdivision of about sixty parcels where the board has granted without elevation conditions for years on the strength of a clean record: zero claims since 1985. The claim file for that subdivision begins in 1985, the year it was annexed; the gauge record and the county's old paper files show a flood there in 1979.
   About 800 words in total.
6. "Hard stops, judgment calls, and hand-offs" — where the procedure returns a refusal instead of a number it cannot honestly produce, and where it must stop and let five people vote because the remaining question is about what we owe each other rather than about water. About 350 words.
7. "What narrowing cannot buy" — shared model error, and anything else that makes a narrow class look more informative than it is, with advice on how to write that into our findings so it survives appeal. About 300 words.
8. "What the procedure cannot do" — about 150 words.
