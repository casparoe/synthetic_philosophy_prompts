I'm giving a seventy-minute talk next month at a law-and-philosophy workshop — about twenty minutes of questions at the end — and I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view or an assessment of whether it's true.

The position I'm defending, which I've been calling relational answerability: I read the question of which variety of free will is worth wanting as a question in conceptual ethics — which concept of responsibility should we keep, reform, or discard and institutionalize — and my answer is that what's worth wanting is not a property of agents at all. Not leeway or alternative possibilities, not sourcehood or ultimacy, and not even moderate reasons-responsiveness construed as a desert base. It's a status an agent holds inside a practice of mutual accountability: being someone to whom demands can be addressed and from whom answers are owed. The argument runs in three steps. First, a functional claim about what the practices of blame, resentment, excuse and apology actually do — coordinate expectations and transmit norms — and a claim that they can do that without desert. Second, a conservative-reform claim: when a practice is under pressure, keep the parts whose benefits are demonstrable and drop the parts whose justification can't be given, and on that test the forward-looking, answer-eliciting functions survive while backward-looking desert does not. Third, a claim about manipulation arguments: their force depends on the thought that manipulation changes something intrinsic in the agent, whereas on my view what manipulation damages is the relationship, which is why the law already treats coercion and exploitation as excusing or mitigating — so the manipulation argument is an objection from inside the practice rather than from outside it. I'm aware that answerability already has a use in the tripartite account of responsibility and that the relational move sounds close to the second-personal account; I gesture at both in the talk and don't settle either, which I suspect is where I'll get hit.

The room is about fifteen people, roughly half criminal law scholars — three of them retributivists for whom desert is the whole point of punishment, two who work on excuse and mitigation doctrine and think in terms of duress, provocation and diminished capacity — and roughly half philosophers: one hard incompatibilist in the Pereboom mold who thinks the reactive-attitudes move changes the subject rather than answering it, one reasons-responsiveness compatibilist who will want to know what my view says about the capacities an agent has to have in order to be answerable at all, and two experimental philosophers who run studies on folk attributions in manipulation and bypassing cases and who think the folk want alternative possibilities.

What I want: the eight or so objections I'll actually get, in the order I'll get them, not the ones I'd get from a room of metaphysicians. Phrase each one the way that particular person would say it out loud rather than in my vocabulary — the retributivist's version, the excuse scholar's version, the incompatibilist's version — and give me for each a reply I could say in about two minutes, a note on where that reply is soft or quietly evasive, and a judgment about which objections have real force and which I can answer and move past. Say what I should concede in the first two minutes, before anyone asks anything, and which single reply has to land if the view is going to survive the session. My third step is the one I feel best about, so attack it specifically and tell me what it can't handle. Finish with the question I'm hoping nobody asks.

Write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you'd write it in an email to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, and signal the ordering with transitions rather than numbers or layout.
