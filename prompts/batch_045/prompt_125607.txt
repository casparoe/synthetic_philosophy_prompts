I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about which varieties of free will are worth wanting, and I came away committed to a position I cannot yet state precisely enough to test. I would like you to help me map its exposure.

The position, which I will call the revisability thesis, is this: of the things that get called free will, the only one worth wanting is neither leeway (the ability, holding the past and the laws fixed, to have done otherwise) nor sourcehood (being the ultimate, underived origin of one's actions), but revisability: a standing and exercisable capacity to call one's own practical identity into question and to alter the standards by which one's ends are assessed, where the alteration must be made for reasons one can presently articulate, must survive a cooling-off period, and must feed back into what one subsequently does. On this view, leeway without revisability is worthless — a coin-flip in the moment of choice adds nothing one could care about — and sourcehood without revisability is barely better, since an unmoved mover with a fixed character is precisely the sort of agent whose freedom would be idle.

To make the thesis bite, I stipulate three cases; please take them as set up, and do not dissolve them by asking whether they are nomologically possible.

The Curator case. An agent's higher-order endorsements are monitored by an intervention which, whenever an endorsement would conflict with a long-standing element of that agent's practical identity, quietly reweights the agent's sensitivity to the relevant reasons so that the endorsement goes the other way. Crucially, it works only through the agent's ordinary deliberative route, so the agent experiences every episode as changing their mind for reasons, and passes every ordinary test of reasons-responsiveness.

The Palimpsest case. An agent can revise any end, and does so frequently and well; but each revision is erased from memory once made, so that the agent always re-endorses the revised end as if it had always been theirs, and never remembers having changed.

The Ratchet case. An agent can revise their standards, but only upwards in reflectiveness: every revision is thereafter unrevisable, and each one narrows the space of later revisions.

What I want is the defeaters, and I want them ranked from fatal to merely awkward. In particular:

Which of these cases, if any, is a genuine counterexample rather than a case the thesis should simply bite the bullet on?

What would a committed defender of the revisability thesis have to concede as counting against it, as opposed to what its critics would say? I am especially interested in conceded defeaters, since my friend's best line was that I have redescribed autonomy, renamed it free will, and then immunised it by declaring every non-reviser coerced or pathological.

Does the thesis collapse into a rival — a theory of personal autonomy, a reactive-attitudes or expressivist account of moral responsibility, or a compatibilist account of sourcehood with extra steps? If it does, is the label free will doing any work, and does the thesis quietly become a claim about what we ought to want rather than about what we have?

Is it falsifiable at all, or has it been qualified into emptiness? If every apparent failure of revisability can be written off as covert coercion, weakness of will, or pathology, then what observation or case would distinguish a world in which the thesis is true from one in which it is false? Say honestly whether the insulation is real, and whether for a thesis of this kind it is a problem.

What does the thesis predict that leeway and sourcehood views do not — about, say, which interventions people would experience as destroying their freedom, or about what agents report missing when they take themselves to have lost it? I would personally be moved most by a case in which leeway and sourcehood are both present and revisability absent, yet we still judge the agent free, or the converse.

Please argue in your own terms; I do not want a literature survey, reading suggestions, or citations, and I would rather you did not lean on named historical positions except where a single familiar label does real work.
