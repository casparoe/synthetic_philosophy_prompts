You are a senior moral philosopher who has sat through fifty-odd job talks, has published both on Kant's ethics and on collective action problems, and is good at predicting rooms. Before you write anything, think the situation through: work out what each faction in this room already believes and what each one is actually trying to protect, and only then produce the prep sheet.

The setting: next month I give a job talk in a philosophy department. My paper defends three linked claims about the Formula of Universal Law. First, contradiction in conception should be read as a fixed-point failure rather than as a logical contradiction: a maxim fails when the success conditions of the action it prescribes presuppose a pattern of other people's conduct that universal adoption of the maxim would destroy. The false promise fails not because universal promising is incoherent but because a promise given in hard times works only against a background in which promises are normally kept. Second, this reading exposes what I call the aggregation gap. Take the maxim: when my own contribution makes no perceptible difference to whether some collective good is produced, I may omit it. Universal omission destroys the good while no individual omission does, so a maxim-by-maxim test never reaches it; at best the maxim generates a contradiction in the will, that is, a merely imperfect duty. Third, the obvious repair, universalizing over the distribution of maxims a population actually holds by asking what would happen if everyone in relevantly similar circumstances acted this way, turns the test into rule-consequentialism in disguise and loses the first-personal deliberative character that makes it a test of an agent's maxims rather than an evaluation of states of affairs. I want to keep that first-personal character and live with the gap.

The room: two Kant textualists who work on the Groundwork and the Doctrine of Virtue, read Kant in German, care about the distinction between contradiction in conception and contradiction in will, about the role of the four examples, and about whether the test is a philosopher's device or something an agent can actually use; one analytically trained ethicist sympathetic to rule-consequentialism who regards the whole apparatus as a crude ancestor of something better; one political philosopher who works on structural injustice and collective action, likes the aggregation gap, and will draw a very different moral from it; and two generalists.

Deliver a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of my view: the eight or so objections I will actually get, each labeled with who raises it and phrased the way that person would phrase it, ordered by how likely and how damaging each is, with a reply I can give in about two minutes and one sentence on where that reply is soft.
