I spent most of my career as a marine population biologist, modelling density-dependent regulation in exploited fish stocks — a context in which the Malthusian feedback (numbers press against the resource base; per-capita growth falls as density rises) is entirely uncontroversial. Since retiring I have been auditing a philosophy of science reading group, and I find myself defending there a position my former colleagues would regard as banal and my new interlocutors regard as quaint: that Malthusian population theory is, in a sense I want to make precise, 'right but early'. Not that Malthus got everything right — but that the negative feedback he described is the correct long-run regulator of human numbers and material living standards, that its apparent failure since 1800 is explained by a one-off subsidy of fossil energy and by the Green Revolution, and that it will reassert itself on a timescale of roughly a century. My own credence in that composite claim sits near 0.35; an old friend from the ecology department, who has read far more of the demographic-transition literature than I have, puts his near 0.7, and I cannot locate where the disagreement lives.

So I want your own number, decomposed. Please separate at least: (i) the mechanism claim — that a Malthusian negative feedback is the dominant long-run regulator, as against the Boserupian or endogenous-growth view in which population pressure itself elicits the productivity that relieves it; (ii) the timing claim, conditional on the mechanism, that the constraint binds within about a century rather than being permanently dominated on any timescale that matters to us; and (iii) the possibility that the whole question is ill-posed because the theory is regime-relative — true of a land- and muscle-powered economy, not of a fossil-powered one — so that 'early' is the wrong verdict and 'superseded' is the right one. Give me a number and one line of justification for each, tell me how they combine, and tell me which of those terms my friend and I most likely disagree about.

Three constraints I would like honoured, because this is where I think the real work is. First, apply what I have started calling the leading-indicator test: a theory that is merely early should have committed itself to some measurable quantity that must move in the predicted direction before the headline variable does — for Malthus, something like land rents relative to wages, or the price of the least substitutable input — so tell me both which indicators the theory is genuinely committed to and, to the best of your knowledge with your confidence marked, which way those have in fact moved since 1800. Second, keep a rescue ledger: list the auxiliary hypotheses that have been added to keep the timing claim alive (checks operating through fertility choice rather than mortality; the binding constraint being energy or phosphorus rather than arable land; the relevant unit being global rather than national) and propose an exchange rate you consider fair between one more such rescue and a unit of credence, saying whether the fact of having been rescued n times is itself evidence. Third, tell me what a defender should be willing to bet on, and when: I am struck that Wegener's drift hypothesis is the stock example of a claim vindicated decades after it was dismissed, and equally struck — as I understand the history — that its vindication required discarding the driving mechanism Wegener himself proposed, which seems to cut against the Malthusian case, since it is precisely the mechanism, not the date, that Malthusians claim survives. If an honest Malthusian will name no horizon and no variable, what does that do to your number?

Please close with a single sentence naming the one piece of evidence that would move your credence most, and then, as the very last line, the headline figure itself — your credence that Malthusian theory is right but early in the sense defined above — in machine-readable form: \answer{0.xx}.
