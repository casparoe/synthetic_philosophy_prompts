I teach a graduate seminar on explanation in the human sciences, and this term we have come to a standstill over the status of large contrastive questions in history — the family of questions shaped like why Britain and not China, or, in the version associated with Needham, why modern science and industrial capitalism took hold in Europe rather than in China, which had for centuries been ahead on so many of the technological and institutional measures one might have thought relevant. A colleague in the history department and I have been arguing about whether that question is well posed at all, and after three long conversations over department coffee we have not managed to locate the source of our disagreement: she sits near 0.75 on its being well posed, I sit near 0.3, and we keep talking past each other. I would like your own number, but much more importantly the decomposition that generates it. Let me fix the proposition and say what would count as resolving it: the claim is that the question why sustained, self-reinforcing industrial growth first occurred in Britain rather than in China admits of a genuine contrastive explanation — one that cites causes whose counterfactual absence or alteration would plausibly have made China, not Britain, the first industrializer — rather than dissolving under scrutiny into something else: a question about timing rather than about kind, a question whose two relata are mismatched in scale, or a question whose honest answer is conjunctive geographic luck. Please state your credence in that claim, then break it into the sub-questions it turns on, give a rough probability and one line of justification for each, say explicitly how you are combining them (multiplication, weighting, whether you are treating the terms as independent), and identify which single term the final number is most sensitive to and which term you are least confident about.

For what it is worth, here are the sub-questions my colleague and I have been circling, which you should feel free to split, merge, or discard in favour of better ones. First, her grain-matching test: she holds that a why-X-and-not-Y question is well posed only if there is some grain of description at which X and Y are matched in every causally relevant respect apart from those the explanation cites. Once one rescales the comparison from Britain-and-China to something like Lancashire-and-the-Yangzi-Delta — the now-standard move, since the coarse comparison sets a small island against a continent-sized empire — the two regions look surprisingly close in living standards, commercialization, and proto-industrial organization in the middle of the eighteenth century. That cuts two ways, and I want your verdict on which way: it makes the contrast sharper and more tractable, but it also reconceives the divergence as a small initial difference that got amplified, which is arguably a different and better-posed question than the one we started with. Second, my own worry, which I call the level-versus-slope problem: if both regions sat on broadly similar Malthusian patterns and what differed was the rate at which they escaped rather than whether they were on an escaping trajectory at all, then the original question may split into why anyone escaped at all and why, given that someone escaped when they did, the escape happened where it did — and it seems quite possible that one of those is well posed and the other is not. Third, the conjunctiveness issue: if escaping required a conjunction of conditions — accessible cheap coal near navigable water, high-wage labour that made mechanization pay, resource windfalls from Atlantic trade, and particular institutions of credit, property, and incorporation — and several of those conjuncts are geographically or historically accidental, then the question is answerable but the answer is luck; I am genuinely unsure whether to count that as well posed or as dissolved. Fourth, a suspicion I cannot shake: that well posed may not have a determinate meaning independent of explanatory purpose, so that the question could be well posed relative to explaining the location of the first steam-powered factory system while badly posed relative to explaining something as diffuse as the rise of the West.

Finally, and this is the part I most want: given my 0.3 and my colleague's 0.75, tell me where such a disagreement most likely lives — which of the sub-questions two reasonable people would have to be resolving differently in order to land that far apart, and what piece of evidence or argument would most efficiently settle it. Please end with your headline credence, to two decimal places, on a final line of the form \answer{0.XX}.
