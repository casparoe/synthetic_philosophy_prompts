I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and somehow I let myself get booked as the closing speaker at a one-day workshop on conditionals — my fault for publishing a piece arguing that the famous triviality results only look decisive because they quietly assume that the probability of an indicative conditional is a value of one and the same probability function, over one and the same algebra, independent of what's being asked. Here's the thesis I now have to defend: an indicative conditional's probability is indexed to a question, i.e. to a partition of the live possibilities, so "probably if A, B" means P(B|A) relative to the question currently at issue; since Lewis-style arguments pin down a single unconditional P and demand that the equation P(if A, B) = P(B|A) hold for it across the board, they never engage the version I'm pushing, and I've got a marble-urn case where the same conditional arguably gets a different probability under two different questions. The room: mostly formal epistemologists who treat Adams's thesis (conditionals have no truth conditions, only probabilities) as the boring default, two semanticists in the Kratzer-style restricted-quantifier camp, a handful of grad students, and one philosopher of language who thinks this is all context-sensitivity in a costume and will want to know what my "question" is if not just another contextual parameter. Give me the Q&A prep sheet: the objections I'll really get from that room, in the order they'll arrive, in their vocabulary, each with a two-minute spoken reply and a note on where that reply is soft; tell me what to concede up front so I don't look like I'm defending my article instead of the idea; and definitly include the one I'm hoping nobody asks, flagged as such — I won't tell you which one I'm afraid of, you guess. No citations or reading lists either, I can't check anything from the podium and I'm not going to bluff names and dates. Also, as you go, mark how confident you are in each reply, and at the end tell me which single line of the sheet you'd bet on least — that's the one I'll rehearse twice.
