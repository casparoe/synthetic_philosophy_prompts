I've been going round in circles about spoiler warnings and I cant work out what's actually going on with them.

The norm is really strict. If you tell someone the twist in a film before they've seen it, that gets treated as genuinely harming them — people get properly angry, subbreddits ban it, you're just a jerk. But nobody can say what the harm is supposed to be. You still watch the thing, you still see all the same scenes, and if it's any good you'll probably still be moved by it. My girlfriend reads spoilers on purpose before watching anything and says she enjoys films more that way, which seems to be true for quite a lot of people. So it's not that the information just destroys the film.

What's bugging me is how this sits next to everything else we do with art. I pick what to watch off scores and reviews constantly and nobody thinks that's cheating or weird — you're taking a stranger's word for whether the thing is good. But if that same stranger tells me what happens in it, I'm furious. And people will tell you a film is beautiful or funny or devastating and you take that on board and go in expecting to feel it, which is also taking their word for something. Yet we still insist you have to see it yourself to really know. Those feel like they're in tension and I want to know how bad the tension is.

Also strange: sports fans do exactly the same thing with scores, don't tell me who won, I'm watching it tonight. But nobody warns you about spoiling a documentary, or a history book, or a museum audio guide that explains what the painting is about while you're standing in front of it. And nobody thinks you can find out that a film is good from being told, the way you can find out that a bridge is safe from an engineer telling you.

Two more things that don't fit: rewatching is fine, even though you know every twist, so simply knowing can't be the problem. And part of me thinks the real complaint is just that the director gets to decide what order you find things out in, but that sounds like a different complaint than the one people make.

So what does the anti-spoiler thing presuppose about what you get from experiencing something yourself versus being told about it? Is it one idea doing all the work or several that could come apart? Which parts of the norm actually need that idea and which would survive without it? And if more than one story fits the facts, say so, and say what would settle which one's right.
