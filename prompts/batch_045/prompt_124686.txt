I am giving a forty-minute paper, followed by an hour of questions, to the technical panel revising our national cost–benefit guidance for long-horizon projects: flood defences, nuclear waste storage, climate adaptation. The panel is five or six applied public economists who use the Ramsey rule daily and currently apply a small positive pure rate of time preference with a declining schedule over the very long run; two financial economists who think the right rate is the government's real borrowing cost or the shadow price of capital; one climate scientist; and one environmental economist who has publicly argued for a rate well below what the guidance permits. They care, in roughly this order, about defensibility to auditors and courts, about not handing the finance ministry numbers that look absurd, and about intergenerational fairness. They are not hostile, but they have very little patience for anything that cannot be turned into a number in a spreadsheet.

My thesis, in brief. The pure rate of time preference should be set to zero: once expected growth in consumption has been counted separately through the ηg term, discounting a benefit merely because it falls later has no defensible ethical basis, and the common practice of reading a positive rate off market behaviour or off revealed impatience confuses a prediction with a prescription. What a positive rate does in practice should be done by two other terms: expected consumption growth, and the probability that there is anyone alive and able to benefit at the relevant date. My one novel claim is that the second term cannot be applied the way the field applies it, as a single unconditional survival probability multiplied into expected benefits. Hazards and payoff size are correlated — the warming a project averts may itself raise the hazard, while the prosperity that raises future benefit plausibly lowers it — so the unconditional shortcut is biased in a direction fixed by the sign of that correlation, and appraisal should discount within survival states rather than after taking expectations.

Please draft my preparation sheet. Give me the seven or eight objections I will actually get from this panel, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each phrased in the vocabulary these economists would use rather than in mine, each with a reply I could deliver in about ninety seconds and a note on where that reply is softest. Tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes, and which single reply has to land if the proposal is to survive the meeting. Include the question I am privately hoping nobody asks. One last requirement: mark the epistemic status of what you give me. Separate what is settled among people who actually work on social discounting from what is live controversy, and flag any place where my argument rests on a claim most specialists would reject, or on a quantity nobody has seriously estimated.
