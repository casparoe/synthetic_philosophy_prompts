Hi — I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background beyond arguing with people on the internet, and I work on a team that ships a coding assistant. Over about two years we've grown this house style for talking about what the model can and can't do, and I genuinely can't tell whether it's a coherent view that we arrived at by accident or just office politics that hardened into rules nobody re-examines.

Roughly how it works:

1. In anything customer-facing we're not allowed to say the model 'knows', 'believes', 'thinks' or 'means' anything. Legal made us rewrite everything as 'the assistant generates' / 'the model may suggest'. Internally basically nobody obeys this. People say 'it thinks that endpoint is deprecated' or 'it doesn't know our SDK changed last month' all the time, and that shorthand drives real decisions — we add a guardrail, we change what we stuff into the context window, we write a doc.

2. We have tiers. 'Outputs' is free, no evidence needed. 'Can do X' requires that *some* prompt somewhere produces the behavior reliably — prompt search is allowed, few-shot examples are allowed, pasting our style guide into the context is allowed. And if nobody can find a prompt that works, we say the model can't do X, not 'we haven't found the prompt yet'.

3. 'Knows X' is much stricter: correct across paraphrases, across formats (multiple choice vs. free response vs. 'finish this function'), and after we tamper with the question — reorder the options, swap a variable name, insert an irrelevant sentence. A single lucky right answer counts for nothing.

4. Nobody writes 'understands' down, but in meetings it gets used when the behavior is robust AND the model can produce an explanation of it that a reviewer finds coherent.

Three things bug me:

- We've caught the model confabulating its reasons. We'll inject a wrong suggestion into its own earlier output — a bad hint in the conversation, basically — and later it explains its answer by citing that hint, or produces a totally clean-sounding derivation that never mentions the thing that actually drove it. We even wrote an internal doc about this. And we *still* count the explanation as evidence of understanding so long as the final answer is right. Isn't that flatly inconsistent with (3), where we demand robustness precisely because we don't want to be fooled by surface-level performance?

- We treat a capability as less impressive if we got it by fine-tuning on reasoning traces from a bigger model than if it showed up on its own. Same behavior, same robustness, different provenance, and people say 'that's just imitating the traces'. Why would provenance matter to anyone whose tests only look at behavior?

- Separately, in the threads where the 'stochastic parrots' jab gets thrown around, some people claim none of this can ever amount to understanding because the model only ever saw text, so its words aren't hooked up to anything. I half-remember a thought experiment about two things talking to each other over a telegraph line with nothing else in the world. Our whole testing setup seems designed to ignore that objection rather than answer it.

So: what theory of understanding (or of meaning, or of knowledge) does our house style actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view, and tell me which rival views it quietly rules out. Is it internally consistent, especially the explanation thing and the provenance thing, or are we smuggling two or three different theories in one vocabulary? Be clear about which parts of the practice strictly require a given view and which parts several competing theories would license equally. And what would have to change in how we test and how we're allowed to talk if we took the text-only objection seriously?
