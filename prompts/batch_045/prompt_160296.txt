Me and my flatmate got into Hofstadter's typogenetics stuff the other week — the toy formal genetics where strands of letters get operated on by enzymes, and the enzymes come from translating strands, so the thing doing the operating is made out of the same kind of stuff as the thing operated on — and we got stuck on the DNA–Gödel isomorphism bit. If I've mangled how typogenetics actually works, just say so and go with the version I've described; don't go hunting for anything, that's not what I'm after. The claim, as I take it, is that DNA is a bit like a Gödel number: a passive-looking string which, once it's fed through a decoder, turns out to specify the very machinery that decodes and copies it. He says that's the deep point and it's what separates real self-replication from mere copying. I said it sounds suspiciously like a quine with extra steps.

So settle it by rating things. Here are eight systems. For each, give me two scores out of 10:

(i) how much of a genuine case of self-replication it is, in the sense that matters here — no sneaking in machinery, energy sources or pre-made parts from outside beyond what's fair;
(ii) how much of the DNA–Gödel isomorphism it actually exhibits.

One sentence of justification per score, plus a short paragraph at the top saying what criterion you're using for (ii), because I think that's where we're actually disagreeing rather than about the cases.

1. A quine — a program that prints its own source code and does nothing else.
2. A self-hosting compiler: a compiler for language L, written in L, which you can point at its own source.
3. Von Neumann-style self-reproduction: a universal constructor plus a tape carrying the constructor's own description, where the constructor also copies the tape and passes the copy on to the offspring.
4. A virus in a host cell: the genome codes for its capsid and for the polymerase that copies it, but the ribosomes and tRNAs that do the translating are the host's.
5. One I made up: a droplet containing a strand of DNA plus small molecules, where the strand codes for every bit of machinery that reads and expresses it — tRNAs, polymerases, the lot — but the strand's own copying is done by plain template chemistry that nothing in the droplet codes for. So the decoders are self-made, but the tape isn't self-copied as far as the code is concerned.
6. A stripped-down synthetic cell: as in 5, except that the polymerase which copies the genome is itself coded for by that genome.
7. A pair of RNA enzymes, each of which catalyses the assembly of the other from short fragments supplied from outside; it replicates, and it can even evolve, but there's no code and no decoding anywhere in it.
8. A RepRap-style 3D printer printing out most of its own plastic parts, with the motors and chips still coming from a shop.

If any of the eight is incoherent as I've described it, or if you think two of them collapse into the same case, say that before scoring rather than playing along.

And the bonus, which is the bit we actually fell out over: I think there are two different things one might mean by the isomorphism — (a) the code encodes the very machinery that reads it, and (b) the products of decoding come back around and act on the code. Those feel identical until you look at the list. Tell me which of the eight, if any, satisfies one but not the other, and what that shows about whether Hofstadter's isomorphism is one idea or two. Then rank all eight by (ii) as well as scoring them — the ordering is much easier to argue with than the numbers.
