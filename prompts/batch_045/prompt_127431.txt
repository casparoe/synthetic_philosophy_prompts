Write a satirical internal memo of roughly 600 words, in the voice of the Head of Tragedy Programming at a large streaming platform, announcing a new slate of features together with the cohort data and A/B results that justified them. The voice should be flatly corporate — engagement metrics, churn, retention curves, one quoted in-house "affect consultant" — and the satire should land on the main philosophical answers to the paradox of tragedy rather than on streaming culture in general.

The features should each be a recognizably faithful, exaggerated working-out of one account: the Humean conversion story, on which the artistry of the presentation itself transforms the painful passion into pleasure; the catharsis or purgation picture, with its homeopathic dosing of pity and fear; the make-believe view, on which the sadness the audience feels is not quite real sadness; and the meta-response view, on which what is savored is the subject's own sympathetic response. The memo should never name a theory or a theorist, and should never announce an objection. A reader who knows the literature should be able to tell which account each feature is running on; a reader who doesn't should still find it funny.

Two invented findings should drive the piece. First, a "grief dial" that leaves the fiction and the plot wholly intact while turning down the felt unpleasantness: it works technically, but the cohorts who use it report no pleasure and cancel within the month. Second, the platform's highest-retention title is unmarked documentary footage of real suffering, which the team cannot square with anything else in the deck and has quietly buried in an appendix. Without ever being stated as such, these should expose what each account has trouble with: why the pleasure is supposed to survive when the pain is engineered out, why heavy users binge rather than being purged, why the audience needs to know the suffering is fictional and also needs to be genuinely moved by it.

Close with a note of no more than 100 words on where the piece takes liberties with these accounts or leans on a contested reading of them, and single out the one joke you are least confident is fair to the view it targets.
