I should say at the outset that I have no academic training in history or philosophy; I work in logistics and spend my evenings on a volunteer project transcribing and indexing the parish registers of one English county (baptisms, marriages and burials, roughly 1650 to 1900). Years of handling that material have pushed me into the modernist camp on nations: the view usually associated with Hobsbawm and Anderson, on which belonging to a nation — a large, bounded community of people you will never meet, who share a deep history and a claim to political self-rule — is a comparatively recent artifact, assembled over roughly the last two centuries out of state administration, mass schooling, vernacular print markets, conscription and censuses, rather than inherited from the Middle Ages or from antiquity. On this view the apparent antiquity of nations is mostly retrofitting:recent practices dressed up as immemorial ones, and a selective story told about whatever linguistic or ethnic raw material happened to be lying around.

I want to know what would count against this. I am not after authors or sources; I want the shape of my own exposure.

Three of the tests I have invented for myself, because they are the ones I could in principle read off my own material:

1. The marriage-distance test. If nationhood is a state-built artifact, then before the modern period the distance between a bride's and a groom's home parish should be organized by walking distance, market geography and confession, and should ignore entirely the lines that later become national borders. After the railways, the school and the border arrive, marriage distances should widen along the state's infrastructure, not along any older community. What I take to be the dangerous result for me: sharp discontinuities in pre-modern marriage patterns running exactly along lines that later become national borders, with no river, upland, dialect boundary or confessional divide underneath them.

2. The unprompted-label test. Registers and court records record how people were described by their neighbours when no official was asking: of this parish, sojourner, stranger, and occasional ethnic or national designations. My view predicts that national-sounding labels should be rare, elite and situational before the modern period, and common and spontaneous afterwards. What pattern here would actually sink me, and what pattern would I be entitled to explain away as mere ethnicity rather than nationhood?

3. The uptake-speed test. When a state first starts counting, schooling and conscripting a population under a new national description, how long until ordinary people apply that description to themselves without being prompted? A generational lag looks like construction; immediate, enthusiastic uptake looks more like recognition of something already there. I am unsure how much of a difference in timing my view really requires.

Beyond these, I would like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know specifically which of them a convinced modernist would concede as counting against the view, and which they would reclassify and dismiss. I also have a nagging worry that the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability: its defenders often say that of course there was older ethnic material, but that nations proper are modern. Is that a principled distinction or a retreat, and if the view turns out to be well insulated, is that a defect for a claim of this kind or a sign that it has simply won? Please also say what the view predicts that its rivals do not — about secession, about how stable very new states should be, and about what should happen to national attachment once a state stops teaching the story. The thing I would personally find most moving is the mirror image of my own case: nation-building projects that had the full machinery available and still failed to take. If such failures are common where the machinery was strong, something pre-existing is doing work I am not crediting.

Please write in American English, and close with a machine-readable line of the form \answer{...} containing exactly one of the labels EXPOSED, PARTLY EXPOSED or WELL INSULATED — meaning respectively that at least one realistic finding would decisively sink the view as I have stated it; that several findings would force substantial revision but not abandonment; or that no realistic finding could sink it, because it can absorb every qualification thrown at it. Then add a final line beginning Verdict: naming, in one sentence, the single thing you would look for first if you were sitting where I am, with the parish registers in front of you.
