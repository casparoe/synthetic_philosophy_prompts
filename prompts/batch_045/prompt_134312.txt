I'm in the final year of a computer science degree, and this term I'm taking two courses that have started to interfere with each other: an advanced course on programming-language semantics, and — as my one philosophy elective — a graduate seminar on moral responsibility. The seminar has just spent two sessions on Galen Strawson's Basic Argument, and I want to write my term paper on it. But I want to write it in the vocabulary I'm actually fluent in and see whether the argument survives the trip.

My semantics course has covered, and I am comfortable with: small-step and big-step operational semantics; deterministic versus nondeterministic and probabilistic transition relations; evaluation contexts and reduction strategies; environments and closures; quines and self-modifying programs; the treatment of recursion, including well-founded versus circular definitions, least and greatest fixed points, and coinduction; the distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic properties of terms; and higher-order software contracts with blame assignment — the setup in which a monitor wraps a function value and, on a contract violation, a blame label is assigned to whichever party supplied the offending value, with positive and negative positions determining which side is blamed.

The argument as the seminar reconstructed it, in four steps:

1. What an agent does, in any situation, follows from the way that agent is.
2. To be ultimately responsible for what you do, you would have to be ultimately responsible for the way you are — at least in certain crucial mental respects.
3. But you cannot be ultimately responsible for the way you are, in any respect at all.
4. So you cannot be ultimately responsible for what you do.

Premise 3 is defended by the self-creation point: to be ultimately responsible for the way you are, you would have to have brought it about, and to do that deliberately you would already have had to be the way you were, so the condition can never be met and the regress is not stopped by adding further steps. As I understand Strawson, the claim is meant to hold whether or not determinism is true. We've been reading it against the reactive-attitudes compatibilists, who treat responsibility as constituted by our practices of resentment, gratitude, and demands for reasons rather than by any metaphysical self-authorship.

What I want from you:

**1. A translation.** Restate the argument in the vocabulary above, as faithfully as you can, and make your mapping choices explicit wherever there is more than one option. Is the agent a term, a term-in-an-environment, a state, or the transition relation itself? Is "the way you are" the program text, the initial state, the parameters, the environment, or the history of transitions? Is "could have done otherwise" a matter of nondeterminism in the semantics, of sensitivity to input, or of something else? Where the regress is needed, say precisely which computational phenomenon you are using to model it: a reduction sequence that never reaches a normal form, a circular definition with no solution, a bootstrap problem, or a program that must already exist in order to write its own source. If a mapping choice is doing philosophical work that Strawson's premises don't earn, say so rather than letting it pass as translation.

**2. A residue report, in three parts.** (a) What translated without loss. (b) What translated only with distortion, with the distortion named. (c) What has no expression at all in that vocabulary. I care most about (c): the force of "ultimate," the intelligibility requirement, the idea that the regress is a regress of conditions on a property rather than a sequence of events, and the step from "cannot be ultimately responsible" to a claim about what we can or cannot make sense of.

**3. Two things of my own that I want tested rather than indulged.**

(i) My paper's thesis is that blame assignment in higher-order contracts gives a working, non-arbitrary notion of blame that requires nothing remotely like self-creation: blame goes to the party that supplied the offending value, it is asymmetric in a principled way, and it is entirely robust to the fact that the blamed component's behaviour was determined by its own code. If that is right, premise 2 is optional rather than analytic, and the interesting question for ethics becomes who gets to write the contract, not whether the agent wrote its own source. Press on the weak point I can already see: contract blame presupposes a specifier outside the system and a norm the parties did not choose, so importing it into ethics may merely relocate the regress from the agent to the contract's author — a designer, a community, a lawgiver. Tell me whether it relocates or dissolves, and what the translation makes visible here that the moral-philosophy framing hides.

(ii) A distinction I have been playing with: in my semantics course, a recursion that never bottoms out and a circular definition that has no solution are different failures. Strawson's regress is usually presented as an infinite regress, but what he needs may be the circularity failure rather than the non-termination failure — the demand would be unsatisfiable even with infinitely many steps, because each step presupposes what it was supposed to ground. Does the translation let us see which failure the Basic Argument is actually committed to, and does it matter? If it is circularity, is the argument then close to trivial — the claim that no system can be its own specification — and does that make it more interesting or less?

Please write in prose, around 1000 to 1300 words, with the three parts clearly separated. Two constraints: I have no library or web access while I write, so no reading lists, citations, page references, or attributions to particular papers — work only from the argument as I have stated it and from the conceptual apparatus I have named. And don't flatter the translation: if the place where the mapping breaks is the only interesting place, say so plainly, and if my contract-blame thesis is just reactive-attitude compatibilism with new labels, say that too.
