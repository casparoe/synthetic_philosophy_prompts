I am writing the final paper for a seminar on rational choice and bounded rationality, and I have become stuck on a question that I would like stated more precisely than it usually is. I would be grateful for a number, and more importantly for the reasoning behind it.

Set up the following stylized comparison. An agent faces a long sequence of choice rounds. In each round it is presented with a menu of about twenty options, whose payoffs are drawn from a heavy-tailed distribution, so that the gap between the best option and the tenth-best is often very large. Only the payoff of the chosen option is ever revealed. The agent has a scarce budget of time or computation, and it can spend that budget on finer estimates of the options' expected values.

Policy A, the optimizer: use experience from previous rounds to estimate the payoff distribution, compute expected utility for the options to some degree of precision, and take the estimated best. Policy B, the satisficer: inspect options in a cheap fixed order at low precision, take the first one that clears an aspiration level, and after each round adjust that aspiration level by a small fixed increment — upward following a success, downward following a failure. Now add two features I have been playing with: the payoff distribution is reshuffled every fifty rounds, so that whatever the optimizer has learned is partly stale, and the satisficer's aspiration level is therefore always chasing a moving target.

Here is my question. What is your credence that, over a sufficiently long run, Policy B yields a higher mean realized payoff net of deliberation costs than Policy A, in the environment just described? I do not want the answer "it depends on the parameters of the environment." I have tried to specify the parameters; if you think the specification is incomplete, say which parameter your number is most sensitive to and give me your conditional numbers.

Please decompose. I have in mind roughly these terms, but correct me if I have carved it wrongly: the probability that this environment belongs to the class where the cost of extra precision exceeds its expected payoff gain; the probability that the aspiration-level update rule is roughly well-tuned rather than unstable, either ratcheting upward until the satisficer searches almost as long as the optimizer or collapsing downward until it accepts terrible options; the probability that the satisficer has not merely relocated the optimization problem one level up, into the setting of the aspiration level; and the question of whether the comparison should be made per decision or at the level of a whole policy evaluated across regime changes, given that the optimizer amortizes its estimation costs over many rounds while the satisficer's cheapness is re-earned each round. Assign each term a rough probability with one line of justification, show how they combine, and tell me which one your final number moves most with.

For what it is worth, my own credence is about 0.6 and a classmate whose judgement I respect puts it near 0.85. I suspect we are disagreeing about something other than the headline number, and I would like to know where you think the real disagreement lives. Finally, tell me which of your terms you are least confident in, and what single empirical finding would most change it.
