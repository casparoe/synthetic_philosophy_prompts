I'm closing the final session of a graduate seminar on social norms. The day's readings were on conventions as solutions to recurrent coordination problems, and on what is supposed to become of that picture once the regularity is taken up as a norm that people feel bound by. I'd like to end by reading a poem aloud instead of summarizing the discussion.

Write me a villanelle of the usual nineteen lines, with one deliberate departure from the form. Of the two refrains, one returns word for word every time; the other returns with a single word changed at each appearance, so that across the poem the sentence it makes slides from something like "it was simply the obvious thing" to something like "it is what we owe each other." The departure is the poem's thesis: the behavior is invariant, and what changes is the thing holding it in place.

Choose one concrete recurrent situation — two people first, then a crowd, then people who arrive after the beginning — and stay with it throughout; I'd rather it not be the business of driving on one side of the road. Use the fixed refrain for the behavior itself and the changing one for the reason its participants would give if asked, and let the successive reasons be recognizable: first a feature of the situation that makes one option stand out while there is no precedent at all, then the fact that it is what was done last time and what everyone expects everyone else to do, then — somewhere in the middle — the moment when a bystander with no stake in the coordination still reacts to a deviation, and finally the stage at which participants would say they comply because it is required of them. What should survive all four stages is that each is a response to what people expect of one another rather than to any property of the act itself. Someone who has read the Lewis-style account of convention should be able to tell which stage is which, though the poem should never use the words coordination, equilibrium, common knowledge, salience, or sanction; someone who hasn't should simply hear a poem that works when read aloud once.

After the poem, add a single paragraph of continuous prose — no headings, bullets, bold, or tables anywhere in your reply — committing to a definite answer on the question the poem leaves open: whether the final stage is still the same kind of thing as the first, or whether internalization adds something that takes the regularity out of that category altogether. Pick a side and give your reason, and say where the poem leans on a reading that someone in the seminar could reasonably reject.
