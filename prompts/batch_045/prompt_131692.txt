I teach The Merchant of Venice in a first-year seminar on ethics and economic life, and next term I want to set it beside Aristotle on money and exchange: the account in Nicomachean Ethics book 5 of money as a conventional measure that renders goods commensurable and makes exchange symmetrical, the distinction in Politics book 1 between acquisition for the household, which has a natural limit in sufficiency, and the art of getting wealth, which has none, and the argument that lending at interest is the most unnatural branch of that art, because money exists to be spent and is instead made to breed more money. My difficulty is that the obvious mapping falls apart on inspection: Venice as unlimited acquisition and Belmont as the household, Shylock as the man who breeds metal and Antonio as the friend who lends gratis, except that Antonio's own fortune rides on speculative ventures at sea and he is as much a practitioner of unlimited acquisition as anyone in the play. My students will arrive already convinced that Shylock is the only honest economic actor and the Christians are gifted idlers, and I want to know whether Aristotle gives me anything better than a set of labels to attach to the characters.

Here are three readings I have been toying with, which I would like tested rather than confirmed. First, that the bond's pound of flesh is a dramatized reductio of commensuration rather than a vivid instance of it: the play shows what it costs to make a living body answer to a sum of money, and no conventional measure can render that exchange symmetrical, so the trial exposes a violence internal to the very operation Aristotle praises. Second, that Portia's speech on mercy is not an escape from justice into something higher but an exercise of equity correcting the written law, a move Aristotle himself has a place for. Third, that the ring plot, in which the women reclaim the rings and the men are made to swear all over again, is about the spoiling of a conventional token, so that oaths function in the play the way coinage does in Aristotle: their value is fixed by agreement and undone by overuse. I am least confident of the third.

What I want is a reading that is faithful in both directions and does not flatten the play into an illustration of the Politics, and does not stretch Aristotle until he fits the script. Please respond as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: (1) whether the Venice–Belmont and Antonio–Shylock mappings survive contact with the text, and where they fail; (2) your verdict on the pound of flesh as a reductio of commensuration; (3) whether mercy here is best understood as equity, and what the play gains or loses on that reading; (4) whether the rings and the repeated oaths really are a dramatized theory of conventional value, or whether I am overreading; (5) what, if anything, the play shows about money and exchange that Aristotle's treatises cannot say, and which scene or exchange you would put in front of students to make that point.
