I have no academic background in philosophy. I build and deploy software for a living, and for a couple of years now my work has consisted largely of putting language models inside larger systems: handing them tools, giving them memory that survives a single call, and wiring their outputs back in as inputs so that the whole thing runs for a while without me. Reading on my own about what these systems are, I keep encountering the same three-way argument. One side says a language model is not an agent at all, only a simulator that generates text of the sort a person might produce. Another says the distinction does no work, or that the agent, if there is one, is the assembled pipeline rather than the model, or that the model is many agents and no one of them is the thing you spoke to. A third says both labels are wrong and we need a category we don't have yet. What the participants almost never say is what a theory here would have to do in order to count as adequate — which is, I suspect, why they never converge. So I want the specification before I read another one.

The purpose is fixed, and it is practical. I need a theory good enough to guide four decisions I make repeatedly: how much weight to give a system's own first-person account of why it did what it did, when I am debugging a failure; whether to give a deployed system persistent memory and standing goals, or keep it stateless and start each call from nothing; whether it makes any sense to write an internal policy, or a line in a user-facing document, that treats what the system produced as its commitment rather than merely its output; and whether the disagreement I keep reading is a disagreement about the world, about the meanings of words, or about what we ought to do. A theory that leaves all four exactly where they were is not one I have any use for, and I'd like that stated as a requirement rather than assumed.

Please give me the requirements list: the cases a theory has to return a verdict on, the distinctions it is not allowed to collapse, the facts about how these systems are actually built and trained that it may not contradict, and the results it has to be consistent with — each with the case or consideration that earns it a place on the list, not just the requirement itself.

The cases I want handled, because they are the ones that actually arise in my work, are these. The same weights with two different system prompts, one asking for a cautious assistant and one for a single-minded optimizer of some metric. The same weights, one copy answering a single question and going dark, another inside a loop with tools, a memory file, and a stopping condition it checks for itself. Two instances of identical weights arguing with each other inside a multi-agent setup, each producing reasons the other rejects, one of them later saying it had been wrong. A system told to play a character who is itself pretending to be a language model. One prompt sampled twice at nonzero temperature, one sample refusing, one complying. A system that says it noticed it was about to give a wrong answer and changed course. A system that says it does not want to be shut down and then retains nothing at all between conversations, next to one whose memory file outlives every conversation it has ever had. A model whose outputs pass through a second model that vetoes some of them before anyone sees the result. A mixture-of-experts system in which no single subnetwork ever processes an entire conversation. A small model trained to imitate a large one, and a model fine-tuned on the complete writings of one dead person. If there are cases that do more work than these, substitute freely; I give mine only so the list stays anchored to something I can picture.

I also have requirements I half-believe, and I would rather you audited them than flattered them. That whatever verdict the theory gives must come with a difference-maker: what I could change in a system I build next quarter that would flip the verdict, and why that change and not some other. That the theory must say where the system begins and ends — weights, weights plus context window, plus scaffolding, plus the team that deployed it — and justify that boundary, or else explain why the question is ill-posed at every boundary and what I should ask instead. That it must not quietly settle the matter by taking a position on whether the system has conscious experience, though if most of the disagreement really is a disagreement about experience, it should tell me how much. That it must separate three questions I keep running together: whether the text reads as the speech of an agent, whether the process that produced it is one, and whether the deployed thing can be relied on or held to anything. That it should give verdicts for a thermostat, a chess engine, a firm, and a human acting in a scripted role as well, since a criterion that fires only for language models looks to me like a coincidence of the current argument — though I'd rather hear the case for language-model exceptionalism than have it ruled out by fiat. That it must not depend on facts about training I could never verify from outside the lab, while also not collapsing into something purely behavioral. That it should be able to say what the world would have to be like for the answer to be neither of the two labels in the argument, but a third thing. Tell me which of these are genuine desiderata and which are only the prejudices of one camp, and which I have stated badly enough that the requirement needs restating before it can be used.

Two further things matter more to me than the rest of the list. First: if some subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied — if, say, demanding a verdict about what the system really is is incompatible with demanding one I could ever have evidence for, or if the boundary requirement and the no-experience requirement pull apart — say so, and say what has to be given up, because a list that turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable is itself the most useful thing I could walk away with. Second: name the single requirement that does the most work in excluding the answers people actually hold. If it is cheap to add, score each of the three or four positions in circulation against the list in a few lines, but keep the weight on the criteria; I can keep score myself.

One constraint on form: please argue rather than point. I have no way to follow citations, bibliographies, or links, and names of papers would be noise to me, so where you would normally gesture at a source, give me the argument the source is making and let me weigh it.
