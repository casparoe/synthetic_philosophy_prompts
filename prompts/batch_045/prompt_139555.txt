I sat up far too late last night arguing with a friend about whether physics ever really tells us what the world is made of, and this morning I have to draft the recommendation our panel votes on Friday. I'm one of seven members of the research council's Foundations panel. The item before us is an eight-year programme, "Structural Continuity in Quantum Gravity" — four nodes, twelve postdocs, and no new empirical prediction promised at any point in the period. Its case is that across every major theory change the central entities were abandoned while the mathematics reappeared as a limiting case of whatever replaced them, so what pays off is not a picture of the furniture of the world but an account of which structural commitments are likely to survive, and the programme deliberately refuses to commit itself on whether spacetime, fields or particles are real.

Our charter requires funded programmes to be empirically anchored: applicants must be able to say what observation could in principle disconfirm their central claims. Their answer is that a successor theory retaining none of the structures they flag would refute them. Three of us think that is evasion wearing the vocabulary of realism; three think it is simply how this kind of work has always proceeded. The same money would otherwise go to a fully costed tabletop experiment testing whether a gravitational field can be placed in a superposition, which we have already rated fundable.

My friend's objection is the one I keep returning to, and I want it answered rather than defused: "structure survives, ontology doesn't" is close to hollow unless you say which relations are doing the work and what the structure is a structure of — almost any formal structure is realized by some collection of objects or other, so the claim threatens to be trivially true, and any attempt to thicken it (the relations must be natural, causal, dynamically basic) quietly reinstates the ontological commitment you said you were suspending.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate. Recommend one of: fund as proposed, reject, fund with conditions. Sections, in this order: Recommendation (about 100 words), Why the discarded-ontology argument overreaches (about 250), The strongest case for the option you reject (about 200), Conditions (about 200).
