Longtime LW lurker, first time posting. A guy at my local meetup keeps defusing embedded agency with the software/hardware picture: the agent is the program, the world is the machine it runs on, sensors and actuators are the API, so (he says) the objective is just whatever's written in the source and the world cant reach it. He uses that to argue that boxing, corrigibility patches, "just write the right utility function" — all of it is a normal engineering program, and the 2019 arXiv "Embedded Agency" survey is philosophers inventing a problem nobody actually has.

I tried to push on it with a concrete case. Call it the mirror-channel setup: you build the agent so that its sensor stream includes a live readout of its own memory, and that readout carries more bits per second than the agent has internal state. Now draw the agent/environment boundary. My sense is that where you draw it stops being a fact about the machine and turns into a fact about your bookkeeping, and that the software/hardware picture is exactly what's hiding that.

Can you audit the analogy? I want the correspondence table (program, machine, API, registers, crash → ?), which rows still hold in the mirror-channel case and which stop making sense, and what the picture quietly imports that the target doesn't have: a boundary somebody already drew for you, code that can't also be data, a substrate the program can't tamper with. Then the payoff — does the conclusion he wants (the real objective is the one in the source, and it's insulated from the world) survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? Suggest a better analogy if there's one. And say what would change your mind about the audit itself, and where its reasoning is most likely to fail. American English please, and no bibliography — I want the argument, not links.
