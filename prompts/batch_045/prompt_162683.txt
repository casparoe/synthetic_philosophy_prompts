I sit on a retrospective review panel that rates long-run forecasts in demography, resource economics and agricultural economics. When a forecast misses its headline date, we have to return one of three verdicts: "Falsified", "Right but early" (the mechanism survives and only the timing was wrong), or "On time" (no miss, nothing to adjudicate). We have issued four verdicts that I would like you to treat as a track record, and we now have a fifth forecast to rule on.

Our four earlier verdicts were these.

1. A late-eighteenth-century argument that human numbers, left unchecked, increase faster than the means of subsistence, and that a country cultivating a fixed area with a given technique will therefore be pushed back towards subsistence, with famine or disease as the check. The argument implied that this would bite in its home country within a generation or two. It did not: real incomes rose over the following century and the check never arrived in the predicted form. Our verdict was "Right but early". The pressure of numbers on a finite cultivated base was, we held, a real mechanism; the date slipped because two things the argument had taken as given changed — new grain lands were brought into cultivation abroad, and yields per acre began a sustained rise from techniques the argument did not anticipate.

2. A 1960s forecast that world famine would kill hundreds of millions within the following two decades, on the ground that population was outrunning the world's agricultural carrying capacity. The famines that did occur in that window were local and political rather than global and Malthusian, and world calorie production per person rose. Our verdict was "Falsified". We held that this was not a mere error about the clock: the forecast's structural claim was that the race between mouths and food had already been lost, whereas the data showed the food side of the race was in fact pulling ahead. The variable that saved the day was not an outside shock; it was one of the two runners.

3. A mid-twentieth-century forecast that a single country's extraction of a non-renewable resource would peak in a specified year and decline thereafter. Production peaked within about a year of the predicted date. Our verdict was "On time".

4. An early-2000s forecast that world extraction of that same resource would peak around the end of that decade. It did not. A drilling technology, developed for unrelated purposes, unlocked reserves that had been counted as unavailable, and production rose again. The forecasters kept the core mechanism — that the cheap, easily reached stocks are produced first and get progressively harder to replace — and moved the date. Our verdict was "Right but early". The technology came from outside the depletion process the forecast described, and the core comparative claim was untouched.

The fifth case is this. In the late 1990s a team published a Malthusian forecast for a semi-arid farming region with fast population growth and a land-and-water base projected to shrink through degradation. Their model contained a term for population, a term for the effective land and water available per person, and — this is the important part — a term for yields that was itself modelled as a function of labour per hectare and of capital per hectare, where capital means irrigation equipment, fertilizer and improved seed. They did not hold yields constant as a background condition; they tried to predict them. Their structural claim was conditional: at constant capital per hectare, shrinking land per person must eventually reduce calories per person. Their headline prediction was that the decline would begin by 2020. The date has passed and per-capita calories have risen steadily. The reason is that capital per hectare rose far faster than they projected. The structural claim has since been checked directly and holds: in the districts where capital per hectare stayed flat, calories per person did fall as land per person shrank. The team now says the miss was a rate error in one auxiliary projection — capital deepening — and that the mechanism is intact. Critics say the model claimed to govern exactly the variable that moved and got it wrong.

What I would like from you:

(a) Set out the two or three rules about falsification and rescue in long-run predictions that fit all four earlier verdicts. I am fairly confident that at least two genuinely different rules fit them, and that they are not the same rule in different words. One candidate turns on whether the variable that averted the predicted outcome lay inside or outside the mechanism the forecast claimed to govern; another turns on whether the miss was a failure of the mechanism's structural or comparative claims or merely a failure of a quantitative auxiliary about timing. If there is a third rule that fits all four, give it too.

(b) Say what each of those rules predicts about the fifth case, and whether the rules diverge there. My own sense is that they do.

(c) Give your best guess at what our panel will — or, if you prefer, what it should, for consistency with its own record — decide about the fifth forecast, and say why you prefer the rule that yields that verdict over the rival.

(d) Name one further case, or one variant of the fifth case obtained by changing a single feature of the facts, that would best tell the competing rules apart, and say what each rule predicts about it. I am interested in the discriminating test, not in more examples.

Please be explicit about which of "Falsified", "Right but early" and "On time" each rule returns for the fifth case, and about where exactly the rules come apart.
