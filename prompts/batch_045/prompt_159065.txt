hi — I'm doing a philosophy of emotion module this term and I need something for our last seminar, which is meant to be a fun one.

Our tutor splits literally every question three ways: (1) the judgment / cognitivist view, where being afraid of the dog basically *is* judging (or assenting to) "this is dangerous"; (2) the perceptual view, where emotions are perceptions of evaluative properties — you see the situation as dangerous, so the emotion has correctness conditions and can hang on even after you learn better, the way a visual illusion does; and (3) the feeling view, where the emotion just is the bodily upset and everything else is scaffolding around it.

She also sprang a category on us that I can't find anywhere in the readings: "cold" or vestigial emotions. You still think the dog is dangerous, you'd still say "I'm afraid of that dog", but the felt part has completely gone — you're just standing there holding the evaluation and remembering what being scared used to feel like. We ran all three theories against it in the seminar and none of them handled it definately well, and I thought it would make good material.

Could you write me a parody IT knowledge-base article — a tech-support troubleshooting page, 600–700 words, deadpan corporate voice, no winking — titled something like "KB-4471: fear response persists after belief update", with the usual sections (Summary, Symptoms, Cause, Resolution, Known Issues, Escalation).

What I want from it:

- Each of the three theories should show up as a different tier of the support script, and each should be the real version, not a strawman. The judgment tier should insist that a customer who reports fear while also reporting "the bridge is safe" must not really believe it, and route them to belief reconciliation. The perceptual tier should conclude that the sensor has picked up a danger-property the customer's own report is missing, and advise them to get off the bridge. The feeling tier should say that if all that's left is heart rate and trembling then there is no fault — and also no object.
- Let the straight-faced logic run somewhere the reader slightly recoils: e.g. fear that persists after a successful update getting logged as a compliance issue rather than an emotion issue.
- Include a Known Issues entry for cold / vestigial emotions, where the diagnostic runs backwards (evaluation reports normal, sensation reports absent) and support genuinely cannot tell whether there is an emotion there at all, so the ticket just sits open. If you can fit it in, a second known issue where the unit fires at a target the customer knows doesn't exist — call it cinema mode — would be great.
- No footnotes or explanations of the philosophy, and nobody in the article should ever say "this is absurd". Ideally someone in my seminar who actually holds one of these views still laughs, and thinks it was fair.
