hey — I'm putting together a short piece for a local policy zine and I need a scene rather than an essay. Can you write me a dialogue? Details below, pls use them instead of inventing your own setup.

The city of Halden is voting tomorrow on something called the Continuity Grants. The rules:

- Any association in the city (a neighbourhood assembly, a congregation, a union local, a language society, a mutual aid fund) that can show 60 years of continuous self-government and an unbroken public story about what it is for gets a block grant, plus a formal consultative role on any city decision that touches it.
- The association sets its own membership, subject to a "narrative continuity" test: it has to state in one paragraph what it's for, and show it's been saying roughly that, in public, for 60 years.
- Exit for individuals is unconditional and costless — you can walk away, keep using the grant-funded services, and nobody may penalise you.
- Entry is not guaranteed. An association may refuse applicants for any reason except grounds already banned by the city's anti-discrimination code.

Characters: Mira and Dev, both on the commision that drafted the thing, both stuck in a committee room at 11pm the night before the vote while Mira types up a minority report. Mira argues against on liberal grounds, Dev argues for on communitarian-ish grounds. Both of them good at this, neither one a strawman: whenever one of them scores a hit, the other should concede the part that's actually right and then push back on what's left over, instead of just repeating their slogan louder.

Please get them into at least these weeds:

1. Mira: the city is now funding and officially certifying thick identities, i.e. ranking conceptions of the good; and the 60-year/narrative test rewards whatever is old and already articulate, so a 12-year-old Somali-Haldeni tenants' union can't qualify no matter how much good it does.
2. Dev: the liberal city already sustains one thick identity and just launders it as neutrality — the identity of the person who stands back from their ends and revises them. Schools, the exit right, the anti-discrimination code itself all train for that. The grant only admits that some identities worth keeping aren't the state's own product.
3. Mira: distinguish enforcing the *conditions* under which you can revise your ends from subsidising a *particular* end — call it the literacy/creed distinction, and have her admit it's fuzzier than it sounds, since literacy is never content-free.
4. Dev: some goods are constitutive — you only have them by being inside a practice that started before you and will outlast you. Make it concrete with a language: you can leave a language community, but you cannot take a living mother tongue with you; it dies with the community. So costless exit protects your freedom to choose and protects nothing at all about this kind of good.
5. Mira: then exit is the wrong right here. For constitutive goods what you'd want is a secession floor — if some share (say a fifth) of members want to continue the practice differently, they take a proportional slice of the grant and of the story with them. That's a liberal repair, not a veto.
6. Dev: the floor gives splinter groups money and no standing, because standing is exactly what the continuity test withholds; and it drags the city into adjudicating schisms, i.e. deciding which faction is the "real" continuation. Ask him whether the liberal state has avoided those quasi-theological questions or just privatised them and then re-administered them through the courts.
7. The reform problem: a congregation that admitted women to its governing board in the 1980s has to tell the city whether that was growth or a break. Whoever certifies continuity is certifying a story about change as either faithfulness or apostasy. Have them disagree about whether that's avoidable, and about who ends up holding that power.

Don't wrap it up neatly. They can converge on exactly one narrow thing if it comes naturally (e.g. that the anti-discrimination carve-out is where the actual fight will land), otherwise end mid-argument — but the disagreement should still be live at the end.
