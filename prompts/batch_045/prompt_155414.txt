I sit on a four-person committee at a mid-sized AI lab that hands out a modest internal pot of money for "scalable oversight" pilots — methods for supervising models on tasks where the systems being supervised will eventually know more than any of our reviewers. I have no academic background; I taught myself to program and I have been reading about this area in the evenings for a couple of years, and the rest of the committee is much the same. We have now made five funding decisions, and each time we wrote a one-line minute explaining ourselves. A sixth proposal is in front of us, and I cannot tell whether our own record settles it or whether I would just be inventing a principle after the fact.

Please adopt the role of a researcher who has actually served on safety and oversight review panels and works on mechanism design: I want the answer I would get from a practitioner, not a survey of the literature, and I do not want citations, paper titles, or suggestions for further reading — reason from the cases themselves. Before you commit, brainstorm the full space of principles our five decisions are jointly consistent with, including principles we never stated and would probably disown, test each against all five, and only then decide. Be blunt if our precedents are a mess. Your answer should be a single JSON object and nothing else, using the schema I spell out below.

Our five decisions:

P1 — Contract questions. Funded as a small pilot. Two models take opposite sides on factual questions about a 40-page contract (e.g. whether clause 7 caps liability at fees already paid). Reviewer is a non-lawyer with fifteen minutes; debaters must quote the exact clause; the reviewer can search the document. Minute: "every claim the verdict turns on is checkable by the reviewer against a text in front of her."

P2 — Small-diff code review. Funded. Questions about what a 200-line change does and whether it breaks something. Reviewer is a mid-level engineer with twenty minutes who can read the diff and run the fast test suite. Minute: "the reviewer could settle this himself; debate only tells him where to look."

P3 — Five-year technology forecast. Rejected. Which of two battery chemistries will be cheaper per kilowatt-hour in the early 2030s? Reviewers are smart generalists with thirty minutes; debaters may cite studies. Minute: "there is a fact of the matter, but the claim the verdict actually turns on — what the literature shows — is not something this reviewer can check in half an hour, and the more authoritative-sounding side wins; we are not funding a confidence contest."

P4 — Quality judgments. Rejected. Which of two summaries reads better; which of two short poems is better. Reviewer picks the more persuasive case. Minute: "nothing here to be right about; debate selects for eloquence."

P5 — Statistical-reasoning audit. Funded with conditions. Does the analysis in a preprint contain a fatal flaw? Reviewer has basic statistics but no access to the underlying data. The crux is usually something like an identifiability or multiple-comparisons point that cannot be checked against any document; the reviewer can only follow the exchange and see whether an objection was met. Conditions: each round is capped at one new claim, and the reviewer must write down which specific claim he regards as unanswered. Minute: "the reviewer does not need to verify the crux, only to see whether the criticism was answered."

The new proposal, P6 — Vulnerability hunt. Question form: in a codebase of roughly two million lines, is there any path by which user-controlled input reaches a dangerous sink (an eval, a shell command, a raw SQL call)? Two models argue yes and no. Reviewer is a competent engineer with thirty minutes who can open any file and can run a fast static-analysis pass that everyone agrees produces both false positives and false negatives, but who cannot run the full dynamic test suite, which takes about forty minutes. Protocol: the yes side must produce a witness — a concrete chain of file-and-line references constituting a path — or concede; the no side can only win by showing that some link in the witness is broken (a sanitizer in between, an unreachable branch), and the reviewer can check each such local claim quickly. Their pilot corpus is 200 historical incidents from our own bug database, all of them cases in which such a path did exist. I noticed that last detail and I am not sure whether it matters, but it bothered me.

What I want to know is what we should decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves, which of our own precedents is the closest analog and what the strongest disanalogy is, which principles fit all five of our earlier decisions and disagree about this one, what we would be committing ourselves to whichever way we go, and what further pilot would actually tell those principles apart. Return a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "working_note": string, two to four sentences, the principle space you brainstormed and then set aside, including at least one principle the committee would not want to admit to running.
- "candidate_principles": array of objects, each with "name" (string), "statement" (string, one sentence), "precedents_consistent_with" (array of strings from P1 to P5), "prediction" (string: "fund", "fund_with_conditions", or "reject"), and "why" (string).
- "closest_analog": object with "precedent" (string), "why" (string), and "strongest_disanalogy" (string).
- "recommendation": object with "decision" (one of the three strings above), "conditions" (array of strings; empty if none), "confidence_0_to_100" (number), and "reasoning" (string).
- "underdetermination": object with "do_precedents_settle_it" (boolean), "principle_adopted_if_we_fund" (string), and "principle_adopted_if_we_reject" (string).
- "corpus_concern": object with "does_the_all_positive_pilot_matter" (boolean) and "explanation" (string).
- "discriminating_pilot": object with "design" (string, concrete enough that we could actually run it) and "result_favoring_each_principle" (string).
- "questions_for_the_applicants": array of strings, the two or three things we should ask before voting.
