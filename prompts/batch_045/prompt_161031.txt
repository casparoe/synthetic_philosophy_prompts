I am the principal of a large public high school, and on Monday I have to give a seven-minute address at a mandatory all-school assembly. It will be the first thing anyone in authority has said about two weeks in which a hallway fight, a leaked group-chat screenshot, and a parent petition kept feeding one another. The district has told me I may not describe or refer directly to any of the three. The room will be: about nine hundred students; parents who have been invited and will fill the back rows; the entire faculty; two school-board members; and, in the front row, a sociologist from the state university who studies how communities handle insult and grievance and who has written on the usual three-way contrast between honor, dignity, and victimhood cultures.

Here is the problem I want the speech to solve. My families are not uniform. A substantial number come from backgrounds in which an insult left unanswered is an invitation to be insulted again, in which a wrong has to be answered by the person wronged or by that person's own people, and in which an apology delivered by a third party on someone else's behalf buys nothing. Others — most of my faculty, both board members — come from a background in which the correct response to a slight is not to notice it, in which self-respect is meant to be interior rather than something other people can hand you or take from you, and in which calling in an audience is itself the breach of etiquette. And a great many of my students, of every background, now live inside a third set of expectations: standing attaches to being seen as the one who was hurt, offenses get escalated upward and outward to authorities and to the feed instead of settled between the two people involved, and the apology that is demanded is never the apology that would be enough.

I need the speech to be genuinely prescriptive — that people in this building should stop answering slights with their hands and stop answering them with an audience — without implying to anyone present that the way they were raised is backward, childish, or cowardly. I am not asking you to settle which of these three ways of living is better; I am asking for a speech that declines to settle that in public while still telling the room to stop doing two specific things. I also want to give something real to the families in the first group. I have been turning over the idea that restraint can be a way of paying a debt to your own standing rather than a way of refusing to pay it — that the person who does not hit back because they are secure enough not to need to is not, in their own terms, doing the smaller thing. I would like that idea to carry a load-bearing part of the address if you think it can be made to carry weight with fourteen-year-olds; if you think it collapses under pressure, say so in the commentary and use something that holds.

Two hard constraints. First, the address may not use the vocabulary: no "honor culture," no "dignity," no "victimhood," no "anthropology," no "norms," none of the sociologist's words. The analysis has to be present in the structure of what is said — in what the speech asks of whom, in what it praises and what it pointedly declines to praise, in what it leaves unmentioned — so that the front row can reconstruct the whole thing while no one else in the room notices there is an analysis. Second, no hedging that the front row will read as weaseling: where the speech makes a claim about what we owe each other, it has to be a claim and not a survey of opinions.

Please write it in these sections, in this order:

**The address** — 900 to 1,100 words, written to be spoken aloud, no headings within it, no names, no reference to the specific events, addressed to the whole room at once.

**What is carrying the weight** — about 250 words: which sentences do which work for which part of the room, and what the speech deliberately leaves unsaid.

**Lines I tried and cut** — about 150 words: two or three lines that would have worked for one group and failed with another, with each failure named.

**If someone pushes back afterward** — about 100 words: one sentence I can say to a father who tells me I taught his son to take it, and one sentence I can say to a student who tells me I told her to shut up.
