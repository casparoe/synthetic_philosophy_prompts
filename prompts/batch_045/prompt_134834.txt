I spent about forty years as an experimental physical chemist, mostly on nucleation and crystal growth, and since retiring I have been sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the local university. I have just finished a well-reviewed popular book on how science works and want to know whether to inflict it on the group. Its treatment of thought experiments in science is what I want checked. I have no access to a university library and no wish to chase down references, so please argue the points rather than directing me to the literature: if the positions in this debate have standard names, you may mention one in passing, but nothing in your answer should rest on my recognising a name or a title.

Here is the gist of what the book says, as I have reconstructed it — my paraphrase rather than the book's exact wording, broken into numbered sentences so that you can refer to particular ones:

[1] A scientific thought experiment is just an argument with the apparatus left out: the premises come from a theory you already accept, the imagined experiment is the inference, and the result was already contained in what you knew.
[2] This can still be useful: such an argument can expose a contradiction between two things you believe, and it can show you what a theory commits you to in a case you had never considered.
[3] But nothing new about the world can come out of it, because nothing new goes in — no new data, hence no new empirical content.
[4] What gives thought experiments their apparent power is intuition: when you imagine Galileo's two stones or Einstein chasing a light beam, you are consulting a store of tacit experience, so the thought experiment is really a way of performing, in your head, an experiment you have in effect already done.
[5] That is also why they are only to be trusted once real experiments have confirmed them; the famous cases were confirmed, and the ones that were not have remained curiosities.
[6] And it is why they are dangerous: psychologists have shown how unreliable intuition is, so a thought experiment is a source of hypotheses, never of knowledge.

My own instinct, which I flag so that you can discount it if it is a prejudice: [4] looks like the weak point, because in my own field the intuitions such exercises consult are frequently just wrong, and the occasions on which I have genuinely learned something were occasions of calculation rather than recollection. But I also find [6] harder to swallow than [1], and I cannot tell whether that is because [6] is worse philosophy or because it flatters me. I suspect the book's conclusion is too strong without being able to locate the mistake.

Since generalities are cheap, here are three cases from my own side of the fence. Please also check my physics, and tell me if any of them is a bad test — because it begs the question, or because the answer I take for granted is not in fact agreed.

A. The unlocked paddle. A perfectly insulated cylinder holds a monatomic gas at equilibrium. A paddle inside, on frictionless bearings, is driven by an external motor until the gas is well stirred; then the motor is removed and the shaft left entirely free, so the paddle can turn only under molecular impacts. Does it ever settle into a systematic rotation, drawing work out of the gas? Every colleague I have put this to answers instantly: no, never, however cleverly the vanes are shaped; it does nothing but jiggle with its equipartition share of rotational energy. What interests me is my route to that answer. I cannot produce a derivation for an arbitrary, arbitrarily asymmetric paddle. I can only say that the second law forbids it. So either the thought experiment is an argument whose premise is the very law it is supposed to be testing — in which case it yields knowledge of the law's consequences, not of the law — or I know the answer some other way, and I should like to know what that way is meant to be. Nor have I ever seen it tested, and I cannot imagine what would count as a test of never, for any shape.

B. The pursuer. Two craft share a circular orbit around an airless planet, one a kilometre ahead. The trailing craft fires a short prograde burn to close the gap. It does not: the burn lifts it into a higher and slower orbit, and after one revolution it is further behind than it began; to catch up one must slow down and drop into a lower, faster orbit. Everything here follows from theory I have known since I was eighteen, and yet the naive answer is the other way round. Nobody has any relevant experience of orbital rendezvous.

C. A case of my own, from nucleation. Take a scrupulously filtered pure vapour, free of ions and surfaces, at a temperature and pressure where the liquid is stable but homogeneous nucleation is slow. In a one-litre vessel, classical nucleation theory gives a finite rate per unit volume and an expected waiting time for the first droplet so long that one says: never. Now delete the vessel — the same vapour, the same temperature and chemical potential, filling infinite empty space, no walls and no impurities anywhere. The same finite rate per unit volume multiplied by an infinite volume gives infinitely many droplets at any positive time, so the liquid appears everywhere at once. The answer appears to flip from never to immediately when I remove the walls, although in the original problem the walls were doing nothing at all. I do not think this tells me anything about vapour; it seems to tell me something about my model, namely which quantities survive the passage to an infinite system. I would welcome being told the physics is wrong, but either way I want to know what such a case is for.

What I want from you is an accuracy audit I can act on, in the following form. Keep prose cells to two sentences each.

Table 1, "audit": one row per sentence [1]–[6], columns item | verdict | keep | reject_or_qualify | load_bearing. Verdict takes exactly one of: accurate, defensible_but_contested, simplification, misleading, wrong. Keep says what a specialist in this debate would accept; reject_or_qualify says what they would deny or insist on qualifying and why; load_bearing says whether the book's overall conclusion needs the sentence to stand as written, using one of yes, partly, no, with a clause of explanation.

Table 2, "probes": one row per case A, B, C, columns probe | physics_check | on_the_account | is_that_right | what_it_shows. Physics_check takes one of correct, correct_with_caveat, wrong, with the correction or caveat stated. On_the_account says what verdict the book's account, as I have summarised it, would have to give about the case — one of knowledge, not_knowledge, account_has_nothing_to_say, indeterminate. Is_that_right takes agree, partly, disagree. What_it_shows says whether this is a case the account handles well, one it misclassifies, or one lying outside its intended scope, and what follows.

Table 3, "terms": rows for any term — in the book's sentences or in my own cases — that carries a technical sense in this debate which I have used in an everyday one. Columns term | everyday_sense | technical_sense | does_it_matter_here (yes, no, or only_for_my_probes).

Close with a single replacement sentence of at most forty words that I could put in place of [6] to make the passage fair with the least loss, and then a bottom line of at most 150 words: does the book's sceptical conclusion survive once the errors are corrected, which of my three cases is the most damaging test and why, and what — if anything — I should tell the reading group that thought experiments actually do.
