I'm an undergraduate in a seminar on the evolution of human cooperation, and next week I'm defending my term paper in front of the whole seminar. My thesis is that applications of costly signaling theory to human behavior — ritual, conspicuous generosity, costly religious requirements — usually do no more than establish that a behavior is expensive and then infer that it is therefore an honest signal. But the handicap principle, in its formal versions, does not say that cost makes signals honest; it says honesty is enforced by a particular differential cost structure, roughly that the signal is less costly, or has a better cost-benefit ratio, for the higher-quality signaler. Large cost on its own does no equilibrium work, and most human applications never measure the cost for more than one type. My constructive suggestion is that many of these cases are better handled either by Spence-style differential-cost models, where what matters is that the same behavior is cheaper for the type who has more to gain from it and waste is incidental, or by index accounts on which the display cannot be faked rather than merely being dear, or else by plain incentive-alignment or commitment stories with no signaling at all. The methodological upshot I'm defending is that a human costly signaling paper ought to have to estimate the cost curve for two types rather than the cost for one, and that this demand is where the framework should be held accountable.

The room is about fifteen people. Roughly half are evolutionary anthropologists and human behavioral ecologists who use costly signaling in their own published work on ritual and cooperation: they'll be charitable but protective of the framework, and they tend to treat measured cost plus a predicted behavioral correlate as confirmation.
