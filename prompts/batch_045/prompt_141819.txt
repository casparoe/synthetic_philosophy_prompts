I serve on the grants committee of a private foundation whose charter, drafted decades ago, directs us to spend the endowment to reduce the suffering of sentient beings. It says nothing about time horizon, nothing about non-human animals, and nothing about minds implemented in anything other than biology, and it places no limit on how far into the future we may look. We have one large grant left to award this cycle, and the committee is evenly split over a single application.

The applicants call theirs a program of civilizational restraint. Their thesis is that the largest foreseeable reservoir of suffering is not anything in the present but a technologically mature civilization that becomes very large and very long-lived: enormous populations of artificial and substrate-independent minds, ecosystems seeded on other worlds, no institution reliably tracking the welfare of most of the minds that exist, and no way to reverse what has been created once it exists. They propose to spend three years identifying and promoting restrictive measures — delays, moratoria, and norms that lower either the probability or the eventual scale of a large future — and they argue that on nearly any way of running the expected-value calculation this dominates work that merely improves conditions now.

Half the committee takes this to fall squarely within our mandate. The other half thinks the reasoning is self-consuming: the same logic that says a smaller future holds less suffering also says that the single largest available reduction in suffering would come from ending the project of civilization altogether, and it is not clear where the applicants' own principles stop short of that conclusion. I cannot dismiss either side, and I am also troubled by something the applicants themselves barely address — that we do not know whether a large future would on balance be good or bad, and that our forecasts here are not merely imprecise but plausibly unstable in their sign, so that an intervention can look clearly beneficial when judged for its local effects and flip sign once its effects on the size and character of the far future are included.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to the committee before our meeting on 14 November. It should give a bottom line — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — together with a term, a review gate, and the concrete conditions, stated so that the applicants could actually respond to them. It should also state a principle the committee can apply to future applications of this general shape; I have been toying with two candidates and want your view on them: that we should preferentially fund measures which reduce the severity of bad futures rather than measures which reduce the size of the future, and that we should prefer measures whose effects are reversible to measures that foreclose options permanently.

Two further constraints. First, the memo must treat the strongest version of the applicants' case honestly rather than restating our mandate back at them; the losing side should be recognizable to its own supporters. Second, one trustee has raised an objection I do not want either dismissed or allowed to end the discussion: if the foundation spends its money making future artificial intelligence systems more suffering-averse, the likeliest failure mode is not indifference but overreach, since a system that weights suffering sufficiently heavily may conclude that the dependable way to eliminate suffering is to eliminate sufferers — which would be a program of restraint in the applicants' own terms. The memo should say what, if anything, our conditions should do about this.
