I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on federalism and multilevel governance: 24 students, one 75-minute session. They have already read the standard statements of subsidiarity (the EU's, the German Basic Law's necessity test, and the Swiss constitutional version), so I do not want anything that restates them.

Design a simulation that makes them feel one difficulty the reading slides past. Subsidiarity tells you to presume in favour of the smaller unit, but it does not say who gets to apply that presumption, and every candidate allocator — the union, the member units themselves, a court, a constituent convention — has an interest in the answer, because allocating competences is itself the most centralising power there is. I want them to run into this rather than hear it: they should spend the first part of the session arguing about which level should decide some concrete policy, and only later discover that the disagreement underneath was about who decides that question. Ideally I do not say the phrase "who decides who decides" until after they have tried to write a rule.

Concretely: six regional delegations of four, each with a written brief giving it (i) a local preference that diverges from the union median on some issue, (ii) a spillover it imposes on or suffers from its neighbours, and (iii) at least one issue on which it would prefer union-wide rules — so that every delegation has a reason to want localism for itself and centralisation for others. Give me the policy domain, the six briefs, a scoring scheme using points or legitimacy tokens rather than money, a timeline with minute marks, and, the part I care most about, the moment where I hand them a ballot asking only who decides who decides and they have to draft a workable rule on the spot, with the moves I should have ready for the proposals they will predictably produce.

Then a debrief that maps moments in the session onto the distinction between subsidiarity as a presumption about outcomes and as a rule about authority, says what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and is frank about which feature of the problem the simulation cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in discussion.
