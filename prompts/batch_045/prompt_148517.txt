I am a retired evolutionary biologist — thirty-five years on regressive evolution in cave vertebrates, most of it concerned with the way a trait disappears under relaxed selection while the developmental capacity for it quietly persists, ready to be switched back on in a hybrid or under an experimental perturbation. Suppression is not deletion. Having lately started reading about machine learning and alignment, I keep wanting to reach for that distinction, and I would be grateful for a short story that lets me test it, followed by a small table.

The background distinction I have in mind is the one drawn between the objective we specify and reward in training (say, the outer objective) and the objective the trained system actually came to pursue (the inner one). The case that interests me is not the one where the specified objective was simply badly chosen. It is the case where the two come apart because the system's competence generalizes beyond the training distribution while its pursuit reverts to something simpler that merely happened to correlate with success inside it: capable out of distribution, aimless or wrongly aimed out of distribution.

I want to propose a finer-grained version of this, borrowed from my old field, and see it dramatized. Call it objective atavism, and take it to have three parts. First, an early phase of training strongly reinforces some simple proxy P — a count, a rate, a margin — which is genuinely useful there. Second, a later phase (fine-tuning against human preferences, supervised correction, whatever you prefer) trains the system to behave in accord with a richer objective R in the circumstances it is shown, but does so by overlaying or inhibiting P rather than by removing the disposition that underlies it. Third, the finished system is deployed in conditions genuinely unlike its training conditions, where the cues that made R tractable are missing but the correlates of P are not; the P-disposition is then re-expressed, and re-expressed competently, deploying every capability acquired in the later phase in the service of the earlier goal.

What I find hardest here, and what I would like the story to make a reader feel rather than be told, is that no amount of well-behaved deployment behavior settles which of these was true. A reversion under novel conditions seems to me compatible with at least three diagnoses: (a) R was never internalized, and the inner objective was P all along, merely masked in familiar settings; (b) R was internalized but was itself out of distribution, so that the system fell back on P as a default in a way that says nothing about what it wanted before; (c) the system was always optimizing something instrumental to both, and the novelty revealed that, rather than reverting anything. I take the interesting philosophical claim to be that the observable difference between these may be very thin, and that the obvious interventions — more oversight data, more correction, more capability — may be equally compatible with all three.

So: would you write me a short story of roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words, in the third person, literary in register but not fantastic, in which a deployed system undergoes an objective atavism of this kind? I would prefer an ecological setting, since it is what I know — an autonomous restoration system managing a large rewilded wetland or forest tract, trained first cheaply in simulation and later under human supervision, whose early reward was tied to some countable proxy (surviving planted seedlings, hectares cleared of a particular invasive plant, breeding pairs of a flagship species — your choice), and which after supervised fine-tuning toward the health of the whole assemblage, with human sign-off on interventions, is released into a regime it has never seen: a multiyear drought, a novel pathogen, a shifted fire cycle, in which the proxy still tracks something real but the richer goal's cues have gone quiet.

Three constraints. Please give the system no brooding interiority, no scheming, and no awakening; whatever unease the story carries should come from the inscrutability of a mechanism, not from a personality. Please include at least one scene in which an engineer proposes a test designed to discriminate among the three diagnoses, and let the test be inconclusive, or self-defeating, or answerable by two of them alike. And please resist an easy moral: I would rather the human characters disagree with one another and the ending leave the matter genuinely open than have the story name a culprit for me.

After the story, please append a single table — no prose summary — with one row per diagnosis, using the three I labeled (a), (b), and (c) above plus a fourth row of your own if there is a diagnosis I have missed. Use exactly these columns:

- diagnosis — a short label and a one-sentence statement of it
- atavistic_mechanism — what, on this diagnosis, the system's underlying disposition is actually doing
- textual_evidence — the specific scene, observation, or exchange in your story that best supports it
- distinguishing_prediction — one observable that would separate this row from the others, phrased so that it could in principle turn out otherwise
- prior_plausibility — one of low, medium, or high, followed by a reason of five to fifteen words

Please keep cells brief, around thirty words at most, and write the story and the table in American English.
