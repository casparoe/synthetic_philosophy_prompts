ok so a guy I share an office with backed me into this over coffee last week and I still can't find the exit. It's about Jeffrey conditionalization and I think it proves way too much, but I can't locate the seam.

Setup, so we're using the same words. P is my credence function now. {E_i} is a partition. An experience shifts my credence in E_i to q_i without making any cell certain — candlelight on a cloth, I go from 50/50 on green to 70/30 and I'm not certain of anything. Jeffrey's rule says: P_new(H) = Σ q_i P(H|E_i). The load-bearing assumption is rigidity: P_new(H|E_i) = P(H|E_i) for every H. All fine so far.

His argument:

(1) An update rule is adequate only if it's universal: for any prior, and any experience that shifts credences across some partition, it delivers a new credence function.
(2) For genuinely uncertain evidence — nothing learned outright — Jeffrey's rule is the correct rule.
(3) Jeffrey's rule stands or falls with rigidity, and rigidity is stated for every proposition: P_new(H|E_i) = P(H|E_i) for all H.
(4) Let D be the proposition that after the experience my credence in E_1 is q_1. D is a proposition, so rigidity covers it: P_new(D|E_i) = P(D|E_i).
(5) P_new is my credence function after the experience; D is true then, and I have authoritative access to my own current credences. So P_new(D) = 1, hence P_new(D|E_i) = 1 for every cell with q_i > 0.
(6) So P(D|E_i) = 1 for every such cell: before the experience I was already certain, conditional on each cell, that this exact shift was coming.
(7) Averaging over the cells: P(D) = 1. Before undergoing the experience I was already certain that I'd undergo exactly it, with exactly this effect on my credences.
(8) That's nuts. If I already knew the experience was coming and knew what it would do to me, it isn't evidence at all; and plainly I am not certain in advance of every future experience and its exact credal upshot.
(9) So rigidity fails for every real experience; Jeffrey's rule never applies; and by (1) nothing else that leans on rigidity is adequate either. There is no universal rule for learning from experience — and learning from experience is the only kind of learning there is.

Then he rubs it in with a lemma. For any Jeffrey update on {E_i} to q where every cell with q_i > 0 has P(E_i) > 0, pick ε > 0 small enough that q_i·ε/P(E_i) ≤ 1 for every i. Extend the algebra with a fresh proposition D* that is independent of everything in the old algebra conditional on each E_i, with P(D*|E_i) = q_i·ε/P(E_i). Then P(D*) = ε, and conditionalizing on D* reproduces the Jeffrey output exactly on the old algebra. So, he says: Jeffrey's rule *is* ordinary conditioning on something; the something just has prior probability ε instead of 1; step (5) is therefore the only place the first argument can be stopped; and my options are to deny that I know my own credences, or to admit that rigidity has a scope restriction nobody ever wrote down, or to give up (1) and stop pretending there's a general rule.

He also has a meaner variant. Forget propositions about my own credences: run (4)–(9) with D = something perfectly ordinary that the experience settles and that isn't in the partition — the light in the room was dim, say. Then I can't wriggle out by saying D isn't expressible in the old algebra, because of course it is.

I'd like to keep (2) — Jeffrey's rule really does seem right for the candlelight case — and I'd like to keep (1) if I can. (9) I can't accept. Which step do I actually reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost me? Specifically: if the answer is that rigidity only ranges over some privileged sub-algebra of propositions, what picks that sub-algebra out, and what do I owe once I've said that — do I inherit the sensitivity to how finely I carve the partition, and the failure of updates to commute? And does his lemma help him, or does it hand me the way out?
