**Grue isn't about time**

**1.** The setup as I understand it. Something is grue if it's green when examined before some fixed time t, and blue when examined after t. Every emerald anyone has examined so far has been examined before t and been green, so every one has been grue. So my evidence supports "the next emerald examined will be grue" exactly as well as it supports "the next emerald examined will be green." If that next examination happens after t, the two predictions say opposite things. Piling on more green emeralds doesn't help, because each new one is another grue one too. So the evidence can't pick between the predicates, and the thing that does the picking has to be something other than "more instances."

**2.** But time is not the point. Here's grum: a thing is grum if it's green when you look at it in daylight, and blue when you look at it under lamplight. Suppose everything examined so far was examined in daylight. Then all examined emeralds are grum, and "the next emerald examined will be grum" is supported exactly as well as "will be green" — and they come apart the first time somebody examines one under a lamp.

You can do this with anything that sorts observation-situations into piles. Date. Lighting. Which mine the stone came from. Whether the observer has had lunch. Being grue isn't a property of time, it's a property of a *way of dividing up circumstances*, and the date is just one divider among endless ones. So the real question isn't "why is grue unprojectible." It's: which dividers are allowed to matter when you predict?

**3.** Here's my answer, and I think this is the actual idea in the essay. A prediction is a bet that comes with a contract about what's being held fixed. When I say "the next emerald will be green," I am not saying "green no matter what." I'm saying: green, given that the setup hasn't shifted in the ways I'm implicitly freezing — same sort of stone, same sort of light, same sort of looking, same sort of observer. Every prediction has a clause like that in it, and normally the clause is invisible because the frozen things are frozen by everyone in the room.

What grue (and grum, and the others) do is keep the word you already trust for the cases you've seen, and quietly un-freeze one background condition. With grue the unfrozen thing is the date. The reason grue feels like cheating, I want to say, is not that it's disjunctive or weird or un-entrenched. It's that we can't *see* what it's holding open, because the date is the one background condition we never think of as a condition at all. Grue isn't a rival hypothesis about emeralds. It's a rival contract, smuggled in while everyone is looking at the word green.

**4.** First pass at a rule: you're allowed to index a prediction to a condition only if that condition could make a causal difference to what you observe. Lighting passes — it changes which wavelengths reach your eye. The date fails — nothing about the calendar reaches into the emerald. So grum is a legitimate re-contracting and grue is not.

Now the case I'm least sure about, regrue: a thing is regrue if it's green the first time it's examined and blue every time after that. All emeralds examined so far were being examined for the first time, so all of them are regrue, and "the next examination will be regrue" predicts that looking at this stone again turns it blue. This is grue on a different axis: instead of dividing up *new objects*, it divides up *repeat looks at the same object*. Our normal practice assumes examination doesn't change the thing examined, and that assumption is not itself given by the evidence — it's part of the contract, and usually a good part. But for some systems it's false in a way that matters.

**5.** The objection I can hear coming. Whether the date could make a causal difference is itself the sort of thing you only learn by induction — things have stayed green across dates so far. So my causal-relevance rule uses induction to license induction, which is circular.

My answer: it's not a circle, it's a ratchet. You don't have to *know* that the date never matters. You need a standing policy: treat every condition as irrelevant until something forces you to treat it as relevant. Being wrong is cheap — you get surprised once, and then you add a condition to the contract. Being right is very valuable, because without the policy you face an infinite menu of grues and no way to choose. So induction isn't justified, exactly; it's the cheapest available bet in a situation where you have to bet. And the ratchet has consequences I like: new conditions really do get added over time (we learned to index predictions to whether food has been left out, whether a sample has been exposed to air), and the more conditions a contract already carries, the more suspicious a new grue-shaped addition looks — which is why grue feels worse than grum.

**6.** Where I start to itch. On this account, a community that had grown up with a grue-shaped colour word, and a second word for what things look like after the switch, aren't making a mistake. They have a different contract. And the only thing that could ever show them wrong is a future observation. Part of me thinks that's exactly right and part of me thinks there must be more to say.

Also: greb. A thing is greb if it's green when I look at it and blue when anybody else does. Every observation I've made supports greb. This one feels like a different disease than grue, but I can't say why, and I don't know whether my contract stuff handles it or falls apart on it.
