I am writing a thesis chapter on unilateral action under disagreement among agents who broadly share ends, and I have got as far as the standard statement of the unilateralist's curse: where many agents could each separately bring about a large outcome, and where errors in expected-value estimates are asymmetric, a group does better if each agent refrains when her private estimate of an act is positive but the group's is not. I now want to turn that into something that also covers cases where the unilateral option is an omission, where the agent's dissent is evaluative rather than empirical, and where the errors of the group are correlated with hers. Below are my verdicts on a set of cases, which I have tried to record honestly rather than consistently. I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

1. A virologist could, on her own initiative, run an experiment that makes a pathogen more transmissible in order to validate a vaccine platform. Her private expected-value estimate is strongly positive; her lab and her field are strongly negative. She should not proceed, and I do not find it close.

2. The same experiment, but only two laboratories in the world have the samples and the clearance, and the other has considered and declined. The canonical argument says the effect scales with the number of agents who could act. I still think she should not proceed, and I find this barely less clear than case 1. When I press myself on why, what comes out is that it is not how many of us there are that bothers me, it is that the people who looked carefully came out the other way. If that is right, the number of possible actors is doing much less work than the standard presentation suggests; alternatively, this is the verdict that has to go.

3. A small team wants to release the weights of a capable model. Most of the field thinks the risks dominate; the team does not. Full release: not fine. Release to vetted researchers under licence, with the reasoning published: fine. I cannot tell whether I think the gated option is genuinely less unilateral in the relevant sense, or merely the same act with a smaller variance behind it. (Relatedly: the stakes here are large but not civilisation-ending, and I still feel the pull. Is the variable the size of the stakes or the irreversibility of the act?)

4. A mid-level employee has documents showing that her employer is still dumping toxic waste illegally, and that internal complaints have been buried. Her colleagues and family think going to a journalist is reckless and will ruin her. She may do it, and she may do it after taking advice from one lawyer rather than after deferring to anything wider. This is the pair that I cannot reconcile with case 1: one agent, private positive estimate, majority negative, big stakes, irreversible. My only stab at a difference is that the people around her are objecting on the basis of the costs to her and the firm rather than on the claim that the dumping is harmless.

5. An engineer privately believes, against internal consensus, that a system about to be deployed will cause serious harm. His unilateral option is to slow the deployment by a few weeks; if he is wrong, the cost is delay. He should do it, and I think the curse reasoning gives no support whatsoever for restraint here. But if dozens of engineers could each delay, and errors are asymmetric, the same structure should generate a curse of obstruction, and I do not feel its force. I would like to know whether that is an inconsistency on my part.

6. A programme officer with a discretionary budget holds, as a moral view, that a neglected cause is orders of magnitude more important than her colleagues think, and redirects funds to it on her own authority. Not fine. Yet the argument I started from is stated entirely in terms of expected-value estimates and seems indifferent to whether the outlier judgement is factual or evaluative, so either the principle needs a clause about that or this verdict goes.

7. She works in a field where everyone's estimates come out of the same models and the same training, and she has a concrete reason to think the shared framework has broken down. The group agrees, and is confident. Here I think the presumption against acting is much weaker than in case 1 — weaker precisely where the group is most confident, which sits badly with any simple reading on which being an outlier just raises the probability that you are the one who is wrong.

8. The case I keep reversing on: a methodologist finds an error in a widely used risk model that a professional body has already reviewed and approved, and considers going public over their heads. I lean towards yes if the evidence is genuinely hers and hard to transmit, and towards no if what is really at issue is how to weigh facts everyone already has.

My own first pass at a principle is this. An agent may act unilaterally on a contested high-stakes judgement only if (i) the loss conditional on her being wrong is not much greater than the gain conditional on her being right; (ii) her dissent rests on evidence or reasoning the relevant others have not been able to assess; and (iii) she has taken the cheapest available steps to expose that reasoning to correction before acting. The presumption against acting strengthens with how much of an outlier she is among those who have looked carefully, and weakens with how far their errors are likely to be correlated with hers. This seems to me to get 1, 5 and 7 about right, to mishandle 4, where clause (ii) fails because her colleagues have heard the argument and rejected it, and to say nothing at all about 6.

Please test that formulation against the cases and repair it, or replace it. I would like a principle, or a short list of candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself, with the case that motivates each clause; its verdicts on cases I have not listed — a civil servant bypassing ministers to stop a policy she privately judges disastrous, and a researcher who publishes her dissenting reasoning, gets no engagement, and then acts anyway; and an honest account of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which I ought to abandon.
