I'm working on a piece about how the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness tend to get used in arguments, and what I need is a case built to order rather than another restatement of a theorem. My thesis is that these results are indexed to a partition of the population and that nothing in the mathematics selects the partition, so the theorems get cited as though they settled something when the load-bearing choice was made before the arithmetic began. I want a case that isolates that one variable and holds everything else fixed, and I want it generated by the numbers rather than stipulated to come out a certain way.

Setting: something ordinary — a bank's small-business lending book, a hospital's discharge or triage tool, a pretrial risk instrument, your pick. No perfect predictors, no auditor who can see more than the institution can see, and a case a competent analyst could actually have on a spreadsheet. One paragraph for the scenario itself.

The specifications:

(a) Two headline groups whose outcome prevalence is the same. Equal base rates is the textbook escape hatch in the calibration-versus-error-rate results, and setting it to zero is the point: I want the conflict to appear without it. If equal prevalence at the coarse level is not by itself enough to make the three criteria jointly satisfiable, say what the minimal further condition is instead of forcing it — and correct me if the result is narrower than I am stating it.

(b) Inside each headline group there are two subpopulations with different prevalences, and the features available to the classifier do not reveal which subpopulation a case belongs to. The subpopulations have to be ones a regulator could plausibly have named — a region, an industry, a prior-relationship flag — not a conjunction engineered to break the criteria.

(c) Show, with actual numbers rather than by pointing at a theorem, that calibration within groups, equal false-positive rates and equal false-negative rates can all hold when demanded for the two headline groups, and cannot all hold when the same three are demanded for the four subpopulations — same people, same features, same outcomes, same classifier, only the partition moves. Then give me three variants of that classifier, each satisfying a different two of the three at the subpopulation level, each something the institution could plausibly ship. I want to see that this is a genuine three-way conflict and not one stubborn criterion doing all the work.

(d) Name the feature that is doing the work, say what happens to the conflict if the partition is coarsened back or refined further — I expect it dissolves under coarsening and worsens under refinement, but tell me if that is wrong — and then give me the strongest available reply from someone who thinks the impossibility results are decisive. The two I can anticipate are that the fine partition is the honest one and the coarse one is concealing disparities, and that the criteria were never the right target because what matters is whether each group ends up better off than under the human baseline. Steelman both.

One last thing, which I do not see discussed and which I suspect matters more than the theorem does. In the real version of this setting the outcome is only observed for cases that were approved: the bank never learns whether a rejected applicant would have repaid. So the prevalences the result takes as input are partly an artifact of the previous policy — and the audited partition is the partition that gets optimized, the usual Goodhart story, except here the target itself is unobservable. Build the case so this is visible: state which quantities the institution observes, and say what the theorem's inputs even are when half the outcome data is missing by construction. If the honest answer is that the formal result goes silent under that condition, say so plainly. For my purposes that is a conclusion rather than a way out.
