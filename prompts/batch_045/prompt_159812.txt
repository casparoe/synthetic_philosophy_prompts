I chair the methods working group for a national flood and infrastructure risk assessment programme. Our assessments are written by domain scientists — hydrologists, structural engineers, actuaries, a couple of economists — and every few years we rewrite the section of the reporting manual that governs how authors state uncertainty. We keep finding that authors who write identical-looking sentences are relying on incompatible accounts of what the sentence means, and that arguments about the manual are really arguments about that. Before we finalise anything I want to run a ninety-minute workshop in which each author finds out which account they are actually using. So I need a classification instrument: a branching decision tree that starts from a commitment any author can recognise and ends at a named position, plus a scoring key for me to run it with.

Constraints on the instrument:

1. No question may use philosophical vocabulary. An author must be able to answer in under a minute with no training in the subject, so the questions may not mention priors, coherence, Dutch books, previsions, additivity, calibration, admissibility or decision rules. Put the content in our idiom: gauges, sensors, catchments, design standards, budgets, expert teams, records.

2. No answer option may name a position or paraphrase one. Each option must be something an author could say about the case, and no question may ask what they call themselves or whether they are some kind of Bayesian.

3. Branching, not a flat score sheet. At most five questions on any path, with the answer to each question determining which question comes next. Give me the tree in text form with numbered nodes, and a table mapping every terminal path to a leaf.

4. Leaves: at least eight, at most fourteen. These must appear: strict precise probabilism, on which there is always one number and any range in the text is only a summary of a distribution; hierarchical or second-order probabilism, including the variant that permits a non-neutral attitude to ambiguity; robust sensitivity analysis, on which there is a family of analyses reported as a range while one designated analysis does the real work; strict imprecision governed by a worst-case rule; strict imprecision governed by a permissive rule, on which every option that is best under some member of the family is allowed and the author is not told which to pick; an optimism–pessimism weighted rule; belief-function style uncertainty, with mass attached to sets rather than points; comparative or ordinal uncertainty, on which the author ranks but will not give numbers or intervals; and the error-statistical refusal to attach probabilities to hypotheses at all, as opposed to attaching error probabilities to methods. Add further leaves if the logical space has room for them. If you are not confident that a cell has a canonical label, mark it unnamed and describe it in one line rather than inventing a name.

5. Build from the logical space rather than from the list of names. Lay out the questions and the combinations of answers they permit first, then locate the positions in that space, then tell me which combinations are unoccupied and why — incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored — and which pairs of leaves your tree cannot separate. Report those pairs honestly instead of forcing a distinction between them. One case to check in particular: the view that an author may publish any number inside the stated range provided they are consistent in using it. If that view has no home in your tree, say so and explain what absorbs it.

Cases. Use these, in whatever order the tree needs; you may add at most two of your own. Start from case (b), which should sort in a single move the authors who will not give numbers from those who will.

(a) Two gauges. Gauge K has been calibrated against a reference set and reads HIGH for exactly half of it. Gauge U is uncalibrated and reads HIGH or LOW in unknown proportions. The author must choose, separately for HIGH and for LOW, which gauge to stake a claim on.

(b) Buying and selling. For a one-off event in the assessment — say, a particular catchment exceeding its design flood within twenty years — ask for the most the author would pay for a ticket paying one unit if the event occurs, and the least they would accept to sell such a ticket. Options: the same number both ways; a gap, with the selling price strictly above the buying price; no numbers at all, but a willingness to rank events against one another.

(c) Thin evidence. One imperfect study, no earlier records, a quantity nobody in the field has measured. What goes in the report: a number fixed by indifference; a wide but not full interval; the full interval; nothing quantitative.

(d) Two teams, equally credible, reporting 0.3 and 0.7 for the same event. Average them; report the span and nothing in between; report both with a note; refuse to aggregate until the disagreement is explained.

(e) Widening. A switch is set to 0 or 1 by a fair coin. Two monitoring stations, North and South, each print a reading of the state. Either both stations are wired correctly and each prints the true state, or South alone is cross-wired and prints the opposite of the true state. The author has no evidence about which. Ask what they would write down for the probability that South prints 1 before looking at North's printout, and then what they would write after North prints 1. This is the case I most want the tree to handle, because it is where our authors actually come apart: some insist that reading an instrument cannot make them less certain, others insist that here it plainly must.

(f) Straddling ranges. Two interventions, scored in net benefit units. Option A falls between 3 and 9 across the analyses the author is willing to use, option B between 4 and 7, and the two move together: A is the better option when the parameter is favourable and the worse one when it is unfavourable. Ask which they recommend. Options: the one with the better worst case; the one with the better best case; either, with the choice left to judgement; a fixed weighting of worst and best cases; defer the decision and commission more work; act on a single most-likely value.

(g) Accumulation. After ten years of records an author's interval for a failure rate runs from 0.1 to 0.4. Ten more years of the same kind of records arrive. Does the interval narrow, stay about as wide, or does the answer depend on what is making it wide?

Two further things I need.

First, the distinction the working group cares about most, which I will define because it is our term and not a standard one: reporting imprecision versus deliberative imprecision. An author has reporting imprecision if a range appears in the published statement while the recommendation is still reached by acting on a single analysis; they have deliberative imprecision if the width of the range itself drives what they recommend. We call the first pattern laundering when nobody notices it. The tree must separate the two, and every leaf should carry one line on what an author at that leaf is permitted to write in our assessments, and one line on the most awkward consequence of that position for our conventions.

Second, a short section on inconsistent response patterns: which combinations of answers no single position can make sense of, and what the workshop leader should do when an author produces one — the follow-up question to put to them, or the note to record against their name.

Do not cite anything: no papers, no authors, no reading lists. I need the instrument, the key and the diagnostics, and nothing that depends on looking a fact up.
