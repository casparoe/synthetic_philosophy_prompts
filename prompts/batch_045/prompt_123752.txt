Pretend you're a philosopher of mind who actually specializes in the timing of consciousness — someone who has spent years on questions about when experience happens relative to the brain processes that produce it. Please brainstorm a bit before you commit to an answer, and think it through carefully; I'd rather have a real attempt that gets stuck somewhere than a tidy "the literature is divided, both views have defenders."

I'm a night-shift nurse, no philosophy background, and I got hooked on this on my own after stumbling on the color phi thing and the whole "Orwellian vs. Stalinesque" argument about it. As I understand the labels: Orwellian means I really was conscious of something at the earlier time and then the memory got rewritten afterwards; Stalinesque means nothing was there at the earlier time at all — the experience got built later and then backdated, like a trial transcript written before the trial. I've been going through cases and asking myself which one I think is happening, and I want the principle underneath my verdicts, if there is one. Here they are.

1. Red flash on the left, then maybe a tenth of a second later a green flash a few inches to the right. What I see is one dot sliding across that turns green partway along, and I'm told people place the color change in the middle of the trip — before the green flash has actually happened. My verdict: Stalinesque, and not a close call. There was no moment where I saw a red dot sitting still and then had my recollection of it tampered with.

2. Three quick taps on the forearm — wrist, elbow, shoulder — and you feel taps hopping up the arm at evenly spaced steps, including spots that were never touched. Worse version: if you have people hit a button the instant they feel the middle tap, the one that doesn't exist, the press lands before the shoulder tap has been delivered. Verdict: Stalinesque again, and even more clearly. There wasn't anything sitting in memory yet to edit, because the thing that supposedly does the editing hadn't happened.

3. One white flash on a screen, but two short beeps, one just before it and one just after. People report seeing two flashes. Verdict: Stalinesque — and this is the one that feels most like cheating to me, because a sound, arriving after the flash, puts an extra flash into what I saw. (I might be misremembering whether it still works if both beeps come after the flash; assume it does for the sake of argument.)

4. Watching a face say "ga" while the audio says "ba", and you hear "da". Verdict: this isn't a revision case at all. That's just what hearing is like when you can see the person's face. Nothing was edited, because there was never a moment at which I heard "ba" and then it got changed into something else.

5. One I made up, because I couldn't stop thinking about it: taps at the wrist and the elbow, but instead of the third tap at the shoulder, a small light flashes near the shoulder. My gut says you would not feel a tap at the shoulder — you'd feel two taps and separately see a light. But that pushes me toward "revision only happens within a single sense," which would kill my verdict on case 3. So one of 3 and 5 has to go and I genuinely don't know which.

6. The memory-suggestion stuff: you watch a crash video, and later someone asks about the stop sign when it was actually a yield sign, and now you remember the wrong one. Verdict: plainly Orwellian, no revision of experience at all. But the only real difference from case 3 seems to be that the suggestion arrives seconds or minutes later instead of fity milliseconds later. Is the line between "your experience was fabricated after the fact" and "your memory was rewritten afterwards" just a number in milliseconds? Because that seems like an insane place to draw a line about what you were conscious of.

7. You snap your eyes onto a clock and the second hand seems to hang there for noticeably longer than a second before the first tick. Verdict: I don't know what to call this one. The content doesn't seem fabricated, it's the timestamp that's wrong — a real experience getting backdated. Feels like it wants a third category, and if so the Orwell/Stalin pair isn't exhaustive.

8. The blind spot, where the optic nerve leaves your eye: you never see a hole, the pattern just continues through. Verdict: neither. There's no first draft that gets revised, there's just no gap in the first place. (Though people who lose a patch of retina to injury sometimes describe the patch taking days or weeks to fill in, which sounds much more like something being constructed late.)

9. The one I keep flipping on. I'm walking in the woods, there's a brown shape in the bushes, and it's just a shape — then my friend says "that's a bear" and it's a bear. Not "I now infer it's a bear"; it looks like a bear. Stalinesque, with my later belief writing the earlier visual experience? Or is that not a timing case at all, just interpretation riding on top of an unchanged experience? Related: I wake up from a muddled dream and "realize what it was really about" — did I revise the dream after the fact or not?

10. Same two flashes as case 1 but half a second apart: I just get red, then green, two seperate events, no sliding. So the whole question only comes up inside some window. What sets the window, and should a principle about what I was conscious of really be allowed to hinge on a millisecond cutoff?

My own attempt at a principle, to be tested and repaired: later stuff can reach back and rewrite what I was conscious of only if (a) it arrives while the earlier experience is still under construction — inside the window where the brain hasn't finished it — and (b) it arrives through the same sense as the thing being rewritten. Once the window closes, later stuff can only rewrite memory, which is the Orwellian case.

Tell me where that breaks against my verdicts, and fix it if it can be fixed. I'd like: the principle stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause; its verdict on the bear case, on case 7, and on whether a debriefing afterward ("actually there was no motion, just two flashes") can rewrite an experience you already reported out loud; at least one case I haven't listed where it gives an answer I probably wouldn't have guessed; and an honest account of which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and which you'd overturn. Also: if you think the honest answer is that for the in-window cases there is no fact of the matter which one happened — that the distinction collapses — argue for that instead, and tell me what experiment, if any, would settle it even in principle.
