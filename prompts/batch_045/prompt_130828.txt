I'm in a small reading group — a friend from law school, a colleague who works in machine learning, and me — and we've been working through free will material that all three of us remember badly from college. Last week my friend forwarded a long-read article from a magazine I subscribe to (I'd rather not name it; I don't want this to become a fight with the author) whose whole second half rests on Strawson's *Freedom and Resentment*, and specifically on one paragraph that does all the work. Here it is, as close to the original wording as I can reconstruct from my notes:

"Strawson's great paper finally closed the free will debate, and it did so by pointing at something the metaphysicians had overlooked: the reactive attitudes. Resentment, gratitude, indignation, and guilt are moral emotions, and emotions are not the sort of thing we can switch on and off at will — we can no more decide to stop resenting someone who has wronged us than we can decide to stop feeling pain. That psychological fact is what makes determinism irrelevant. The determinist says that no one could ever have done otherwise, but since we could not abandon the reactive attitudes even if we tried, no practical question survives, and the ancient worry about whether anyone is ever really morally responsible turns out to be empty. What matters for Strawson is not metaphysics but the stance we occupy. Most of the time we occupy the participant stance: we treat one another as people who owe each other a certain measure of goodwill, and we answer slights and kindnesses with the reactive attitudes, which is useful, because blame and praise regulate conduct and hold relationships together. Sometimes, though — with small children, with the severely mentally ill, with the outright psychopath, and with anyone whose action is excused, say because it was an accident or because they were coerced — we withdraw into the objective attitude: we set blame aside and treat the person as a thing to be managed, handled, trained, or cured. And that withdrawal, and nothing else, is what moral responsibility amounts to: to be morally responsible is to be someone toward whom the objective attitude would be out of place. Why would it be out of place? Because blaming them works, and treating them as a mere object would not. That consequentialist core is also what lets the view travel so far beyond the cases Strawson cared about. If the point of holding someone responsible is that it improves their conduct, then nothing in his argument confines holding responsible to human beings: resentment directed at a company's chatbot for a piece of dangerous advice is as apt as resentment directed at the human employee who gave the same advice, provided the reaction gets better results next time. And by the same logic, if we ever found some other device that regulated conduct more reliably than blame does — a well-designed incentive scheme, a pill that reliably improved behavior — Strawson would have to say that blame had been superseded and that we should give it up. Finally, since guilt is simply resentment turned inward, a person who feels no guilt at all, the clinical psychopath, is on Strawson's own terms not a morally responsible agent and belongs wholly on the objective side of the line."

I have two half-memories that make me suspicious of this and one that makes me want to defend it. I remember reactive attitudes being described as attitudes rather than emotions, with the point that you can have the attitude without feeling particularly stirred — but I can't reconstruct why that distinction is supposed to carry weight here, and my notes don't say. I also remember someone in a seminar insisting that Strawson isn't offering a criterion of moral responsibility at all, and that reading him as giving one (in terms of which stance is appropriate to take up) is to miss that he is changing the subject rather than answering the question; if that's right, then the sentence about what responsibility "amounts to" is the load-bearing error, and everything downstream inherits it. Against that, my law-school friend thinks the paragraph is basically sound and that the only real howler is the chatbot remark, because "you can't be in a relationship with a language model" — which strikes me as the wrong objection, or at least an unhelpfully quick one, since I don't see why being in a relationship would be the test. The end of the paragraph matters to me practically: I work on a team that ships software, colleagues really do argue that expressing blame toward a system is fine so long as it changes behavior, and I want to know whether that follows from anything Strawson is plausibly committed to or only from the article's version of him.
