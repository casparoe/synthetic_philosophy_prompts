Me and my mate got into a proper arguement after the departmental seminar last night — we were walking back from the pub and it went on for the best part of an hour — about whether it even makes sense to be uncertain about a mathematical claim. His position was that logical uncertainty isn't a thing: whatever follows from the axioms follows, so a sentence is a theorem or it isn't, and any number you attach to it tells you about how ignorant you are, not about the maths. I said okay, but the agent you're comparing us to is logically omniscient and hence useless to us, and there's that logical-induction framework — the one where you're allowed to be incoherent on any given day provided no trader can extract unlimited profit from your prices over time — which gives you something like a real standard of good reasoning under boundedness. He said that's just a dressed-up way of betting on how long a computer takes to find the proof, which I think is too quick but I definately couldn't put my finger on why at the time. The thing I kept circling back to is that a decent reasoner's prices should start drifting toward the truth before the answer arrives: if your probability only collapses at the moment you check, you weren't reasoning, you were waiting. He said that's mysticism, or at best curve-fitting to how fast you expect to be convinced. We left it there and I've been stewing on it since.

So — I help run a fortnightly reading group on formal epistemology, and I've got a 90-minute slot coming up with about 14 people (mostly final-year philosophy and CS undergrads, very mixed, a couple of them scary good at maths), and I want them to feel the force of my side rather than just be told it. Could you design me a runnable in-class activity? I'm imagining some kind of market: you open order books on mathematical claims that are already settled as a matter of logic but not settled for anyone in the room, and people trade. I need actual rules, materials, a scoring scheme with no real money, and a debrief. Where I'd like you to be inventive is in the mechanics that smuggle in the parts of the view people don't get from the slogan: (i) a deliberately graded set of claims — a couple anyone can settle by hand, a couple a laptop in the room settles in seconds, one or two that probably won't be settled by anyone during the session at all — with a staggered settlement schedule announced up front, and some rule for what happens to the final price of a claim that never resolves; (ii) a "troll" who is allowed to introduce brand-new claims, including trivially decidable ones, at any point during trading, since a real reasoner has to price sentences it didn't see coming, and it's the cheap ones that should be the embarrassing ones to get wrong; (iii) one self-referential claim about the group's own prices (say, about where the median price on some particular card will sit at the halfway mark), plus asking everyone to write down at the start what they expect their own price on a given claim to be in twenty minutes — the point being that you can't be a good reasoner if you can't see your own updates coming; and (iv) an explicit arbitrage rule, so that the only way to be punished is by logic: if you post prices violating P(A) + P(not-A) = 1, or post a higher price on a claim than on something it provably implies, someone can take points off you — even though no random event ever happens and nothing ever "comes up".
