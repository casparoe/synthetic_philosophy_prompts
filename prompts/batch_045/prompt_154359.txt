Final exam for my third-year metaphysics seminar goes to the printers on Friday and I've completely stalled on the infinity question. I need you to write it for me, plus a model answer and a marking scheme.

Context: it's a closed-book final, one essay, 45 minutes, students have done a unit covering Hilbert's Hotel, Cantor's theorem and the hierarchy of cardinals, the potential/actual infinity distinction, the lamp-switching style supertask paradoxes, and a week on finitism. Total for the question is 25 marks.

Here's the half-formed thing I keep circling round and I want the question built out of it. Every version of the argument that Hilbert's Hotel shows actual infinities are impossible treats the reshuffle as if it were free: manager asks each guest to move from room n to room 2n and suddenly there are infinitely many vacancies. But in a concrete hotel the guests have to physically walk down the corridor, every single one of them has to move, and the person in room 10^9 has to travel a billion rooms while the person in room 1 travels one, so there's no upper bound on the distance and no last guest to finish. Which makes me think the argument, if it has any force, is really an argument against supertasks or against infinitely many coordinated causal processes being completable, and that the bare cardinality fact — that a set can be in one-one correspondence with a proper subset of itself — is doing no independent work. I'm not sure I'm right and I want students to have to take a side on it.

Two more things I want squeezed in. First: Cantor's theorem and the endless hierarchy of bigger infinities normally get wheeled in by the anti-infinity side as extra evidence that infinity is pathological, but you could just as well say they show that infinity is precisely the sort of thing mathematics handles with complete rigour, which makes it less spooky rather than more. Make them say which way that cuts and why. Second: the finitist running the Hotel argument usually also wants to keep continuous space or continuous time, which hands them uncountably many points or instants anyway; so either the argument overruns its own target or the finitist has to go discrete or point-free. I want them to deal with that, even if only in a sentence or two.

What I'd like back:

- The question as it'd appear on the paper. Two parts: a short part (a) that forces a clean reconstruction or distinction, and part (b) the real assessment. Word it so that nobody can pass by narrating the Hotel and then sketching a diagonal argument. And no named positions, authors or texts in the question itself — describe the views instead, since it's closed-book and I don't want marks riding on whether they remember who said what.

- A model answer, 700 to 900 words, at the level I'd want from my best student. It has to commit to something at the end rather than balancing, and it should be honest about where the disagreement bottoms out.

- A marking scheme out of 25. Name the five or six moves a first-class answer has to make, say what each is worth, and for each one give the specific way students get it wrong. Some I've seen a lot: conflating 'no empty rooms' with 'no bijection left over', assuming the Hotel needs Cantor's theorem when it only needs the Dedekind-infinite property, and dismissing the walking-down-the-corridor worry with 'it's only a thought experiment so the physics doesn't matter'. Then a line or two on what separates a 2:1 from a first, and what would cap someone at a third.
