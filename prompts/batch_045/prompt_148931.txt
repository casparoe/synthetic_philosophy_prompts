I am convening a one-day workshop on what kind of thing transformative AI is, built around the three historical analogies that currently dominate the discussion — nuclear weapons, the printing press, the Industrial Revolution — and I want to close the day with a poem rather than a summary. Please write that closing poem as a sestina: six six-line stanzas with rotating end-words, plus the three-line envoi.

The form is doing the argument, so I want the mechanism specified rather than left to chance. Choose six end-words and assign them in pairs, two to each analogy. They should be concrete nouns from the historical material — fission, enrichment, type, press, spindle, mill, factory, wage, literacy, treaty — not abstractions like risk, progress, or power. One of the six, though, should be a word that legitimately belongs to all three stories in different senses (engine, speed, scale, power, chain), so that when the rotation carries it into a new neighbourhood it changes meaning rather than simply repeating. The last line of a stanza belongs to whichever analogy owns the end-word that lands there; over six stanzas the standard rotation will give each analogy the floor exactly twice, and I want no analogy to win the poem.

What each stanza must be answerable to, when its analogy holds the floor:

- Nuclear weapons. Grant the strong version: a capability can arrive as a cliff rather than a slope, a small classified programme can produce it, and the people who build it may not be the people who decide how it is used. Then let the break show: a bomb is a technology whose value lies in not being used, so the governance the analogy recommends — secrecy, a monopoly, a test ban, a deterrent equilibrium — has no counterpart in a capability whose value is entirely in its being used, and which arrives as a gradient of many systems rather than one threshold event.
- The printing press. Grant the strong version: it is the analogy that gets the mechanism right — cheap replication of a cognitive artefact, the collapse of a gatekeeper's cost advantage, consequences mediated by literacy and institutions rather than by the hardware itself. Then let the break show: the press multiplied the distribution of thought while leaving its production to human beings, and it did not print the next press.
- The Industrial Revolution. Grant the strong version: it is the analogy that gets the economics right — a general-purpose technology whose measured gains lag the invention by decades, because the real gains come from the complementary reinvestment and reorganisation (the factory, not the frame) and because the diffusion of a technology is slower than the diffusion of the machine. Then let the break show: steam and mechanisation substituted for muscle and dexterity, not for the judgement about what to do with muscle and dexterity, and the displaced skill was not the deciding skill.

Two distinctions from my own notes that I would like the poem to carry, without naming them as distinctions.

First, the tempo mismatch. Every one of the three cases pairs a speed of spread with a speed of consequence, and the pairings differ: the bomb was recognised at once and then deliberately not spread; the press spread within a generation and took a century to matter; the engine was in the factories long before it was in the productivity statistics. Each stanza should let a reader feel both tempos of its analogy without the poem ever using the words fast or slow.

Second, the direction of substitution. The press substituted for copying, steam for muscle, the bomb for nothing at all — it added a capacity rather than replacing a worker. None of the three substituted for the activity of deciding what to do, which is precisely the activity this workshop consists in. That is the thought I want the envoi to carry: the six words gathered into three lines, no longer rotating, and the recognition that the missing analogy is the one in which the thing being replaced is the thing doing the replacing.

What I do not want: a poem that resolves into alarm or into reassurance, a poem that ranks the analogies, or a poem whose last move is a shrug. The three should still be in the room with each other at the end. It has to be a poem first — someone who has never thought about any of this should be able to read it as a poem about people meeting something they have made — but someone who has followed the debate should be able to say, for each stanza, which analogy is speaking, what it is claiming, and where it is quietly conceding. Unrhymed, no fixed metre, line length yours to vary; keep to roughly the traditional thirty-nine lines, and keep the diction plain enough to be read aloud to a room of historians, economists and machine-learning researchers without anyone needing a footnote.
