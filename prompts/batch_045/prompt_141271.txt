At dinner after a department colloquium on rational choice, two of my colleagues got into an argument that I have not been able to settle for myself, and I would be grateful for a careful adjudication of it.

The case they were arguing about: Sam is choosing between three flats, A, B and C, and his preferences are cyclic. He prefers B to A, C to B, and A to C. Each of those is a settled, all-things-considered judgment on his part; they simply do not line up.

1. Owen opened with the money pump. Suppose a letting agency controls all three flats. From A it offers Sam B for a fee small enough that the exchange still looks like an improvement to him; once he is in B it offers him C on the same terms; once he is in C it offers him A. Sam accepts each time, since each exchange is one his own preferences endorse, and after three moves he is back in A and poorer by three fees. The agency can then run the same cycle again, and again. So, Owen concluded, cyclic preferences are irrational, and rationality requires transitivity.

2. Nadia replied that the argument only works on a myopic Sam. A Sam who sees the cycle coming can simply decline the first exchange and stay in A at no cost. Better still, he can take the first exchange and then stop, ending up in B for one trivial fee, which by his own lights is an improvement on A. What the pump shows, she said, is that failing to look ahead is expensive. It shows nothing about the rationality of the preferences themselves.

3. Owen's answer was that the trouble is not myopia but that there is nowhere to arrive. Any policy of the form move k times and then stay put is one Sam will want to abandon at exactly the point where he is supposed to stay put: in B he prefers C, and in C he prefers A. And never move is no better, because moving once and then staying put would leave him better off than staying in A — it is just that once he has moved he cannot then stay put. So there is no stopping policy that his own preferences, applied step by step, will let him carry out. Foresight does not rescue him; it reveals that the sequence has no resting place. Avoiding the pump therefore requires either moving forever or sticking to a policy that at every step looks to him like an unnecessary loss — which is to say, a form of self-command whose availability the argument was supposed not to assume.

4. Nadia then reversed the direction of payment. Suppose the agency is paid a commission by the landlord on every reassignment and is happy to share part of that commission with Sam in order to get him to move: it offers him B plus ten pounds, then C plus ten pounds, then A plus ten pounds, with the landlord footing the bill. Sam runs exactly the same cycle and ends each round in A, thirty pounds richer. His preferences are unchanged, the cycling is unchanged, and the exploitation has turned into a money fountain. So, she argued, what the pump really shows is that whoever controls the sequence of offers determines where Sam ends up, and whether that works out well or badly for him depends on what the counterparty happens to want — a fact about his environment rather than a defect in his preferences. Nor is exploitability as such a mark of irrationality: a monopoly landlord extracts money from a tenant whose preferences are perfectly coherent, and we do not call that tenant irrational for paying.

5. Owen replied that the sweetener case is one in which somebody else's project happens to require Sam to move and he is paid for it; it is not one in which his intransitivity earns him anything. What intransitivity contributes in both versions of the case is that it makes him movable indefinitely and cheaply — a tenant with transitive preferences can be induced to move as far as his best available option and then stops, whereas Sam can be cycled without end, which is why the agency has a business model in the first place. Whether the resulting flow of money runs toward him or away from him is then settled by the institutions around him, and a requirement of rationality is supposed to be one you can satisfy before you know which way it will run. And if the alternative is that Sam resolves never to transact, or to stop after a single move, that is precisely the policy his own preferences will not let him execute.

I would like a point-by-point verdict. For each of the five moves, say how strong it is, on a scale running from clearly wrong through defensible but incomplete to clearly right, and justify the verdict in a sentence or two. Then say who is more right overall. Finally, I suspect both of them have missed something structural about what an argument of this shape can establish — in particular, whether a money-pump argument could ever establish transitivity as a requirement on the preferences one holds at a time, as opposed to a requirement on how one conducts oneself over time, and whether the argument needs an adversary with exploitative intentions at all rather than merely an unlucky sequence of opportunities. I would like you to say what that missed point is.
