Eight months ago I was appointed as one of the two lay members of the clinical ethics committee at a large teaching hospital, and I have been asked to run a forty-five minute session for the committee on how we ought to reason when we cannot settle which moral framework is the right one. Our live docket is whether to fund one more place on a very expensive therapy that produces a large benefit for a single patient, or to spend the same money on a change to discharge and follow-up procedures that produces a very small benefit for a very large number of patients. I have to write the recommendation, so this is not academic for me.

The committee is thirteen people: five physicians, two nurses, a hospital lawyer, a chaplain, a health economist, two lay members and the chair. Nobody has any philosophy training, and several are openly impatient with anything that will not change what we do on Monday. I have a whiteboard and a single one-page handout I will write from your plan, and I do not want to hand out or cite any readings.

Do not explain the topic to me. Give me a plan for explaining it, covering the following.

The order the ideas must come in, and for each step what it depends on in the previous step: in particular, where the distinction between uncertainty about the facts and uncertainty about the framework has to be introduced, and why it cannot wait until later.

The one case that should carry the whole session, built from our docket. Tell me how to construct it so that three frameworks genuinely come apart on it — an aggregating one, one that gives priority to the worst off, and one that treats certain side-constraints as not tradeable at all — and tell me what the case must be stripped of. My instinct is to remove every bit of empirical uncertainty, so that nothing in the disagreement is about how the world is, and also to remove any vividly described named patient; tell me if the second removal is a mistake.

The misconceptions, in the order the committee will form them, and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. I anticipate these: that the middle option is the neutral or fair response; that one should average the frameworks' verdicts the way one would average two experts' probability estimates; that uncertainty about which framework is correct is just another empirical uncertainty and so expected-value reasoning settles it in the obvious way; and that if the frameworks cannot be ranked, any decision is arbitrary and we should simply vote. Say which ones I should let surface before answering, and which would poison the rest of the hour if voiced early.

The hardest thing I need them to see: that the frameworks may not share a scale, so that "how much better is this than that, according to framework one" is not automatically comparable to the same question for framework two, and that our answer on the docket may shift depending on how the scales are lined up. Where in the arc must this go, what has to be in place before it can be said without baffling people, and how do I present it so it does not read as a licence to do whatever we liked anyway?

One thing I should openly refuse to explain at this level, announcing that I am not explaining it. My instinct is that it should be how weights get assigned to the frameworks in the first place, but tell me if something else is the better omission.

A check I can run in the last five minutes that distinguishes understanding the structure from having learned my vocabulary. I would like it to be a new case with the property that the compromise action is ranked last, or near last, by every framework on the table, so that someone who has grasped the point refuses to split the difference and someone who has not does it confidently.

Finally, one complication I want handled rather than set aside: our uncertainty is not only inside each person's head. Different members are confident in different frameworks and will not change their minds in an hour, so for us the question is as much what a body should do when its members' moral convictions differ as what an individual should do when their own do. Tell me whether to build the session around the individual case and translate at the end, or to run both from the outset, and what is lost either way.
