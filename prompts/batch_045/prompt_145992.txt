I write a weekly column on science and ideas, and maybe twice a week I also get handed someone else's piece for the fact-check pass before it goes up — twenty minutes, no paywall access, and whatever I write has to survive a read by our lawyer. The thing I keep running into, and keep bodging, is claims that are neither true nor false. I want a procedure, not a theory essay.

Some of what I'm dealing with. A source says, in the same breath as his denial: "what I am telling you right now is not true." A press office sends over a line to run as a pull quote: "no major outlet has ever accurately reported this meeting" — in a piece that would itself be a major outlet reporting the meeting. A sympathetic researcher, whose data I've seen and believe, ends her statement with "my account of the incident is accurate," and I want to be able to write "she's telling the truth" and I suspect I can't. Plus the ordinary mush: "hundreds of scientists," "the treatment works," "experts say," and the self-exempting kind — "any attempt to check this claim is itself part of the disinformation" — which I've started calling shields in my notes. My desk slang: loopers for claims whose truth depends on themselves, shields for claims that try to disqualify their own evaluation, fuzzies for the vague ones.

What I half remember from one philosophy elective: something like Tarski on not being able to call sentences in your own language true, and the idea that some sentences just sit in a gap, and that "not true" is not the same as "false," and that if you patch that by saying "not true" you get a new version of the same problem back. If any of that is wrong, correct it, but only as much as the procedure needs.

What I want is something my desk can actually run in twenty minutes on a claim, in this order: what to ask first, what counts as a good enough answer at each step, and which verdict to print. Specifically: when the verdict is a plain true or false; when it's "no truth value, and here's the one-sentence way to say that to readers without sounding like a philosophy major"; when the only honest move is to quote the sentence and describe its shape instead of grading it; and when the procedure has to stop and hand the thing to an editor because it's a values call, not a factual one — I think the false-versus-misleading choice is one of those, tell me if it is. Say explicitly where judgement can't be removed, and what the checklist refuses to rule on (I don't need it to adjudicate claims about the future, and it shouldn't try).

Then run it on the three above, and add one case where the answer you get is one I won't like — I have a feeling it's the researcher one, where I want to defend her and the procedure won't let me print that she's telling the truth. And at the end, the two or three sentences I should put in the style guide so this is consistent across writers who've never thought about any of this. No bibliography, I don't need citations, just the procedure.
