You are a professional moral philosopher who works both on contractualism and on the ethics of cost-effectiveness reasoning in public health and philanthropy. Please think this through carefully and answer in two passes: first, reconstruct the argument below in your own words and sketch every way you can see of rejecting or defusing it, along with what each option costs; then commit to the diagnosis you think is strongest, and say what a non-specialist reader should take away from the whole exchange.

I'm a journalist writing a long-form piece about how institutions decide which causes to fund — global health, disaster preparedness, AI safety — and about the philosophical discomfort with the cost-effectiveness framework, which routinely treats a very large number of small benefits as outweighing one person's catastrophic loss. A philosopher I interviewed walked me through the following argument. I can't find the mistake in it, but I'm suspicious of it, because it seems to prove far too much: as far as I can tell the same template could be run against rights-based views, against incommensurability views, against anything that takes the separateness of persons seriously. I think I may have built a machine that eats every non-consequentialist morality, and I want to know which gear is slipping.

(1) [Contractualism] An act is wrong if and only if it would be disallowed by any principle that no one could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced general agreement.
(2) [Individualist restriction] Whether a principle can be reasonably rejected is settled by comparing, one person at a time, the burden that principle imposes on her with the burdens alternative principles would impose on other people. Nothing is added up across persons.
(3) [Numbers drop out] It follows from (2) that the mere number of people who would bear a burden does no work. Only how each of them fares, measured against how others fare under the alternatives, matters.
(4) [The case] A funder can either save one identifiable person from death or prevent a painful but minor chronic condition in a hundred thousand people. Take the principle: always prevent a death when a death is preventable. Each of the hundred thousand has a complaint; the person who dies has, on any ordering of burdens, a much larger complaint under the alternative. By (3), the hundred thousand complaints carry no additional weight. So the principle is not reasonably rejectable, and the funder is required to save the one.
(5) [Absurdity] That verdict is not merely counterintuitive. It dismantles the entire practice of weighing many small gains against a few large losses, which is essentially what cost-effectiveness analysis is.
(6) [The dichotomy] So numbers must be allowed to count. But the only way to let numbers count is to sum burdens across persons, and a view that sums burdens across persons is utilitarianism in a contractualist costume: whatever was distinctively non-aggregative in (1) and (2) has evaporated.
(7) Therefore contractualism is either absurd, as in (4), or extensionally utilitarian, as in (6). Either way it is not the view it advertises itself as.

Three escape routes have been suggested to me and I trust none of them; please price each one.

(a) Numbers re-enter inside a single person's complaint. What grounds my reason to reject a principle is not only the size of my own burden but the relational fact that the principle would spare me only by imposing comparable or lesser burdens on vastly more people — an unfairness-shaped complaint whose strength grows with the number of others without any summing of burdens. Does this simply smuggle in objecting on other people's behalf, which (2) was supposed to forbid?

(b) Lexicalism by kind rather than by size. Contractualism never compares across incommensurable kinds of burden; death and chronic pain are not on one scale. Within a kind, numbers count normally; across kinds the comparison is not resolved in favour of the one but is simply indeterminate, so (4) should yield no verdict rather than an absurd one. Does a view that falls silent exactly when the stakes are highest still count as a guide to a funder who must act anyway?

(c) The unit of assessment is wrong. Contractualism assesses principles, and a principle must be general enough to serve as a basis of agreement across an open-ended range of cases. Step (4) gerrymanders a principle to a single decision, and always prevent a death when a death is preventable has implications elsewhere that plenty of people could reasonably reject. The absurdity in (5) is thus an artefact of how the case was built. Does this relocate the same problem to the level of whole principles, where the argument simply reappears?

Which step do you reject, and why did it look sound? If I want to keep the individualist bite of (2) and (3) — the part that makes contractualism something other than utilitarianism — what exactly am I committed to, and does a contractualist who accepts that commitment still have anything useful to say to someone allocating a budget? Finally: is my worry that the argument generalises into a refutation of every non-aggregative view correct, or is there something specific to contractualism that makes it vulnerable here?
