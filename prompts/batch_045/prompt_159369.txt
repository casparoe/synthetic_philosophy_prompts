I convene a fortnightly reading group — roughly half epistemologists, half philosophers of language — and we have just spent several sessions on what norm, if any, governs assertion. The standard handouts for this topic are useless to me: they line up three or four candidate norms, attach a famous name to each, and stop there, so readers end up choosing whichever label sounds most plausible instead of finding out what they already think. I want a single instrument that sorts people by their verdicts on cases and only then tells them which norm they have committed themselves to. I also want it built from the logical space rather than from a list of views: first the branching questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then the occupied cells located in that space, with empty cells named and briefly diagnosed (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored). Please keep proper names to a minimum — my readers switch off when a handout turns into a name-dropping contest — but do give each occupied leaf its usual label: knowledge norm, truth norm, reasonable- or justified-belief norm, warrant norm, stakes-sensitive and interest-relative variants of these, the audience- or context-relativist versions, and the deflationary view that assertion needs no norm of its own beyond the intention to get someone to believe something.

The instrument should come in two parts. Part A is a short decision tree over the meta-questions, each phrased so that a student can answer it without knowing where it leads: whether a norm of assertion is constitutive of the speech act or merely one standard among several by which assertions can be criticised; whether violating it makes the assertion incorrect in the way an ineligible move in a game is incorrect, or merely makes the speaker blameworthy, or neither; whether the required epistemic property is binary or admits degrees that track practical stakes; whether the norm is uniform across subject matter and institutional setting or is relativised to the practice in which the assertion is made; whether it attaches to the speaker's standing relation to the content or can shift with the audience's epistemic position; and whether a satisfactory norm must be one the speaker can tell, from the inside and without further investigation, that she meets. Part B is a ten-item quiz of concrete cases, each with three verdict options — defective even if blameless, in order, or not defective but pointless and infelicitous, the trouble being pragmatic rather than normative. Please use cases of roughly these kinds, reshaped as you like: an environmentally unlucky justified true belief; a bare statistical-evidence claim of the lottery sort; a true and well-evidenced claim asserted once the stakes of being wrong have been raised; an impeccably justified claim that turns out false; a self-fulfilling future-tense assertion of one's own intention, where saying it helps settle whether it is true; a redundant assertion of something the audience already knows; a borderline-vague or under-precise claim whose content seems to lack a determinate truth value; an assertion issued by an institution or automated system where no individual person is in the required epistemic position; a hedged assertion whose speaker explicitly disclaims the stronger property; and an assertion made inside a practice with different aims, such as testimony under examination or a clinician's prognosis given to a patient who has asked to be told.

The scoring key should map patterns of answers onto leaves rather than treating any single item as decisive, should say what to do with tied or internally inconsistent patterns, and should include a small table recording which pair of norms each item is there to pull apart. Where your tree cannot separate two neighbouring positions — I suspect the knowledge norm and any no-luck-style surrogates for it, and possibly the truth norm and the knowledge norm on hedged or vague contents, will be hard to prise apart — say so explicitly and suggest what further case would be needed, rather than manufacturing a distinction the cases do not support.
