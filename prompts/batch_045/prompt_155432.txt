Hi — I chair the grants committee of the Larkspur Trust, a tiny foundation (about £400k a year) that funds animal-welfare work in our city. I came to this from a public-health background — vector control, of all things — so I'm fine with the rodent biology but completely out of my depth on the ethics, and we're split down the middle, so I need something I can circulate before Thursday's meeting.

We have two proposals left and money for roughly one. Proposal A is from a small rescue: veterinary care, sterilisation and supported feeding for an established colony of about 120 feral cats behind a supermarket — animals that already exist and will keep existing whatever we do. Proposal B is the one causing the fight. A group working on wild rodent welfare proposes sealing the voids under a disused dockside warehouse and putting out contraceptive bait; no rat is killed, the current ~600 live out their lives, but they estimate it prevents something like 40,000 births over three years, and their argument is explicitly suffering-focused: a wild rat's life is on balance bad (starvation, injury, parasites, being eaten), each birth prevented is a whole life's worth of suffering that simply never happens, nobody is made worse off because the rat who isn't conceived isn't deprived of anything, and so prevention is a pure gain in a way that treating the cats never is — that old Popper-ish line about the job being to minimise suffering rather than maximise happiness implies, they say, that we should be funding prevention and little else, and that our cat program is basically cosmetic because it leaves the underlying arithmetic untouched.

Here's the knot: our donor's 1998 deed says the money is to be used for "the relief of suffering in animals" and specifically bars funding "the destruction of animal life." B's critics on the committee say that means B might be outside our mission entirely — no existing animal's suffering gets relieved, there's just fewer animals later — while other members say that reading makes the charter hostile to the most effective thing we could possibly do. Two more wrinkles you should probably deal with: our one ecologist points out that the net-negative claim rests on about three weeks of camera-trap footage and the uncertainty easily spans zero, and that the rats are the main prey for a barn owl pair nesting on the site, so fewer rats may mean fewer owls — which the applicants cheerfully count as an additional gain, and which is what set off our vet, who thinks the whole framing has stopped being about helping animals. What I need is a short decision memo: recommend a concrete course — fund A, fund B, or fund B with conditions — with a time frame, reasons the applicants could actually respond to (I don't want to just tell them no), and an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side, including, if you land on B, the worry that we've just signed up to a logic whose natural endpoint is fertility suppression and eventually extinction as the actual agenda. Please give me a real bottom line rather than "both have merits" — I have to put something to a vote.
