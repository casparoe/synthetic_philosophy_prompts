I'm applying for a twelve-month dissertation fellowship and need a two-page research proposal I could hand to a committee in political theory or historical sociology. The area is Weber's thesis about ascetic Protestantism and the *spirit* of modern capitalism, but I do not want the proposal to be about whether that thesis is historically true. I want it to be about one knot in the logic of the thesis: what kind of claim is it, such that it could be true or false at all, and what would have to be shown to establish it.

My half-formed idea is that the long-running fight over whether culture or values can be a cause is stalled because it is three claims wearing one coat, and the people arguing are usually defending or attacking different ones.

The first is an asymmetry claim under intervention: in the relevant historical setting, a change in doctrine or devotional practice would have moved economic conduct more than a comparable change in economic structure or legal form would have moved doctrine. Note that this is not the same as saying ideas matter, which almost everyone concedes; it says something about which difference-making relation is the stronger one in that setting.

The second is a worry rather than a thesis, and it is the one I keep coming back to: outcome contamination. The thing to be explained is partly constituted by the attitudes whose causal origin is at issue — a disposition to treat gain as a duty, to order one's whole life methodically, to reinvest rather than consume. If the effect is specified in attitudinal terms and the cause is also an attitude or value-representation, the thesis threatens to collapse into the claim that certain attitudes produced attitudes of the same kind, with observable economic behaviour demoted to a symptom of something already explained. So the second question is whether the thesis can be restated with a non-attitudinal outcome — behaviour, bookkeeping practice, reinvestment rates, firm longevity, the legal form of enterprise — and still be Weber's thesis rather than a different and weaker one, and whether Weber's own object of explanation, an ethos, is the sort of thing that can figure as the effect of a cause at all.

The third is a transmission claim across generations. Whatever causal work the doctrine does, it has to keep working after the belief weakens — later generations behave as the first generation's theology prescribed without holding the theology. It matters a great deal whether what is transmitted is a belief, a disposition, a practice, or an institutional arrangement, because these have different causal profiles, different degrees of visibility to the agents themselves, and different decay rates; and if what is transmitted is a disposition or a practice rather than a belief, it is a further question whether the resulting claim is still a claim about culture as a cause in the sense the debate is about, or has quietly become a claim about the persistence of institutions.

What I want the proposal to do is decide which of these three claims is load-bearing and argue for the choice. Concretely: state the question so that it has a determinate answer; explain why each of the two standard positions in the fight — the one that treats ideas as reflexes of material interests with no causal power of their own, and the one that treats the interpretation of meaning as a primitive in social explanation rather than something reducible to covariation — leaves that question open; and set out the method phase by phase across the twelve months, distinguishing what would be reconstructed from historical material from what would be argued on conceptual grounds.

I also want the method to commit to two devices, which you should improve or reject if they are weak. The first is a resolution test: the mechanism must be stateable at the scale of a single household's or workshop's weekly decisions — what this believer declines to spend, what records he keeps, what work he takes on or refuses, how he treats an unexpected surplus — on the principle that a causal story about values that cannot be cashed at that resolution has not yet said anything. The second is a double contrast: communities matched on region, legal status and occupational mix but differing in confession, and, within a single confession, stricter and laxer congregations or successive phases of the same congregation, so that attribution to doctrinal content rather than to social position, network effects, or self-selection can be argued for with something with the shape of a difference in differences instead of a narrative of elective affinity.

Then: say what the possible conclusions are and which considerations would decide between them; say what would count as the project failing, where failing means failing as a dissertation rather than merely yielding a negative result — for instance, if it turns out that the thesis cannot be restated with a behavioural outcome without ceasing to be the thesis Weber was advancing, is that a refutation of the thesis, or a clarification of what sort of claim it always was? And give an honest assessment of scope: is all three claims too much for one year, and if one has to be cut, which, and what does the project lose by cutting it?

Two constraints. Do not spend space on a literature review or on attributing positions to named authors; I will add the citations myself and I would rather the two pages go to the argument. And assume the committee knows the standard apparatus — elective affinity, the metaphor of ideas as switchmen setting the tracks along which interests move, ideal-typical construction, the distinction between value-relevance and value-judgment, and the demand that adequate causation be more than statistical relevance — so use those materials rather than explaining them.
