I have no training in philosophy or economics; I read about this in the evenings and argue about it with people who are equally untrained, and I have come to think that most argument about the undeserving poor is resting on a picture nobody states out loud. The picture is insurance. Contributions are premiums, the taxpaying population is the risk pool, job loss or illness is the insured event, claiming is filing a claim, and the person who draws on a scheme he never paid into, or whose own idleness brought the loss about, is a free rider on everyone who did pay in. In my circles the picture is doing real argumentative work: it is why conditionality is thought to need no justification, why benefit fraud sounds like a serious crime rather than a bookkeeping failure, and why making people work for their benefit sounds reasonable rather than punitive. I want to know how much of that work the picture can actually bear.

So: audit the analogy. Give me the correspondence table first — what plays the premium, the pool, the insured event, the claim, the loss assessor, the exclusion for self-inflicted loss, the duty to mitigate, the fraud investigation — and say which of those have any counterpart in a modern means-tested system with work-search conditions and sanctions, and which have none at all. (By means-tested I mean you qualify by being poor rather than by having paid in; I take it that most of the fighting is over that kind, not over the contributory kind.) Then the disanalogies, and specifically the ones that bear on the three conclusions the picture is wheeled in to support: that conditions on benefit are fair as such, that fraud enforcement is worth what it costs, and that work-for-benefit is reasonable as such.

Four places where my own thinking has got stuck, and which I would like you to take seriously rather than smooth over.

The first is what I have started calling the desert switch. Desert here seems to be assessed two different ways: a membership test (you paid in, so you are covered, and what you get is yours by right) and a conduct test (you brought this on yourself, or you are not really trying). Public argument slides between them constantly. My suspicion is that the insurance picture genuinely licenses the first and only borrows the second, and that the borrowed half is doing most of the work in talk about the undeserving. Is that right? And note that real insurance is not purely membership-based either — it excludes losses you caused and refuses to cover obvious carelessness — so I want to know how far the conduct test is actually inside the analogy rather than smuggled in beside it.

The second is a category the picture seems to have no room for: the entitled person who never claims. I call it the silent claimant. In insurance this is unremarkable and even welcome — a policyholder who does not claim after a covered loss is no one's problem, and certainly no one's free rider. In welfare it is treated as a policy failure, and in some quarters as a budgetary saving. That asymmetry seems to me to be telling, but I cannot say what it tells.

The third is what I think of as the hazard problem. An insurance pool is normally joined voluntarily and can be left; here membership is compulsory and cannot be exited, and the same institution that writes the policy also controls the hazard — through macroeconomic policy, and through the design of the conditions themselves, it affects both how many people are out of work and how many of them count as looking. In insurance terms it is as though the insurer set the terms of cover and also determined how often houses burn down. Since the point of what I have seen called moral hazard is precisely that the insured's behaviour responds to the terms of cover, I want to know what the analogy says about an insurer in that position, and whether it says anything at all.

The fourth is a category I have no good name for. Call it compliant non-participation: someone who satisfies every stated requirement of a work-search condition — the appointments, the logged applications, the paperwork — while doing nothing that could plausibly result in employment. The deserving/undeserving split has no settled place for them, and the insurance picture, which mostly asks whether the event happened and whether you caused it, does not seem to either. I would like a verdict on them from inside the analogy if one can be had, and an admission that there is none if it cannot.

Then two general questions. What does the picture quietly import that the target lacks — actuarial fairness, the idea of a claim as an entitlement rather than a petition, the idea that every pound paid out comes out of identifiable other people's pockets, the idea of the loss as something that just happens to you? I expect at least one of these imports cuts against the harsher uses of the analogy rather than for them, and I want that made explicit rather than left for me to find. And where is the picture simply silent — about dignity and humiliation, about what the transfer is for if it is not risk-pooling, about whether the state's interest is in the poverty of recipients or in the visible compliance of the population? Say where it is silent rather than making it speak.

Finish by telling me whether the three conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying them the whole time. If conditionality survives in any form, say what shape it has to take to survive — incentive-shaped, cheap to comply with, non-moralized, or something else — since that is the part that would actually change how I argue. If a different analogy does the job better, offer it; if the honest answer is that no comparison carries this, say so and say why.

Two ground rules. I have no way of looking anything up, so please give me no reading, no names to follow up, and no figures to check: argue from how these arrangements plainly work. And I do not want anything to turn on disputed statistics. Assume only that administrative error and unclaimed entitlement are each, in money terms, at least as large as deliberate fraud; if you think that is wrong, say so in one sentence and then continue without resting anything on it.
