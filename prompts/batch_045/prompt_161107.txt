I work as a systematist at a natural history museum, and I've been asked to write a short position paper on species delimitation that our curators can read before we revise the collection database. The point of the paper is to make explicit what our delimitation protocol already assumes, because our lists feed conservation assessments and because the same group of specimens keeps getting different counts depending on who is doing the counting. Before I write it, I want an audit of the commitments I've ended up with, since I suspect they don't all sit together. Here is the set, as I would state it:

1. Species are individuals rather than classes or kinds: they are historical, spatiotemporally bounded particulars with origins and terminations, and an organism is related to its species more as a part to a whole than as an instance to a property.
2. Pluralism: no single species concept is uniquely correct. Interfertility, ecological distinctness, and genealogical divergence are each legitimate, and applied to one group of organisms they can yield different, equally correct species counts.
3. Species are real and mind-independent — not stipulated by our interests or our convenience — even though nothing essential in the intrinsic-property sense fixes membership. What makes an organism a member of a species is a genealogical relation, and that relation holds whether or not we ever track it.
4. My working criterion is the general lineage conception, and I require a species taxon to be monophyletic: a group that is paraphyletic with respect to another is not a species.
5. Some boundaries are genuinely indeterminate. In ring-species complexes and in hybridizing swarms there is no fact of the matter how many species are present.
6. Two verdicts I don't want to give up: first, the polar bear and the brown bear are two species even granting, for the sake of the audit, that the molecular evidence makes brown bears as usually circumscribed paraphyletic with respect to the polar bear; and relatedly, an ancestral species can survive the budding-off of a peripheral isolate and remain the same species it was. Second, asexual lineages — bacteria, bdelloid rotifers — have species in the same sense sexual ones do, and recognising them is not illegitimate inflation of the species rank.

A colleague tells me (1) and (5) are inconsistent, and I don't think that's right, but I also can't say why.

Please audit the set rather than survey the literature; I don't need references. Rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say precisely which commitment has to give and what giving it up would cost me downstream in the paper. Say which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection and what the dissolving move is. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and whether the practical need for one stable public list adds a further commitment I haven't stated. For each judgement, state how confident you are that it is a real tension, and single out the judgement you are least confident in; also flag anywhere your answer depends on an empirical claim about which organisms I haven't given you enough information.
