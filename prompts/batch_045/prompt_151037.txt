My neighborhood has spent four months fighting a 40-unit building proposed for a corner lot that's currently one house on a big yard. The project isn't asking for anything exotic — the height and density are roughly what the city's own plan says belongs there — but because it needs a rezoning, there have been three public hearings where anyone who shows up gets to speak, and the council treats sustained neighborhood opposition as a reason to kill it. I own a place two blocks away, Ive been to all three, and I keep coming back to something I can't straighten out.

The neighbors who get a say here get no say at all over a family of eight buying the house next door and filling it with kids and cars, or over someone turning that house into a loud home workshop — effects pretty similar to the ones they say they're worried about. And the people who'd actually live in the new building have no standing; most of them don't exist to the process yet, or they rent a basement across town. I do, because I bought in 2019. A friend of mine has started calling this temporal feudalism: the idea that what gives you authority over a place isn't use, or need, or membership in a community, but just having gotten there first, and that the entitlement runs with the built form rather than with the people in it. Meanwhile the same city runs ads welcoming newcomers.

So: what does this setup presuppose about who has a right to the city? I want the implicit theory reconstructed — about property, about who counts as a stakeholder with standing, about whether a neighborhood is a thing with interests of its own over and above the people in it — and I want to know which parts of that theory the practice strictly commits you to versus merely goes well with, and where several quite different theories would license the exact same practice. If the arrival-time reading is roughly right, is there any version of it that isn't just an apology for incumbency?
