I've been reading about transcendental arguments for a seminar and I keep feeling like I don't know what I'm supposed to be asking of one — everyone scores them with a different rubric and nobody says which rubric they're using. So before I read any more of them I want the spec.

My rough picture, correct it if it's off: a transcendental argument starts from something about us — that we have experience at all, that we can identify and re-identify objects over time, that we can even formulate a doubt — and argues that some further claim is a necessary condition of that, so that the claim gets a necessity ordinary empirical arguments can't deliver. Usually pointed at some skeptic. Is that roughly the thing?

What I actually want: the list of requirements an adequate transcendental argument has to meet — the cases it has to handle, the distinctions it can't collapse, the moves it isn't allowed to make — with the consideration behind each, plus your view on which are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's prejudices. I care much more about the list than about scoring particular arguments.

Things I've seen people demand that I can't tell apart as real requirements vs. tribal preferences:

- the starting point has to be something the skeptic themself grants (and does "grants" mean they beleive it, or that they're committed to it just by raising the doubt?)
- it has to be fully a priori, no empirical premise smuggled in
- the conclusion has to be about the world, not merely about what we're constrained to believe
- the necessity has to be real necessity, not just "we can't picture it otherwise"
- it can't quietly lean on verificationism
- it has to rule the skeptical alternative out, not just make it look unappealing
- it shouldn't be reducible to inference to the best explanation with an a priori sheen on top
- the same argument shape should generalize — external-world skeptic, other minds, the Humean about induction — instead of being custom-built for one adversary
- and I don't only mean the anti-skeptical ones: some conclude something about the self, or about what's constitutive of agency or judgment. Does one list cover those too?

Two worries I want handled head-on. I'm naming them so we can refer back:

"Modal laundering" — the step from "we cannot but take P to be true" (or "you couldn't so much as raise that doubt unless P") to "P is true," or worse to "P is necessarily true." Something has to do the work in the middle: what would count as an acceptable bridge and what's just a handshake? Is the bridge ever more than a verificationist principle in disguise? And does it change if the conclusion is read as being about our conceptual scheme rather than about mind-independent reality?

"Adversary relativity" — is it a defect if an argument works against only one skeptic, or is a transcendental argument that worked against every skeptical scenario at once necessarily cheating? My hunch is that the "generalize to all adversaries" requirement quietly kills more otherwise decent arguments than any other, but I'm not sure.

Also: does the list change with what the argument is *for*? I can think of at least three jobs — actually refuting the skeptic, showing the doubt is unmotivated or idle, and just making explicit what some discourse or practice already presupposes. I'd guess the first job makes the set of requirements jointly unsatisfiable and the second makes it satisfiable; I want to know where the break falls and which requirement you'd drop first.

If some subset genuinely can't be satisfied together — either because of the classic "you've only shown we must believe it, not that it's true" objection, or because the premise set a skeptic will grant is never rich enough to yield a substantive conclusion — say so explicitly and show the squeeze. That's the part I most want to see.

Then, cheaply, two lines each: how Kant's transcendental deduction and the Strawson-style arguments from identification do against your list. I'll build the real scorecard myself. Table or list, whatever's clearer.
