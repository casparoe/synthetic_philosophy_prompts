I have no academic background at all, I work in freight logistics and got into this stuff from a blog post about Wittgenstein's last set of notes, the ones published after he died as On Certainty, and the idea that some of the things I'm most sure of aren't things I have evidence for at all — they're the thing my evidence sits on. I've been chewing on it on my own for maybe a year and I keep feeling like I can tell a hinge when I see one but I can't say what I'm seeing, and every account I read seems to get one case right and quietly ignore another.

So before I read any more accounts of this, I want the spec: what does a theory of these ungrounded ground-floor certainties actually have to get right? Give me the requirement list, with the case behind each requirement, and mark which ones are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one camp. Keep the weight on the criteria, not on scoring the theories — you can briefly say how the three big pictures do (hinges as rules of the game rather than moves in it; hinges as just very basic beliefs we can't help having; hinges as commitments that aren't even the kind of thing that's true or false) but I care most about the list itself.

Cases I think it has to handle, you can add your own:

- The guy who held up his hands and claimed he knew they were hands, versus a fact I'm extremely sure of from evidence, like that my city is north of the equator. Those feel different in kind, not just in degree, and I want to know what makes them different.
- Things nobody has ever bothered to doubt but that clearly aren't hinges, like the number of screws in my bike's derailleur cage. Being unquestioned can't be the whole story.
- Things that moved in and out: that the earth goes around the sun used to be a live, dangerous claim and now it's part of the furniture for me. What is it for something to migrate like that, and can a hinge go back to being contestable?
- Kids. I was raised by people who took astrology seriously and by eighteen I thought it was nonsense, and I genuinely cannot point to the argument or the afternoon when it flipped. So: how do hinges get installed, and what happens when the people who installed them were unreliable?
- Whole groups with different furniture. Two communities whose ground-floor stuff clashes, and each side's members think the other side is not just mistaken but broken. Does a theory owe me any non-question-begging way to talk about that, or is it allowed to just say there's nothing more to say?
- Whether a hinge can be false. Some accounts seem to build in that it can't, and that feels like cheating to me, but I can't say why.

Here are three tests I came up with on my own and I can't work out whether they're three tests or one test in a costume. A hinge is: something where I'd sooner distrust my own doubting than distrust it; something I can't argue for without already assuming something equally big; something where, if you denied it, I'd conclude you were confused or playing a language game wrong rather than that I might be wrong. Do these ever come apart, and which one is doing the real work?

Two last things: flag any pair of requirements you suspect can't both be satisfied, because if the list itself turns out to be impossible that's the part I most want to know. And tell me which single requirement knocks out the most otherwise-attractive accounts. A table is fine if that's easier. Sorry if some of this is half-baked, I'm defintely making up my own vocabulary as I go.
