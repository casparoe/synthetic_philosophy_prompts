I've been working through the question of whether people should be allowed to be paid for kidneys, and I built an argument for my own side that now seems to prove much more than I believe. I'd like to know which step to give up, and what giving it up costs me.

(1) If it is permissible for someone to do A for nothing, then it is permissible for them to do A in exchange for a benefit. It cannot be that adding a benefit to an already permitted act makes that act forbidden — otherwise tips, thank-you gifts, medals, and hospitality would all become suspect.

(2) It is permissible to donate a kidney, to give blood, to carry a pregnancy for someone else, to vote for the candidate one prefers, to testify honestly about what one saw, to have consensual sex, and to care for one's own children.

(3) So each of these may permissibly be done for payment.

(4) If each individual may permissibly do A for payment, then a market in A is permissible: a market is just many individuals each doing what each of them is permitted to do, and nothing happens in the market that does not already happen in some individual transaction.

(5) A market in votes, a market in the custody of one's own child, and a market in sexual access are all simply instances of (4).

(6) But each of those is wrong — not merely distasteful, wrong.

(7) Therefore at least one of (1) through (5) is false.

The trouble is that I believe (1) and (2) quite firmly and (6) even more firmly, so I keep coming back to (4) — and yet I cannot say what is wrong with (4). It really does look as though a market is nothing over and above the individual transactions that make it up.

Two things make it worse. First, I can run the argument in reverse on myself: a judge who may permissibly sentence a guilty defendant may, by (1), permissibly sentence him in exchange for a bribe — which is absurd. But the only way out seems to be to say that the bribe changes what the judge is doing, and if I say that, I appear to have given up (1) already, since changing what the act is in virtue of the benefit is exactly the move (1) was supposed to rule out.

Second, I invented a case I cannot place. Call it the reciprocity pool: no money changes hands at all; instead, anyone who registers to donate an organ moves up the transplant queue if they ever need one themselves. The benefit is in kind, non-transferable, delayed, and pays out only if the donor is unlucky. Everyone I describe it to says it is obviously acceptable, or at least far better than cash. But my own argument seems to say it is a market like any other — a benefit is being given in exchange for an organ — and that if the pool is acceptable, the fact that some other scheme's benefit is cash looks like a detail. And if the pool is not acceptable, I owe a story about why cash is different that isn't just disgust wearing an argument.

So: which step do I reject, and why did that step look safe? I would rather keep (1) and (2) than (4), but I want to be told plainly what keeping them commits me to — in particular whether I end up having to defend markets in votes. And I want the reciprocity pool placed: is it a market, and does the answer to that settle anything about cash?
