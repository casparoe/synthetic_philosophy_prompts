I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on Victorian literature and the history of secularism; there are 16 students and one 90-minute session available. I want a structured, runnable activity that makes participants feel the force of a particular claim about George Eliot's view of religion before I ever name it. The claim is that, in her fiction, the cognitive loss of Christian doctrine does not simply clear away religious practice, because devotional practices can leave behind what I call liturgical residue: a trained discipline of attention, confession, gratitude, and sympathy that continues to do moral work after the supernatural beliefs that first organized those practices have been abandoned. Put otherwise, belief and practice can come apart, and it is often the retained practice—not the abandoned doctrine—that preserves a person's or a community's capacity to respond to suffering with moral attention.

The staged case should be an original scenario set in an early-Victorian English parish, not a reenactment of a specific scene from George Eliot, and it should work even if students cannot recall particular chapter or character names. Assume only that they know she lost her Christian faith but continued to write about religious feeling with sympathy; do not rely on their knowledge of her essays, letters, or secondary criticism. The participants should encounter the idea by being forced to decide, from inside the case, whether to continue devotional practices they no longer believe in, and then by facing a later situation in which the moral capacity sustained by those practices is either available or has atrophied. The rules, roles, constraints, or payoff scheme (using tokens rather than real money) should be arranged so that the felt cost of abandoning practice, and the moral resources retained by continuing it, show up in what participants actually do, not in anything the facilitator says up front.

Please design the activity and provide: the framing and minimal setup script for the facilitator; the roles or constraints assigned to students; the sequence of forced choices or small deliberations, with a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes; any materials; the token payoff scheme and how it is tracked; and the exact point at which the facilitator remains silent so that participants run into the doctrine/practice distinction themselves. Then give a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the distinction between abandoning doctrine and retaining practice, onto the liturgical-residue claim, and onto George Eliot's own religious views, and that states clearly what participants should come away seeing. Finally, say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this—including any way participants might collapse the distinction between practice and doctrine too quickly, or treat continued practice as mere nostalgia—and how to handle it; and be frank about which feature of Eliot's position the activity cannot reproduce and must instead be supplied by the facilitator in the debrief. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I will add the criticism myself.
