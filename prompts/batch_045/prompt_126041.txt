I run a fortnightly philosophy reading group at my local library — eight of us, mostly retirees plus one A-level student who is much cleverer than the rest of us put together — and I'm putting together a handout on that old chestnut about the donkey who starves to death between two identical piles of hay. Someone told me the example really starts with Aristotle rather than Buridan, something about a man who is equally hungry and thirsty, but I dont care about the attribution, it's the structure I'm after. The handout's thesis is that some choices get settled by deciding rather than by weighing, and my problem is that every version of the case I write myself is rotten. Either I sneak in an asymmetry without noticing — the two bales arent quite equidistant, the two job offers differ in commute, one of the twins is marginally better at sharing — and my A-level student spots it in about four seconds flat, or the symmetry is fine and the whole thing collapses into "well, flip a coin, obviously", which is precisely the response I'm trying to get past.

So please build me one. The distinction I keep failing to make vivid — and which the case is supposed to separate — is between what I've started calling tie-breaking and tie-dissolving. Tie-breaking: the two options stay exactly as good as each other, and something that isn't her view of the reasons — a coin, a whim, a rule like "always take the one on the left" — settles which one gets done. Tie-dissolving: with no new information and without either option becoming independently better, her deciding makes it the case that this is what she has most reason to do; the balance isn't tipped by a new weight on one side, it stops being a weighing at all. I want a case where tie-dissolving is clearly the right description and where tie-breaking would be a failure. Constraints. One paragraph, one-shot, ordinary life: no desert islands, no demons, no perfectly symmetrical hay arranged by a Cartesian trickster. She must know everything relevant, and the options must be exactly equally good — and to defeat my student, build the symmetry in structurally, so the situation is invariant under swapping the two options and there's no property left for anyone to poke at. Not choosing must be determinately worse than choosing either, or "let it drift" wins and there's no case, but keep the stakes mundane: nobody dies, nobody is saved, I dont want the answer carried by how much is at risk. And the important bit — randomising has to fail as a solution because of what the choice is for, not because I stipulate that coins are banned. In my intended case she has to be able to own the choice afterwards, to say truthfully and without evasion why she did this rather than that to someone affected by it, and "I tossed a coin" would be a failure to do the thing the choice was for rather than merely an option she happens to dislike. Two ways of cheating I'll be watching for: if she simply acquires a preference for one option and then acts on it, the choice isn't preference-free any more and my topic evaporates; and if the deciding works by changing the payoffs, so that afterwards the chosen option really is better on independent grounds, that's changed circumstances, not a resolution of the tie. Any reason the decision generates has to be a reason because of the decision, not a discovery that the option was better all along.

After the scenario, do the check: show briefly that each constraint holds — the structural symmetry, the cost of drifting, why a coin doesnt solve it — and then tell me what three views would say and why: that either option is rationally permissible; that rationality always singles out one best option, so a tie means something has gone wrong with the description; and that the right response to a tie is to hand the choice over to some procedure or rule outside the reasons. Then: which single feature of your case is doing the work, what happens to the case if you remove that feature, how the procedure-person would attack it (be honest — if it's cheating, say it's cheating), and whether my tie-breaking/tie-dissolving pair survives your case or is really one thing wearing two coats. If there's established vocabulary for that distinction I'd love the words, but please don't substitute a literature dump for an actual case — and no just asserting that she should pick one, the case has to generate that from the setup rather than stipulate it. British English throughout, please.
