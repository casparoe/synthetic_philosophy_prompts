I've been sitting in on a graduate seminar on free will this term, and I've noticed that my own reactions refuse to line up with the way the reading is organized. The compatibilist/incompatibilist dispute leaves me strangely cold; what I actually seem to care about is which varieties of freedom are worth wanting. So I've started recording my gut verdicts on concrete cases before any theory talks me out of them, and I'd like help finding the principle, if there is one, that they add up to. Nora, my baseline case, is a woman whose every decision is the deterministic output of a character she spent decades shaping and now wholeheartedly endorses; she weighs reasons, sometimes badly, and acts. I say: fully free, and it is precisely the condition I would want for myself. Now give Nora a small amount of genuine indeterminacy in her deliberation, so that on close calls the outcome is not fixed by her prior state. My verdict: this adds nothing I want, and if anything slightly diminishes her, since a decision that could have gone the other way for no reason of hers looks less like hers. Put a "variance damper" in her brain that removes the jitter and makes her fully deterministic: no loss at all, possibly a gain. Next, a hidden handler: an unnoticed device monitors Nora, and had she shown any sign of turning down the job, it would have forced that choice instead; she in fact turns it down on her own. I say: free, and not one bit less free than baseline Nora. But now change the device so that it doesn't force anything — it simply ensures that no serious inclination to accept the job ever forms in her, adjusting her attention and mood upstream, so that the alternative is unavailable before deliberation begins. Here I balk and want to say her freedom is diminished or gone, even though she isn't coerced, isn't manipulated in any moment of choice, and ends up doing exactly what she most wants. Then Marcus, raised from infancy inside a closed community that installed his deepest values and also taught him the only methods of reflection he has; he has since re-endorsed those values at every level available to him. I balk here too, but not all the way to "unfree," and I cannot say what distinguishes his unchosen starting point from Nora's unchosen starting point. Otto, by contrast, can skip the meeting only because his character contains a streak of laziness he disapproves of; a fully integrated agent in his position could not skip it. That ability to do otherwise strikes me as worth nothing at all. And an implanted "moral governor" that guarantees Nora never acts against her own considered judgment, leaving everything else about her deliberation untouched and never once noticed by her: this feels like a serious loss, which is odd, because it only ever makes her do what she already most wanted to do, and I said baseline Nora's integration was the height of freedom.

Two cases keep me up. In one, Nora is scanned and a hundred physically identical copies are run in parallel in identical situations; each deliberates in the ordinary way and does the same thing. My verdict flips from hour to hour: sometimes each copy looks obviously free, sometimes the fact that all hundred had to converge makes the whole setup look like the least free thing on my list. In the other, the universe is a block: the future is fully determinate, every choice of Nora's is already there in the four-dimensional whole, and nothing causes it in the ordinary forward sense. I genuinely do not care about this, which is strange, because it means she could not have done otherwise in exactly the way the moral governor means she could not have done otherwise, and the moral governor bothers me a great deal. The best principle I can assemble from all this is something like: an agent has the freedom worth wanting just in case her action issues from a motivational structure that is genuinely hers rather than imposed on her, that is responsive to reasons in the sense that she would have acted differently in some nearby situations where the reasons were different, and that she would endorse on reflection — with a fourth condition I keep adding and deleting, that the structure not be ultimately a matter of luck she had no control over, which I suspect is the clause doing all the damage since it would condemn baseline Nora, whom I refuse to condemn.

Here is what I want from you. Test that principle against my cases, in both its three-clause and four-clause forms, and either repair it or tell me it should be replaced wholesale. Give me the principle, or the two or three serious candidates, stated precisely enough that I can apply it myself, and for each clause tell me which of my cases forced you to include it. Then tell me what your account predicts about cases I haven't listed: a person whose reasons-responsiveness was deliberately engineered by another agent who also arranged which reasons she would encounter; a person who responds beautifully to reasons but only to reasons someone else puts in front of her, never generating a counter-consideration of her own; and an artificial system trained by reinforcement learning whose policy is a fixed function of its weights but which models its own goals and revises its plans in the light of them, where I'd also like to know whether the question is even well formed for such a thing. Be explicit about which of my verdicts your account cannot accommodate: I would rather trade a verdict I have than accept a principle with a clause invented for each case, but I want to see the price quoted both ways before I choose. Please write your reply as continuous prose in the body of an email, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
