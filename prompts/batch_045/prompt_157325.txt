In my intro epistemology seminar one student keeps leaning on a single analogy: being a brain in a vat is just like wearing a VR headset you can never take off. The headset feeds you a world, that world looks exactly like an ordinary one, and your body is irrelevant to what you experience. He's using it to move the whole debate onto empirical ground — if a vat is basically a headset, then scepticism is just the question how likely it is that we're in a simulation, and the semantic-externalist move (the one about how a vat-dweller's word "vat" could only ever refer to an image-vat, so the hypothesis can't even be coherently thought, let alone be true) is a red herring, because simulated worlds contain headsets, so someone inside one can perfectly well wonder whether they're wearing one.

Can you audit the analogy for me? I want to use it, or kill it, in next weeks lecture. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the headset, the sensors, the rendered world, the body, the person who takes it off, the lag or the glitch — then the disanalogies, sorted by whether they actually matter for the work he's making the analogy do. In particular: does it quietly import a standpoint outside the simulation — an operator, an off switch, a body you return to, a community that shares one real world, memories acquired before the headset went on — that the vat scenario is supposed to put out of reach? And does it defeat the externalist point or invert it, i.e. does letting headsets into the simulated world turn "I might be wearing one" into something true but trivial rather than something false? Which conclusion, if any, can the analogy actually carry, and is there a better comparison for what he's trying to do with it?
