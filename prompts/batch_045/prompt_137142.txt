I work in assurance for a company that deploys autonomous systems, and I'm writing an internal report on how we should talk about harms after they happen. I keep needing a distinction I don't have a name for, and I'd like to know whether the philosophical literature has one.

Here is the shape of the case I keep meeting. A fleet of autonomous delivery vehicles uses learned navigation policies with a deliberately randomized element: the vehicles do not do exactly the same thing in similar situations, and they were designed that way on purpose, because the deterministic predecessor failed in identifiable, repeated ways at a particular kind of crossing. The review that approved the change estimated the risks, compared them with the risks of leaving things as they were, and chose the randomized policy. Some months later one of the vehicles strikes and kills a pedestrian. The investigation finds no component failure, no violated procedure, no ignored warning, and no individual who could have prevented that particular sequence of events by acting differently.

What I want to say about that case is that the people who made the choice are not off the hook in the way people usually describe autonomous systems as putting them off the hook. They knew the risk, they chose it, they could have chosen otherwise, they had no excuse, and they were not ignorant of anything anyone else knew. If being responsible requires knowledge and control, they had both. What seems to be missing is something else: nobody can be responsible for the particular outcome. The family asks why him, on that street, that morning, and the honest answer is the dice — which is to say, nothing about him. Being responsible for setting up the lottery does not seem to be the same thing as being responsible for who loses it. But I cannot tell whether that is a real moral distinction, or whether I am only registering the fact that a death demands a reason and a risk does not supply one.

The same shape shows up where nothing dramatic happens at all: a ranking model is known to worsen outcomes slightly and unevenly for some identifiable group, the designers accept that tradeoff for reasons they can state, and then an individual applicant asks why her application failed her. The organization can take responsibility for the design decision and still has no answer to her.

Three things it is not. It is not the responsibility gap as I have seen it described, where the system's behavior is unpredictable or uncontrollable so that no agent meets the conditions for responsibility; here the agents do meet them, for the choice they made, and the gap appears one step later, at the outcome. It is not the problem of many hands: I can name the people who decided, and they do not deny deciding. And it is not a case of blame being redirected onto a convenient human operator so that the organization can insulate itself — in the cases I care about nobody is being shielded, and the organization will say plainly that it accepts responsibility, which is exactly when the gap becomes visible. The acceptance covers the decision and still does not reach the person who was hurt.

A colleague says this is just the old problem of outcomes being matters of luck, and that there is nothing further to say. I half believe her. But cases where the risk was created deliberately, and where leaving the outcome undetermined was the whole point of the design, feel different from the case of a careless driver who happens to hit someone rather than missing.

So: is there an established concept for the gap between being responsible for imposing a risk and being responsible for the outcome the risk produced, in the specific case where the outcome's being undetermined was itself a design goal? If the nearest established concepts only partly fit, I want to know which they are, exactly how my case differs from each, and which fits best — a bare name will not help me. If nothing fits, I want a name and a definition, with the boundary cases fixed: an ordinary accident, ordinary negligence, a case where the victim was in some sense targeted and the question does have an answer, and a case where the outcome is undetermined but the harm is trivial. And I want to know whether the question the family asks is one a moral theory should care about at all, or whether naming the gap would just be dignifying a retributive itch.
