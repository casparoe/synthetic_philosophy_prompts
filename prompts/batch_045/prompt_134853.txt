I spent much of last evening arguing with a friend about whether truth is the sort of thing that could have a theory, and the argument ended with him sending me a survey article on deflationism with the note that it states the position as its defenders themselves would, and that I should say what exactly I still disagree with. I read it, and I genuinely cannot tell which sentences are the view and which are the popularizer's gloss. I would be grateful for a sentence-by-sentence audit rather than a verdict on the position as a whole.

The passage doing the work reads roughly as follows:

Deflationism is the view that truth is not a real property of anything. The argument is simple: to say that a statement is true is just to say the statement itself, so calling the sentence 'snow is white' true adds nothing whatever to asserting that snow is white. The word 'true' is therefore a redundant device, and the correspondentist's project of explaining how true statements manage to match up with the facts was an attempt to solve a problem that does not exist. Two consequences follow. First, deflationists cannot hold truth apart from warranted assertibility: if a statement's being true is exhausted by the equivalence between it is true that p and p, then truth cannot outrun what we are justified in believing, which makes deflationism a species of verificationism. Second, they cannot make sense of truth as the aim of inquiry, or of the thought that a theory we are fully justified in accepting might nevertheless be false — and it is for this reason that most critics regard the view as unable to do justice to the debate between realists and anti-realists.

Here is what I want, concretely.

Which of these sentences would a deflationist accept as stated, which would they accept only with a qualification that changes what is being claimed, and which would they reject outright? I am especially unsure about two transitions. The first is the slide from the biconditional to the claim that 'true' is redundant and eliminable; my friend says redundancy is just what the schema buys you, and I have a feeling it is fair for some uses of the word and not for others, but I cannot say which or why. The second is the jump to warranted assertibility and verificationism: from the little else I have read, I had the impression that deflationists insist on a gap between truth and justification and treat collapsing the two as a substantive claim they are not making. Is that right, or is the article drawing out a consequence that the view is stuck with even if its defenders disavow it?

My friend also drew a distinction in the argument that does not appear in the article, and I want to know whether it does real work or merely changes the subject. He said there are two jobs the notion of truth performs: one where we use it to endorse a batch of claims at once without repeating them — everything in the inspector's report is true, that sort of thing — and one where we use it as a standard that inquiry aims at and that our evidence can fall short of. His claim was that deflationism is only plausible as an account of the first job. Is that a fair way to divide the territory, does the article's summary blur the two, and does the blurring matter?

Finally, the piece closes by saying that if deflationism is right, then the realist's central claim could never have been about truth in a way that admits of resolution, since a deflationist cannot so much as state that our best current theories might be false. Does that follow from the parts of the summary that are accurate, or does it rest on the parts that are not? I am not asking for a survey of the literature or for anything to read next — I want to know what to concede, what to push back on, and whether the article's conclusion survives once its misstatements are repaired.
