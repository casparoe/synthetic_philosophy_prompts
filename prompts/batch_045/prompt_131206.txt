I am a third-year undergraduate in cognitive science, currently enrolled in an upper-level seminar on representation and computation, and I lead the weekly discussion section attached to it: fourteen students, a single seventy-five-minute slot, a standard seminar room with a whiteboard and movable tables, no homework I can assign beforehand, no money, and only me to run it. Next week's topic is the symbol grounding problem. Everyone in the room has already met Searle's Chinese Room and will recite a position the moment I name the topic, so I want to rig the session so that they hit the problem before anyone says the words grounding, referent, or intentionality — ideally no earlier than the last fifteen minutes.

The specific asymmetry I want staged is one I have not seen staged before, and it is the part I care about: the gap between translation success and action success. My sense is that a purely syntactic system can be made to look competent in two ways that are cheap — it can be internally coherent, and it can be translated perfectly into another syntactic system — and that both of these are demonstrably independent of whether any symbol in it is hooked up to anything. What is not cheap is getting a *new* object, or a novel combination of properties that never appeared in the training set, right under a probe nobody has used before. So I want the activity organized around that: a first phase in which the symbol-manipulating role succeeds and looks like the smart role in the room, and a second phase in which exactly that role fails and the role with restricted perceptual contact succeeds. The reversal is the thing I want them to feel, not to be told.

My rough idea of a frame is a warehouse with an opaque internal code: parts in closed bins that most participants never open, a restricted inspection channel (a fixed menu of three permitted measurements and nothing else — no looking, no naming), and three roles — inspectors who touch the parts and must record them as code tokens on a manifest; analysts who never touch anything but receive the inspectors' complete manifest and, later, a parallel corpus pairing the warehouse code with a second warehouse's code, from which they can build rules and translate flawlessly; and a picker working behind a screen who must use whichever notation they are handed to retrieve a part satisfying a *new* request. Please design this properly: the actual rules, the materials and how many objects and how many properties, a scoring scheme with no money involved, a minute-by-minute timeline for seventy-five minutes, and what the facilitator is allowed to say at each stage. Please also build in the case where two ungrounded systems agree with each other perfectly and still both fail the retrieval test, since I think that is the version that survives the obvious objection.

Then the debrief: map specific moments onto the parts of the problem, tell me what will predictably go wrong on a first run (someone will smuggle in ordinary English; the retrieval test will be too easy if the parts are too discriminable; the analysts will try to negotiate meanings instead of using the corpus) and how to handle each without breaking the illusion. And be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in words: in particular, whether success on novel probes is sufficient for grounding or only necessary, and whether the whole setup is smuggling in what it was supposed to explain, since I am the one who fixed the code and the parts in advance and my intentions may be doing all the semantic work. I would like that last objection to be the note the session ends on rather than something I have to deflect.
