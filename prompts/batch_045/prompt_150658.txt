The question the students were given is this. Mira leads a small team maintaining a payments service, and a series of outages looks to her as though only one teammate, Dev, could have caused them; Dev denies it, and the audit logs are genuinely ambiguous. Mira is moved by the following thought: if she believes Dev is acting in good faith and says so openly, the team will settle down and the outages will probably stop, whereas if she treats him as a suspect the collaboration will break down and the project will fail either way; the launch review is in four days, so she cannot simply wait for better evidence. She also considers a third route, which is to suspend judgment entirely while behaving in every outward respect as though she trusted him. Students are asked to use James and Clifford to assess whether Mira is permitted to believe that Dev is acting in good faith, in three required parts: apply James's conditions for a genuine option to her case; state Clifford's objection; and answer an objection the instructor labels "pretend instead," namely that whatever good the belief does could be secured just as well by acting as if one believed while withholding belief, so that James's argument licenses at most a policy of pretence and never licenses belief itself. I should say that I find the pretend-instead point intuitively persuasive myself, though I'm told it is contestable, and I don't want my own reaction to be what decides the mark.

The answer I am grading reads as follows. "James holds that where an option is genuine and the intellect cannot settle it, our passional nature may and indeed must decide the matter, and Mira's case meets each of his conditions. The option of trusting Dev is live for her: given their history of working well together, believing in his good faith is a real psychological possibility rather than something she simply could not bring herself to do. It is forced, because the launch review is in four days and her manager is pressing her to settle the question now, so she has to take one side or the other. It is momentous, because the opportunity here is unique, the choice is effectively irreversible, and what is at stake — the project, the team, her working relationship with Dev — is significant rather than trivial. The further constraint, that the intellect cannot settle it, is also satisfied, since the logs are ambiguous and no more evidence will arrive in time. Clifford would object that it is always, everywhere and for anyone wrong to believe anything on insufficient evidence; his shipowner example makes the point, since the owner quiets his doubts about whether the emigrant ship is seaworthy and lets her sail, and the wrong consists in how the belief was acquired rather than in whether the ship happens to come to grief. Clifford's principle would also rule out Mira's merely entertaining the hypothesis that Dev is innocent, since on his view any positive attitude towards an unproven proposition counts as believing it, which shows how much stricter the Clifford line is than common sense allows. Against Clifford, Mira has a distinctly Jamesian resource: the truth she would be believing is partly a social fact about how the team will behave, and that is the sort of thing her own belief can help bring about, so this is not a case of hoping something is true in the teeth of the evidence. Finally, the pretend-instead objection fails. If Mira merely acts as if she trusted Dev, the gap between what she says and what she feels will show; and in any case acting as if you trust someone while not trusting them means asserting something you do not believe, which is a form of lying. James's argument is therefore untouched by the objection, and Mira is permitted to believe."
