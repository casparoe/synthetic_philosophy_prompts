In a reading group on the causal exclusion argument, one member treats the physical world is causally closed as a conversation-stopper: grant it, he says, and it follows immediately that my decision to raise my arm did nothing, since the neural cause already did all the work. I would rather bring a menu of precisifications than a bare objection.

Please set out the distinct precise theses that sentence could be expressing — over what relata (events, facts, properties), whether the physical cause must be sufficient or need only raise the probability, whether it excludes non-physical causes entirely or only distinct sufficient ones, and whether it is a metaphysical claim about what exists or a methodological claim about the completeness of physics. For each reading, say whether it is near-trivial, defensibly substantive, or false or question-begging, and note which readings quietly presuppose determinism.

Two questions matter most to me. First: suppose neurotransmitter release turns out to be indeterministic, so that many physical events have no sufficient physical cause. Which readings survive that supposition, and do the surviving ones still do any excluding? Second: is any single reading enough on its own to reach his conclusion, or does every reading need an extra premise — a ban on systematic causal overdetermination, say, or a claim about the mental not being suitably distinct from the physical? If some further premise is doing the real work, say which one it is and whether it is more contestable than closure itself.
