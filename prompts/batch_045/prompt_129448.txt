For the discussion section I help run on determinism (first-year philosophy of science, we did Laplace's demon two weeks ago and the butterfly effect last week), I need a case built to order, because the one I sketched for myself doesn't survive my own objections.

What I'm after: a scenario in which someone knows the laws governing a system exactly, knows its present state to as many decimal places as they like, has as much computing power as they could want, and still cannot recover one particular fact about the *past*. Constraints: the dynamics is fully deterministic, not merely statistically regular; the system's *future* is perfectly predictable from the present, so this isn't a case where prediction just fails wholesale; the past fact really obtained one way rather than another and isn't vague or observer-relative; and it's the sort of thing an ordinary person would pay money to know — I keep imagining something forensic or insurance-flavoured, who shoved what and how hard and from where. Ordinary objects, no quantum, no exotic cosmology, no infinity tricks, no self-referential "you can't predict your own action" business, and no supernatural machinery, though unlimited pencil-and-paper time for the reasoner is fine. And the important clause: the failure must not come from chaos. No sensitive dependence, no "run the film backwards and the rounding error explodes." It has to be structural.

My own attempt was a hardback shoved across a dining table: it slides, dry friction brings it to a stop, and from where it came to rest together with the fact that it is at rest you cannot tell where it started or how hard it was pushed, even though the friction law is perfectly deterministic. But I don't think this works, and I want to know why exactly. I only specified the present state as position-plus-zero-velocity, and the obvious reply is that this isn't the *full* state: the tabletop is a trace warmer, the wood has phonons rattling around in it, the air in the room moved, and given all of that plus reversible underlying laws you could in principle integrate backwards. So either repair my case so it survives that objection, or tell me it can't be repaired and build a different one that can, or tell me honestly that nothing satisfies all the clauses at once and say which clause has to be dropped and what the nearest satisfiable case then looks like.

Whatever way it goes, here's what I want on the page: the scenario itself in a paragraph or two of plain prose; then the check — state exactly what the present state is taken to be, describe the set of pasts compatible with it, explain why extra precision in the present doesn't collapse that set to a single history (or, if it does, say so honestly and explain why that still doesn't hand you the fact), and show why the future is predictable; then identify the one feature doing all the work and say what happens to the case when that feature is removed; then the strongest version of the objection from someone who holds that determinism plus the laws plus one complete state settles every other state — my guess is that this turns into a fight about whether determinism guarantees uniqueness of the past or only uniqueness of the future, but you tell me — and finally, if the phenomenon has a settled name in this area, give it, along with the neighbouring notions it could be confused with. Don't just hand me a label, though; the section will tear a bare name apart, so I need the boundaries: what falls under it, what only looks like it, and where it stops. Last line on its own, in this form: \answer{...} naming the single load-bearing feature in five words or fewer (mine would be something like "friction destroys information", if that's even close).
