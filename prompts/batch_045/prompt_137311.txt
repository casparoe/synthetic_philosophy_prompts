Hey — I'm putting together a one-page handout for a first-year consciousness seminar on what the split-brain cases actually show about the unity of consciousness, and I'd rather it be a flow chart than prose. Start everyone from the same commitment: in commissurotomy patients each hemisphere can be fed information the other doesn't get, the left one does nearly all the talking, and the left one will cheerfully invent a reason for something the right one set in motion. From there give me 5–6 branch questions, each phrased as something a student could answer without knowing which view it leads to, and each branch labelled with the claim that does the splitting rather than with a name.

Leaves should be positions about how many subjects and how many streams of experience there are: one subject with a single stream that's merely inaccessible to half the brain, one subject with two streams, two subjects, no fact of the matter / it's indeterminate, unity as a matter of degree, plus whatever else you think belongs in the space.

Two cases I specifically want used as nodes, since I think they do the real work. First, a hypothetical 'bandwidth dial': imagine the callosal link isn't cut but continuously tuneable, so inter-hemispheric traffic can be dialled from normal down to zero and back up — the question at that node is whether there's a sharp threshold where one subject becomes two, and if so what the student says about the dial sitting just above it versus just below. Second, an atypical patient where both hemispheres have full speech and, asked the same question about one decision (say, whether to take a job in another city), give confident conflicting answers out loud — two subjects disagreeing, or one subject who is of two minds?

For each leaf give a one-line statement of the position and the most awkward thing it's stuck with. Note any combination of answers the tree allows that nobody actually occupies, and say briefly whether that's because it's incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any two leaves your questions can't really pry apart, and any node where you think students would just be guessing because the case doesn't settle anything. No citations or reading lists — the handout is for people who won't look anything up — so name the positions descriptively by what they say about subjects and streams, and only attach a philosopher's name where you're actually confident. British English please, and keep it to the chart plus a short note on whatever stays ambiguous.
