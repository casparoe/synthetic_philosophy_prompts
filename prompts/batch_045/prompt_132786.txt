I've been reading about unions lately and I cant tell whether the thing that strikes me as weird is a bug or the whole point. Here's the practice as I understand it (set me straight if I've got it wrong): if a majority of workers in a workplace vote for a union, that union becomes the exclusive bargaining representative for everyone in the unit — including people who voted no and people who'd rather negotiate for themselves. It has to represent all of them fairly, the employer isn't allowed to cut individual deals with workers in the unit, and a worker can't opt out and bargain on their own terms. But actually joining is voluntary, and the law also protects the right to have nothing to do with the union.

So: individual choice at the entry point, one compulsory collective outcome at the exit, and a voluntary association that dissenters are required to be represented by but not required to join.

A friend says this is just how democratic elections work — you lose, you're bound anyway. I dont think that's right: losing an election binds you to a decision for a term, it doesn't make someone your standing spokesperson or stop you speaking for yourself. And I suspect the bit doing the real work is the ban on individual deals, which looks less like a claim about association and more like a rule against workers competing with each other over terms.

What does an arrangement like this presuppose? What view of association, or of what a contract and a strike actually are, makes it hang together, and what rival views does it quietly rule out? Is the front-end voluntariness genuinely inconsistent with the back-end compulsion, or does it only look inconsistent because I'm picturing the union as a club? Which features does the practice strictly need and which could go while the rest survived? And what would actually be lost under a members-only setup where two rival unions in one workplace each bargained only for their own members?
