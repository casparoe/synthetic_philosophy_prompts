I work in patient safety at a mid-sized hospital — I'm one of the people who sits in the room after something has gone badly wrong and helps write up the report afterwards. I'm also doing a part-time certificate in clinical informatics on the side, and I keep bumping into the same oddity in two different places at work, so the other night I got into an argument about it with a friend who does data science, and I still don't feel like either of us won. I wanted to ask someone who thinks about this stuff properly. (Please don't send me a reading list — I want the argument, not papers to chase.)

Here's the thing. In our root-cause analyses everyone somehow just knows which things count as "the cause." Say a patient got the wrong drug because two vials looked nearly identical on the shelf, and because the nurse was covering two floors at once. The report will say the cause was the look-alike packaging and the short-staffing. Nobody writes down "oxygen was present in the room," or "the patient's heart was already failing," or "gravity," even though obviously if you took away the failing heart nothing bad would have happened. Our reporting software actually bakes this in: you literally cannot save a cause in the form unless you also attach an action item to it, a fix, something we are going to change. So a factor we can't do anything about is automatically not a cause for us, no matter how much the outcome depended on it. Meanwhile, in literally the same hospital, we spend a lot of time on the opposite kind of question. The risk team runs "would this patient have been admitted anyway" comparisons, our harm-scoring asks whether the bad outcome would still have happened if we'd caught the deterioration two hours earlier, and the lawyers lean very hard on that — even though by then nobody can go back and change anything at all.

So what does our practice actually commit us to? As far as I can tell there are two pictures floating around. On one, something is a cause of an outcome just in case, had it been different and everything else stayed the same, the outcome would have been different — causes are difference-makers, full stop. On the other, you can only really call something a cause if it's the sort of thing you could in principle go in and wiggle, hold everything else fixed, and watch the effect move; a variable nobody could ever manipulate isn't a genuine cause, it's just a correlated background condition. Our root-cause reports look like they're built on the second picture, and our harm scoring and legal questions look like they're built on the first. I'd love to know: which of the two does each part of the practice strictly require, and which does it merely sit nicely with? Is the "attach a fix or it isn't a cause" rule just bureaucratic pragmatism — we only care about the preventable stuff — or is it a substantive claim about what causation is? Notice too that in those meetings the nurses' side and the pharmacy side can each produce a completely different, apparently equally true story about what caused the same event, and nothing in either picture seems to settle which one goes in the report; is that a third thing the practice is quietly relying on, something about which difference-maker matters or who we're holding responsible? And concretely: if the hospital decided causes are just difference-makers, what would our reports have to start saying, and what would actually change in practice? If instead we went all-in on causes being manipulable levers, what happens to all these "would it have happened anyway" questions about things we cannot touch, like a patient's age, how sick they already were when they walked through the door, or the weather that night?

I'd like you to work through it in those terms — reconstruct the theory the practice seems to assume, say where the two parts are in genuine tension and where they both just happen to fit fine, and tell me what would have to change if we swapped the assumed theory for the other one. Then, at the very end, put one single line with nothing after it: \answer{...} containing exactly one of INTERVENTIONIST, COUNTERFACTUAL, BOTH, or THIRD — meaning, respectively: the practice as a whole really does presuppose the manipulable-lever picture; it really does presuppose the difference-maker picture; it's genuinely underdetermined and either picture licenses it; or it's actually leaning on some third account that neither of my two pictures captures.
