I run a small fortnightly reading group — none of us, me included, has any formal background in philosophy — and we work through one book at a time. Each fortnight I set a question, members write answers of about five hundred words, and I mark them. I have one I would like marked independently, because I am not confident I can tell the difference between a passage that merely sounds assured and one that actually gets the inference right. I will not say what I made of it, so as not to lead you.

The question I set was:

Could you have come from a sperm and egg other than the ones you actually came from? In your answer: (i) say whether the claim that you could not is intended as a necessary truth, and what, if anything, makes it a posteriori rather than a priori; (ii) say whether it is a claim about you yourself or only about anyone very much like you; and (iii) answer the following objection — identical twins arise from a single sperm and a single egg, yet they are two different people, so sameness of gametes cannot be what makes someone the person they are.

The answer I want marked:

I will take (ii) first, because I think it carries most of the weight. The thesis is not a generalisation about people of my kind — it is not that anyone with my genome and my biography would have had to come from sperm S and egg E. It is a claim about me: this individual, picked out rigidly, could not have existed at all unless it had developed from S and E. That is why the rigidity point matters: I and my name keep holding on to the same object when we ask what might have become of it, so the question is not whether some stand-in for me, resembling me closely, came from different gametes. And it is not an epistemic claim either. I am not saying that we happen to lack the evidence about my origins; I am saying that the object itself could not have had different ones.

On (i): the claim is meant to be necessary in the strong sense — true however the world had gone — and yet a posteriori, because nothing in logic or in the concept of a person tells me which gametes I came from. I had to learn that from the world: from what my parents told me, and in principle from a DNA test. So the necessity is metaphysical while the route to it is empirical, rather as with the identity of water and H2O.

The derivation is then very short. Identity is necessary: if a = b, then necessarily a = b. Let a be me and let b be the child of S and E. I am that child. Hence, necessarily, I am the child of S and E — which just is the thesis. No further premise is needed.

On (iii): here I think the objection succeeds and the standard view has to give ground. Identical twins arise from a single sperm and a single egg and are two people. So coming from S and E does not settle which person you are, and a principle that leaves the person's identity unsettled cannot be doing the work it was brought in to do. The usual repair — index the origin to the zygote rather than to the gametes — makes matters worse, since in the twin case there is only one zygote to index to. Sameness of gametes is therefore not sufficient for sameness of person, and sufficiency was the whole point.

There is a further cost, which I take to be decisive. If the gametes are essential to me, so is the moment at which they met: I could not have been conceived a month earlier or later, and so not born a month earlier or later either. But plainly I could have been born a month late — the pregnancy could simply have run longer — and nobody supposes that a late birth produces a different person. So the view is too strong. The sensible retreat is to say that what is essential is not the gametes themselves but a broad genetic makeup: I could have come from a different sperm, provided it carried much the same DNA.

Please give it a mark out of 100 and the band you would put it in, and then the marginal comments you would actually write: keyed to particular sentences or moves, saying for each whether it earns credit, whether it is defensible but under-argued, or whether the inference fails and why. I am especially interested in any move that sounds convincing while resting on a conflation — a shift between two things the answer treats as the same — and in how you would distinguish that from a step that is merely missing a premise. Where a step could be repaired, say what the missing premise would be and what the answer would then be worth. Please do not summarise the doctrine for me; I have the reading.
