Hey — I'm writing a term paper on everyday conventions (tipping, queueing, jaywalking) and my flatmate, who's an anthro PhD and enjoys being insufferable, walked me into an argument I now think proves way too much. I've been going in circles for a week and would rather someone else name the broken step.

Here it is:

(1) A big chunk of what we call everyday morality is convention-dependent. Whether leaving nothing on the table is an insult depends on the local tipping practice; whether walking to the front is a theft or just how it's done depends on how the line works; whether stepping off the curb on a red is a small rudeness or a total non-event depends on local pedestrian practice. There doesn't seem to be any practice-independent fact about whether 18% or nothing is owed, or about whether the front of the line is yours if you got there first. If the practice were different, the duty would be different or gone.

(2) The content of a convention-dependent duty is fixed by the practice, and the practice as it actually exists doesn't sort people into locals and visitors. It says something like: someone in situation S at a place like this is required to do R. Nothing in the content of the queueing convention, or the tipping convention, carries a nationality clause. So if you want to say "she's exempt, she's just visiting," you're adding a condition the practice itself doesn't contain.

(3) And there's no in-principle limit on what a local practice can require. Where my flatmate grew up, the shared taxis board as an actual scrum and nobody thinks less of anyone for it. In plenty of places you're expected to refuse an offered gift twice before accepting. In plenty of others the permit application moves because there's cash folded into it.

(4) So a visitor is morally required to shove at the taxi rank, to refuse the gift twice, to hand over the envelope — and by the same reasoning required to tip at the local rate even if she thinks tipping is a rotten system, and required to stand at the crossing for a full cycle with no car in sight if that's the local pedestrian norm.

(5) That's way too much. A traveler who declines to fold cash into the permit hasn't done anything wrong; if anything she's done the better thing. And the argument seems to eat itself: if every duty is fixed by the local practice, there's no standpoint left from which anyone inside the practice can criticize it, and "that's how we do things here" becomes a complete moral answer.

(6) So one of (1)-(4) has to go.

What I want to keep: I'd most like to keep (1), because the whole point of my paper is that everyday morality is more local than my classmates want to admit. I also want to keep the bit of (4) that says the visitor isn't off the hook — I really dislike the tourist who strolls to the front of the line on the grounds that this isn't her country, and I want to be able to say she's doing something wrong. So I'm hoping the damage is in (3) or the move from (2) to (4).

My flatmate says (3) is cheating: a scrum isn't a convention, it's what happens when there isn't one, and the envelope isn't a convention either, it's coercion that happens to have a regular shape. I half believe her, but I don't know how to state the test for which regularities count without it being ad hoc, and I'm worried any test that rules out the scrum will also rule out US tipping, which nobody thinks is *not* a convention. The gift-refusal case is the one that actually scares me, because it looks like a perfectly good convention on the standard coordination story — everyone conforms because everyone else conforms, everyone expects everyone to, nobody has a unilateral reason to stop — and yet I don't want to say a visitor who accepts on the first offer has committed a moral fault.

I've been calling the fix I want "the guest clause": a local convention binds a visitor only if (a) she's taken up the benefits the practice distributes — she ate the meal, rode the taxi, walked the sidewalks — and (b) the point of the practice is served by conformity even when nobody can tell that you're conforming. But I can't tell whether that's a real principle or just a description of the cases I already have opinions about, and whether it proves too much in the other direction (does it let me free-ride?). A related thing I can't sort out: jaywalking, where "everyone does it" and "it's against the law" come apart, and I don't know whether the legal/conventional distinction is doing any work in (1) or is a red herring.

So: which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost me? If you think the argument is valid and I just have to swallow something, tell me which premise is cheapest to give up and what I lose. And if you use a test to separate the scrum from the queue, please say why it isn't gerrymandered — and whether it also gives an answer on the visitor who refuses the gift, the one who doesn't tip, and the one who jaywalks. Also worth flagging if my (2) is doing something sneaky: I have a feeling that any way of exempting the visitor needs moral resources from outside the practice, which would undercut (1) from the inside, but I can't quite make that precise.

Please write in American English, and keep it concrete — I'd rather have the argument diagnosed step by step than a survey of what's been said about conventions generally.
