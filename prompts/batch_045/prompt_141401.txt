I am in the second year of a joint philosophy and Asian studies degree, and I am currently taking an option on classical Indian epistemology. We have spent the last few weeks on the Nyāya account of pramā and the pramāṇas, and the seminar handout we were given compresses the whole position into a single paragraph, which I reproduce below. Something about it feels flattened to me, but I do not yet know enough to say where.

"For the Nyāya, a pramāṇa is simply a reliable belief-forming process: a source counts as a pramāṇa if it tends to get things right. Nyāya recognises four. Perception (pratyakṣa) is bare sensory contact with an object, before any concepts or names are brought to bear. Inference (anumāna) is the five-membered syllogism, and is therefore a matter of valid form rather than of content. Comparison (upamāna) is just reasoning by analogy, and so is really a species of inference and not a separate source at all. Testimony (śabda) yields knowledge only when the speaker is trustworthy, which makes it an appeal to authority. Knowledge itself (pramā) is analysed as true belief produced by a pramāṇa, so Nyāya is committed to something very like the tripartite account of knowledge — and is therefore exposed to exactly the Gettier-style objections that sank that account: a pramāṇa can produce a true belief by luck, and Nyāya has no anti-luck condition with which to exclude that. The handout's conclusion is that Nyāya needs a fifth pramāṇa for the contemporary world, since knowledge that we acquire from a machine — a translation engine, a search system, a large language model — comes from no trustworthy speaker and so falls outside all four."

I would be grateful for an accuracy audit of this paragraph, taken claim by claim rather than as a whole. For each, please say what someone who works on Nyāya would accept without argument, what they would wince at as a simplification, and what they would reject. The points I am least sure about are these:

whether calling Nyāya a form of reliabilism is a defensible reading or a contested modern gloss, and whether pramāṇa is better understood as the instrument or most efficacious cause of a cognition than as a bare reliable process;

whether perception is properly described as pre-conceptual sensory contact, given that Nyāya seems happy to count ordinary determinate cognitions of objects as perception;

whether the five-membered syllogism is the form of inference or only a device for exhibiting it, since what we were told about vyāpti being a substantive relation of invariable concomitance seems hard to square with the claim that inference is "a matter of form";

whether upamāna really is just analogy — I was told in one seminar that its output is knowledge of the relation between a word and what it names, which is not obviously what an inference delivers, and I would like to know what hangs on Nyāya's insisting that it is irreducible;

whether rendering pramā as "true belief" is faithful, given that it is usually glossed as true cognition or true experience, and what work the exclusion of memory and the novelty of the cognition are doing there;

whether the Gettier point is as decisive as the handout makes it sound, since I have seen it argued both that pramāṇa theory is externalist and so untouched by those cases and that it is as exposed as the tripartite analysis;

and finally whether the conclusion about a fifth pramāṇa for machine-generated output follows from the accurate parts of the summary or only from the misleading ones — in particular, what a Nyāya philosopher would have to find present (a speaker who knows, an intention to convey, comprehension of a sentence's meaning) before counting such an output as a source of knowledge at all.

One methodological request. My course has a habit of presenting the reliabilist reading, the reducibility of upamāna, and the Gettier objection as though each were settled, and I would rather know which of them are genuinely contested. Please mark, as you go, what is agreed on all sides against what is a live dispute among specialists, and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus. Where you are reconstructing, extrapolating, or leaning on a modern comparative framing rather than reporting what the Nyāya authors themselves argue, please say so explicitly.
