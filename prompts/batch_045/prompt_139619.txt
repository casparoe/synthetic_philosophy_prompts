I'm a senior at a large public high school, and I run the math club, which mostly means four of us do contest problems and argue through lunch. Last week our faculty advisor showed us the classic two-line proof that there are irrational numbers a and b for which a to the power b is rational: look at the square root of two raised to the square root of two; if that is rational we are done with a and b both equal to the square root of two, and if it is irrational then take a to be the square root of two raised to the square root of two and b to be the square root of two, since that power works out to 2. He clearly thought it was charming. Two of our members, Devin and Noor, then had an argument that lasted the whole period and that I honestly cannot referee. I am not going to tell you which of them I agreed with, because I kept switching sides, and I would rather you just assess the moves.

(1) Devin opened by saying the proof is a conjuring trick: it convinces you that such a pair exists without ever producing one, and a claim of the form there exists an x with such-and-such ought to be established by producing a witness, because that is what there exists is for. As it stands, he said, the argument at most shows that it is not the case that no such pair exists.

(2) Noor said that is not a defect of this proof but rather how existence proofs work: it is a case split, one branch hands you the square root of two and the square root of two, the other hands you the square root of two raised to the square root of two and the square root of two, both branches deliver the existential, so the existential follows. If Devin's complaint were any good, she said, you would have to throw out most of the mathematics anyone actually uses.

(3) Devin replied that he had actually been too generous, because for this particular theorem the non-constructiveness is removable: a standard result in transcendence theory tells you that the square root of two raised to the square root of two is irrational, so you can delete the first branch and the proof becomes constructive. But that is a distraction, he said. Take instead the claim that at least one digit occurs infinitely often in the decimal expansion of pi. The proof is one line: if each of the ten digits occurred only finitely often, the expansion would stop, and pi is not rational. Here you have an existence claim with no witness, no route to a witness, and, as far as anyone knows, no method that would ever tell you which digit it is. So the famous example understates his case.

(4) Noor said he has merely restated his demand with a new example instead of arguing for it. The classical proof is valid, so the conclusion is true, so some digit does occur infinitely often; our not knowing which one is a limitation on us and not a hole in the proof. And she pressed the cost: on Devin's reading, P-or-not-P is not assertible for any open problem, Goldbach's conjecture for instance, which means the truth of a mathematical sentence would depend on what anybody happens to have proved so far. She called that absurd, on the grounds that the digits of pi are what they are whether or not we ever sort them out.

(5) Devin said that is only absurd if you have already helped yourself to the assumption that truth is timeless and independent of what we can establish. On his view, which he called the proof interpretation of the connectives and which he attributed to some initials I think are BHK, the connectives are defined by what counts as a proof of them: a proof of A-or-B is a procedure that tells you which of the two holds, and a proof of there-exists-an-x is a procedure that produces one. On that reading P-or-not-P is not a tautology but a substantive claim, the claim that we have a decision method for P, and of course it becomes assertible when somebody finds one. He added that Noor cannot win simply by using or and there exists in her sense and calling his sense deviant, since which reading is the correct one is exactly what the two of them are arguing about, so neither gets to start from their own.

(6) Noor answered that this is precisely the problem: if Devin is allowed to redefine or and there exists, then he is not denying what she says, he is switching to a language in which his sentences are about something else, and a disagreement about which language to speak is not a factual disagreement, so there is nothing left for him to be right about. Devin said that if changing the language is what it takes to make proofs tell you what they claim to be about, then fine, and the bell rang.

Two loose ends came up that neither of them handled well, in case they matter. Someone said there is a standard way of translating classical proofs into constructive ones, roughly by inserting double negations throughout, so that a classical proof of there-exists-an-x becomes a constructive proof of it-is-not-not-the-case-that-there-exists-an-x; I could not tell whether that helps Devin, helps Noor, or is beside the point. And our advisor mentioned that mathematicians who work constructively prove a great many of the same theorems anyway, which seems as though it ought to be evidence for somebody.

What I would like from you: go through the exchange move by move, all six numbered moves, and for each one say whether it is clearly right, mostly right, mostly mistaken, or clearly mistaken, with a short justification. Then tell me what both of them are missing, the thing someone who actually works in logic or the philosophy of mathematics would say they have both failed to consider. Then commit: say which of them is more right overall, give the strongest version of the case you are rejecting, and note where your own confidence is lowest. I do not want a survey of the literature or a list of things to read; I want you to take a side and defend it.
