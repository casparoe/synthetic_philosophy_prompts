i'm running a 90-minute seminar on probability in the Everett picture in about three weeks. 14 students, mostly third-years who've had one QM course — they can read a state as a superposition of amplitudes and they've seen the two-slit experiment, but none of them has thought about what "probability" could even mean if every outcome happens somewhere. No lab equipment, just a room with tables and a whiteboard, and I can offer a small real stake (chocolate, or a couple of participation points — not actual money).

I've done this session twice as a lecture and it doesn't land. I write up the Born rule problem, someone says "just count the branches", I say counting gives the wrong answer unless the amplitudes happen to be equal, someone says "then make more branches for the big-amplitude outcome and count those", and we end up arguing about what a branch is — wich is the genuinely interesting part, but by then they're passive and I'm lecturing again. So I want them to run into it instead.

What I'd like is a game or staged simulation where they place a bet or make a choice *before* a split, and then have to cope with there being several of them afterwards holding different evidence. The things I want the mechanics to produce, without me announcing any of them:

1. The naive move of counting futures and betting on the majority should feel obviously right and then lose — but it should lose because of something structural they can point at in the setup, not because I rigged the box.
2. The count itself should come out ill-defined: split the room along one description and there are 2 futures, along a finer one and there are 200, and "how many are there really" has no answer. Ideally they build the branch tree themselves out of index cards and discover that their own coarse-graining convention fixed the answer before any physics entered.
3. The payoff rule has to be something they argue about: are a copy's winnings averaged across all of your copies, summed, or paid to each copy separately? My hunch is that this argument *is* the Born rule problem in miniature, but I can't see how to stage it so the argument comes out of the rules rather than out of me.
4. If there's room, a wrinkle where some branches later merge, so a copy that existed after the split isn't there at the end and its winnings have to go somewhere.

And if it fits: have them build the repeated-trials tree and notice that the overwhelming majority of their descendants see statistics close to the Born frequencies — and then notice that "majority" there is itself a count over branches, so the move seems to presuppose the weighting it was meant to derive. If that can happen to them rather than be said to them, great; if it needs a second round of the game, tell me.

Give me rules, roles if any, materials, a timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the room onto specific parts of the problem. Constraints: no framing lecture up front (they shouldn't hear the phrase "Born rule" until at least halfway through), tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be frank about which bit of the problem the game can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself.
