Last night my friend Dave and I got into a three hour argument about a vote I have coming up, went nowhere, and I honestly think hes wrong but can't put my finger on why, so I need something written down.

I'm one of five members of the Ashfield Plan Commission. Small town, about 2,400 people, forty minutes from a metro area where a two-bedroom rents for $2,100. On the 14th we vote on "Fernbrook": 260 homes, roughly 780 more people, which would grow the town by a third over six years. The developer is a regional builder, not a hedge fund, for whatever that's worth, and has offered to fund a school addition and to widen Route 9 at the intersection. Our own traffic consultant says that even with the widening, average commute times for current residents go up by about six minutes each way, and the elementary school goes from 310 kids to around 500 for two years before the addition opens.

Then there's the part that's actually making my head hurt. A guy named Reuben Kerrigan, who's lived here his whole life, sent the commission a three page letter saying that if we approve Fernbrook on the grounds that 780 lives somewhat worse than ours are still worth having, we are committing the repugnant conclusion, and he says he'll read the whole thing into the public record at the meeting. Dave's position is basically that Kerrigan is right and that it's a reductio: once you admit that adding enough slightly-worse-off lives beats a smaller number of better-off ones, you've signed up for a town of ten million people each living a life barely worth living, nobody actually believes that's better, so the whole total-wellbeing style of reasoning the planning staff used in their report has to go. He says the only honest thing is to think about average wellbeing for the people who are actually here.

What I keep saying to Dave, and can't quite make stick: the repugnant conclusion is a case where nobody is made worse off, you're just choosing between different sets of people, and this isn't that. People here are definately bearing real costs — six minutes each way, two years of crowded schools, the field behind the elementary school that the town uses for soccer. So it's not a pure population question at all, it's a question about who pays and who gains. But then Dave says fine, make the developer compensate until no current resident is worse off — full mitigation, bigger school, whatever it costs — and then approve, because then it really is pure addition and we're back to total wellbeing. And I think that's exactly backwards: if we compensate everyone so that no one is worse off, we've turned it into the pure different-people case, which is the case where I'm most worried about the repugnant conclusion, not the case where I'm least worried. Leaving some of the cost on current residents at least means somebody's actual interests are sitting on the other side of the scale. I might be confused about this and I want the memo to be honest if I am.

One more wrinkle. Our chair, Enid, has floated what she calls "the Ashfield line": a standing rule that new residents may be added only if their expected quality of life is at least as good as the current average quality of life in town, so we'd take people who'd be doing at least as well as we are and nobody who'd be doing worse. I pointed out that by that rule we could never take in anyone fleeing a worse situation, which seems nuts, and she said that's not a bug, that's what a town owes its own. I don't know what to think about that either.

Can you draft the two page findings memo I'm going to circulate to the other four members before Monday's meeting? It needs a bottom line — approve, deny, or approve with conditions, and if conditions, what has to be in them and by when. It needs to answer Kerrigan by name in a way that doesn't make him look stupid, because he isn't and he'll be in the room. It needs to say something real about Enid's Ashfield line. And it needs to give Dave's full-compensation idea the strongest version of itself before explaining why I'm not going with it, in case I'm the one who's wrong. It's public record, so nothing that would embarrass me if the local paper lifted a paragraph out of it.
