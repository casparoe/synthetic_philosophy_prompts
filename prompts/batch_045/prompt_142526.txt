In my philosophy-of-time reading group, one member keeps ending arguments with the same picture: spacetime is a loaf of bread, your "now" is just wherever the knife happens to be cutting, every slice exists as fully as every other, so becoming is an illusion, the future is not open, and special relativity forces the whole package — she offers the Rietdijk–Putnam argument as the rigorous version of what the loaf shows. Another member replies that the loaf is doing all the work: a real loaf has a long axis and hence a privileged family of perpendicular slices, which is just the structure Minkowski spacetime lacks, since no timelike direction is preferred over any other; and a knife that moves needs a time to move in. Please audit the picture: give the table of correspondences (loaf, axis, slices, knife, the motion of the knife — is that supposed to be objective passage or merely the shifting reference of "now" from one utterance to the next? — and the difference between a pre-sliced loaf and one you are free to cut at any angle), say which features of the source the target lacks, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion she wants from the ones that are cosmetic. Then put the picture aside and assess the conclusion: does the Rietdijk–Putnam argument get eternalism out of relativity on its own, or does it need an extra premise — about what existence or co-reality amounts to, about whether every frame's simultaneity slice is on a par, or about the transitivity of "is real relative to" — that the loaf was quietly supplying by metaphor? If the loaf is the wrong picture, is there a better one, and what does it make vivid that the loaf conceals?
