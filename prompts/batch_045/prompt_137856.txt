My friend Dana is one of those hard-core "do whatever makes the world best overall" people, and I've started writing down what she actually says about real cases, because I can't work out what rule she's running. Her verdicts so far:

- Voting: she does it, but only because it costs almost nothing — she says the chance of mattering is basically zero, so it's a free shot at a good outcome.
- Giving to the malaria bednet charity: she gives about a tenth of her income and says it's about the clearest good purchase available to a normal person.
- Donating to or volunteering for a national campaign for a policy she thinks is net-good: no. She says a change that big has knock-on effects nobody can predict, and those will totally swamp whatever the campaign is aiming at.
- Giving to the "protect the far future / reduce extinction risk" funds: also no. Her line is that the further out you go, the more our guesses are worth approximately nothing.
- Having a kid: she says it's fine, and not having one is fine too, and that anyone who tells you the very-long-run consequences settle the question is kidding themselves.
- Being harsh to your child on the theory that it'll make them driven and successful later: she thinks that's obviously wrong, and isn't moved by "but you can't prove the long-run effect is bad."
- Tiny actions that could in principle butterfly into something enormous: she doesn't care at all. She once joked that holding a door open for a stranger might rearrange the next eighty years.

New case, and this one actually matters because she's in it. Dana works in a materials lab. For essentially no cost to herself she can do one small favour that will very likely get a cheap clean-energy technology onto the market roughly two years earlier than it otherwise would. The near-term effects look clearly and substantially good — less air pollution, cheaper power, all the stuff she already thinks is good. The long-run effects are the usual fog: which technologies end up mattering, who gets rich, whether some war happens earlier or later. She can't get any grip on the sign of those, and she knows it.

So: what does she do? I care less about the bare yes/no than about the rest of it. What rules fit all seven of her earlier verdicts at once; what does each of those rules say about the lab case; which is your best guess and why; and what one further case would you put to her to separate whichever rules your best guess still leaves tied?

I've also been playing with a rule of my own: only count what happens to people who are already alive, and treat effects on people who don't exist yet as zero. Tell me whether that fits her track record, or whether one of her verdicts kills it.
