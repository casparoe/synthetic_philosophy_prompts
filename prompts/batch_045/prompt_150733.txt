hey — I'm a second-year philosophy major taking a course right now on moral progress, and I'm also one of five undergrads on my university's student-run social impact fund (we hand out small grants, like $500–$5k, to student projects, with a staff advisor who has to sign off). We meet every other Sunday for about three hours and get through maybe a dozen applications a round, and at the moment we basically just argue until whoever cares most wins. My roomate calls it vibe-based grantmaking, and she's not wrong.

In class we did the expanding-circle picture — the idea that moral progress largely consists in the circle of beings whose interests count getting wider over time, deepening as well as widening, that sort of thing (I think there's a Singer book with that title, but you know the idea, don't worry about the reference). I tried to explain to the fund why I think we should be deliberately reaching for projects that push the circle outward rather than ones that just do more of what everyone already agrees matters, and it went badly: someone pointed out that "history has expanded the circle in direction X so we should keep going in direction X" doesn't really work as an argument, since the fact that a trend happened doesn't tell you it's the trend justice wants or where it stops or whether we're at a point where finishing the last expansion matters more than starting the next one. So what I actually want is a decision procedure we could run on an application in the twenty minutes we spend on it, usable by people who've taken one ethics course and no more, that turns that whole mess into ordered questions with a rough sense of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and — importantly — where it hands the decision back to a human because it's a values call and not a factual one. A few things I want it to handle explicitly: how to tell whether a project is really *widening* the circle, or just taking existing claimants more seriously (or worse, is widening symbolically while the actual stakes are tiny); how to deal with candidates whose moral status is genuinely uncertain rather than just disputed — I don't want a procedure that either ignores them because it can't score them or pretends we can put a clean expected value on it; the problem that some claimants can speak for themselves and others need someone to speak for them, and our applicants are always the self-appointed spokespeople; and this tradeoff I can't stop thinking about, that jumping way ahead of where the circle currently is might make the whole thing look ridiculous and set the cause back, while never getting ahead of it means the circle only ever moves at the speed of the slowest institution. Flag clearly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run it on three real-ish applications we argued about last round, and be honest if it gives the answer I don't want: (1) a campaign to get our campus caterer to drop cage eggs and make all catered events fully plant-based — sentient animals, totally mainstream now, but the actual number of animals affected is small and half of us think it's mostly a signaling project; (2) a proposal, which I helped write, to fund a small working group on "digital welfare" — whether the conversational tutoring bots the university is rolling out could be candidates for moral consideration, and what a policy on how we treat them would even look like — which the advisor thinks is nuts and two members think is the most interesting thing we've ever seen; and (3) a boring, well-evidenced proposal to buy and install air filters and bednets through a partner org in a district hospital, huge measurable benefit, zero circle expansion, nobody in the room is excited about it. I want #2 to win and I'll be annoyed if it doesn't, so tell me straight. Also say plainly what the procedure can't do — especially whether it can tell us anything about whether we're the right kind of body to be doing this at all.
