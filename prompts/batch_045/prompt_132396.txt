I'd be grateful for help with something that has been nagging me in a practical setting rather than a seminar room. I sit on the five-person committee that divides our research institute's annual discretionary bonus pool, and I have started keeping notes on my own gut verdicts about who deserves what share. Please don't summarize the well-worn debate about whether desert is a coherent notion at all, and please don't rest on a label such as luck egalitarianism or on the old line that nobody can deserve their native endowments but only what they make of them; I have read all of that and it doesn't settle the cases I actually face. What I want is a principle, or a short list of two or three competing principles, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to next year's docket: one that says what the currency is, whose desert is being assessed, and how much.

My verdicts, as I wrote them down: (a) Two engineers of equal record compete for one promotion, and a coin flip decides. The winner is entitled to the raise, but she does not deserve it; the loser has a complaint about the tie-breaking procedure but no complaint in desert. (b) A colleague solves a problem our whole group was stuck on; a rival lab would have solved it within about six weeks. I give her full credit, and I feel the nearness of the rival is simply irrelevant. (c) A five-person team produces something worth far more than the sum of what any five individuals would have produced; four members were interchangeable, but one occupied an integrator role that nobody else on the team could have filled. The integrator deserves more, but not the entire synergy surplus, and I cannot say how the surplus should be split. (d) Someone's very high output rests on a native patience at debugging that she did nothing to acquire, though she has consciously refined and relied on it for fifteen years. I credit her in full, undiscounted, but I feel shaky about it. (e) Someone puts two years of sixty-hour weeks into a project that turns out worthless because the funding climate shifts through no fault of hers. She deserves real recognition, though less than if it had succeeded. (f) A colleague is industrious because a mentor drilled a work ethic into her at fourteen; she never chose to be that way. I do not discount her desert at all; the disposition is hers now. (g) Another colleague shows exactly the same effort and output, but her diligence is the product of a management wellness program that nudges staff without telling them. Here I do feel her desert is reduced, at least somewhat, even though nothing about her effort or output differs from the previous case. (h) A passer-by is the only person present and pulls a child out of a pond at trivial cost to herself. Praise, yes; bonus, no. (i) A brilliant colleague solves in an afternoon what would take the rest of us months, enjoys it, and sacrifices nothing. I actively resist the suggestion that she deserves less because it was easy for her. (j) A star with a large outside offer gets a retention payment. I will vote for it, but I refuse to call it deserved; that is price and bargaining power, not desert. (k) Someone's contribution is to a project that, unforeseeably, turns out to have caused harm. Neither credit nor blame: zero, not merely a reduced amount.

The two pairs I cannot reconcile are f with g, and h with i: in the first pair effort and output are identical yet I discount one and not the other; in the second, easiness seems to erode desert in one case and to be irrelevant in the other. My own tentative attempt, for you to test against the list and repair or replace: an agent deserves credit for an outcome in proportion to the difference in expected value, assessed at the time of action, between what happened and what a duly careful replacement drawn from the same role would have produced, but only to the extent that the difference is attributable to something the agent can now count as her own, meaning an effort, a choice, or a trait she has over time identified with and sustained. Please test that formulation case by case, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon and what abandoning them would cost me, and if you offer alternatives, name the case that motivates each clause and flag anywhere the principle is silent. Then give me its verdicts on four cases I have no settled view about: two people who independently produce the same solution on the same day, each of whom would have produced it without the other; a colleague who obtains a crucial result by orchestrating a machine-learning system that performs the actual derivation, while she poses the problem, designs the checks and interprets the output; a contribution judged mediocre at the time that becomes enormously valuable thirty years later because of changes outside anyone's control; and the one person who could have flagged a serious flaw, and who never realized she was the only one in a position to see it, but did not act. Finally, tell me honestly whether the pattern in my verdicts suggests I should stop talking about desert here and reach instead for entitlement, incentives, or some egalitarian consideration, and whether desert, if I keep it, even comes in quantities comparable across people. Citations aren't needed; I can supply those myself.
