ok so I had a four-hour argument with my friend Sam last night about the Colston statue in Bristol and I need someone to actually do the theoretical work on it because we just kept going in circles and I think we were talking past each other by about hour two.

Facts as I remember them, take them as given, don't correct my history unless something is load-bearing: the statue of Edward Colston was put up in 1895, about 170 years after the man died, right in the middle of the big civic statue-building craze. The way I heard it, Bristol's council was worried the city was being eclipsed by Birmingham, Liverpool and Manchester, they set up a commission to pick someone to commemorate, abolitionists were floated as candidates, and the Society of Merchant Venturers leaned on them to pick Colston instead, with his role in the Royal African Company's slave trade quietly left out. The plaque apparently described him as one of the most virtuous and wise sons of the city. Debate rumbled from the 90s, in 2018 the council tried to add a second plaque giving the slave-trading context and it got stuck in a fight over the exact wording, then on 7 June 2020 during a Black Lives Matter protest the thing got pulled down, defaced and rolled into the harbour. The council fished it out, showed it lying down and still graffitied in the M Shed museum, the four people who helped topple it were cleared of criminal damage by a jury in 2022, and from what I can tell this year they put a brand new plaque on the bare plinth that drops the "city benefactor" language and describes the statue's removal instead.

The part I want analysed is the framework Sam and I sort of built on the fly, which I'm sure has a literature somewhere but we were making it up as we went:

1. The uptake theory. A monument honours someone only if the relevant public is actually disposed to treat it as honouring them. Honour is a speech act and it needs uptake, so it can fail silently: a statue nobody reads as honouring does not honour, even standing upright on its plinth. And symmetrically, keeping it up is not a neutral baseline, it's continued speech by whoever maintains it.
2. The substitution test. If the commemorators would have been nearly as happy unveiling a different figure, then the monument was never really about the person, it was self-praise by the living with the dead man as a placeholder. If a monument passes this test, the whole object-level argument about whether he deserves it is aimed at the wrong target.
3. Commemorative luck. Borrowing from moral luck: what your monument means in 120 years is not under your control, so you can't treat the 1895 donors as having made a present-tense claim that we are now rebutting, and equally the people who toppled it didn't get to authorise what the footage would do.

Here's what I actually want. Run the Colston case through that framework properly and be disciplined about the mapping: who counts as the speaker at each stage, what exactly is the utterance, who is the relevant public whose uptake is supposed to matter, and what would even count as evidence of uptake — council votes, petitions, poll numbers, vandalism, whether coach tours stop there? Then tell me what it explains that the normal public debate gets wrong. My guesses: that the 2018 plaque row is not a technocratic fix that bureacracy bungled but a second contested speech act, so "just add context" was never the cheap compromise people called it; that "it's just history, leave it up" cannot even be stated under this view; that the graffitied statue lying on its side in a museum is a genuinely third option that is neither honouring nor erasing, which both Sam and I had missed completely.

Then break it. Where I think it falls apart: the theory has no account of standing — nothing in it says whether the city council, the crowd in the street, or twelve jurors gets to speak for Bristol, and it silently helps itself to "the public" as if that were given. And uptake looks cheap: one afternoon of protest and a viral video manufactures uptake, decades of campaigning manufactured apparently none, so the theory threatens to collapse into "whoever is most photogenic wins." Is that a bug in my framing or a real feature of the case? Also it seems to have nothing at all to say about the criminal damage trial, because the law was asking about property and the theory is only about meaning — say if that's a genuine silence rather than forcing it.

And the bit I care about most, running the other direction: does the case damage the theory? Does it force a durability or legitimacy condition onto uptake, and does adding one smuggle in all the democratic questions the theory was trying to avoid? Does the substitution test come out so strongly for Colston that the theory ends up saying the people actually honoured in 1895 were the Merchant Venturers and not Colston, which would mean half the arguments since have been about the wrong subject? And if the new plaque on the empty plinth narrates the toppling, hasn't the plinth just become a monument to 2020 Bristol's self-image replacing a monument to 1895 Bristol's self-image, in which case my framework predicts that we are always just talking to ourselves about ourselves? Tell me if that's a real implication or if I've built a theory that can only ever say that, which would make it useless.
