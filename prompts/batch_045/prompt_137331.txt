Hey — I need a runnable activity for one 90-minute session of a reading group on inferring what people want from what they do. About 14 people: mostly first-year CS PhD students, plus four or five philosophy grad students, mixed background, no shared reading beyond intro ML and some philosophy of action. Room has a projector and I can print stuff and tape a grid on the floor.

What I want them to feel, not be told: that the same observed behaviour is compatible with wildly different stories about what the agent cares about, and that this isn't a "you didn't collect enough data" problem — it's structural. Watching more of the same setup doesn't help. What does help is changing the choice set and making assumptions about *how* the agent picks. I want them to hit that themselves.

Two design constraints:

1. No framing lecture. Nobody should hear the labels — inverse RL, reward function, identifiability, revealed preference — until the debrief starts. They should be doing something: moving tokens across a real grid on the floor, or a paper grid, or a shared sheet if you think that works better.
2. The wrinkle I want at the centre is the gap between *can't* and *don't want to*. Roughly half the room are secretly "demonstrators" and each gets a card. Half the cards say: you strongly dislike the shaded tiles, avoid them if you reasonably can. The other half say: you are simply forbidden from entering the shaded tiles. In environment one everyone behaves identically. The rest are "inferrers": they watch, write down what they think the demonstrator cares about, then must predict what happens in environment two, where the shaded tiles become reachable by a route that didn't exist before. That's where the two hypotheses should come apart — but only if the demonstrator is a bit noisy, so build the noise in on purpose rather than leaving it to chance.

So: give me rules, printable materials, a minute-by-minute timeline for 90 (including setup, which always takes longer than I think), and a payoff scheme with no money — points, tokens, candy, whatever. Tell me what the facilitator actually says at each transition, because I'm bad at improvising that bit.

For the debrief I need the moments mapped onto the idea: what the inferrers wrote down, where their predictions broke, why more demonstrations from environment one wouldn't have saved them, what re-designing the grid buys you and why that's the analogue of varying prices in revealed-preference theory (I might be mangling that analogy — say so if I am), and the two degeneracies I half-remember: that a totally flat "nothing matters" reward explains any behaviour at all, and that you can add a difference-of-potentials style bonus across a step to a reward without changing which path is best, so the reward isn't pinned down even when the policy is. If I've got either of those wrong, correct me.

Also: an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time. I can already see two problems — one of the philosophy students will spot the flat-reward degeneracy in the first ten minutes and say it out loud, and at least one demonstrator will accidentally show their card. Tell me how to handle both without killing the exercise. And be frank about which part of the idea the game simply can't stage and I'll have to supply in words.

Optional: is it worth a third round where the demonstrators are told they're being modelled and scored on making the inferrers wrong? If it fits, sketch it; if it eats too much time or muddies the point, say that instead.

One epistemic thing: when you explain the background, separate the settled math from what people in the field genuinely fight about — e.g. whether modelling people as approximately rational planners is defensible at all, whether preferences/values are even the right thing to be inferring as opposed to norms or reasons. Don't present a contested modelling assumption as the consensus.
