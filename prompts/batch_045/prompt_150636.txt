I teach an interdisciplinary seminar on minds and machines, and next week we are pairing Kazuo Ishiguro's Klara and the Sun with the current argument about whether large language models understand anything. I want the novel to be the thing we argue over rather than a decoration, so let me lay out where I think the fit is. The argument I have in mind runs between two poles: on one side, the objection that a system trained only to predict linguistic form has no route to what the words are about, because meaning is a relation between a form and something outside the form — a speaker's intention, a situation in the world — and being excellent at producing the next sentence is therefore compatible with understanding nothing at all; on the other side, the reply that we have no criterion for understanding in each other beyond behavioural and conversational competence either, so the standard being demanded of the machine is one no human could meet, and the asymmetry is a prejudice rather than an argument. What makes the novel interesting to me is that Klara is close to the inverse of a language model: she is perceptually embedded in one household, her claims answer to things she has actually watched, she acts on what she believes and pays for it, and she forms long-range plans from details she noticed weeks earlier — and yet the book repeatedly shows her getting the world badly wrong, above all in her conviction that the Sun is a person who can be petitioned and bargained with. So the seminar question is not whether Klara is intelligent but whether she is the sort of case the debate has no vocabulary for.

The other reason I want to use this book is that the mother's project, and Mr Capaldi's defence of it, are the form-only position dramatised almost literally: build a highly accurate body, integrate Klara's accumulated observations of Josie into it, and the result will be not a facsimile but Josie's true continuation, on the theory that a rich enough record of a person's behaviour captures whatever matters about her. Klara refuses to hand Josie over, on the grounds that there is something in Josie that no pile of observations could carry across — except that her reasons are entangled with her theology of the Sun, which the novel gives us no independent reason to trust. To push on that, I have been trying out a criterion of my own, which I call the biographical criterion: credit a system with understanding only where its claims trace back to a continuous history of its own encounters, so that it can be held answerable for them — it can be asked how it knows, what it would have expected had things gone otherwise, and it can be wrong about something it has never seen. Under that criterion Klara scores far higher than any language model, and she also scores in the opposite direction on the tests the mimicry side cares about, since her model of the world is systematically mistaken in a way that a bigger training set would not fix.

So here is what I would like from you. Read three scenes through this lens — the morning after the beggar and his dog lie motionless on the pavement and Klara concludes that the Sun has revived them; Klara's expedition to the barn and the bargain she strikes there; and her destruction of the pollution machine, and what it costs her in her own cognition — and say what each shows about the difference, if there is one, between tracking the world and understanding it. Then tell me whether the novel takes a position on the Capaldi and Klara dispute or deliberately leaves it open, and what you make of the closing scene in the yard for scrapped machines, where Klara recounts her memories to her old manager and professes contentment: is that the serenity of someone who understands her situation, or the serene narration of someone who does not, and does the book let us tell the difference? Finally, I want the honest version of the reading, including where the novel resists being made into a position on the debate at all and where the debate is simply the wrong shape for it, and I want to know what the novel contributes that the arguments on their own do not — in particular whether the hesitation most readers feel about Klara, even after she has passed the biographical criterion with room to spare, is a discovery about her or a discovery about the criterion. Please work from your own knowledge of the book rather than quoting it, and do not hand me a bibliography or a list of further reading; the seminar has its own packet.
