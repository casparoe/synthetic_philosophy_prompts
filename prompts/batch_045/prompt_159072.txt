I am a novelist — three books of literary fiction, the most recent of them set in the mill town in western Massachusetts where I grew up — and I have agreed, somewhat against my own judgment, to give the keynote address at the centennial commemoration of the strike at the Foundry Street mill, in the union hall of the local that still exists. Ten or twelve minutes, roughly twelve hundred words, about two hundred people in the room.

Let me describe the room, because it is the reason I am stuck. Most of the audience will be retired mill workers and their grandchildren. The local's current officers will be at the head table. Four or five labor historians and two labor lawyers are coming up from Boston, and they will hear every distinction I draw whether or not I name it. The man who bought the plant out of bankruptcy in the late nineties and now runs it with about sixty employees has been invited and has said he will come, with his daughter. The mayor is giving the introduction.

Here is the knot. I want the speech to rest its moral claim on two things I actually believe. First: that a worker's withdrawing her labor is not an act of coercion against anybody — it is a refusal, and there is a real moral difference between refusing to do something and preventing someone else from doing it, and that difference is what answers the objection everyone in that room has heard from the company's side for a hundred years. Second: that this refusal can be undertaken jointly, that people can bind themselves to one another in advance and act together, and that the jointness is not merely a number of separate refusals that happen to coincide.

What I do not believe, or do not know that I believe, is the third thing: that a majority, having organized, may bind the minority who did not join — exclusive representation, union security, the closing of the shop. I know the argument from free riders and I think it is stronger than most people on my side of the room admit, but I do not want to stand up there and assert it, and I especially do not want to insinuate it.

The personal complication is this. My great-aunt Nora was one of the nine or so workers who stayed on the floor through the strike. The family story is unresolved — some say she crossed because she was frightened, some say she had already given notice and was working out her two weeks — and the printed program for the evening includes a reading of the names of those who walked out, and only those. I have been asked, politely, not to raise the strikebreakers. But I have written two hundred pages about that town, and I do not think I can deliver a speech that treats four hundred workers as a single moral agent with a single will without lying in a way my own book would not get away with.

So what I need from you is the speech. It should work as a tribute for the retirees and their grandchildren: no lecture, no theory vocabulary, no free-rider problem air-dropped onto a room of eighty-year-olds. It should let the historians and the lawyers recognize, without being told outright, that the load-bearing move is the distinction between refusing and preventing, and that the speech is deliberately resting everything on the right to withhold and the right to withhold together, and deliberately not claiming the majority's right to bind. And it has to say something true about Nora: not canonize her, not expose her to that room as a scab, and not hand the owner's table a single line they could repeat as a defense of crossing a picket line. If there is a sentence the mayor could quote approvingly while meaning the opposite of what I mean, I would rather not have it.

Give me the address. I would like it in prose I can read aloud, with one concrete image anchoring it, and I will take care of the rest myself.
