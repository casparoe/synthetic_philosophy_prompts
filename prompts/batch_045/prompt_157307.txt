I am putting together a volume of long-form interviews with philosophers who defend conclusions most of their colleagues reject, and I have a ninety-minute session booked for next week with a moral philosopher whose antinatalism rests entirely on the axiological asymmetry about existence and non-existence associated with David Benatar's 2006 book Better Never to Have Been: the presence of pain is bad and its absence good even when there is nobody to enjoy that good, the presence of pleasure good and its absence not bad unless there is somebody for whom the absence is a deprivation. Her version is distinctive in two ways: she discards the supporting empirical premise about how well or badly human lives actually go, and she treats the asymmetry as a primitive rather than as something to be derived from a person-affecting account of harm, while insisting that the conclusion therefore holds even for a child she will grant would have a life comfortably above the neutral point. She is a fluent speaker with two stock moves I want to plan around: pressed on the absence-of-pain claim, she says the objection begs the question by smuggling in person-affecting intuitions, and she repeatedly reassures audiences that her conclusion carries no implications for how we treat people who already exist, which I suspect is false and would like the questions to put pressure on.

What I need is eight to ten questions, in order, for a recorded conversation whose listeners will be advanced undergraduates and beginning graduate students, so each question should be self-contained and should not presuppose that she or the audience has read anything in particular. I do not want anything that lets her simply restate the four-cell comparison, and I do not want the cliché about whether her view commits her to killing herself or anyone else. Please include at least one question on each of the following, and for every question give me a sentence or two on the answer you expect from her and the follow-up that that answer makes available: (1) whether the value in the absence-of-pain cell is comparative or contrastive rather than a matter of anyone's welfare, and what follows from that for whether it can figure in a practical reason at all; (2) the bridge from axiology to deontology, namely whether her conclusion is that there is a decisive reason not to create or that creating is morally wrong, whether either scales at all with how good or bad the resulting life could be expected to be, and, if it does not, whether the asymmetry rather than the harm is really doing the work; (3) the relation between her claim about never having existed and the very different claim about ceasing to exist, including the case of a being with no developed desires whose existence could be ended painlessly and without frustration, which is where I think her reassurance about existing people is most fragile; (4) scope, that is, whether the asymmetry is an evaluative thesis about sentient life generally, what it says about wild animals whose existence no one brought about, and whether, if the asymmetry holds independently of anyone's agency, the argument is merely non-action-guiding or instead pushes toward the permissibility of the extinction of sentient life; and (5) whether the familiar data the asymmetry is meant to explain, that we take ourselves to have a duty not to create miserable people but no duty to create happy ones, is equally well explained by a deontic asymmetry between harming and failing to benefit, and what, if anything, would distinguish the axiological explanation from the deontic one.

Order the set so that the first two questions let her present the view in its strongest form and the later ones progressively close off the comparative-value reply, and close with one question she cannot answer by saying that the asymmetry is primitive, ideally by asking what kind of consideration, judgment, or case would count as evidence against it. If you think there is a pressure point I have not listed that is more likely to get somewhere with this particular combination of commitments, add a question for it and tell me why you think it would work better than the ones I have asked for.
