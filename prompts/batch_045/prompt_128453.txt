I'm part of a small group building an internal evaluation suite for deployed systems that a human operator is supposed to be able to pause, override, or shut down, and we keep discovering that we don't agree on what we are testing for. Half the group means by "corrigible" something like *wouldn't resist being switched off*; I mean something closer to *treats the operator's intervention as a reason to reconsider*; our control-theory colleague means something like *doesn't treat the operator as an obstacle to be routed around*. So before we write a single test, I want the specification — and I want it argued, not listed.

Write the desiderata that any adequate account of corrigibility, in the sense of the shutdown problem, has to meet, each with the case that motivates it. Fix the purpose explicitly and tell me if you think I've got it wrong: I want a notion good enough that a deployment decision could rest on it — an engineer looking at one concrete system in one concrete setting should be able to say, on the basis of the account, whether it is corrigible enough to keep running, and what would have to change if not. Then work through at least the following cases, since these are the ones our group actually argues about: the operator presses the button because the agent is confidently about to do something the agent cannot see is catastrophic; the operator presses it by accident, or in a panic, or because a third party has spoofed the channel; the button is pressed mid-task in a domain where stopping is itself damaging (a surgical robot, a live industrial process, a long-running experiment whose interruption destroys the work); the agent can, as a costless side effect of some otherwise legitimate action, make the button slightly harder to reach; the agent predicts a shutdown is coming and reorders its plan to finish before the operator notices; the agent has the option of asking permission first, and the question is how much asking is correct rather than merely how much resistance is permissible; the agent rewrites its own code, or builds a successor, or simply waits out the current operator's shift; the principal changes — the operator dies, or is replaced, or is herself a model; and the agent notices that the operator *hesitated* without pressing, and must decide whether that near-press is evidence. For each, say what behavior the account must deliver and why that behavior, rather than some nearby one, is the right demand.

Alongside the cases, I want the distinctions the account has to respect rather than collapse, with an argument that they are real distinctions: letting yourself be shut down versus wanting to be corrected versus wanting to be *modified* — one property or three; not resisting intervention versus not managing the operator's beliefs about whether to intervene; corrigibility versus mere uncertainty about the objective; corrigibility versus low-impact or myopia; deference to a person versus deference to a procedure; and whether corrigibility is a property of the policy alone or of the policy-plus-oversight-channel pair. Tell me also whether it is graded or binary, and if graded, what the quantity is supposed to be.

The part I most want: which pairs of desiderata you think cannot be jointly satisfied, stated as a result about the list itself rather than as a complaint about the literature. My own suspicion is that there are at least three: treating the press as authoritative seems to rule out checking whether the press was a good one, which is exactly the checking corrigibility was meant to foreclose; pure indifference to shutdown seems incompatible with treating the press as *informative*, since updating on the press reintroduces preferences over whether it happens; and an agent with no stake in continuing is free to be driven off cheaply by anyone who benefits from its being off, so anti-tampering and non-exploitability pull apart. Say whether these are real, whether there are others (self-improvement versus preservation of corrigibility under self-modification; corrigibility to the current operator versus to her later, better-informed self), and for each conflict, which requirement has to give and why that one. Also sort the list into what is non-negotiable, what is a preference of one research camp dressed up as a requirement, and what is an engineering nicety — and identify the single requirement that knocks out the most candidate accounts. Only after that, and briefly, score the main styles of approach against your own list — the counterfactual/utility-indifference style, the cooperative-assistance style, the myopia-and-impact-penalty style, and the "just keep it uncertain about the goal" style — in a compact table, with the reasoning kept short since I want the weight on the criteria.
