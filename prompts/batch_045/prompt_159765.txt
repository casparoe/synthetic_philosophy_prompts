Write a satirical piece, in the form of a letter of submission to a fictional standards body, proposing a regulation you can call the Mandatory Elaboration Standard: the rule that no sentence may be handed to a machine translation system until the speaker has stripped out of it every feature whose correct rendering depends on anything outside the sentence itself. Among the features the letter should legislate against, with worked examples, are at least these three: a pronoun whose referent flips when a single adjective changes (a sentence like "the sofa won't go through the doorway because it's too wide" beside one that ends "because it's too narrow"); an idiom or a lexical gap, where the target language draws a distinction the source language does not, so that the right word cannot be picked from the words alone; and a deictic expression — "here", "she", "tomorrow" — that only means anything to someone who knows the situation of utterance. The letter must be deadpan and bureaucratic in voice, written as though the proposal were sober and overdue, and the comedy must come from getting the underlying point exactly right: each of these really is a place where a system that maps forms to forms has nothing to go on, and each reform really would remove the difficulty, at a cost the reader should feel rather than be told. Let the reductio emerge from the detail — as the compliance requirements multiply, it must become visible that there is no principled stopping point, that every attempt to spell the context out produces a new sentence with its own unstated context, and that what is being regulated away is not an inconvenience but the fact that speakers share a world — and, if you can manage it, let the letter's own prose betray this by leaning on something it has left implicit. Do not name, quote, or wink at any real person, company, or publication: the target is the position that translation is exhausted by the mapping of forms onto forms, not any of its defenders. Aim for about six hundred words, in British English, written as one continuous letter with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
