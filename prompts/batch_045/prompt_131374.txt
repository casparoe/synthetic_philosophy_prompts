I've been going in circles for about a year on a question in metaontology, and what I want from you is not a verdict on the first-order dispute but a diagnosis of why the second-order one never moves.

Here is the standoff as I experience it. Take an ordinary-looking ontological dispute — whether there are composite material objects, or whether there are numbers. Camp A says that once each side's semantic conventions are made fully explicit, the appearance of a factual clash dissolves: the parties are proposing different candidate meanings for the quantifiers and the relevant idioms, no one candidate is privileged over the others, and the choice among them is settled, if at all, by expressive power, simplicity, or practical convenience rather than by how the world is. Camp B says that there is a fact of the matter about how the world is structured, that a quantificational apparatus either matches that structure or fails to, and that Camp A's central thesis — that there are many equally good candidate meanings and no privileged one — is itself advanced as a true claim about meaning, not as a piece of semantic bookkeeping. Each camp's reply to the other's main argument has been stable for a long time. Camp A says Camp B's notion of structure can never be stated non-circularly and so does no work in an explanation. Camp B says Camp A is performatively inconsistent, since the thesis that ontological disputes are merely verbal is not itself offered as merely verbal.

I have two hypotheses and I want them tested rather than indulged. The first is that the two camps are running different tests for substantiveness and neither notices. Call world-sensitivity the question whether the answer would vary with how the world is, holding language fixed; call consequence-sensitivity the question whether the answer constrains anything downstream, such as which inferences from ordinary sentences are licensed or which enquiries are worth pursuing. A dispute can score low on the first and high on the second, and my sense is that Camp A tests only for the first while Camp B's arguments actually trade on the second. If so, this is two questions under one heading. The second hypothesis is deeper: that both camps accept a bipartite taxonomy in which any dispute must be either about the world or about words, and that this shared presupposition is what keeps the exchange on rails. If there is a third thing a dispute can be — say, a dispute about which representational scheme to adopt, where schemes differ in genuine virtues but not in world-sensitivity — then I want to know whether Camp A collapses into it, whether Camp B can accommodate it, or whether it is a genuinely distinct position that nobody has occupied.

I also want to know whether the diagnosis has to be uniform. The composite-objects case and the numbers case seem to me to differ: in the first it is arguable that something hangs in the balance for ordinary assertions, in the second it is arguable that nothing does beyond the structure claim itself. If the right answer is that this is really several disputes sharing a name, that is itself the diagnosis and I want it argued for.

So: which of these is right, what am I missing, and why? Argue from what each side actually claims rather than from labels, and tell me what, on your diagnosis, would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a reframing of the question, a changed burden of proof — or else argue that no progress should be expected and say why. I have read the literature, so please do not summarise positions, attribute claims to particular papers or books, or give me anything bibliographic; engage the arguments themselves. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question. British English in your answer, please.
