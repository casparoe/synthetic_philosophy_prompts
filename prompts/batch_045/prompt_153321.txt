QUESTION — "Middle knowledge: usefulness and coherence."

Some critics hold that the doctrine of middle knowledge (scientia media) is either theologically idle or metaphysically incoherent; others hold that it is the only way to reconcile providential control with libertarian creaturely freedom. Answer all four parts.

(1) Set out the threefold distinction among God's natural knowledge, middle knowledge, and free knowledge (knowledge of vision). Explain what "middle" means here: what is middle knowledge supposed to be prior to, and why does that priority matter for the doctrine's usefulness?

(2) Explain the difference between strongly actualizing a state of affairs and weakly actualizing one, and explain how a Molinist claims God can bring it about that a creature freely performs a given act without determining the creature's will.

(3) State the grounding objection to middle knowledge as precisely as you can, and assess the Molinist reply that counterfactuals of creaturely freedom are true independently of God's will.

(4) Suppose for the sake of argument that middle knowledge is coherent. Would it follow that God could have actualized a world in which free creatures exist but no creature ever freely does what is morally wrong? Explain why or why not.

STUDENT'S ANSWER

(1) God's knowledge is standardly divided into three. Natural knowledge (knowledge of simple intelligence) is God's knowledge of all necessary truths and of everything that could possibly exist; it depends on nothing but God's own nature, and God has no choice about its content. Free knowledge, or knowledge of vision, is God's knowledge of the actual created order — everything that will in fact happen; its content depends on which world God decides to create, and so it follows the divine decree. Middle knowledge sits between the two: it is God's knowledge of what every possible free creature would freely do in any possible circumstances in which it might be placed. Its objects are contingent but not fixed by what God actually does — counterfactuals of freedom, true independently of any decree of the will. That is why it is called "middle": it is logically prior to God's creative decree, and indeed prior in the order of time as well, since before settling on a decree God surveys what each possible creature would do; but it is not part of natural knowledge, because its objects could have been otherwise. Its usefulness lies exactly in this priority. Without it God would either choose a world in the dark or else determine our choices; with it He can plan a world in full knowledge of how free creatures would respond.

(2) To strongly actualize a state of affairs is to cause it to obtain directly: God can strongly actualize whatever lies within His power — that a person exists, that certain circumstances obtain. To weakly actualize a state of affairs is to arrange circumstances such that, given the true counterfactuals of freedom, a free creature would freely bring it about. God does not cause the choice; He arranges the situation in which the creature, left to itself, would make it. So a Molinist can say that God weakly actualizes everything in the world except the free choices themselves, having known from eternity which choices those would be. The creature is the sole cause of its act, and the act remains free in the libertarian sense — in the very circumstances in which it acts, the creature could have done otherwise — even though God knew it with certainty and arranged those circumstances precisely so that it would occur. Providence and libertarian freedom are therefore compatible.

(3) The grounding objection is this: middle knowledge would make God dependent on His creatures. If the content of God's knowledge is settled by what free creatures would do, then God's plan is hostage to us, and His sovereignty is limited by something outside His control: He must consult something independent of Himself before He can act, which compromises His aseity. The Molinist reply is that the objection gets the direction of dependence wrong. God freely decides which creatures to create and which circumstances to place them in, so nothing is forced on Him; the creatures exist only because He chooses to create them, and He surveys the counterfactuals and then decrees exactly as He pleases. He is not dependent on them. The objection rests on confusing knowledge with causation: to know what someone would do is not to be constrained by them.

(4) No, it would not follow. Middle knowledge tells God what would happen in any circumstances, but it does not tell Him that the counterfactuals are favourable. God can weakly actualize only worlds that are weakly actualizable for Him, and it may be that every creaturely essence is such that, in some of the circumstances in which it might significantly exercise its freedom, it would go wrong — the possibility that goes under the name of transworld depravity. In that case no world containing free creatures is free of moral wrongdoing, and this is a limit on what God can weakly actualize rather than a limit on His power as such. So what middle knowledge secures is control over which of the feasible worlds obtains; whether a world with free creatures and no moral evil is among the feasible ones is a further question that the doctrine does not by itself settle.
