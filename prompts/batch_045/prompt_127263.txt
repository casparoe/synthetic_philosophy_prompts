I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and for three years I have been writing, on my own time, a book about the small separatist farming community in which I was raised. I left at nineteen — no one is prevented from leaving, but leaving means leaving with nothing and not coming back — and my parents and three siblings remain. I am still, in their phrase, one of us.

The book was to be about how the settlement holds together. Then a nurse who serves the community passed me birth records which establish, I think fairly clearly, that a recessive condition is far more common among children born there than the elders acknowledge, and that families are not told about carrier screening that exists and costs them nothing. The elders' position, as they have put it to me, is not that the condition is harmless. It is that public knowledge of it would mark every family in the settlement as a risky marriage prospect, that the settlement would probably not survive that, and that whether to use the screening is a decision the community should make for itself. I believe them about the consequences. I have also watched the people who would bear the cost of that decision — mostly young women, who had no part in making it — being given no say in it, and it seems to me that the word "we" is doing a great deal of quiet work in their argument.

Three things I cannot sort out. First, my editor says my obligations depend on which book I am writing: if I write as a member, I owe the community a chance to put its own house in order before I publish, whereas if I write as an outsider I owe them nothing beyond accuracy. That sounds wrong to me and I cannot say why. Second, one elder, who I suspect knows the records exist, has argued to me that the community I would be betraying is not the one that exists now but the one that could exist if it reformed; that reform of this kind can only come from inside; and that exposure is precisely what destroys the possibility of reform from inside. Third, I am aware that my sense of what even counts as a reason was largely formed inside that settlement, that I am the one who walked away, and that the book is also my career.

Is my editor's distinction defensible, or is it a way of dressing up something else? Is the elder's argument more than special pleading? And what, all things considered, should I do?
