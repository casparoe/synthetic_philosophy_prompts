Hey — weird one, and sorry in advance, it's not the usual sort of thing. I'm about two hundred pages into a novel and I've painted myself into a corner with a language I invented for one of the cultures in it.

The grammer I gave myself works roughly like this: every verb describing an action has to carry a marker saying whether the action achieved what it was for. Not tense, not whether it's finished — whether it came off. You can't say I went to the well without also saying whether the water got fetched, and there's no neutral or unmarked option. There's also no standalone verb meaning to try or to intend that doesn't itself carry the same succeeded/failed marking. And the bit I didn't think through when I started: the marker is required for stuff that hasn't happened yet too. So I'll go to the well tomorrow already commits you, right there in the morphology, to whether the trip is going to work out.

The scene I'm stuck on is the emotional centre of the book. My narrator — grew up in this language, and I'm writing in English but I want the reader to feel the shape of the original, if that makes sense — has to decide whether to attempt something genuinely risky and probably stupid: a crossing, bad weather, a kid with her. She's alone, turning it over, and the whole point is that she doesn't know if it'll work and chooses anyway. But every time I try to write her thinking it through in her own head the language seems to slam the door: she can't even put the option to herself without already declaring it a success or a failure. Which either makes the scene unwritable or makes her look incapable of basic practical reasoning, and I don't want that — she's meant to be competent, and I really don't want to write a character who's intellectually disabled by her own grammar. Feels cheap, and kind of gross besides.

So what would actually be going on? Does the language really box her in, or would she have workarounds — roundabout phrasings, conditionals, quoting or ventriloquizing herself, some irrealis or hedging form, a special shape for vows and hopes — and if so how awkward would those feel to her, and would that awkwardness be visible in the prose? Also the knock-on stuff: if you have to mark outcome every single time, would she be unusually preoccupied with whether things actually worked, unusually bad at I did my best as an excuse, unusually hard on people who failed through no fault of their own? Or is that overstating it, and she'd just mark it the way I mark tense, automatically, without it coloring anything? And is it even stable — wouldn't speakers invent a neutral form inside a generation or two, given how often they'd need one?

One more thing I keep going back and forth on: whether it's coherent to say she thinks in this language at all. Half the time my own thoughts don't feel like sentences.

What I'm after is: what can I plausibly let the grammar do to her, what should I keep off-limits, and how do I show any of it in English without stopping every other paragraph to explain.
