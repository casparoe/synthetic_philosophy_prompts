I am a novelist, and I have been asked to write a long essay for a general-readership magazine on what I think I learned about a certain kind of wrongdoing while writing a novel about it. The book is finished; the essay is not. What follows is the spine of the argument I am considering, set out as notes, plus one short passage of actual prose from the novel that I intend to quote in the essay. I would be grateful for comments before I commit to it. Please do not suggest sources, citations, or further reading: I am working away from any research library and want to know what the argument itself is worth, not what has been written about it.

The novel's central figure is an invented mid-level official in a bureaucracy of transportation and allocation — not a policy-maker, not an ideologue, not a sadist. He is competent, mildly ambitious, affable, mildly bored at dinner. He does not hate anyone. Over the course of the book he never once decides to kill anyone, and by the end he has been indispensable to the killing of a great many people. I wrote him because I wanted to test, in the only medium available to me, a claim I had long half-accepted and half-distrusted.

The target. The familiar version of the claim about the banality of evil says, roughly: the frightening thing is not that such agents are demonic but that they are shallow — that the inner life behind the atrocity is ordinary, cliché-ridden, careerist, unreflective. I take this to be broadly what Arendt got from watching the Eichmann trial: a functionary who struck her as remarkably lacking in the depths one expects of a villain, whose courtroom speech was made largely of officialese and stock phrases, who presented himself as a man merely doing his job and who was apparently incapable of occupying anyone else's point of view. She insisted this was not stupidity but something closer to a failure to think; and she insisted, too, that the deeds themselves were anything but banal. My essay wants to keep that last insistence and to relocate the banality somewhere other than the man's psyche.

Three worries about the familiar version, as I restate it. First, the trial-setting worry: a defendant being tried for his life has overwhelming reasons to perform ordinariness, and critics have argued on the basis of material he produced in exile that his courtroom persona was precisely that — a performance, and one that worked on observers. If the banality was partly an artifact of a courtroom, it is thin evidence about the moral psychology of perpetrators generally. Second, the enthusiasm worry: plenty of people in comparable positions were not shallow, not careerist, not bored — some were zealous, inventive, eager. A thesis that fits only one temperament among many is in danger of being a thesis about one man. Third, and most important for my purposes, the exculpation worry: the more convincingly one shows that the agent's inner life is drab, the more one invites the reader to conclude that there was nothing there to blame. Shallowness starts to sound like a diminished-capacity argument with better prose.

My revision. I want to propose that we stop looking for the banality inside the person and look for it in the interface — the set of mediations through which the killing reaches him: job descriptions, routing forms, timetables, euphemisms, the division of a task into competences, the signature at the bottom of a chain. No item in that interface is itself a killing; each is intelligible, even laudable, in isolation; and the agent can be perfectly sincere in reporting that he never did the thing. Call this the distinction between agentive banality (a claim about the quality of a person's inner life) and interface banality (a claim about the structure that stands between a person and what he brings about). My argument is that interface banality survives all three worries above, whereas agentive banality does not survive the first two and generates the third. It survives them because it is compatible with zeal, with performance, and with any depth of character whatever: a fanatic and a time-server can both be connected to a mass killing through the same machinery of forms.

Two consequences I want to draw, and they are the parts I am least sure about.

The first is a recasting of thoughtlessness. I suggest in the essay that what fails in such an agent is not feeling but narrative: not an inability to suffer with another person but an inability to compose his own acts into a single story. He is perfectly capable of episodes — the memorandum, the phone call, the timetable revision — but not of the plot that those episodes jointly constitute. Evil's banality, on this reading, is a vice of authorship rather than a vice of temperament: the agent lives inside the chapter and never inside the book. This is obviously self-serving for a novelist to argue, and I want the essay to be honest about that.

The second is what I have been calling the interchangeability test. Suppose we ask of my character whether, had he refused, the killing would have proceeded much as it did. Suppose the honest answer is yes. I argue that this is not exculpatory but aggravating, in a specific sense: it removes the consolation that eliminating this person would have eliminated the harm, and so it makes the harm over-determined rather than merely caused. Individual guilt remains — he did what he did, and no one else's readiness to do it is his excuse — but the explanation of the harm cannot be housed in any individual will. A novel can make this felt in a way that reportage about a trial cannot, because a novel can simply show you three chapters about three substitutable officials and let the substitution accumulate.

The risk I most need help with is what I call the compassion drift. The ordinary, rendered well, is charming; the reader's identification with a bored, decent, mildly put-upon man is exactly the aesthetic effect a novelist knows how to produce; and if the essay recommends rendering the interface rather than the monster, it may simply be recommending a more sophisticated route to sympathy. There is a related risk in my own vocabulary: once one starts talking about structures and interfaces, fatalism creeps in at the seams, and the reader hears that no one was to blame because everyone was.

Here is the passage from my own manuscript (my own invention, not a quotation from any source) that I intend to use in the essay to open the section on the interface:

He did not mind the forms. That was the thing he would have said for himself, if anyone had asked, and no one did. A form was a thing that arrived and a thing that left, and between those two events there was a small interval of competence, which he filled neatly, and which was the pleasantest part of his day. Elsewhere in the building there were other intervals of the same kind, and he understood in a general way that they were meant to fit together, the way the timetables fitted together, though he had never seen the whole of the fitting and had no occasion to. What he liked about the work was that it was possible to do it well.

What I would like from you:

1. Accuracy. Where my restatement of the Arendtian claim goes wrong, or flattens something that matters, say so plainly. I am less interested in being corrected on details than in whether I have misdescribed the shape of the position I am trying to revise.

2. Whether the agentive/interface distinction is doing real work, or is just a relabeling of what everyone already meant, or collapses into some distinction I have not noticed that it is competing with.

3. What a hostile reader would say I am dodging. I would particularly value objections that come from a direction I have not laid out above — the thing that makes the argument look evasive rather than merely wrong.

4. Whether the two consequences — thoughtlessness as a failure of narrative, and interchangeability as aggravating rather than excusing — are genuinely implications of the distinction or are smuggled in from my novelist's interests. Is the first one, in particular, anything more than a metaphor about authorship?

5. Editorial judgment, for an intelligent general readership unfamiliar with the scholarship: which of these threads is worth developing at length, which is a private hobbyhorse I should cut, and whether the passage above would actually do the work I want from it or would be read as an elegant way of making the reader comfortable.
