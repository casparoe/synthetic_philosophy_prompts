You are a veteran examiner in moral and political philosophy. You have chaired pre-university and first-year honours assessment boards for years, and you have a reputation for setting questions that a student cannot answer by reciting definitions, however fluently. I need your help constructing one question for a real assessment, and I would like you to proceed carefully rather than quickly. Please work in three labelled stages before you write anything final.

Stage 1 — Diagnosis, in note form. List the three or four things a well-prepared student is most likely to say if this topic is set loosely, and for each, one line on why it would be summary rather than argument.

Stage 2 — Crux. Sketch two or three candidate cruxes for the question. Against each, write one sentence on whether it separates a student who understands the issue from a student who has memorised the labels, and pick one.

Stage 3 — Deliverables. Only then write the question, the model answer, and the marking scheme.

Constraints on the deliverables: no bibliography, no citations, no references to authors, titles, or dates anywhere in the materials. The exam is closed-book and closed-device; everything a student needs, including the typology itself, must be stated inside the question paper, since not every candidate will have encountered it before.

Who I am and what this is for: I am a sixth-form student and I run my school's Philosophy Club. Each spring our club sits a two-hour certificate paper that our faculty advisor reviews, and the senior members mark it under her supervision. The paper is marked out of 25. I want one question, worth the full 25, on the threefold typology of honour, dignity, and victimhood cultures and on whether any one of them is morally superior to the others. I do not want a question that can be answered by writing out what each culture is and then concluding that each has strengths and weaknesses; a candidate who does only that should be capped at roughly 11 out of 25, and I want the scheme to say where exactly that cap bites.

The scenario I have been mulling over, which I would like built into the question: a co-educational day school of about nine hundred students with a socially mixed catchment. Two years ago, after a series of fights following a football match, the school introduced two things at once: an anonymous reporting app administered by an outside charity, and mandatory restorative circles for anything that gets formally reported. After two years, physical fights have fallen sharply, while formal reports have risen roughly fivefold. About a fifth of the reports concern things that happened online and off campus. Teachers report that students increasingly refuse to apologise to each other face to face and instead say the other person should put it in the app. The counsellor says a small number of students generate a large share of the reports, and that being reported is now spoken of as a defeat in a status competition. Several parents say their sons are being taught to inform rather than to stand up for themselves. A group of older girls says the app is the first thing that has ever protected them from what staff used to dismiss as drama.

Please build the question so that it contains, in some form, the following four ideas, which I have not seen handled together and which I think are where the real difficulty lies:

1. The audience-reliability condition. A victimhood mechanism works by recruiting third parties, so its output depends on whether the audience is well informed and unbiased. Candidates should have to say whether a culture whose central mechanism only functions when the audience happens to be reliable can be called morally superior, given that reliability is not under the control of the person using the mechanism, and whether unreliability is a moral defect of the culture or merely bad luck.

2. The standing-recovery test. In an honour culture the person who has done wrong can restore his standing by a recognised act. Candidates should have to ask whether a mechanism that downgrades someone's standing with no routinised way back can be just, and what this implies about a regime that combines public accusation with no formal finding.

3. The degeneration thesis. Where status competition has not disappeared but has moved into the reporting system, a victimhood culture may be an honour culture with the fists removed, so that the interesting question is not which culture a school has adopted but which one its mechanisms silently regenerate.

4. The composability problem. Honour, dignity, and victimhood logics may not combine. Anonymous reporting may raise the perceived cost of a direct, face-to-face apology, so the two reforms in the scenario may each be defensible and jointly self-undermining. Candidates should have to say what this shows about evaluating a culture as a package versus evaluating a mechanism.

Structure the question in two parts: a short application part worth about 8 marks, in which candidates must locate elements of the scenario within the typology and handle the composability problem; and an essay part worth about 17 marks, asking whether any one of the three cultures is morally superior and requiring candidates to state what would have to be true for their answer to change.

Then give:

(a) A model answer of about 700 words, written at the level of a strong seventeen-year-old rather than a journal article. It must take a determinate position rather than surveying options, and it must disambiguate morally superior into at least two distinct axes, since I suspect most of the disagreement in our club comes from people answering different questions without noticing.

(b) A marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth summing to 25, and for each point one line on how candidates typically get it wrong. The scheme must name the specific moves an A answer must contain and the specific moves that would hold an answer at a B, so that two markers would broadly agree. Please also state which single omission should cost the most marks and why.
