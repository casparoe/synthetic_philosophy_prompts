I'm writing a term paper for a course on the philosophy of history and I keep circling around a puzzle I can't quite formulate precisely. It isn't mainly about which start date is correct; it's about what the exercise of fixing one is supposed to be doing. As I understand the practice, a proposed epoch can only become a formal unit of the Geological Time Scale if it is anchored to a Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point: a single physical section of rock, somewhere on Earth, in which some signal is designated as the boundary marker and can in principle be correlated with distant sections, so that the boundary is the same instant everywhere. On that basis the Anthropocene Working Group settled on the sediments of Crawford Lake in Ontario, with fallout plutonium from mid-twentieth-century weapons testing as the marker, putting the boundary around 1950; the proposal was voted down in 2024, I gather largely because the sedimentary record is so thin and the boundary so recent. Meanwhile historians, Indigenous scholars and climate ethicists keep proposing quite different dates — 1610, the 1780s, the Neolithic — and they seem to be doing something else entirely: picking the moment when, in their view, the causally or morally decisive thing happened. And as far as I can tell, the Anthropocene would be the only unit on the chart named after its cause rather than after a place, a rock type, or its position in the sequence, even though its formal definition is supposed to be a purely physical signal.

So my question is what this practice presupposes about what a historical period is, and about what it is to get one right. My best guess is that it presupposes something like: a period is individuated by a globally synchronous physical state of the Earth system, detectable in the material record, and not by a cause, an intention, a meaning, or the point at which some agent became responsible for anything; the boundary has to be correlatable, which is why a diachronous, spreading process supposedly cannot found a unit — some stratigraphers have suggested on exactly this ground that the Anthropocene be formalized as a geological 'event' rather than an epoch, since events are allowed to be diachronous. But I can't tell how much of that is strict presupposition and how much is just methodological convenience that the practice happens to fit well with. Is the synchrony requirement a metaphysical claim about periods — that real periods are simultaneous global states — or only a convention for making a shared nomenclature work, in which case the discovery that many established boundaries turn out to be mildly diachronous wouldn't be a refutation at all? Does the practice really need the causal-exclusion principle, given that designated markers routinely function as proxies for things we actually care about, such as isotopic excursions standing in for climatic or oceanic upheavals? And what are we to make of the fact that the same community, having refused to ratify the epoch, said the term would continue to be used across the sciences and the humanities as a descriptor of human impact: does that presuppose a conventionalism about units combined with a realism about signals, and does it quietly concede that the 'who decides' question was asked about the wrong object?

One more thing is bothering me, and I'd like it addressed rather than smoothed over. Someone in my seminar proposed what she called the foresight criterion: date the beginning of the Anthropocene to the moment at which those responsible for the bulk of cumulative forcing could first reasonably have foreseen the global consequences of what they were doing — so that the boundary marks the onset of culpable ignorance rather than any physical spike. That proposal is obviously not stratigraphic in the ordinary sense, and my instinct is that it commits a category error by confusing why we care about a boundary with where the boundary is. But I'm not confident, and the name pulls the other way: the spike actually chosen is plutonium fallout, produced by a handful of states' weapons programmes, while the name of the unit attributes the whole period to humanity as such, so the practice seems to combine species-level attribution with a marker generated by a very small and unrepresentative subset of the species. Please reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, tell me which rival views of periodization the practice strictly rules out and which it merely sits badly with, say whether the foresight criterion is a confused rival or an unacknowledged element the practice already contains, and describe what would have to change in stratigraphic practice — what we would lose, and what correlation work would become impossible — if a causal or responsibility-based criterion like that one were adopted instead.
