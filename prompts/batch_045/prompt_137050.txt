I work on the compliance side of a seafood certification scheme, and I keep running into one pattern that I cant get a handle on philosophically, so I'd like you to reconstruct what it presupposes.

Here's the pattern. On a salmon site, the same operation keeps a veterinary health plan, treats a bacterial outbreak because untreated fish would suffer, and reports mortality, feed conversion, oxygen and stocking density as its headline welfare numbers. Then at harvest the fish get crowded, pumped, and killed by asphyxiation in ice slurry or in air — the exact sort of death that for a pig or a cow would be straightforwardly illegal, since mammals have to be stunned before slaughter and the stunning itself gets audited. On the shrimp side, a completely standard thing to do to a breeding female is cut or crush her eyestalk to make her spawn, and it barely registers as a welfare issue — and this is all happening while some jurisdictions have started writing decapod crustaceans into their animal sentience law.

So what view of welfare does this package presuppose? Three hypotheses, which I'd like you to kill or refine rather than just adopt. (1) It presupposes that fish and shrimp only nocicept and don't feel pain — except that the same bodies fund fish pain research and write sentience language into law, so maybe the empirical question is doing all the work here, or maybe it's a red herring. (2) It presupposes something like a brief-life discount: distress matters less in an animal with a short life and a short memory, so months of crowding count for less than seconds of panic at the kill — which is backwards from how we treat mammals, where long-term confinement is the scandal. (3) It isn't a view of welfare at all but a hidden exchange rate: a salmon is a few kilos and a pig carcass is tens, so per kilo of product a real stunning requirement is maybe an order of magnitude more expensive, and everyone has implicitly settled on a cost-per-unit-of-suffering threshold nobody will state out loud.

Please also deal with the fact that every welfare metric we collect is population-level (mortality, FCR, oxygen), so the practice can't even represent what an individual fish experiences — does that mean it presupposes that welfare is a property of a stock rather than of an animal? And is there a real inconsistency in treating a sick fish and then drowning a healthy one, or does one consistent theory cover both?

Assume for the sake of the answer that salmon and shrimp are sentient — but tell me if that assumption is load-bearing for your reconstruction. Give me sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

- The pattern, restated (~120 words) — including anything you think I've described wrong
- The implicit theory (~300 words)
- What it strictly needs vs. what it merely fits (~250 words)
- Where it's underdetermined (~200 words) — which rival theories would license the same practice
- What would actually have to change (~250 words)
- Bottom line (~80 words)
