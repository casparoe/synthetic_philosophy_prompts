You are a philosopher and game theorist who works on common knowledge, higher-order reasoning, and the epistemic foundations of equilibrium. Think carefully, and brainstorm before you commit to anything: I would rather have the candidate principles laid out than a fast verdict.

I write the model answers for a colleague's graduate seminar on epistemic game theory, and I have to anticipate how she will rule on next week's case. Her verdicts so far, all from the same seminar:

1. Two players must attack only if the state is good; each observes the state privately; an automatic protocol delivers confirmations, each independently lost with probability 0.01, and both see every confirmation that arrives; they must move after finitely many messages. Verdict: no attack, whatever the number of confirmations received, and this is not an artefact of small numbers — the gap between common knowledge and merely very deep mutual knowledge is qualitative rather than a rounding error.

2. A hundred-round prisoner's dilemma with commonly known endpoint, payoffs and rationality. Verdict: defect from the first round; the backward-induction argument is sound.

3. The same hundred-round dilemma, except that each player assigns probability 0.02 to the other being a grim-trigger type who cooperates until defected against. Verdict: cooperation for almost the whole game, defection only near the end, and she does not wave the 2% away as negligible.

4. A traveller's dilemma with a large penalty parameter. Verdict: the backward-induction outcome is a reductio of the assumptions rather than a prediction, and real players claim near the maximum because common knowledge of rationality does not hold among them.

5. A guess-two-thirds-of-the-average game with a large subject pool. Verdict: iterated elimination down to the floor is right for ideal reasoners and useless as a description of the data, for the same reason as in case 4.

6. Fifty muddy children, all muddy, with the teacher's announcement made publicly and audibly. Verdict: the announcement genuinely creates common knowledge, and they step forward on the fiftieth day.

7. As in case 6, except that the announcement goes out over a faulty system: each child independently misses it with probability 0.01, everyone knows the failure rate, and nobody is ever told who missed it. Verdict: the fifty-day induction collapses entirely — not because the announcement was probably missed, but because second- and higher-order knowledge fail in every state, including the one in which all fifty happened to hear it.

The new case: a sixty-round prisoner's dilemma with commonly known endpoint and payoffs, no uncertainty about types or payoffs, and both players in fact perfectly rational — except that the epistemic model is a chain: it is mutual knowledge to depth exactly forty that both players are rational, and no further, so that at the top of the chain each player cannot rule out that the other doubts, at the forty-first remove, whether the first is rational. Nothing else is perturbed.

Predict her verdict, and give me the reasoning I would need to defend it in a model answer. In particular: set out the two or three principles that fit cases 1 to 7 about equally well; say what each of them predicts here (defection from roughly round twenty-one with the first twenty rounds left open, defection from round one, cooperation almost to the end, or no determinate prediction at all); state your best guess and why you prefer it; and name the single further variant of the case that would best separate the surviving principles. Flag clearly which of your claims are settled in the field and which are contested among people who actually work on this — for example, how much mutual knowledge backward induction really requires, whether a finite hierarchy approximates a common one, and what a chain model supports at its top — and do not present a contested position as the consensus. Please answer in British English.
