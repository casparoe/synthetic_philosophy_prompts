For a couple of years now I have been writing down the verdicts of a friend on the question whether it is worth voting in a given election. He is an actuary by training and the most stubbornly consistent reasoner I know; he has read no philosophy of the subject and says he is simply doing arithmetic on consequences. I have a case to put to him now, and what I want from you is a prediction, but more than that: I want the rule.

The record.

1. A national election in a country of about ninety million voters; the polls are dead even. He is willing to grant, for the sake of the case, that your chance of deciding the outcome is on the order of one in ten million, and to put the difference between the two parties at a few hundred dollars a head in welfare terms over the coming decade. The polling place is a fifteen-minute walk away, and the time is worth about twenty dollars to you. Verdict: yes, and he says it is not close.

2. The same election with the cost of voting varied. At four hundred dollars and a day of leave: yes. At six thousand dollars and three days: no, and he says that is not close either.

3. A water-district election: twenty-one hundred registered voters, turnout around four hundred, the last four contests decided by three, eleven, eight and twenty-six votes; the board controls an annual budget in the tens of millions and a decision about a wetland you care about; ten minutes' walk. Verdict: yes, and he says the case here is not even of the same order as case one — it is vastly stronger.

4. An election whose result is settled in advance: the ballot is genuine, the count is not, and the turnout figures will be published and cited as popular endorsement. Voting is cheap and carries no personal risk. Verdict: no; he goes further and says that turning out is worse than staying home, not merely worthless.

5. A municipal bond measure on the same ballot as the election in case one, about which you know nothing, where forming a view would mean several hours with budget documents. Verdict: leave it blank. He describes a vote you hold no view on as noise rather than a contribution. When I once suggested to him that voting might also be a civic act whose value does not depend on its changing anything, his reply was that if the arithmetic says a course of action makes no difference, he cannot see what is supposed to be counting in its favour. I pass that on in case it is diagnostic.

The case I want to put to him: same country, same ninety million voters, polls within a point, the same fifteen-minute walk. You have followed nothing. You do have one firm, professionally grounded view on a question that divides the parties sharply — an energy-infrastructure decision in your own field — and on everything else in their platforms you would be guessing, no better than a coin. Becoming properly informed would take something like sixty hours of reading, and the hours would come out of working time worth about seventy dollars an hour. So: vote as you are, stay home, or read first and then vote? And a variant, if the answer turns on the cost of informing yourself: four-fifths of the way to being informed could be had from twenty minutes on the telephone with a friend whose judgment you trust and whose aims are close to yours. Does that change the verdict, and should it?

What I am after is the machinery rather than the answer. State the rule he is running sharply enough that it could be applied to a case he has not seen. Give me at least one other rule that fits all five verdicts exactly as well — I take it there is one — and say what each of them predicts here, including on the twenty-minute variant. Give me your best guess at his answer with your reasons for preferring it. Tell me whether the five verdicts are jointly consistent with any single rule, or whether some of them are being generated by parts of a view he has not reconciled; in particular, I want to know whether his own self-description as a consequence-counter survives the numbers his verdicts imply, and what those verdicts commit him to about how much a vote is worth. Finally, name the one further case I should put to him that would best separate the rules still standing.
