For the past year I've sat on my faculty's conduct committee, and twice in the last month I've been on opposite sides of the same maneuver: I told a colleague that a decision of his had been shabby, and he shot back that I had made the same kind of decision three years earlier; a week later I caught myself thinking that a senior colleague who has spent a decade arguing that academics must stop flying was in no position to tell the rest of us to take the train. Both times I felt the you-do-it-too was beside the point and that the standing point was not, and I'd like to know whether that combination is defensible before I hand in a one-page statement of my views to the reading group I'm in on Sunday. Here is roughly where I've landed. (1) Whether blame is apt depends only on the target: the act, her reasons, the quality of will it displayed, whether she had an excuse. Nothing about the blamer can make a correct blame-claim incorrect, so when a hypocrite blames, what she says is true and her blame is in that sense right. (2) Even so, some would-be blamers lack standing to blame — hypocrites paradigmatically — and when they do, the target has a genuine complaint and may properly decline to engage with the blame. (3) The tu quoque is never a defense of the act: that you do it too shows nothing about whether what I did was wrong. (4) Standing is neither comparative nor relational: I don't need to be better than you, or as good as you, and I don't need to stand in any relationship to you — public criticism of wrongdoers I will never meet, including people who died before I was born, is in order. (5) What costs you standing is a failure to be committed to the very norm you invoke, a readiness to exempt yourself, not merely a failure to comply with it. (6) Verdict on a case I keep coming back to: the campaigner who flies long-haul several times a year while telling the rest of us that flying is now indefensible has no standing to blame me for my flights — even in the version of the case where she genuinely believes it, votes and donates accordingly, and is genuinely wretched about her own travel. (7) A government that itself detains people without trial can still properly prosecute and publicly denounce private violence, and its prosecutors do not lack standing to do so. (8) Blame is a form of address: to blame someone is to hold her to a norm in a way that aims at her recognizing that she is being held to it, not merely to form an attitude about her.

What I want is an audit of this package rather than a survey of the literature. Go through the commitments and tell me which pairs are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and how severe each real tension is — ranked, not listed as equal. For each one, say precisely what would have to be given up and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the set, then tell me which single revision would clean up the most at the least cost. Two things I specifically want answered along the way. First, if the hypocrite's blame is true and apt in the sense of (1), what exactly is left for the target to complain about in (2), given that by (3) he cannot claim she is wrong about the act? Second, does the account of forfeiture in (5) actually deliver the verdict in (6)? It looks to me like it might not: the unhappy campaigner seems to be precisely someone who is committed to the norm and fails to comply with it, which is the case (5) was supposed to spare. If that is the crack, say so plainly, and tell me whether the cheapest repair is to tighten (5), to weaken (6), or to reconsider whether compliance and commitment can be pulled apart as cleanly as I have been assuming. One member of the group insists that hypocrites never lose standing at all, on the ground that moral address is something we owe one another as equals; I don't find that convincing, but if the argument for it is stronger than I think, tell me that too.
