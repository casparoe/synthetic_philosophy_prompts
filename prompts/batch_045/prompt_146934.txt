I'm on the clinical ethics committee at my hospital and I've been asked to write the memo for Thursday's meeting, which is a bit awkward because it isn't hypothetical for me: my brother is four surgeries into the same protocol, so tell me at the end if I should even be the one drafting it.

The case: we run a gradual cortical replacement program for a degenerative disease. Small regions of tissue are swapped out one at a time over years, each implant tuned to reproduce the spike-train behavior of the patch it replaces. Two things our neuro people are upfront about: the implants don't do the slow chemical side of things (no dopamine or acetylcholine wash), so they substitute a fixed infusion schedule; and nobody can predict how many steps a given patient gets before replaced and unreplaced tissue stops resynchronising.

Our patient is roughly 60% through. Before starting he signed a directive saying we should disregard any later attempt to stop and finish the job, his reasoning being that a half-replaced version of him isn't yet the person whose change of heart should count. He's now refusing the next surgery. He's competent on every test we have, he's changed partners and quit his job. His adult daughter wants the old directive enforced; the surgeons point out the funding only covers completion and the PI says nobody has data on what stopping at 60% looks like.

What I need is the memo we'd actually vote on: a recommendation — honor the current refusal, enforce the earlier directive, or something staged — with a timeline, who has to be satisfied of what before the next step, and reasons the losing side can recognise as reasons rather than as a brush-off. Two things I want engaged seriously instead of narrated past. First, our neurologist's line that if replacing the whole thing in one operation would be a death, then doing it in two hundred steps can't be two hundred non-deaths, so either gradualness is doing no work or you have to say what work it's doing. Second, whether "he consented to a process, and mid-process demurral is exactly the event he asked us to discount" is a real argument or a loophole he talked himself into.

Keep separate what's settled (our policy, the capacity testing, the law on advance directives) from what people who actually work on this disagree about, and where it's contested just say it's contested rather than picking whichever side sounds judicious. And no citations or reading suggestions — none of us will look them up.
