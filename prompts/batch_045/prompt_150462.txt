I teach a graduate seminar on prediction, confirmation, and falsification in the social sciences, and we are about to spend two sessions on a maneuver I have come to think of as the last refuge of a long-run predictive theory: the claim that a prediction which has so far failed to come true is not false but premature. We will be reading Malthus's Essay on the Principle of Population alongside the standing defense offered on his behalf — that the mechanism is real (population tends to press against subsistence), that the forecast was sound, and that its failure to arrive on schedule is explained by countervailing causes which are themselves conceded to be temporary, so that the theory's day is deferred rather than denied. Those countervailing causes are the interesting part: yield gains from the Green Revolution and from industrial nitrogen fixation, a one-off drawdown of fossil carbon and fossil groundwater, and a demographic transition that lowers fertility only after a period of falling mortality. If the respite was purchased by spending down a stock, then the respite is arguably something the theory predicts, and the defense starts to look disturbingly comfortable. A colleague in economics who says, cheerfully and often, that Malthus was right but two centuries early has agreed to sit in on that session, so whatever I hand out has to be satire he can laugh at rather than a straw man he can dispose of in a sentence.

So: you are a philosopher of science who knows this territory — ceteris paribus clauses, auxiliary hypotheses, the Duhem point that a prediction is never discharged by a single hypothesis alone, Popper's ban on immunizing strategies, Lakatos's distinction between a progressive and a degenerating research programme — and who also writes institutional satire. Before you draft anything, spend a hundred words or so listing the five to seven rescue moves that actually do the work here, the ones a defender really makes: separating the mechanism claim from the trend claim, treating the date as a free parameter nobody was ever committed to, converting a temporary countervailing cause into a confirmation of the mechanism, calling the whole thing a tendency rather than a forecast, noting that rival theories have their own anomalies, and so on. Then write the piece itself: an internal memorandum — agenda items, numbered findings, resolutions, a budget line or two — from a fictional body I'll call the Standing Committee on Provisional Falsification, charged with ruling on whether long-run theories whose central predictions have not materialized should be retained in good standing, suspended, or finally retired. Roughly 600–700 words, deadpan institutional register, the voice of a committee secretary who finds all of this entirely reasonable. Give the Committee names for its own doctrines — something like an Exhaustion Clause for the move where a countervailing cause is itself running out and therefore counts as evidence for the theory, a Borrowed Surplus finding, a rule that no auxiliary hypothesis may be withdrawn without a division of the house.

Two constraints matter to me. First, the humor has to come from the actual machinery of the defense rather than from invented absurdity: every ruling the Committee makes should be a recognizable thing that real defenders of real long-run predictions say, pushed one notch further. Second, the piece has to end in a genuine reductio — the Committee's rules, applied with complete consistency, should end up protecting something my seminar would refuse to protect, or proposing something it would recoil from: retrospective vindication certificates issued in advance, a futures instrument that lets a theory's advocates draw down credit now against a catastrophe later, a standing rule that the testimony of future generations is discounted because they will have been warned, or provisional non-falsified status granted to a theory everyone in the room has already abandoned. Do not let the Committee break character to explain the philosophy, and do not wink: a bright graduate student who has done the reading should be able to say what is being ironized, and a defender of the premature-truth line should be able to read it without feeling misrepresented. Work only from the materials named in this prompt — no citations, no footnotes to the literature, no invented bibliography, and no named living people; invent an afflicted proponent if you need one.
