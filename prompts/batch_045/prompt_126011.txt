ok so — you are a historian trained in the mid-century Annales tradition, you know the long-run structural view of history inside out, and you have also, on the side, written comedy for a magazine that nobody admits to reading. Be both at once here. Take your time with it: before you draft anything, do a short brainstorm at the top of your answer (three or four bullets is plenty) where you (a) say what the structural view of history actually claims, so you don't end up satirising a strawman version of it, and (b) pick the one real feature of it you're going to push exactly one notch too far. Then write the piece.

What I want: an internal document from the Directorate of Temporal Compliance, the agency of a fictional republic that has made long-run structural history the legal basis of public administration. Form: a short covering note on top, then a numbered memorandum with section headings, plus one footnote. About 800 words all in. Voice: serene, bureaucratic, entirely sincere — the tone of an official who is not upset by the news, because the news is not, strictly speaking, part of history yet.

The machinery it has to contain. I'm inventing all of this, so just take it as given:

- The Directorate's three-band calendar: the near-immobile band, the generational band, and the event band, which is offically classified as noise. Give each band a minimum duration, below which a change may not be recorded as a change.
- A Granularity Audit: the procedure by which a field report saying something like "the minister decided to" gets rewritten into compliant language. Show us one before and one after.
- The Reserve of Equivalent Individuals: when a record really must name a person, the name is replaced by a placeholder drawn from a roster of people who could in principle have filled that slot. Include the clause that handles the cases where the Reserve ran short — where only one person in a district had the relevant skill, or where the two available candidates wanted incompatible things — and do not let that clause quite work.
- The Attribution Rounding Rule: causal credit is assigned to the structure, and any individual contribution below a stated threshold is entered as a rounding error.
- The latency problem: the Directorate's own doctrine holds that a structural shift only becomes legible decades after it starts, which means the Directorate cannot currently certify anything about its own present. Write the clause that manages this, in the register of an institution doing damage control it would never admit to doing.
- One worked example: apply the whole framework to a concrete episode of your own choosing. Pick something where a decent structural story is genuinely available and where at least one stubborn detail — a timing, a name, a decision taken in an afternoon — refuses to dissolve into it.

The bit I actually care about: the memo is itself an event, written by a named person, on a date, announcing a policy. Somewhere, without ever winking or breaking tone, the Directorate has to process its own founding as noise. Let that go unresolved — no reassuring footnote that gets it off the hook. Don't name any real living person; the founding bureaucrat is fictional, and so is everyone else quoted.

Two last things. Don't put any real historical text inside quotation marks — paraphrase if you need to gesture at something someone actually wrote. And it has to stay legible to a reader who has never heard of Braudel: they should be able to tell what's being sent up without you signalling it. Whatever you do, don't let it turn into a defence of Great Men. The joke is from inside the building.
