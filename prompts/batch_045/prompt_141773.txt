I've been going round on the same thing with two people in my lab for about a year — split-brain patients, and whether there's one subject of experience in there or two — and we have completely stopped moving. Every meeting we recite the same facts, someone says "well, it depends what you mean by one", and we all get annoyed and go home. I want an actual diagnosis of why it's stalled, not another run through the evidence.

The shape of it: everyone agrees on the data. Callosotomy for severe epilepsy; the left hemisphere talks, the right one mostly can't but can point, draw, sort objects and follow simple instructions; the intermanual conflict stuff where one hand buttons a shirt and the other undoes it; people born without a corpus callosum who seem pretty ordinary; adults who've had a hemisphere removed and who seem to be one fairly normal person afterwards; and the more recent re-testing that's supposed to show cross-half-field reporting where the classic results found none. Each side reads all of that as supporting them. The positions in play in our group: two streams of consciousness at once; one stream that alternates between hemispheres; one subject whose consciousness is simply disunified; and the view that the whole question has no determinate answer and we should stop asking it.

My hypothesis, which I can't defend properly: "one subject of experience" is doing three different jobs and the cases pull them apart. Sometimes it means a unit of co-consciousness — everything you're experiecing at a given moment is experienced together. Sometimes a unit of access — one system that can recruit anything into thought and action. Sometimes a unit of persistence — one thing with a first-person point of view over time. If that's right the debate is three debates under one heading. But maybe the real work is being done by a hidden empirical question, whether there are two simultaneous global-broadcast-ish systems or one alternating one, and we just don't have the tools to tell.

Two cases I made up, which seemed to break the tie differently for different people:

1. The delay bridge. Suppose a surgeon can reconnect the hemispheres with an artificial channel whose delay you can dial: 0ms, 200ms, 5 seconds, 5 minutes, never. Does one person become two, and where? If there's no clean threshold, is that because the number of subjects is a matter of degree, or because our concept just runs out? And does the person who says "vague, but there's still a fact" actually disagree with the person who says "no fact at all"?

2. The lockstep case. Two hemispheres — or, if that's cheating, two seprate brains — kept permanently in identical-input lockstep, so their contents never diverge. One subject or two? This split people by whether they count subjects by phenomenal unity or by the hardware doing it, which might be the actual crux.

Be committed: give me one story about what kind of disagreement this is — verbal, substantive but about standards of evidence, hidden empirical, different questions under one name, or ill-posed — with reasons, and tell me what would actually count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction to adopt, an experiment, a formal result, an argument that the question should be dropped, or an argument that we should just stipulate and move on. Also, does any of it matter outside the seminar room — would anything change about what we tell patients before surgery, or about responsibility when a hand does something the person disowns? And say how confident you are in each part and what would move you off it.
