I sat in on an ethics reading group where two members argued over a case one of them had invented. They asked me to referee and I genuinely could not tell who was winning.

The case: a runaway trolley will kill five people on the main track. The only way to stop it is to throw a switch sending it onto a spur where a workman is standing; the spur's buffer is broken, so the trolley stops only by hitting him and killing him. But the workman is already doomed: a cracked gantry above him will drop a steel beam and crush him in about ten seconds whatever anyone does, and there is no way to reach him or shout in time. So he dies either way, and the five live only if the switch is thrown.

Dana: Throwing the switch is permissible, and the case shows that the third condition of the doctrine of double effect — the bad effect must not be a means to the good — is doing no real work. What matters morally here is that the workman is no worse off than he would have been anyway, the five are saved, so proportionality is satisfied trivially. The means-versus-side-effect distinction, she said, is a rough proxy for something like non-worsening, and it misclassifies cases where the victim is already doomed.

Owen: The doctrine asks what you are doing, not how the victim's day was going to go. You are aiming at a man's body in order to stop a vehicle; his death is a step in your plan. If "he is no worse off" licensed that, it would license a surgeon's cutting up a patient who will die within the hour in order to save five others — a case everyone in the room agreed is impermissible, and there too the victim is no worse off than he would have been. So non-worseness cannot be what is doing the work.

Dana: The surgery case is disanalogous in a statable way. The surgeon brings a new lethal process to bear on someone who was not part of the emergency; the workman is already inside the situation that threatens people, and throwing the switch adds nothing to his peril — it redirects an existing threat onto someone already under it. Double effect has always distinguished diverting a threat from using a person, and on her case the act is best described as diverting.

Owen: Then she has smuggled in a fifth condition — do not worsen anyone's baseline — which is not among the four, and once it is in, the doctrine permits what it exists to forbid. Change the standard footbridge case so that the large man is standing on a trapdoor that will open in ten seconds and kill him anyway. On her principle you may push him, and the only reason you may is that the death count is unchanged. Her view, not his, has collapsed into counting bodies.

Dana: She accepted that her view permits the trapdoor variant, but noted that it still forbids the ordinary footbridge case, so it has not collapsed into consequentialism. And she turned it around: on his reading the workman's losing nothing is morally irrelevant, so the doctrine ends up forbidding an act that makes no one worse off and saves five lives — a doctrine, she said, that has forgotten what it was for.

Owen: Calling it "makes no one worse off" just builds her conclusion into the definition of harm. A view about what agents may do is entitled to forbid acts with harmless-looking outcomes; that is the claim, not a defect in it.

Then say who is more right overall, and what both parties are missing — in particular whether there is a distinction the argument needs that neither of them has drawn. Finally, say what would change your own mind about the central question (whether the switch may be thrown), and where you think your reasoning here is most likely to break down.
