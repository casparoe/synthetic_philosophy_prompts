I'm closing the final session of a graduate seminar on set theory and philosophy of mathematics, and I would like to end with a villanelle on Skolem's paradox rather than with a summary of the term.

The form should do the work of the idea. Two refrains, typographically distinguished — italic and roman — and given the same metrical shape and roughly the same length, so that a listener hears them as two rival candidates for the same slot in the line. One refrain is the verdict from inside a countable model of the set theory: the set the model takes to be uncountable is, by the model's own lights, without any pairing with the numbers, and no such function is to be found. The other is the enumeration from outside: someone with the whole model in view numbering off those very same objects, first, second, third. They alternate as the closing lines of the five tercets, and in the final quatrain they must appear in the same breath without the poem deciding between them. A reader who knows the Löwenheim–Skolem theorems should be able to tell which refrain is which before the first tercet is out, and should be able to say why the missing object has to be a function rather than an element.

Particulars that matter to me, since several people in the room know the material well:

- I have been using a figure all term, and you are welcome to it: a walled town whose own registry office certifies that the inhabitants cannot be listed, whilst a surveyor outside the walls, working from the county roll, is listing them. No inhabitant is absent; what is absent is the list. Do not let the poem suggest either that the registry is simply mistaken in a way it could discover by looking harder, or that the predicate the town reads as uncountable never meant anything in the first place.
- The turn into the quatrain is the move to the metatheory: the standpoint from which both refrains are true and the contradiction is located precisely, as a function available on one side of the wall and not the other. Do not let this turn sound like a solution someone could simply adopt.
- The closing couplet should refuse a comfortable moral. It should leave open whether the relativity of countability is a discovery about the sets themselves or a discovery about what we can say, and it should register the residual worry that our own claim about the continuum is hostage to the same relativity.

Give the poem a title, and beneath it on the handout add a note of at most a hundred words, addressed to a visiting philosopher who knows no set theory, saying which refrain is which and identifying exactly what is missing from where.
