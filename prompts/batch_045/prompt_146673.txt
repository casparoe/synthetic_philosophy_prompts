I'm a high school student doing an independent project on the Ship of Theseus, and I've landed on a position I now want stress-tested. My view is that questions like "is this still the same ship?" have no fact of the matter: there are only momentary states of the world plus causal relations between them, and "same ship" is a description we adopt because it is useful for tracking, ownership, and storytelling — not because it marks a real boundary in nature. On this view the puzzle is dissolved rather than answered: the repaired ship and the ship reassembled from the original planks are both eligible candidates, and nothing settles which one wins.

The case I find most supportive is one I made up rather than found in a book. Suppose that as each old plank is taken out of Theseus's ship, it is fitted that same day, in the same position, into a second hull being assembled right alongside it. At the end there are two complete ships, and neither ever spent a moment as a scattered heap of timber or a half-finished frame: both have equally continuous histories, and they differ only in which one actually did the sailing. My intuition is that this version is even harder to decide than the usual reassembly version, and that removing the "gap" exposes the question as a choice of description — which I take to support my view.

What would count against it? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: the observations, cases, or consequences that should move me, including ones that arguably already tell against the view. Please separate what a defender of my position would themselves concede as counting against it from what only its critics say, and tell me what my view predicts that its rivals don't. I'd also like an honest verdict on whether the position has been qualified into something unfalsifiable, and whether, for a view of this kind, that is a legitimate feature or a sign that it has stopped making a claim. For what it's worth, I don't find bare intuition-mongering persuasive in either direction; I'd be moved most by cases where some practice of ours — ownership, blame, promising, memory — seems to require a determinate answer, or by an argument that my view quietly collapses into a rival it was meant to beat.
