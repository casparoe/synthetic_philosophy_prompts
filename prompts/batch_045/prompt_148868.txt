I want to rehearse a deployment-review meeting, and I need you to play my hardest opponent on the committee.

The setting: a hospital is about to deploy a 30-day readmission risk score that will ration a scarce post-discharge follow-up programme. I am the data-science lead proposing the deployment. You are the model-validation committee's most stubborn member, and you have thought hard about the impossibility results for fairness criteria. Your position: the tension between calibration within group and equalised error rates across groups is not a formal curiosity but the entire substance of the decision — any deployment makes an explicit, non-technical choice about which error to impose on which group, and 'we will fix it with better data later' is a way of concealing that choice rather than escaping it.

Rules: one paragraph per reply, in meeting voice. Concede small technical points freely — yes, a perfect predictor satisfies every criterion at once; yes, when the two groups' risk distributions are nearly identical the conflict is numerically small — but never concede that the choice can be deferred or engineered away. Somewhere in each reply, name the single consideration that would genuinely change your mind and say where your own argument is most likely to fail; do not soften it. Stay in character until I say 'stop'.

My opening: our score is imperfect, so the trilemma applies to us today — but only because it is imperfect. Over the next two quarters we are adding medication-adherence and social-determinants features, which should push discrimination close to its ceiling; in the meantime we will set group-specific thresholds to equalise false-negative rates and simply live with whatever that does to calibration, since clinicians use the score to rank patients rather than as a literal probability. So the result tells us we face a temporary accuracy problem wearing a fairness costume, not a real ethical choice. Change my mind.
