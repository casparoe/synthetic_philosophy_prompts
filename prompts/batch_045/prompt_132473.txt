I'm a 4th-year PhD student in early modern intellectual history and I'm TAing our upper-division historiography seminar ("How Do We Know What Happened?") for the first time, and I get to write one of the three questions on the take-home final. Constraints: 48-hour take-home, closed-book, no library, no reading allowed, 1,500 words max, worth 20 marks. About fifteen students, mostly third-year history majors. They can all write "periodization is a social construct" in their sleep, and roughly half of them have picked up the opposite reflex (no, something really did happen around 1600, dont be silly), so I need something that can't be answered with either slogan.

Topic is periodization — specifically whether "the Middle Ages", "the Renaissance" and "the Scientific Revolution" are the same *kind* of thing. The angle I keep using in my own chapters, which I want to smuggle in without turning the exam into a lecture about my dissertation, is a three-way distinction: periods that are *self-named* (people living through them thought something new was happening and said so); periods that are *retrospectively discovered* (nobody at the time had the category, but the pattern is really there in the record); and periods that are *residual* (a leftover category, defined mostly by what came before and after it, or by what it supposedly lacks). My hunch is that our three names are roughly one of each, and that the good student move is noticing that "real or later invention?" has a different answer in each case.

What I want from you:

1. The question itself, as it would appear on the handout. I'm imagining three parts. (a) Get them to state the three-way distinction in their own words, apply it to all three names, and say what would have to be true for a given name to move into a different one of the three boxes. (b) The discovery-vs-decision bit: two people can agree on every dated fact in the record and still disagree about whether the period is real — so what exactly are they disagreeing about, and is it the kind of disagreement more evidence could settle? If yes, what evidence; if no, what kind of disagreement is it? (c) A criterion they have to *evaluate* rather than apply. I've been calling it the ratchet test: a stretch of time earns the name "revolution" only if the changes it names were self-reinforcing, so that each year's gains made reversal less likely and the boundary marks a genuine point of no return. Make them run it on all three periods and say whether it delivers the verdicts we'd actually want, and whether it's too demanding or not demanding enough. Then a short closing beat: which of the three names would they keep if they were redesigning the survey syllabus, and are they keeping it because it tracks something real, because it's pedagogically useful, or just because it's too entrenched to remove.

2. A model answer, around 800 words, that opens with its verdict in the first sentence. No padding, no name-and-date flexing, and definitely no reading list — I want the argumentative moves, and it has to be writable by someone with no library access.

3. A marking scheme: five must-hit points, 4 marks each, 20 total, each with a one-line note on how students typically get it wrong. The A/B line I have in mind is that a B-answer gives one uniform verdict for all three periods ("they're all constructions" or "they're all real"), while an A-answer says the three names are doing different kinds of work and can say what would change its mind case by case. If you think that's the wrong place to draw the line, say so and argue for a better one.

Last thing: at the end, add a short note on how confident you are that this question would actually separate the students the way I want — i.e. that it isn't secretly unanswerable or circular — and single out the one claim in the model answer, or the one marking decision, you're least sure about.

Format: start straight with the question text as it goes on the handout — no preamble, no restating what I've asked for — then the model answer, then the scheme.
