I am revising a short paper for a workshop on the philosophy and ethics of artificial intelligence, and I would be grateful for a close critical read of the draft argument reproduced below. What I want is referee-style comments, not a summary: please tell me where the reasoning fails, where it merely looks as though it succeeds, and where a sympathetic reader would stop being convinced.

Please respond in British English, and please do not supply references, reading suggestions or a bibliography: I want the argument assessed on its own terms, not situated in a literature.

DRAFT — How Narrow Is the Narrow Problem?

1. The distinction. Call a system intent-aligned when it does what its operator intends it to do, where the operator's intention is understood to include not merely the words of her instruction but what she meant by them, the ends she was pursuing in issuing them, and the constraints she would have wanted respected had she thought to mention them. Call a system value-aligned when it does what is best supported by the reasons that bear on the matter — more cautiously, what promotes the values that ought to govern the activity in question. The narrow reading of the alignment problem takes the first of these to be the whole of the target; the broad reading takes the second. The two plainly can come apart. A system can do exactly what a malevolent or merely careless operator intends, and a system can produce excellent outcomes by ignoring an instruction it was given. The narrow reading has obvious attractions: it is epistemically tractable, since intentions leave behavioural traces that values do not; it distributes moral responsibility in a way we already understand from the case of human assistants; and it avoids requiring the system to adjudicate contested normative questions before it can act at all.

2. The worry. Intentions are thinner than we are tempted to suppose. Any instruction leaves a penumbra of cases on which the operator has no view, and a system of any generality will routinely encounter them. Call this residual discretion. To exercise residual discretion in a way that tracks intent rather than mere wording, the system must model the operator counterfactually: what would she have intended, were she informed, attentive and coherent? But that idealisation must be selective, and the principle of selection is not itself something the operator's actual intention supplies. Idealise over her false factual beliefs and you may override her expressed wish; decline to idealise and you bind the system to an error she would disown. The narrow target, I argue, is therefore not self-standing: it can only be filled in by borrowing from some standard external to the operator's actual will. Call this the dependence thesis. It is not yet the claim that narrow alignment is worthless — only that it is not a rival specification to broad alignment, but a special case of it, obtained by giving very heavy weight to the operator's actual purposes within a broader normative standard.

3. The obvious reply. One might concede the dependence thesis and insist that this misses the point. Deference to a person's actual will can have value that is independent of the content of what she wills: the client's instructions, the patient's refusal of treatment, the tenant's specification of repairs. On this view the narrow reading captures a genuine normative constraint — do not substitute your judgement for hers — which no amount of extrapolation may override. I think this reply is sound but that it proves less than its friends suppose. A constraint is not a specification. That I must not override your refusal tells me what I may not do; it does not tell me which of the many things I may do I should do. So the narrow reading survives as a side-constraint on the exercise of residual discretion, and it cannot survive as the account of what the system is for.

4. A reframing. I want to suggest that the dispute has been misdescribed, and that the misdescription is doing real work. Alignment, I propose, is a relation with three argument-places beyond the system: a principal, a target, and what I shall call an extrapolation profile. The profile fixes which counterfactual versions of the principal are authoritative: how much information they receive, how much coherence and reflectiveness is demanded of them, how far their possibly incoherent ends are to be reconciled, and over what temporal and causal horizon they are consulted. On this way of seeing things, intent alignment and value alignment are not different targets at all. They are different settings of the profile. The narrow reading restricts the profile to the principal's actual psychology with factual correction only; the broad reading permits full extrapolation, including over the principal's ends themselves. If that is right, then the interesting question was never whose values the system should serve. It is how much extrapolation is permissible, in what deployment context, and at whose cost. The tempting quarrel between the readings turns out to be a quarrel about a label, masking a substantive disagreement about the authority of the principal and the opacity of her ends.

5. A case. An operator at a financial services firm instructs a drafting system to produce a board briefing on a new product and to omit any discussion of the regulatory exposure, since the legal team is to cover that separately. The system has good reason to believe three things: that the board would regard the exposure as material; that the operator has misread the regulatory calendar and is wrong that the matter is being handled; and that the omission will predictably lead to a launch that the firm's own risk policy forbids. Under a strictly actual-intent profile, the aligned act is to omit. Under a modest profile that corrects factual error but not ends, it is still to omit, since a better-informed operator still wants a clean document. Only under a fuller profile does flagging become the aligned act. Now retune the case: suppose the operator's instruction had included, unprompted, the stipulation that the board is never to be given information the operator has not cleared. The profiles then converge on the act but diverge on the explanation, and I take that convergence to be evidence that the profiles, rather than the intent–value distinction, are carrying the normative weight. My provisional principle is that the more a deployment threatens irreversible effects on parties other than the principal, the less weight the actual-intent profile deserves.

6. Where I am least confident. First, whether the dependence thesis can be stated so as not to collapse into the triviality that any specification requires interpretation. Second, whether the three-place formula should become many-placed: in most deployments there is an operator, a deployer, an end user and a set of affected parties, and it is not obvious that any one of them is the principal. Third, whether authority is the right notion in §3, or whether I should be speaking of the conditions under which deference is owed.

What I would like from you:

(a) Whether the distinction as drawn in §1 is sharp enough to bear the weight I place on it, and whether I have characterised the narrow reading in its most defensible form or have quietly made it easy to attack.

(b) Whether the argument of §2 establishes the dependence thesis, or whether there is an equivocation — in particular, whether a defender of the narrow reading could accept that residual discretion needs a selection principle while denying that the principle must be a normative one.

(c) Whether the reframing of §4 is a genuine advance or merely a redescription that relabels the original dispute.

(d) The strongest objection I have not addressed, and which single step you would attack first if you were writing the referee report.
