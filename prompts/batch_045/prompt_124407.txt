After a long argument with a friend last night about what Aumann's agreement theorem actually shows, I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Her position is that the theorem is close to a proof that persistent disagreement between well-informed peers cannot be fully rational, so if you find yourself in one you should either move or conclude that something has gone wrong with you or with your interlocutor. Mine is that it is a conditional whose antecedent — a common prior over a fixed state space, plus common knowledge of the two posteriors — real disagreeing agents almost never satisfy, so all it shows is that something must give, not which thing. Neither of us moved, and the pattern felt familiar: both readings have been around for a long time, everyone knows the arguments on the other side, and nobody converts.

The case we finally converged on, which I'll use as the anchor: two economists who have read everything each other has written, spent years in conversation, agree on the data and on what would count as evidence, and still publish estimates of the same quantity that differ by a factor of three. She says the theorem tells you this cannot be fully rational, so look for the fault. I say the theorem tells you their priors or their information partitions differ, which we already knew, and that the interesting question is what kind of difference it is and whether it is one anyone could be blamed for.

My hypothesis is that at least three disputes are being run under one heading, and that part of the impasse is that neither side notices the slide between them. First: whether the common prior is a requirement of rationality or a modeling convenience, where the standard move against giving it up is that you then have to treat prior disagreement as a brute fact about where each person started, something no further argument could touch. Second: how strong the conclusion actually is — whether common knowledge of the posterior of a single event forces agreement on that event alone or on the agents' entire probability functions — since if it is local, agreement is a much thinner achievement than either side's rhetoric implies, and agreeing to disagree might be reconcilable with the theorem by concurring about some events while differing about others. Third: whether common knowledge is the right idealization of an exchange at all, or whether the real content of results of this kind concerns how much communication agreement takes and what happens when communication is bounded or the information structure is not neatly partitional.

Is that decomposition right, and which of the three is load-bearing in keeping people apart? More generally, what kind of stalemate is this: a substantive normative disagreement about rationality; a verbal one about what disagreement and rationality are supposed to be; a difference about what counts as data, in the sense that a fully aired, well-documented disagreement between competent specialists is read by one side as evidence that the premises fail and by the other as evidence that someone is being irrational; or a hidden formal question that nobody has actually settled? Given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress and what would not — a distinction, a result about bounded or approximate common knowledge, a re-description of the case, or nothing — and be honest about whether you think this literature is stalled because the question is hard or because it is ill-posed.
