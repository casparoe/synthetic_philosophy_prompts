I write about science and ideas for a living; before that I spent eight years as a data journalist, so the vocabulary I reach for without thinking is git's — commits and commit graphs, branch tips, detached HEAD, merge conflicts, rebase versus merge, the reflog, force push. Use that jargon freely; I don't need it explained.

Please restate Dennett's account of the self as a center of narrative gravity in those terms, as faithfully as you can. The position, as I understand it: the self is not an extra inner object but an abstractum — the center around which a stream of stories about a body and a life gets organized — real in the way a center of gravity is real, doing genuine predictive and explanatory work without having mass of its own; and the stories are composed and revised on the fly, with no privileged draft, no fixed record of how they actually unfolded, and no audience in a Cartesian theater. I want that in my terms: what is the graph, what plays the role of HEAD, what does rebase versus merge capture, and what becomes of the theory once the history has been rewritten so thoroughly that the original commits are unreachable?

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually need: which claims translate cleanly onto a repository, which survive only by being distorted, and which cannot be expressed in this vocabulary at all. One piece I don't trust myself on: git presupposes a committer identity configured from outside and never derived from the commit graph, so any translation leaning on it seems to assume the very self it is meant to explain — I want to know whether that is a real circularity in the theory or an artifact of my framework. And the harder question: is there anything here corresponding to there being something it is like to be the person whose story this is? If not, is that a limit of version control as an ontology of minds, or a sign that the question this account answers was never the one its critics were asking?
