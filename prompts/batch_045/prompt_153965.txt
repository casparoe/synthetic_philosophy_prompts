Here is the situation. I was at a dinner after a conference and two of the people there — neither of them a close friend, and neither of them obviously more authoritative than the other — got into an argument about the standard statue-and-clay case that I couldn't referee. The case they were arguing about: a sculptor spends an afternoon shaping a single lump of clay into a statue; the museum then catalogues and displays the statue. Call the statue Goliath. They agreed from the start that the lump of clay existed before the afternoon in question and that the statue did not, and that if someone were to roll the whole thing into a ball, the result would still be a lump of clay but would no longer be a statue. The disagreement was about what follows from that.

(1) Nadia opened by saying the statue and the lump of clay are not identical. Her argument was Leibniz's Law in modal form: the lump of clay has the property of being such that it could survive being rolled into a ball, and the statue does not have that property; so they differ in a property, so they are not the same thing. She added a temporal version of the same point: the lump existed on the morning before the sculpting, the statue did not, and nothing can both have and lack the property of having existed at a given time. She said the correct description is that the lump of clay constitutes the statue without being identical to it, which is a perfectly ordinary relation — a ship's hull constitutes the ship, a body constitutes an animal — and that this is why only one of the two objects has the aesthetic and art-historical properties, and why the museum's scale does not register twice the mass.

(2) Tobias replied that the modal argument is question-begging, because the relevant modal statements are not sortal-neutral. On his reading, 'could survive being rolled into a ball' is elliptical for 'could survive being rolled into a ball while still counting as a lump of clay,' and 'existed on the Monday' is elliptical for 'existed on the Monday as a lump of clay.' Both are consistent with there being exactly one thing there: one and the same object satisfies the lump-relative versions of these claims and fails the statue-relative versions, because the sortal is built into the content of the claim. So the data underdetermine the metaphysics, and parsimony decides it: one object, described in two ways, no coincidence of numerically distinct things in one place, no mystery about why only one of them seems to have the aesthetic properties or why nothing gets double-counted.

(3) Nadia answered that this proves too much. If every modal and temporal claim is sortal-relative, then there is no sortal-neutral answer to any question of the form 'does it survive?', and for any change whatsoever one can find some sortal under which the thing survives, so persistence claims collapse into arbitrary reclassification. She pressed this with a case of her own: the museum's conservators, worried about cracking, replace the clay one grain at a time with a visually indistinguishable synthetic material, one grain a month for a decade, so that at no point is there an observable change but at the end nothing is clay. Here, she said, there is no sharp event to hang a sortal shift on, and the one-object theorist has to say both that the object survived a complete change of matter and that it was once made of clay and later not — so he owes us a principled distinction between a change of stuff and a change of object, and sortal-relativity gives him no way to draw one.

(4) Tobias said that distinction is available and not ad hoc. He proposed what he called the Historical Constraint: a sortal-relative survival claim is true only if there is appropriate causal continuity between the earlier and later stages, and not merely continuity but continuity together with satisfaction of the sortal's characteristic matter-condition. Rolling preserves the lump because squashing is the sort of causal process lumps survive and because the result is still clay; a qualitatively identical ball flown in from another studio does not preserve it, despite being indistinguishable. Applied to her case: the causal continuity is unbroken, so Goliath survives the decade of gradual replacement and is the same statue in 2010 as in 2000 — gradual conservation preserves a work, sudden smashing destroys it, which is why conservators behave as they do. But the lump does *not* survive, because a lump of clay whose every grain has been exchanged for polymer is no longer a lump of clay. So the gradual case, he said, actually yields a clean asymmetry rather than a collapse, and it is an asymmetry his one-object view predicts and hers merely stipulates.

(5) Nadia then said this concedes her point in different words: if persistence is fixed by causal history plus a sortal's matter-condition, then persistence facts are substantive rather than merely a matter of how we are counting, and she now has two things with two different persistence conditions at the same place at the same time, which is the constitution view. She also raised a new difficulty for the matter-condition idea: midway through the decade, when the object is roughly half clay and half polymer, it is not clear whether the matter-condition for being a lump of clay is still satisfied, so it is not clear whether there is a lump of clay there at all, and therefore not clear whether Goliath is constituted by one. The standard debate, she said, trades on sharp cases — intact versus squashed — and has no account of constitution coming in degrees.

(6) Tobias closed by saying the vagueness is neutral or favors him. Vagueness in the application of a sortal is ubiquitous — when exactly, during that afternoon, did the clay become a statue? — and no answer to that question is more than a decision. But indeterminate classification of a thing is not indeterminate existence of it: on his view there is a perfectly determinate object at the halfway stage, and the only vagueness concerns what to call it. Whereas on her view, if it is indeterminate whether any lump is present, it is indeterminate whether the constitution relation holds, and that is a genuine metaphysical indeterminacy rather than a merely semantic one. So, he said, the vagueness argument tells against coincidence rather than for it.
