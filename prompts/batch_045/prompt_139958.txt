So I went down a rabbit hole looking at who is actually allowed to vote where, and the rules dont seem to add up to any one idea. I'd like someone to tell me what theory of voting this whole mess presupposes, if it presupposes one at all.

Roughly, as far as I can tell: most places use 18, but Austria has let 16- and 17-year-olds vote in national elections since 2007, Brazil set it at 16 in its 1988 constitution, a few German states use 16 for municipal elections — and the UAE's minimum is 25. Voting is compulsory in Australia, Belgium and Brazil, though as I understand it Australia only requires you to show up and get your name crossed off, not to cast a valid ballot. New Zealand lets non-citizen permanent residents vote in national elections. EU citizens can vote in local elections in whatever member state they live in. The UK lets resident Commonwealth citizens vote. Most countries let citizens who have lived abroad for decades, pay nothing into the system and may never return keep the vote anyway. Prisoners: totally inconsistent.

The candidate principles I keep half-remembering are: everyone whose interests are affected should have a say; everyone subject to the law's coercion should have a say; membership or contribution earns you a say; and competence — you get a vote when you're capable of using one well. None of them obviously fits the package. The affected-interests one should enfranchise children and future generations and disenfranchise the long-gone emigrant. The coercion one should enfranchise tourists and short-term migrant workers. Membership can't explain why local elections let outsiders in. And if competence is the real test, why is the cutoff an age at all rather than a test, and why is it 16 here and 25 there?

So: reconstruct the implicit theory. Which of these does the existing patchwork strictly require, and which does it merely sit comfortably with? Are the parts mutually consistent, or is the mess just accidents and compromises stacked up? Does compulsory voting commit you to something extra — the vote as a duty or a public office you hold on everyone's behalf rather than a right you can waive — and does it conflict with the franchise-as-right picture the rest of the package seems to assume? Does the difference between compelling attendance and compelling an actual vote matter to that? And here's the bit I most want judged: if stake or affectedness is the principle, doesn't it commit you to giving parents extra proxy votes for their kids, or to representing future generations somehow — and if the practice's own logic commits you to that and everyone rejects it anyway, what does that tell you about the logic?

Please commit rather than survey: give me one reconstruction you actually think is right, name the single rule in the mess you think is hardest to defend and why, mark where you're confident versus guessing, and say what would change your mind.
