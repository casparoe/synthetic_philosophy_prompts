You're a professional philosopher who works on moral psychology and metaethics. Think it through properly, do some brainstorming before you commit to anything, and then make the argument for this specific thesis:

MFT can't tell you who is right politically, and not merely because heritability-and-culture explanations cut both ways. What it can do, if it works, is sort left-right disagreements into two kinds, and that's more useful than settling who wins. Where both sides register the same foundation but weight or interpret it differently — fairness-as-equality versus fairness-as-proportionality is the clean case — the dispute bottoms out in conceptual and empirical questions we could in principle settle, so argument and evidence are the right tools and someone can be shown to be wrong. Where one side simply doesn't register the foundation at all — the liberal who feels nothing when a flag is desecrated — there's no shared premise to reason from, so deliberation is the wrong instrument and what's left is bargaining, institutional design, modus vivendi stuff. The lesson of the psychology is therefore about which fights are worth having as arguments, not about who's correct.

Grant the MFT empirical picture for the sake of argument, so don't spend time litigating it. Do deal with two objections: first, why saying someone doesn't register a foundation isn't itself a moral judgement smuggled in as psychology; second, my mate's fallback that on his view the liberal is morally blind the way a colour-blind person is, and you don't negotiate with colour-blindness, you just correct for it.

Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning, no preamble and no restating the question, and write it in British English.
