I'm trying to work out what I actually think about causation by omission, starting from my verdicts on concrete cases rather than from a theory. I'd like a principle that fits the pattern — or, if the pattern doesn't hold together, to be told plainly which of my verdicts has to go.

Here are the cases:

(i) My neighbor promised to water my plant while I was away; she didn't; the plant died. Her not watering caused the death. This is the clearest case I have.

(ii) A man three thousand miles away, who has never heard of me or of the plant, also didn't water it. His not watering is not a cause at all — it isn't even a candidate.

(iii) No rain fell for six weeks and the corn crop failed. The lack of rain caused the failure, as straightforwardly as hail would have. There is no agent, no promise, and no norm anywhere in the story.

(iv) The ICU nurse didn't give the eight o'clock dose; the patient died; the dose would have saved him. Her omission caused the death. Now add that an intruder had locked her in a supply closet. I still want to say the missed dose caused the death, but the omission no longer seems to be hers: I want to point at the intruder, or at the hospital for staffing only one nurse.

(v) The drug would have saved the patient, but hospital policy forbids the physician from administering it, so she doesn't, and he dies. I hear this exactly like the unmodified nurse case: her not administering it caused the death, and the policy makes that omission justified rather than causally inert. Yet she violated no norm, disappointed no expectation, and did precisely what her role required.

(vi) The dean never denied the rumor; her silence was read as confirmation; donations collapsed. Her silence caused the collapse — but the route from omission to outcome here runs through what people believed, not through any physical process.

(vii) The case I go back and forth on: my neighbor and I each promised to water the plant, each assumed the other had done it, neither did, and it died. If either of us had acted, the plant would have lived, and yet each of us can point at the other. Sometimes I say we both caused its death, sometimes that neither of us did, and "both" feels like counting one death twice.

The principle I keep falling back on is roughly this: an omission causes an outcome only when it is a departure from a norm, an expectation, or an established practice — when there is a "she should have acted" somewhere in the story — and otherwise it is just an absence. That gives me the verdicts I want in (i), (ii) and (iv); it seems to give the wrong answer in (iii), where nothing normative is in play at all; and (v) is the case that worries me, because it looks causally identical to (i) while being normatively its mirror image. I have also started marking a distinction between omissions properly so-called, where someone fails to act, and bare absences, where there is simply no agent to fail — but I can't tell whether that distinction is doing real work or just restating the verdicts in (ii) and (iii).

What I want from you: a principle, or a short list of candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself to a new case, with the case that motivates each clause; an honest account of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why that one is the right one to drop; a verdict on (vii) with the reasoning that produces it; and at least one prediction about a kind of case I haven't listed, since that is how I'll know the principle is doing work rather than redescribing my intuitions. If the best account turns out to be disjunctive — one thing going on in (iii)-style absences, another in (i)-style omissions, another in (vi)-style cases that operate through interpretation — say so and say what the cost is. And let me name what I'd count as cheating: I don't want "cause" thinned into a relation on which every absence causes everything it is counterfactually connected to, and I don't want the line that omissions never really cause anything and are at most explanatory or at most grounds for blame.
