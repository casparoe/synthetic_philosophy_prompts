You are a philosopher of mind and epistemology who also works seriously in classical Chinese philosophy — the butterfly-dream passage in the Zhuangzi, and the Cartesian dream argument, should both be things you can reason from the inside. I am a retired physical chemist who has lately started sitting in on a philosophy reading group, and I want a thought experiment built to order, so please think carefully and show your work: begin by sketching two or three candidate designs, say in a line each why they fail or survive, then develop the one that survives, and only afterwards present the finished paragraph.

The point of the exercise is to prise apart two skeptical arguments that my reading group keeps running together. Call them the Cartesian dream argument (perceptual experience cannot discriminate waking from dreaming; add closure; no ordinary perceptual knowledge) and the Zhuangzian worry about the transformation of things (what looked like a stable self is a passing configuration; the boundary between Zhuangzi and butterfly is not a joint in nature).

Specifications for the case:

1. One paragraph, one protagonist, and no speculative technology: no vats, demons, implants, or perfect simulators. The only departure from ordinary life may be a single stipulated feature of the subject's dreaming or memory — one variable, held fixed, everything else as ordinary as a Tuesday morning.
2. Both arguments must apply honestly to the case. Each has to reach its verdict from its own premises, about the same proposition concerning the same subject, without borrowing from the other and without collapsing into self-refutation.
3. The verdicts should be inverted relative to what my group expects: the Zhuangzian line is the one forced to withhold knowledge, and the Cartesian dream skeptic the one forced to concede it. If you think the inversion has to run the other way to be intellectually honest, say so plainly and show why. Take the proposition at issue to be one about the subject herself — that she is the one who acted yesterday, that she is the one who will wake up, that she is Zhuangzi rather than the butterfly — not one about the external world. That is where I suspect the two arguments actually separate.
4. Do not stipulate the verdicts, and do not build the case on a premise that simply asserts that she has no self or that nothing is determinate: the skeptical conclusion has to fall out of something a Zhuangzian independently wants to say about transformation, applied to a concrete arrangement of bodies, memories, and times. I would rather you invent the quirk yourself than import one from the literature.

Then, in this order:

(a) State the proposition, and walk through — step by step, from premises — which view gives which verdict and why.
(b) Name the single feature of the case doing the work, and show what happens to each verdict when it is removed. I want, as we used to say in the lab, a control condition.
(c) Give the best reply available to a Zhuangzian who accepts the transformation picture but rejects your verdict: on what grounds would she say the case is rigged or misdescribed? Take the objection seriously and tell me whether it lands.
(d) Close with one paragraph on what the case does and does not establish: is the difference between the two arguments one of subject matter, one of presuppositions about the self, or something else again?
