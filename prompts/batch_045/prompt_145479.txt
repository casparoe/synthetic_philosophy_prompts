I'm a high school senior working on an independent project about Frederick Douglass's reading of the United States Constitution, and I have run into a scholarly disagreement that I cannot get anywhere near resolving.

The rough shape of it: for most of his early career Douglass repeated the Garrisonian case — that the Constitution was a compact with slavery, and that abolitionists ought not vote, hold office, or swear allegiance under it. After his break with Garrison in the early 1850s (my notes say 1851, but they are at school) he argued the opposite. In the Rochester Fourth of July oration of 1852 he challenges his listeners to find a single proslavery clause in the document taken at its plain reading; he makes the same case at length in his 1860 address in Glasgow; and he held that reading for the rest of his life.

Everyone I read falls into one of two camps, and as nearly as I can tell they have been falling into the same two camps for decades without anyone moving. One camp says he genuinely changed his mind, and says the change is hard to separate from his turn toward political antislavery — the Radical Abolitionist circle around Gerrit Smith, the case for working inside the system, later the Republican Party. The other camp says nothing important changed: that his constitutional claim was always conditional, that an indictment of what the nation actually does and an interpretation of what its founding text actually says were never in tension for him, and that what looks like a reversal is a change of audience, genre, and emphasis.

My own guess — and I would like you to argue with it rather than confirm it — is that the two sides are not disagreeing within one question but answering two different questions under one sentence. Call them the as-written question (what does the text, correctly construed, authorize?) and the as-applied question (what has the nation made the document mean by its practice, and what is the Union therefore, right now?). On that reading Douglass can say, in the same breath, that the fourth of July is a mockery for the enslaved and that the text contains no proslavery clause, because he is making two claims of different kinds; and the historiographical fight is partly a fight about which of the two he is taken to be answering.

So my questions: is this disagreement substantive, verbal, or a case of two well-posed questions being run together under one heading? Is there a hidden factual question doing the work — a biographical one about when and why he shifted, which the surviving evidence may simply underdetermine? Or is the whole did he change his mind? framing the problem, because it assumes a single belief where there may have been two registers? And given your diagnosis: suppose a cache of Douglass's private correspondence from around 1850 turned up in which he concedes that the liberty-document reading is politically useful but says he is not confident it is legally correct. Would that settle anything, or would both camps absorb it?

I should say that I cannot look anything up — I am working from photocopies and the library databases are down — so please do not give me a reading list, citations, or footnotes; I want you to reason from the speeches themselves and from the structure of the disagreement.

Please answer in no more than 600 words, in three short labeled sections: the diagnosis; what in Douglass's own words and situation supports it; and what would or would not count as progress.
