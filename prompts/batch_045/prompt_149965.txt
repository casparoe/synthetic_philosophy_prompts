I'm an epidemiologist — mostly infectious disease modelling, a lot of vaccine and antimicrobial-stewardship work — and I've been sitting in on a badly-organised reading group about individual contributions to collective harms. You know the family of questions: whether it can be wrong of me to eat meat, take a long-haul flight, or not bother voting, given that my one act makes no measurable difference to anything. I half get the shape of the debate, but I keep wanting to translate it into the vocabulary I actually use all day, and I can't tell whether that translation is faithful or whether I'm quietly dissolving the problem. So: restate the whole thing in my terms, and then tell me what doesn't survive the move.

Here's the vocabulary I want it in: potential outcomes and the contrast between unit-level and population-level interventions; SUTVA and interference between units, including herd-immunity-style spillover; but-for causation versus sufficient-cause / causal-pie models (the Rothman pies) and the fact that component causes in a pie don't sum to a hundred percent; population attributable fraction and what it does and doesn't license you to say about any individual case; and power and precision — "we couldn't detect an effect" versus "there is no effect." For each of the main moves I've seen — the inefficacy objection itself, the expected-difference-making reply (my act has some small probability of being the decisive one, so multiply it out), the indirect/signalling reply, and the fairness line where the complaint is really that I'm free-riding on other people's restraint — tell me what it turns into in that vocabulary, and flag which of them become genuinely trivial, which become methodological problems I'd recognise from my own work, and which become things I'd want to wave away as category errors. The bit I especially want stress-tested is my own half-baked model, which I've been boring my partner with at dinner: take a harmless-torturers-style threshhold case where the bad outcome fires once enough people participate — a processor that orders animals in batches of a thousand, a grid that fires up a peaker plant — and let the threshhold jitter rather than sit on a fixed line, so that my single act has some small probability of being the one that tips it. Now scale the problem up: more participants, bigger batches, a larger downstream harm. In my toy version the probability that I'm the decisive one shrinks at roughly the rate the size of the harm grows, so the expected damage traceable to my act stays roughly flat instead of going to zero — which, if it's right, means the inefficacy objection never gets stronger just because a problem is large-scale, even though large-scale problems are exactly where people report feeling most futile. Translate that, and tell me whether the flatness is an artifact of how I built in the jitter or whether there's something real in it.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about: what in this debate has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? My guess is that it's something in the neighbourhood of the gap between "no one is made worse off by my act" and "I stand in no complaint-relevant relation to the harm" — complicity, fairness, the things we say to each other — plus the fact that epidemiology is almost always answering a policy question (what should we recommend to a population, or to everyone) while the moral question is what one agent should do given that nearly everyone else won't comply. But push back on that guess: I want you to sort the residue into what is a genuine blind spot in my framework and what is the philosophers using "responsibility," "harm," and "making a difference" in ways that would never survive a methods review. Please stay in my vocabulary, and don't pad it with literature names or citations — I can't look anything up and I don't much care who said it first.
