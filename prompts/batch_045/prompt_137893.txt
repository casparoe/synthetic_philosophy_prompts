My brother-in-law started an argument at a family barbecue a few weeks ago and I still cant let it go. Ive got no philosophy background at all – I work nights at an assisted living home, which is probably why I keep chewing on it – and ive just been reading bits and pieces online. Near as I can tell the fight is between two pictures: one where a person is wholly present at every moment they exist, the same complete thing at 25 and at 85, and one where a person is more like a long four-dimensional snake stretched out through time, so the 25-year-old chunk and the 85-year-old chunk are different parts of one snake rather than one solid thing showing up twice. I think the fancy names are endurantism and perdurantism but I may have those backwards. My brother-in-law says the law has already picked a side and doesn't know it.

Here's the bit that got us going. Take a man who murders someone at 25, isnt caught till he's 80, and by then has advanced dementia – remembers nothing about the killing, doesn't recognize his own kids, can't say what prison is for. As I understand it he generally can't even be put on trial while he's like that, and if he'd already been sentenced to death he couldn't be executed either, something about the Eighth Amendment, because he can't understand whats happening to him. But at the same time the house is still his, the marriage is still his, the mortgage still comes out of his account, and the will he signed at 30 with a sound mind still decides who gets his stuff when he dies – nobody says that will belongs to a different person now, and a will is judged by whether he had his wits when he signed it, not by whether he'd still agree with it today. Now compare that with two other things. For almost every crime except murder there's a statute of limitations, so time alone wipes out the liability even when the guy is exactly the person he was and remembers everything perfectly. And a university or a religious order will apologize and pay out for something it did 150 years ago, when every person involved is long dead and the whole staff has turned over several times, and we find that perfectly normal, while we'd never fine somebody for what his grandfather did. So the going practice seems to be: same person across a total psychological break when it's property and marriage, not the same person when it's punishment, a slowly fading person for ordinary crimes but not murder, and a sort of super-persistent person when it's an institution.

My brother-in-law's read is that all this proves we're secretly committed to the one-solid-thing picture, and he's got a proposal he calls the fade rule: punishment, and maybe debts and payouts too, should be scaled down by how much psychological overlap is left between the chunk of the person who did the thing and the chunk standing in the dock, so our dementia patient gets something like five percent of a sentence and nobody thinks twice. I don't buy it, or at least I don't buy that it follows from his picture, but I can't say why properly. So: what view of persistence do these practices actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, then tell me which parts of it the practices strictly need and which they merely sit comfortably with, and whether the snake picture could license the same practices for its own reasons – administrability, deterrence, the whole snake being the thing that did it, whatever. Are the pieces even consistent with each other, or are we quietly doing one thing here and another thing there? Does the institutional case use the same notion of persistence as the personal case or a different one, and does it matter if it's different? What would concretely have to change if we adopted the fade rule, or went the other way: would a consistent snake picture force us to stop punishing the demented man altogether, and would a consistent solid-thing picture have to scrap statutes of limitations and stop institutions from paying for their former selves? And please be explicit about how confident you are in each of the main things you claim, and single out the one point you're least sure of – also flag it if I've got any of the legal stuff wrong, since I'm half remembering it from internet reading, and tell me which part of my description is doing the most work in the argument.
