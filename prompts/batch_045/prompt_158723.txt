I'm a PhD student in philosophy finishing up in experimental epistemology, and in three weeks I'm taking over one 75-minute session of our upper-division Knowledge and Justification course: 19 students, mixed majors, a room with a whiteboard and index cards and nothing else. I've been asked to do the session on cross-cultural variation in epistemic intuitions, and I want the students to run into the problem rather than hear about it.

What has failed before: I've watched this taught by putting a slide of bar charts on the screen and comparing verdict distributions across populations. It collapses every time into a methodological squabble about whether the survey questions were any good, with a good part of the room concluding that the subjects must have misread the vignette. I don't want that. And I don't want to put the six or seven students in the room who learned English later, or who grew up outside the United States, on display as specimens: no asking anyone to speak for a culture, no "and what would people back home say?" moves.

Two things I'd like the design to carry, if you think they can be made to work in the time available.

First: what does the philosophical damage, I think, is not the first-order split but the second-order surprise. Each of us tacitly predicts that a competent, attentive person will land where we land, and predicts it with real confidence; that prediction is what licenses us to treat disagreement as a mistake rather than as a difference to be explained. So I want the activity to record that prediction before anyone sees the actual distribution, and to let the gap between predicted and actual distribution be the datum the room discovers for itself. Call it the projection failure, and build the scoring so that it is visible on the board and not merely reported.

Second: I'd like a causal handle, not just a correlation. My idea is to manufacture micro-cultures in the room: split the class by a genuinely random procedure, give each half a few minutes of different training in how to read a certain kind of case — I was thinking of instructing one half to read vignettes the way a lawyer reads a statute and the other the way a friend reads a text message — and then run the cases blind. If a habit acquired in five minutes shifts verdicts, the students can watch cultural transmission act on themselves instead of hearing about it in a stranger. Tell me honestly whether that device is a bad idea and why; if you keep it, tell me how long the training has to be, how to keep it from being transparent, and what it is a model of and what it merely looks like a model of.

Cases: write your own, three or four, not the famous ones from the literature. I don't want the session to become a seminar on one well-known paper, and I don't want anyone guessing where this is going. They should be short enough to read twice, and they should be the sort of case where a "does she know" or "is he referring to" verdict plausibly moves with interpretive habit.

What participants should come away seeing: that an intuition arrives wearing two credentials — it feels like a detection of how things are, and it feels like a verdict any careful person would share — and that cross-cultural variation strips the second credential while leaving the first untouched, which is why "the subjects must have misread the question" is not an answer to it.

Also needed: a debrief mapping specific moments in the activity onto specific parts of the dispute, including the difference between verdicts varying and what that variation would warrant methodologically; an honest list of what will go wrong the first time, including what I should do if the room does not split, which with nineteen people it may well not; and a way of handling the student who says, correctly, that a roomful of undergraduates trained for five minutes tells us nothing about real cultural difference. Be frank about which feature of the real debate the activity cannot produce and that I will have to supply in my own voice.

Skip the literature: no citations, no study names invoked as authorities, no numbers from published data. I have that material; what I need is the design.

Give me the session plan first, then a single JSON object with this schema:

- target_move: one sentence stating what participants discover for themselves, not what I tell them
