I'm helping run a small reading group on vagueness this term, and before we wade into epistemicism vs supervaluationism I want the spec nailed down: what does a theory of vagueness actually have to get right? Not "here's how the two views score" — we'll do that ourselves in a couple of weeks — but the list of requirements, each with the case or intuition that motivates it, and your view on which of them are genuine constraints and which are just things one camp already wanted to hear. No reading list, we've got a syllabus. Some candidates I keep coming back to: the sorites/tolerance intuition; the way higher-order vagueness seems to come back no matter how many "definitely"s you stack on top; the Dummett-flavoured worry that the supervaluationist ends up committed to there being a cut-off somewhere while no particular number is where it is (correct me if I've garbled that); the feeling that the epistemicist's boundary is brute in a way that's meant to be objectionable; and the one I care most about, separating vagueness from plain ignorance.

That last one comes from two cases I made up and can't stop poking at. Case one: a game show announces that the jackpot goes to the first person in the queue whose exact birth weight in grams was a multiple of seven. The hospital records were destroyed in a fire, no other copy exists anywhere, and nobody will ever find out — but there either was such a person or there wasn't. My intuition is that "gets the jackpot" is as sharp as anything gets and merely unknown, not vague in the way "tall" is. So: does your theory predict that? And if it says the difference is just that in this case someone could in principle have found out, I want to know why in-principle knowability gets to do that much work. Case two: a community gets tired of arguing and coins a word, "bhen", meaning borderline tall — you're a bhen if you're neither clearly tall nor clearly not. Now run the sorites on "bhen" again. Does it have its own penumbra, and where does that one come from? Does it collapse back into plain tallness? Or is the question somehow ill-formed? I want to know which of those answers each theory is even allowed to give and what each costs it.

Then the structural part, which is what I most want: flag any two requirements on your list that look like they can't both be satisfied — my hunch is that "vindicate tolerance", "keep classical logic all the way up", and "no brute unknowable facts" can't all hold, but say which horn each of the two views actually takes, and tell me which single requirement does the most work in knocking candidates out. One more thing that's specific to us: our ulterior motive is that whatever account we land on should eventually say something about decision procedures — think of a system that has to make calls on vague predicates (eligibility rules, moderation thresholds) and justify the call afterwards to someone who was denied. So please add whatever requirements follow from that use rather than from the semantics seminar, and mark them, along with everything else, as non-negotiable or as a preference. A short table comparing the two views at the very end is fine, but the criteria and their cases are the part I actually want.
