Write a piece of bureaucratic satire: the minutes of a single meeting of the Faculty Senate of a mid-sized university, together with a short appended memorandum from the University Parliamentarian. Aim for 850–950 words in all, of which the memo takes no more than 200. The voice and format should be those of genuine institutional prose — numbered agenda items, "Resolved," "the Chair observed that," committee reports received — with no authorial winking, no footnotes, no glossary, no explanation of the underlying problem, and no named theorists or citations.

The premise: the Senate has recently reformed its procedure so that, on any motion with a logical structure, it votes on the premises and lets the conclusion follow, rather than voting on the conclusion directly. Play that rule completely straight, and let the absurdity come entirely from what it actually licenses. Take the meeting through four episodes, in this order.

First, a curriculum motion with a conditional structure: a majority accepts that if enrolment in the language requirement has fallen, the requirement should be suspended; a majority accepts that enrolment has fallen; a majority nonetheless votes against suspending the requirement. The Chair announces that the suspension carries. One senator objects that a majority voted against it. The Chair's reply must be procedurally impeccable and quietly monstrous.

Second, later the same evening, a second motion — a hiring line or a budget transfer — turns on the very same factual premise, and this time the majority finds the premise false. The minutes should record both findings without any visible alarm, and the Parliamentarian's memo should give this arrangement a name and defend it as a feature: judgments held relative to, or exhausted by, the question then before the Senate.

Third, a report from the Governance Committee: the Senate has tried conclusion-based voting, premise-based voting, sequential priority, and deferring to a panel of experts, and has certified that no arrangement satisfies all of its desiderata at once — that any combination of individual judgments be admissible, that every senator carry equal weight, that each question be decided on the votes cast on that question alone, that a unanimous Senate get its way, and that the resulting Senate position be logically consistent. The Committee therefore recommends that the Chair be empowered to nominate, per agenda item, which one of these to set aside; it should note in passing that vesting all decisions in a single person would satisfy all but one and was rejected on grounds unrelated to logic.

Fourth, the Senate's own discovery: "bundling," by which several logically connected propositions are fused into one compound resolution, after which no inconsistency can arise. Present bundling, deadpan, as the solution. Build the joke so a reader can see why it works only by shrinking the agenda — the Senate decides everything at once by deciding one thing, and whoever drafts the bundles decides the outcomes. Include one bundle that fuses a factual claim, a moral principle, and a budget figure into a single question, and one senator's protest that the Senate is no longer deciding anything but merely ratifying what the drafter wrote.

Add, somewhere, a senator who insists that the Senate itself holds beliefs and not merely vote totals, and who is corrected for a confusion about collective agency; this senator should be the one who sees what is happening and should get the last objection of the meeting, unanswered.

Two constraints on the whole: everything the Senate does must be something a real premise-based rule would permit, so that a social-choice specialist finds the satire fair rather than merely hostile; and the piece must stay legible to a reader who has never heard of the discursive dilemma, who should be able to tell what is being ironized without being told. The target is the practice of responding to that dilemma by choosing whether to aggregate at the level of premises or conclusions — and the authority that choice quietly confers on whoever sets the agenda.
