I have to give the principal's address at this year's Speech Day at Ashgrove Comprehensive, and it is the last one: from September the school's ranked academic prizes — top of year, subject prizes, the honours board with its cumulative totals — are gone, replaced by a single certificate that every student receives, with no names read out in the hall. The money that funded the prizes is being folded into the general building fund, so no new tutoring, bursary or support for the students at the bottom is being created alongside the change; as far as anyone can tell, the only thing that will actually differ next year is that the students who used to win will stop winning. The governors passed it seven votes to six after two years of argument, and the hall will contain parents who campaigned for it, parents who fought it, staff who are split more or less down the middle, and — in the front row — our head of sixth form and our one philosophy teacher, both of whom know that I spent three years of a part-time doctorate writing about prioritarianism and the levelling-down objection before I left academia for teaching, and both of whom will read every sentence I say as a coded announcement of my own position. My daughter is in Year Nine and has never won anything. I have about ten minutes, I want to be honest rather than merely diplomatic, and I cannot be honest in a way that sounds like a manifesto.

What I want from you is a speech in which that philosophical structure does real work by constraining what I can and cannot say. Two things in particular. First, I must not tell the room that we are doing this for the sake of the students at the bottom, because I don't think I believe that: if their prospects, their teaching, their confidence and their outcomes are all unchanged, then a view that gives priority to the worse off gives me no reason at all to welcome the change — and I'd be saying something I think false to the very students I'd be claiming to help. Second, I must not say that we have made things worse, or that the old system was fine, because that would be dishonest in the other direction and would land as a defence of an honours board whose main function, year after year, was to confirm to a few hundred children where they stood. Between those two silences there is something true to say about achievement, effort and being seen, and I want the speech to find it rather than to hover.

The complication I keep going round in circles on, and which I'd like the speech to settle rather than dodge, is this. The case for abolishing the prizes appeals to a harm that is essentially comparative: the business of sitting in the hall year after year and being publicly known to be near the bottom. If that shame is a genuine component of a student's welfare, then removing its source is not levelling down at all — it is a real gain to the worst off, bought by taking away a good that only ever existed in the comparison. But if I grant that welfare is sensitive to rank at the bottom, I seem bound to grant it at the top as well, which means the winners suffer a real loss too, and then the question isn't whether the change is a pure levelling down but how to weigh a heavily weighted gain to the badly off against an unweighted loss to everyone else — a calculation I can't honestly present to a hall of parents as obvious. There is also the small fact that the athletics cup and the music prize are being kept, which the speech should use rather than ignore. Please write the address: roughly a thousand to twelve hundred words, addressed to a room of fourteen-to-eighteen-year-olds and their families, warm but not sentimental, with no jargon and no named theories — the two people who would catch the argument should recognise its shape without ever hearing me label it — and with the argument above doing the work of deciding which sentences I'm allowed to say.
