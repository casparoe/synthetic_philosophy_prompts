I'm writing a term paper for an existentialism seminar: a case study that reads Milgram's obedience experiments through Sartre's account of radical freedom and bad faith. Assume the facts as common knowledge: the baseline condition at Yale, where every subject went past 300 volts and roughly two-thirds went to the maximum; the four scripted prods, escalating from a polite request to "The experiment requires that you continue" and finally "You have no other choice; you must go on"; the signs of strain Milgram recorded; and the variations, in which obedience fell when the learner was brought into the same room and again when the teacher had to hold the learner's hand on a shock plate, fell further when the experimenter delivered his orders by telephone from another room, and fell from about 65% to about 48% when the study moved from Yale to a run-down office in Bridgeport, Connecticut.

I want the analysis to run in both directions, and I'll add the citations myself, so no literature review.

First, apply the framework: what, for Sartre, was the subject facing? Give me a disciplined discrimination among three diagnoses that are easy to run together — ordinary self-deception about one's motives; bad faith in the strict sense, the project of fleeing a freedom one cannot be ignorant of, since one cannot lie to oneself as one lies to another; and the spirit of seriousness, treating the value of science and the laboratory's hierarchy as written into the world rather than chosen. Which fits the material best, and what in what the subjects said would tell between them?

Second, turn the case back on the theory. Sartre holds that a situation has only the significance the agent's project confers on it, so the laboratory cannot serve as an excuse; Milgram's own conclusion is that the situation explains the behavior. Do the systematic variations show subjects freely conferring different meanings on the same procedure, or do they show that "radical freedom" is really a name for sensitivity to features of the setting the agent did not choose — and if Sartre would call that diagnosis itself bad faith, is there anything that could convince him otherwise? Does the minority who walked out count as evidence for Sartre or against him?

Be explicit about where the framework falls silent rather than forcing it to speak: about the sweating and trembling, about what the debriefing revealed, about whether an absorbed subject is in bad faith at all. Close with a verdict: does Milgram corroborate the bad-faith diagnosis, expose it as unfalsifiable, or point at something the theory has no vocabulary for?
