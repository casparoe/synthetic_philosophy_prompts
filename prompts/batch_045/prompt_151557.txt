I've got a joint session next week — our HPS group is hosting the economic history and demography people for an evening — and I'm giving a paper I've put off writing longer then I should have. Roughly twenty in the room: mostly philosophers of science (two Lakatos people, one Popperian who never quite gave it up, and a formal epistemologist who does Bayesian confirmation theory and treats everything else as noise), plus two economic historians and a demographer who spend their lives with actual population and wage series. I want the Q&A prep sheet, not the paper.

The thesis, in three sentences. Malthus got a mechanism right — more labor on a fixed land base, diminishing returns, a subsistence check on population — and got the boundary condition wrong: he didn't anticipate that the pressure he described would itself trigger the technological response that dissolved the constraint, so the headline prediction failed for two centuries. "Right but early" is a genuine verdict and not only a face-saving move, but it's only genuine if we discipline it: a long-run prediction should be issued with a deadline, and with mid-run diagnostics — observable intermediate variables that track whether the mechanism is still running while the headline prediction is failing. Without those two, "right but early" is a license to keep any theory alive indefinitely, and I want to say that's a methodological failure rather than charity to a good idea.

Things I'm conceding up front so I don't get killed later: I am not going to make the "and now look at resource constraints and climate, so Malthus wins in the end" move — the demographer will eat me alive and I don't think the argument needs it. I'm also conceding that Malthus's own 1798 text is doing a lot of work with vagueness about timing, so it's not obvious the theory as stated had a deadline to violate in the first place.

What I want from you: the seven or eight objections I'll actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage they'd do, phrased the way these people would phrase them. I specifically want the economic historian's version of the Boserup point — that Malthus may have gotten the sign of the feedback wrong (population pressure induces innovation) and not merely the date — and the formal epistemologist's version of "a deadline is just a prior over time-to-onset, so why is this a norm rather than good bookkeeping." For each objection: a two-minute reply I can actually say out loud, and a note on where that reply is soft. Then: what I should concede in the first two minutes, the single reply that has to land for the paper to survive, and the question I'm hoping nobody asks, with something I could say if they do anyway.

Be straight with me about the meta stuff too. Tell me where you're guessing about what this audience will care about.
