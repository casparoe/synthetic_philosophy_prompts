The topic is Bertrand's paradox and the principle of indifference, and here is the half-formed idea I want turned into a proposal.

The standard dialectic strikes me as stuck in a way that hides the real question. On one side, the underdetermination response: the phrase random chord does not specify a selection method, three apparently indifferent-compatible methods give 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4, and there is nothing in the problem to choose between them, so the problem as stated has no answer. On the other side, the invariance response associated with Jaynes in his 1973 paper: the problem statement fixes neither the position nor the size of the circle, so any objective solution must be indifferent to both, and the scale- and translation-invariant distribution over chords is unique and yields 1/2. I also vaguely recall a later paper arguing that invariance reasoning of this kind can be pushed to recover some of the other answers as well, which would soften the second response considerably. What neither side addresses, as far as I can see, is a prior choice: whether the indifference is supposed to range over the chords of the *figure* or over something in the *ambient space* that produces chords. The invariance response works by embedding: one imagines straws scattered over a plane and then looks at which ones cross a circle drawn on it, so the randomness lives in a homogeneous space of lines and the figure merely selects a subset of them. Every intrinsic method, by contrast, randomises inside the disk. My suspicion is that the whole dispute reduces to this choice of randomization site, and that the interesting question is whether the ambient site can be justified a priori or only by smuggling in empirical information about how chords are actually generated.

I also have a small technical observation that I want the proposal to build on or discard, and I would like you to check it rather than take my word for it. Let a selection rule be a family of densities f_R for the perpendicular distance x from the centre to the chord, one density for each circle radius R; the chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle exactly when x is less than R/2. Consider two constraints. First, scale covariance: since nothing in the problem mentions a scale, the rule for radius c should be the rescaled rule for radius R, so f_c(x) equals (R/c) times f_R(xR/c), equivalently f_R(x) = h(x/R)/R for a single density h on the unit interval. Second, concentric restriction consistency: if a chord drawn by the rule for the radius-R circle happens to cross the concentric circle of radius c less than R, which is to say that x is less than c, then its distribution should be the same as that of a chord drawn directly by the rule for the radius-c circle; formally, f_R conditionalised on the interval from 0 to c equals f_c. My calculation says that these two conditions together force h(s) to have the form C times s raised to C minus 1, for some positive C, so that the probability in question is one half raised to the power C. That family contains the uniform-distance rule at C equal to 1, giving 1/2, and the uniform-midpoint rule at C equal to 2, giving 1/4, but it excludes the random-endpoints rule, whose density is proportional to the reciprocal of the square root of R squared minus x squared; that rule is scale-covariant but violates the restriction condition. And because C is unrestricted, the surviving family still permits every probability strictly between zero and one. If that is right, it is a sharper version of the underdetermination claim than the usual one, since the usual one rests on the accident of having written down three methods; and it also shows that one natural-looking strengthening of Jaynes's own style of argument, restricted to concentric shrinkage and stripped of translation, does no work at all. If it is wrong, or if it is a trivial consequence of a known general result about conditioning-consistent families, I would rather know now.
