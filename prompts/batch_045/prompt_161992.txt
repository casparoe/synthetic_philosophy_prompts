I'm giving a work-in-progress talk in three weeks on money pumps, and I know this room well enough to be nervous. Can you build me a Q&A prep sheet for the question period.

The thesis I'm defending: money-pump arguments are used to show that cyclic preferences are irrational — you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, someone charges you a penny at each step, you end up back with A and three pennies poorer, so something is wrong with *you*. I want to argue the argument can't deliver that verdict on its own. What it establishes is a claim about policies across time, which means the explanatory direction everyone assumes is backwards: diachronic rationality is the basic notion, and the synchronic coherence axioms are what you get when you add the assumption that the agent can't bind herself.

Sketch of how I get there, so you know what to aim at:

1. Split the pumps in two. In an *open* pump you end up with something you prefer at the time you end up with it; calling you worse off requires a premise about the sequence as a whole. In a *closed* (loop) pump you end where you started minus the money — the only kind that gives you a "worse off by your own lights" verdict without extra help. So most of the scary examples in circulation aren't doing the work people think they are.
2. Even the closed pump needs two premises nobody states. (i) It needs you to keep trading rather than notice the loop coming and just refuse the first trade. Call the ones who refuse *loop-closing* agents: the pump only bites the myopic. (ii) The strong version — you get drained of everything — needs you to move for arbitrarily small amounts, or for nothing at all. If you'll only move for a penny, the exploiter's take is bounded by her own stake. So the punch of the example depends on an unstated assumption about how fine-grained your willingness-to-trade is.
3. Conclusion: exploitability is a property of an agent-in-an-environment, not of a preference ordering. The irrationality verdict has to come from somewhere else, and the somewhere else is a claim about which policies you could have adopted ex ante.

The audience, precisely: Friday WIP, department heavy on formal epistemology and social choice. Most of the room thinks of rationality as coherence among representational states — credences, utilities, preference orderings — and is allergic to anything that smells like a substantive claim about what you have reason to want; they'll read my positive view as smuggling in a normative notion of planning. Two people work on akrasia and weakness of will and will be on my side too fast and for the wrong reasons (they'll want to use it for something about resolve, and I don't want the talk hijacked by that). There's a small group that does sequential and dynamic choice and thinks in terms of policies and working backwards from the last node; they're the dangerous ones, because they'll think the conclusion is old news and say so. A senior person in the corner is a Humean about practical reason — reasons come from desires plus means-ends — and she'll say the whole "policy" framing is normative glue she doesn't have any use for. And there are three grad students who last term watched someone use "you'd be money pumped" as a knockdown and have never seen it resisted.

What I want: the objections I'll actually get, ordered by how likely they are times how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that particular person or subgroup would phrase it (I want to hear the social choice person's version, not a generic version). For each, a reply I can give in about two minutes, and a note on where the reply is soft. Then: what I should concede in the first two minutes so the room doesn't spend the whole Q&A on it; the single reply that has to land for the thesis to survive; and the question I'm hoping nobody asks. Mine is something like "if the loop-closing agent can just refuse the first trade, doesn't your view entail that the person who can't bind herself to quit smoking isn't irrational, just unlucky about her environment?" — but if you see a worse one, use that instead.

One constraint: I can't lean on citations. The room will ask "who says that?" and I'd rather answer the objection than the footnote, so no reply should depend on pointing at a paper or a name.
