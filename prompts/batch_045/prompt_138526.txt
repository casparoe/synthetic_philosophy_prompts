I am writing a novel set largely inside a fictional Academy of Prognostication, an institution that issues ten-year forecasts of harvests and epidemics and that, in the scandal of my third act, has quietly begun issuing written prognoses of individual deaths. The Academy is not a satire of anything real, and I do not want its internal factions to be strawmen. I want them to be the actual positions in the contemporary debate about determinism, chaos, and predictability — not the two or three that a general reader already has a name for — and I want the disagreements between them to be ones my characters could have in dialogue rather than in a seminar.

What I need, then, is a decision tree I can use as a casting grid: a route from a commitment a non-philosopher can recognise in themselves to a named position in the debate, where every branch point is a question a character could answer without already knowing which position it leads to. Please build it from the logical space first — the questions and the combinations of answers they permit — and only then locate the real positions inside that space.

Constraints on the instrument:

The questions must be concrete cases, not labels. Where they do useful work, please use cases from the book: a coin left spinning on a marble table; a three-week weather forecast; an implanted device that warns a patient of a seizure seconds before it arrives; a friend choosing between two jobs; a pendulum re-run after being recorded to six decimal places; a harvest forecast that alters the planting decisions it was issued to inform, so that the forecasters are inside the system they forecast; and a written prognosis that the person it concerns reads and then tries to defeat.

The distinctions I care about, and which I take the tree must separate explicitly, are these. First, whether the future is fixed (exactly one physically possible continuation), whether it is inferable (something could know it in advance), and whether it is controllable (an agent could have done otherwise); I strongly suspect these can come apart in more ways than my draft allows. Second, the different sources of unpredictability: sensitive dependence on initial conditions, the sheer cost of computation, self-reference when the predictor is embedded in the predicted system, and the possibility that the laws are deterministic yet not computable by any procedure at all, so that even an unbounded reasoner with unlimited time could not produce the outcome. Third, statistical orderliness without individual predictability: perfectly stable aggregate frequencies alongside no forecastable individual event. Fourth, whether an open future means that many futures are possible or only that no evidence available at the time settles which one arrives — and whether the second, merely epistemic openness is enough to underwrite deliberation, regret, and the experience of suspense. Fifth, reliability against error in the initial data as distinct from reliability against error in the model itself, since my Academy forecasts from models and I want to know whether those two kinds of robustness can diverge.

Leaves that must be reachable: hard determinism including its block-universe variant; classical compatibilism; the deterministic-but-chaotic view on which forecast is never available in practice; the indeterministic-but-unfree view on which the extra openness is only dice; event-causal or agent-causal libertarianism; something in the neighbourhood of superdeterminism, where the investigator's own choices are correlated with the system investigated; deterministic-but-uncomputable; and, if you judge it a genuine position rather than a mood, what I think of as 'narrative openness' — the text is already complete, the characters inside it cannot find out what happens, and that ignorance is no defect, because the norms governing anticipation are internal to the telling. Add a leaf for the view that the question partly dissolves because the future has no determinate content in a relativistic setting, if you think it belongs.

Size and audience: eight to twelve branch nodes, and I would rather have eleven sharp questions than twenty; no more than fifteen leaves. No node may be phrased so that only someone who already knows the answer can answer it: is the world deterministic at the fundamental level is useless to me, whereas the instruments disagree about the sixth decimal place and the question is whether a perfect instrument would settle the matter is not.

Finally, mark the combinations of answers that nobody occupies, and for each say whether it is empty because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unclaimed. Flag any two leaves your tree cannot separate rather than forcing a distinction between them.

Please do not give me reading suggestions, and do not attribute these views to particular books, papers, or authors; the novel has no footnotes, and I would rather have the geography of the positions right than their history.

Structure your reply in six titled sections, in this order: The branching questions (about 350 words, each question with its case and the exact split it forces); The space before the names (about 250 words, the live combinations and how many there are); The tree with named leaves (about 450 words, each leaf named and given one sentence on its most awkward commitment, which I intend to steal for character flaws); Unoccupied cells (about 200 words, each diagnosed as incoherent, unattractive, or unclaimed); Where the tree cannot separate (about 150 words); Casting note (about 120 words, on which single-node disagreement would make the best scene, since two characters who agree everywhere except one branch are the only ones who can genuinely argue on the page).
