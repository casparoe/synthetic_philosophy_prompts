Hey — I'm putting together a two-page dissertation-fellowship proposal and I keep going back and forth with a friend in the ML group about whether the no-free-lunch results actually say anything philosophically interesting about induction, or whether its just Hume's problem dressed up in sums over objective functions. I've got a half-formed idea I want turned into something a committee would actually fund. One ground rule first: I'll drop in the citations myself, so please don't include any, and don't hang anything on a specific paper's title, year, or exact formal statement — describe positions and arguments in your own words instead, as if you were explaining them to someone who will look up the sources later.

Here's where I am. The usual move on the philosophy side is to read NFL as either a sceptical result — look, no learner beats any other, so induction is unjustified after all — or a deflationary one — the theorem only bites if you average over every possible objective function, and that average is itself a prior, and a wildly opinionated one, so this is Hume again with extra notation. I think both readings miss something. What the theorem really gives you is a diagnostic: it tells you, in the form of a restriction on a class of worlds or a prior over them, exactly what premise you'd have to add to get a learner off the ground. That reframes the problem of induction as a measurement question rather than a justification question — what's the weakest structural assumption about the world (computable? bounded complexity? stationary? exchangeable? something about the *size* of the space of worlds rather than its shape?) under which some inductive method is optimal, and can that assumption be defended without the usual circularity or regress. The half-formed bit I most want sharpened: maybe the old talk of a "uniformity of nature" principle is better cashed out as a *complexity bound* on the world than as a claim about laws, and if so the interesting question is how weak that bound can get before learning collapses.

Three wrinkles I want the proposal to own rather than hide, because I suspect that's where the real work is. First, the standard result is proved for a finite space of instances where a uniform average is available, but the space of possible worlds a Humean sceptic quantifies over isn't like that at all — it's not finite and there's no natural uniform distribution on it — so the bridge between the two debates has to be built, not assumed. Second, on the computable side of the literature there's a universal-learner style result that looks like an actual free lunch, and its not obvious which of the two results is supposed to bear on induction, or whether "induction" is even one thing here. Third, "no assumptions" is itself an assumption: the uniform average is a very specific way of representing ignorance, and I want the proposal to say what work that choice is doing instead of letting it pass as neutrality.

What I need: the proposal itself, written for a one-year dissertation fellowship and aimed at a commitee that's half philosophers and half ML people who've read at most a page of each other's literature, so keep notation to a minimum and define anything you do use. About 1100 words, in these sections, in this order: "The question" (~250 words), stated so it has a determinate answer and isn't just a gesture at a topic; "Why the existing readings leave it open" (~250), covering the sceptical and deflationary readings and what both miss; "Method" (~250), what I'd actually do, term by term; "What a result would look like" (~200), including what a *negative* result would look like, since I think this project could easily conclude that the two literatures are talking past each other; and "Failure modes" (~150), honest ones, including the risk that this is three projects wearing one coat.
