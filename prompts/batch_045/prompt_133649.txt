I'm in a philosophy of mind seminar this term, and we've been working through the user-illusion family of views: the idea that what we call conscious experience is a simplified interface the system presents to itself and to action, rather than an accurate readout of the processes actually doing the work. I trust my verdicts on cases more than I trust any of the theories, so I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to. Here is where I land.

1. In the choice-blindness setup: someone is shown two faces, points at A, is handed B without noticing the switch, and then fluently explains why B is more attractive. I say there was no such preference; the feeling of having preferred B all along is manufactured on the spot. Illusion.
2. A man with an amputated arm reports a clenched, painful fist in a hand that no longer exists. I say the pain is fully real — not an illusion of pain — even though there is nothing in the hand for it to track. This is the pair I can't reconcile with 1: in both, a state or report with nothing in the world answering to it, and I call one bogus and the other real.
3. A blindsight patient sorts stimuli in her scotoma at well above chance while insisting she sees nothing and is guessing. I say: no visual experience there, and her failure to act on that information outside the lab is evidence for the same conclusion, not a separate fact.
4. A fluent typist has no experience of individual keystrokes. I don't think that makes the keystroke-level processing unconscious; the interface is simply coarse-grained. So unreportable does not mean unexperienced.
5. A scene that flickers and changes is reported as stable and uniformly detailed. I say the stability and the detail are renderings, not facts about the processing. Illusion.
6. Two hypothetical systems. First, an AI assistant with no separate introspective channel: when asked whether it found a problem hard, its answer is produced by the very same next-output machinery that produced its answer to the problem, and there is nothing the report reads off. Second, the same system with an added self-monitoring module that reads a distinct internal register (a learned confidence number) and reports that. My verdict is that the two don't differ much: in both cases I see another output, not a look inward. But that seems to cost me case 2, since the pain is also just something the system generates internally.

My tentative principle is that an interface report is trustworthy only where feedback could in principle correct it, so that a report with nothing to be checked against is decoration. That handles 1, 5 and 6 but appears to rule against 2, which I don't want to give up. Is there a principle — perhaps one that separates what the subject undergoes from reports that purport to be about something else — that recovers most of this? Please state it precisely, say what motivates each clause, tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and apply it to a case I haven't listed: a person who performs a skilled action accurately while reporting no sense of agency or ownership whatsoever. Is the sense of agency itself the experience, or a rendering whose absence shows nothing?
