I TA an undergraduate decision-theory course, and last term a few of us started playing centipede games against each other for coffee money between seminars, so I now have a pile of gut reactions I can't get into shape. The setup, so we are talking about the same thing: one pot, starting at ten dollars and doubling whenever someone passes; at your node you either take, getting seventy percent of the current pot with thirty percent to the other player and the game over, or pass, handing the move to the other player; if the game reaches the announced final node and the mover there passes, the pot is split evenly. My verdicts. Final node at two, I take: seven dollars now beats the six I get if they take at the last node, and I don't believe for a second they'd pass there. Final node at six, I pass. Final node at a hundred, I pass, and noticeably more confidently than in the six-node case, which I can't justify, since length shouldn't matter to an argument that doesn't care about length. Same hundred-node game against a housemate who has taken at the first opportunity in every game I have watched her play: I take. Against a stranger, about whom I know only that roughly three people in ten pass until near the end: I pass, and I think I'd still pass if it were one in ten. Having reached node five with my opponent having passed at nodes two and four: I pass, and I feel better about it than I did at node one, even though what I have learned is that they are not the sort of agent the backward-induction story describes, and noticing that makes me want to cooperate more, which feels backwards. Against an opponent who is a literal copy of my own decision procedure, moving at the even nodes with no communication possible: I pass, on the feeling that my passing and their passing are the same event, though I know that can't be quite right. Stakes version, where the pot eventually grows big enough that the difference between the seventy percent and the thirty percent share is the difference between a house deposit and a holiday: I take, earlier than I like to admit. Slow-growth version, pot up twenty percent per pass instead of doubling: I take immediately, and all the interest goes out of it. Version in which at each node an independent five percent chance ends the game with an even split and no one choosing: I pass, and I have the strong sense that the argument which made me take in the short game cannot even get started here, because there is no last node to begin from, though I can't tell whether that is a real structural difference or just a feeling. A friend in the econ department tells me it is a theorem that common knowledge of rationality forces taking at the first node; I don't need the reference, but I do want to know what such an argument has to assume and which of my verdicts it kills outright.

The only principle I have managed to formulate for myself is something like this: pass if and only if my current probability that the opponent passes next, times what I get if they do, plus my probability that they take times what I get then, beats what I get by taking now, where that probability is not a fixed number but something I read off how much they would gain by taking and how much they have already passed, updated by treating each observed pass as evidence that they are not a taker. I don't trust it. It seems to make my willingness to pass depend on my estimate of their irrationality in a way that ought to embarrass me, it gives me no grip at all on the copy case or the random-ending case, and it does not explain why the length of the game and the size of the stakes move me when, on paper, they shouldn't.

So: can you state a principle, or a short list of candidate principles, precise enough that I can run it on a case, and tell me which of my verdicts each clause is there to save, which verdicts it forces me to give up, and what it says about cases I haven't listed: whether I should pay a dollar before play to be bound to take at node one; a three-player version with the move cycling; a version where the taker's share is only fifty-one percent; and a version where I'm told my opponent has read exactly the same decision-theory textbook I have and nothing else. And there is the case I keep going back and forth on: the twenty-node game against someone who has credibly and truthfully committed, before play, to passing at every node until node eighteen and taking there. My gut says pass at node one and take at node seventeen, but if I can see that, she can, and I can't tell whether that makes me sophisticated or merely untrustworthy, or whether it means the commitment was never worth anything in the first place.
