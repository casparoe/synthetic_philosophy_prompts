Hi — I wonder if you can help me with something. I'm a retired experimental physicist (thirty years on magnetic resonance hardware, mostly), and since I stopped I've been going along to a philosophy reading group at the library. A few weeks back we did the famous teletransporter material — the machine that scans you here, destroys the body, and rebuilds an exact copy on Mars — and I went home and built an argument that I think wrecks the whole "identity isn't what matters" move. But it wrecks it so thoroughly that I can't believe it, so I'd like to know which step to throw out. Sorry if this is old hat; I've only been at it two years.

The set-up. Suppose the manufacturer ships a software upgrade, a blend function. You load in two people — me and my colleague Hal — and set a dial λ somewhere between 0 and 1. At λ = 1 you get an exact replica of me on Mars; at λ = 0 an exact replica of Hal; in between you get someone whose psychology is a mixture — memories of both, traits averaged, body somewhere in the middle. Both source bodies are destroyed as usual. The company's demo footage shows the intermediate people apparently doing fine, chatting about both childhoods, no obvious confusion. My neighbour, who used to work in neuroscience, says a 50/50 blend of two brains would be mush rather than a person, and I don't know whether that matters — the demo footage would look convincing either way.

Here's the argument. Numbered, because that's how I was trained to write things down.

(1) In the plain teletransporter case — exact replica, original destroyed — what matters in survival is preserved. The trip is as good for me as ordinary survival. This is the bit my group was happy to accept, and the bit I'd most like to keep.

(2) What matters doesn't turn on numerical identity, so the verdict in (1) can't depend on whether the output is numerically the same person. It depends on psychological continuity between input and output.

(3) Small differences don't matter. If a run at dial λ is as good as survival, then for a small enough nudge of the dial, the run at λ − ε is as good as survival too. I lose more psychology than that to a bottle of wine on a Friday and I don't think I die.

(4) So run the machine repeatedly: trip one at λ = 0.99, then feed each output back in with the dial nudged a bit further toward Hal. After enough trips the person on Mars is a replica of Hal — none of my memories worth speaking of, none of my projects, Hal's body.

(5) By repeated application of (3), every trip in that series is as good as survival for me. So being destroyed and replaced by a replica of Hal is as good as survival for me.

(6) But that's just death with a bystander left over, and it's exactly the outcome that "identity isn't what matters" was supposed to separate from the teletransporter case. If the move can't tell the two apart, then it does no work; if it can, then something in (1)–(5) is wrong.

(7) And it gets worse. I can run the same reasoning with no machine at all. My psychology now and my psychology fifty years ago overlap very little, and the changes came in small steps. So by (3) and (5) ordinary survival preserves nothing that matters either, which is absurd — I'm fairly sure I have a reason to care about next Tuesday.

I want to keep (1), and I want to keep that being erased and replaced by Hal is bad for me in the way death is. So something else has to go. I've tried two patches and neither does anything: saying what matters has to have the *normal* cause doesn't help, because every trip in my series has the same kind of cause as the plain teletransporter trip; and saying the continuity has to be non-branching doesn't help either, since my series never branches.

So: which step am I being cheated by, and what does giving it up cost me? I'd rather have a diagnosis than a list of positions, but if the usual replies each have a price attached, tell me who pays what.
