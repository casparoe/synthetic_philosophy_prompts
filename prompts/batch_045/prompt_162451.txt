I'm a final-year PhD student in philosophy, and on Thursday I'm presenting a paper to the joint Consciousness and Vision seminar — it runs jointly with the perception group, so the room will be roughly twenty people in three or four fairly sharply different camps. I have thirty minutes of questions after the paper, and I'd like you to build me the prep sheet for that half hour.

The paper. The case is the color phi phenomenon: a red spot is flashed briefly at one location, and a fraction of a second later a green spot some degrees away, and observers report a single spot travelling between the two positions whose color changes abruptly partway along the path. Since the change is apparently seen at a point the spot passes before the second flash has even occurred, something has to give. Either the experience as of the unrevised sequence — two stationary flashes, no motion, no midpath change — was conscious and then written over in memory (the Orwellian revision), or the record was doctored before it ever reached consciousness, so that nothing was ever experienced as unrevised (the Stalinesque revision). The received view, inherited from Dennett, is that the two hypotheses make no empirical difference: any probe you run finds the same drafts either way, so there is no determinate answer to the question of when, exactly, the change was experienced, and the residual urge to ask it is just the Cartesian theater reasserting itself. My paper argues that this is too quick.

My positive proposal, which I'll call interruptive triage, has four moving parts. First, titrate the interstimulus interval and spatial separation per subject until unspeeded reports are effectively always "one moving spot, changed midpath." Second, on a random third of trials, present a high-contrast pattern mask covering both flash locations and the path between them, at a delay after the second flash that is jittered across the presumed integration window. Third, require a speeded three-alternative forced choice — "red only," "green only," "moving spot that changed midpath" — with a response deadline enforced by a go signal and with late responses discarded. Fourth, look for a band of mask delays at which the speeded choice tracks the unrevised content even though the same subjects, on trials with the same timing but an unspeeded report, describe a single moving spot changing color midpath. The argument is that if, within one stimulus regime, there is a time at which the system's answer to a deliberate reportable query is the unrevised sequence and a later time at which it is the revised one, then at the earlier time there was content as of the unrevised sequence available to the subject for report — and availability for deliberate report is the only grip we have on what was consciously experienced. So the distinction is locally decidable after all: for these parameters, revision is Orwellian, or else it is Stalinesque, and either way the question was not empty.

The room. Two committed multiple-drafts theorists, one of whom has published on the no-empirical-difference argument, will say that my probe measures availability to a response system and not consciousness, and that I am helping myself to a fixed latency for revision when that is exactly what is in dispute. A vision scientist who works on apparent motion and spatiotemporal integration will press on whether there is any earlier stage with the unrevised content at all, rather than one construction that never had a "before." A masking psychophysicist will insist that a mask does not interrupt consciousness but interrupts particular channels at particular retinotopic locations, with timing that depends on contrast, eccentricity and spatial frequency, and that a mask laid along the path may destroy the motion signal rather than freeze a draft. Someone from the no-report and metacognition side will say that a deadline forced choice indexes decision evidence or metacognitive access rather than phenomenal experience, so my criterion is precisely the report-based inference those paradigms were built to avoid. If a neuroscientist turns up, expect the point that episodic memory cannot be overwritten on a hundred-millisecond timescale, making a positive result look like a paradox rather than a finding.

What I want from you:

- The seven or eight objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they would do, not by how famous they are.
- Each phrased the way the relevant person in that room would phrase it, in their vocabulary — a masking person should sound like a masking person, not like a philosopher.
- For each: a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and an honest note on where the reply is soft.
- What I should concede in the first two minutes, before anyone asks anything, and the single claim I must not give up because the paper has nothing without it.
- The weakest joint in my own argument that I should prepare to defend rather than hope nobody finds.
- The objection I am privately dreading, which I would like included even though I would rather it not be asked: that the dissociation, if it appears at all, will turn out to be parameter-dependent, so that shrinking the interval or changing the mask flips the verdict, and I will have traded one empty metaphysical question for an endless parameter sweep with no answer to what anyone actually wanted to know.
