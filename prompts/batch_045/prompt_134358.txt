I am writing a critical notice of a recent monograph that defends a relational, Ubuntu-informed conception of personhood, and I have arranged with its author to close the piece with her reply to one case that, as far as I can tell, she has never ruled on. Before I send it, I want to anticipate her answer, because it determines which of two objections I develop in the body of the notice.

Her verdicts so far, as I have tabulated them from the book and from the papers that preceded it. She holds that personhood is attained through incorporation into a web of relations rather than given with biological humanity.

1. A boy found at fourteen after being raised without sustained human contact, who acquires language and reciprocal exchange within two years of rescue: he was, she says, a person before he was found; what was done to him was a wrong of exclusion; rescue is not the moment at which he became one.
2. A man in a persistent vegetative state, with no family, no friends and no visitors: no longer a person; what remains is a living human body owed respectful handling and decent burial, and life-prolonging treatment may be withdrawn. In a footnote she remarks that the case would be harder if relatives were at the bedside speaking to him daily, and declines to say what she would hold then.
3. A chimpanzee reared from infancy in a human household, taught signs, addressed as a member of the family: not a person, but its relational embeddedness raises its moral status well above that of other animals, and it could not permissibly be enrolled in the kinds of non-therapeutic study we permit for laboratory animals.
4. A newborn infant: not yet a person in the full sense, but owed nearly the whole protection owed to persons, and in any case more than the enculturated chimpanzee in (3).
5. An anencephalic infant: never a person. The devotion of its parents generates duties concerning the infant, not duties to it, and she insists that this distinction is load-bearing for her account.
6. A recluse who has severed every relationship and refuses contact: still a person, and his refusal must be respected. Any account, she says, on which a community could divest someone of personhood merely by withdrawing recognition is unacceptable.
7. She sets aside the case of ancestors and the dead, on the ground that whether being remembered can itself carry status depends on claims about the persistence of the subject that she does not want her view to rest on.

The case I want to put to her is this. Mrs K. is seventy-eight and in the advanced stage of Alzheimer's disease. She no longer recognises her daughter, does not initiate exchange, and has almost no speech; she still settles to her daughter's touch, smiles at music she has known since childhood, and becomes agitated in unfamiliar surroundings. Her daughter visits daily, speaks to her, tells her about the family, and reports — as do the neighbours who call in — that she is still addressed and still treated as a full member of the household. The clinical team proposes enrolling her, with her daughter acting as proxy, in a trial of a new compound that offers her no prospect of benefit, requires weekly lumbar punctures, carries a small but real risk of serious harm, and is expected to yield knowledge that will help future patients. Her daughter consents.

What I would like from you is the following. First, set out the two or three rules that fit verdicts (1) through (6) equally well — my own suspicion is that the fault line lies between views on which the capacity for mutual recognition does all the work and views on which actual relations can carry personhood once that capacity fades, but I want to know whether there is a third formulation that fits the record just as well. Second, say what each rule implies both about whether Mrs K. is still a person and about whether she may be enrolled; note that (3) blocks any quick inference from not being a person to being available for research. Third, tell me which verdict you expect her to give, with your reasons for preferring that rule — and in particular tell me whether the rule that best reconciles the footnote to (2) with the remark in (6) is genuinely consistent with that remark, since on its face a view that lets relations sustain personhood after the capacity is gone also seems to let abandonment extinguish it, which is precisely what she says no acceptable view can allow. Finally, give me the further case that would separate whichever rules survive that test.

One variant I am also considering, and would like your read on: the daughter has emigrated, and Mrs K.'s only daily relational contact is with a commercial companion system that speaks to her for an hour each day in a voice modelled on her daughter's, drawing on the family's photographs and stories; the daughter reads the transcripts and visits twice a year. How much should the verdict change, and which of the rules is most sensitive to the substitution of simulated recognition for actual recognition, and why?
