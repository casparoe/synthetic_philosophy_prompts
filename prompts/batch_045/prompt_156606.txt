I help moderate a large public forum devoted to history — the volunteer kind, with a rules page, a queue, and an appeals thread — and I have no academic training in anything at all; what I know comes from fifteen years of reading and arguing on that forum. Our recurring problem is not the troll. It is the sincere poster: someone arrives with a long, courteous, footnoted argument that a lost civilization built the megaliths, or that the accepted chronology of antiquity was fabricated, and the moderation queue suddenly has to serve as a jury on a question that none of us is qualified to settle. So I want to stop trying to adjudicate substance and write down instead what I should be entitled to demand of a historical claim before I let it stand. What I'm asking for is a specification of that standard, not a verdict on any particular theory.

Let me fix the purpose, since I assume adequacy is relative to it. The standard I need is a triage rule for a general-purpose history forum. It has to be usable in a few minutes by a moderator who knows a fair amount of history in general but nothing special about the period in dispute; it has to be applied in public, in front of an audience that will accuse us of gatekeeping for the academy unless we can show our work; and it has to output an action — remove, leave alone, leave up with a label, or ask the poster one specific question — rather than only a classification. It does not have to tell us what is true. It does have to be honest about the difference.

So: the list of requirements any such rule must meet, with the case or consideration behind each one, and each marked as non-negotiable, as a preference of one camp, or as a demand that only looks like a requirement. Specifically, I want you to tell me which of the things people demand here are real desiderata and which are prejudices, which pairs of desiderata cannot both be satisfied (I suspect there are several, and I want that stated as a result rather than smoothed over), and which single requirement does the most work in ruling candidates out.

The cases it has to sort correctly. On one side: a sunken island civilization described in a philosopher's dialogue and treated by enthusiasts as real geography; the ancient-astronaut books of Erich von Däniken; a Russian mathematician who argues from statistics over chronicles and astronomical records that the conventional timeline of antiquity is a fabrication and the real past far shorter; and the material that circulates almost entirely as photographs — nineteenth-century buildings with windows below the present street level, star-shaped earthworks on old maps, a civilization supposedly buried in a flood and scrubbed from the record — which has no author, no book, no organization, and nobody in charge of the claim to interrogate. On the other side: a meteorologist who proposed that the continents move and was rejected for decades because nobody could supply a mechanism; a city from an epic poem that turned out to be a real place with real walls; a people known only from a single ancient text, widely suspected of being an invention, whose capital and archives were later excavated; and a land described in Norse sagas and dismissed as storyteller's invention until a settlement was dug up in Newfoundland. It also has to handle a period where specialists openly disagree among themselves about what happened — the collapse of the Bronze Age palaces, say — without treating that disagreement as an open door, and it has to place nationalist history that is methodologically ordinary next to nationalist myth that is not, and to distinguish both from honest error, from over-confident popularization, and from fiction that borrows the apparatus of research.

Here are the hunches I've accumulated, which I'd like you to accept, correct, or discard:

Any rule I can state mechanically is one a determined author can satisfy in form, because the movement has learned to sound tentative and to cite.

The rule has to be tested against the past using only what was known at the time; otherwise every vindicated outsider looks like it was always obvious.

Criteria that don't require expertise get gamed, and criteria that require expertise collapse into "ask a professor," which is exactly the accusation I'm trying to answer.

A theory should have to explain why the entire evidentiary record looks the way the mainstream says it does, not merely why its own anomalies are puzzling — and if it explains that appearance by suppression, I want to know how much suppression it needs and how many independent people would have to be in on it.

It should apply a proponent's own evidentiary standard to some claim they reject, and I want to know whether that symmetry test is evidence about the theory or only about the person.

The unit of assessment needs fixing: a claim, a book, a research program, or a community — and if it's a program, what happens when the founder was a crank and the followers behave impeccably?

The rule needs an explicit error-cost profile, because being wrong about a real revision and being wrong about a fabrication are not the same kind of mistake for a forum.

And I want to know whether the form of publication — a video, a self-published book, a documentary, a journal article — can count either way.

Finally, and this is the question I care about most: after the specification is written, does "pseudohistory" survive as a single thing, or does it fall apart into two or three separate failings that we should be naming separately?

Keep the weight on the requirements and their motivating cases. At the end, in a short paragraph or a compact table, score the obvious contenders against the list and no more: deference to specialist consensus, falsifiability, responsiveness to criticism, burden of proof, whether the research program is generating new results or recycling old ones, and the size of the conspiracy the theory requires — and tell me which of these are really the same test wearing different clothes. Please don't give me a bibliography or suggest reading; I can't chase references, and I don't want to be told to go ask an expert, since that is the thing I'm trying to replace.
