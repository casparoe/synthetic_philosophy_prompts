I'm in a small reading group on interpretations of probability — we spent a week each on frequentism, on subjective Bayesianism, and on propensity views, and one member of the group, who is a fairly hardline subjectivist, gave me an argument I have not been able to answer. I'm fairly sure it proves too much, because it ends up saying that there is no objective probability of anything at all, and that seems to me to be false in a way I can feel but not articulate. The case I keep coming back to is a single atom of a synthetic isotope that a lab near ours made one Tuesday afternoon; the funding ran out, the apparatus was dismantled, and no atom of that isotope will ever exist again. People happily attached a number — call it 0.3 — to its decaying within the hour. My friend says that number was just someone's credence, and that there was no further fact of the matter for it to track. I want to say there was a further fact, and that the number could have been wrong in a way that is not just a matter of someone's later embarrassment.

Here is his argument, in my own words and numbering (I have tried to be fair to it):

(1) A probability is whatever plays the uncertainty-role in deliberation: a quantity such that the options open to a rational agent are ranked by expected utility computed with it. Anything that made no difference to what anyone should ever do, or expect, or bet on, would not be a probability at all — so if two assignments of "probability" guide expectation and choice identically in every possible circumstance, there is no difference between them.

(2) Take a long sequence of tosses of a coin I regard as roughly fair. My uncertainty about it is exchangeable: I give the same probability to any two outcome-sequences of the same length that contain the same number of heads, in whatever order. This isn't a further empirical discovery about coins; it seems to be part of what it is to treat the tosses as repeats of one and the same experiment.

(3) A theorem we read (de Finetti's, I believe — I may be garbling the statement) says that if an agent's probabilities over an infinite sequence of trials are exchangeable, then those probabilities are mathematically indistinguishable from those of an agent who treats the trials as independent conditional on some unknown parameter p, holds a personal prior over the values of p, and updates that prior by conditioning on the frequencies observed. Moreover, on that agent's own assignment, with probability one the limiting relative frequency of heads exists and equals p.

(4) So for any prediction about the sequence and any decision that turns on it, positing an objective chance p adds nothing that I do not already have in my prior over p together with the observed frequencies. The chance is a dispensable intermediary: cut it out, and every expectation and every choice comes out exactly as it did before.

(5) By (1), a posit that makes no difference to expectation or choice is not a probability, and what is not a probability is not a fact about probability. So there is no objective chance of heads for the sequence — no fact of the matter beyond agents' priors and the frequencies, no fact for a dissenting agent to have got wrong.

(6) The coin was an arbitrary case. A one-off event is the limiting case of a sequence of length one, and there the argument is easier still, since there is not even a frequency for anyone to appeal to. So there is no objective chance of the single atom's decaying either: the 0.3 was a credence and answered to nothing.

(7) Therefore there are no objective chances anywhere; there are only credences, constrained at most by coherence and by the frequencies that happen to turn up.

Steps (1) through (4) each seem to me individually compelling, and I cannot accept (7). Two things make me think it is false: first, a weather forecaster's 0.3 seems answerable to something beyond her own confidence, in the sense that a long run of forecasts can convict her of having been wrong and not merely unlucky in her feelings; second, in quantum mechanics there appear to be single-case probabilities that are not expressions of anyone's ignorance and are not frequencies of any actual infinite sequence, since there is only one world and most of its events are never repeated. If I have to choose, I would rather give up (1) than give up the claim that the atom had an objective probability of decaying, but I do not want to give up (1) if doing so leaves probabilism with no normative force, because the attraction of subjectivism for me was always that it explains why incoherent credences are criticisable and not merely eccentric.

So: name the step where the argument breaks, say why that step looked sound, and tell me what each of the alternatives would cost me — including what happens to the argument if the flaw I suspect in the move to one-off events in (6) is repaired rather than rejected. I do not need citations or a reading list; I have those, and I want the diagnosis.
