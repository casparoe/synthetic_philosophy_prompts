I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy background -- I got into this through a book club that got out of hand -- and I've been stuck for about a week on an argument I built myself about Nozick's experience machine, the one from his 1974 book. I invented a variant because the original felt like it was cheating by swapping your entire life at once: mine does it part time. Call it the duty-cycle machine. It runs your consciousness in simulation for a fraction f of your waking hours and in reality for the rest, and it guarantees three things: (a) while a simulated stretch is happening it's indistinguishable from a real one, (b) the stretch is at least as good as the real stretch it replaces, by whatever standard you like, and (c) it leaves no trace on the rest of your life -- no memory gap, no after-effects, nobody notices you were gone, your projects and relationships continue exactly as they would have. My argument: (1) for small f, say one amazing afternoon a month, basically everyone takes the deal, including people who say they'd refuse the full machine -- an afternoon like that is just a very good immersive film, and nobody thinks a life is made worse by being absorbed in a film, even though everything in it is fabricated; (2) there is no non-arbitrary f at which the stream stops being one life, since for any f your consciousness is continuous, you remember the whole thing as yours, you plan across it, and everyone else treats you as the same person throughout; (3) for any f short of 1, if a life at duty cycle f is no worse than a fully real life, then a life at f plus one more simulated afternoon is no worse either, because the difference between them is exactly one more afternoon of the kind you already accepted in (1); (4) so by applying (3) over and over, a life at f = 1 is no worse than a fully real one; (5) a life at f = 1 just is the experience machine; (6) so the experience machine is fine.

The problem is that (6) is the one thing I refuse to accept, and it gets worse when I run the same reasoning about my four-year-old rather than about myself: I'd want her to have a real, mediocre life rather than a simulated wonderful one, and I don't want an argument that tells me I'm being irrational there. The obvious move is to blame (3), since tolerance premises of that shape are the classic sorites, but that feels like a way of saying no rather than a reason, and it doesn't tell me where the difference between f = 0.02 and f = 1 is even supposed to live. One friend says the whole thing begs the question: clause (b) is already the hedonist's assumption, because anyone who thinks reality matters will simply deny that a fabricated stretch can be as good as a real one. That sounds too easy, but I can't answer it either -- if the stretch is indistinguishable from the inside, I don't know what being worse off is supposed to consist in while it's happening. A second friend says the real mistake is treating a life as a sum of stretches when what matters is global: whether your projects are really yours, whether the story hangs together, whether you're in contact with anything. I half believe that, but it seems to eat (1) too, because then "one more afternoon of the same kind" stops being a reason at all.

Of (1) through (6) I'd most like to keep (1) and (6), so the cost lands somewhere in the middle. Which step do you reject, or is the setup broken before the argument even starts? What does rejecting that step cost me, and where exactly did it look harmless? And what does each of the three usual families of theory about well-being -- pleasure-based, desire-satisfaction, objective-list -- have to pay here: does any of them get f = 1 and f = 0.02 to come apart for a reason rather than by just stipulating that they do? Please answer in continuous prose, the way you'd write an email: no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, and don't reformat my numbered steps back at me, just talk me through it.
