I convene a monthly open session at which residents of a small city may put questions to the municipal water authority about a decade of intermittent contamination incidents, disputed test results, and a series of contracts awarded without competitive tender to a single engineering firm. The sessions have become an unintended seminar on the epistemology of conspiracy theories, and I now have to write the panel's guidance on how such claims are to be handled.

A colleague who works in risk communication keeps pressing one analogy on me, and it is carrying a great deal of argumentative weight. Conspiracy belief, she says, is pareidolia in the social domain: the same over-eager pattern detection that finds a face in a cloud, or a predator in the rustle of grass, finds a hidden hand in a coincidence. Like a smoke alarm set too sensitively, the mechanism is tuned to asymmetric error costs — a missed fire is catastrophic, a burnt-toast alarm merely annoying — so it fires on noise, and its firing tells you about the detector rather than about the world. Her conclusion is that the panel should stop treating residents' accounts as candidate explanations to be adjudicated on their merits. On the analogy, the sensible response to an alarm you have independent reason to think is false is not to investigate the toast; it is to recalibrate, reassure, and inoculate. Distrust is a public-health problem, and engaging with the content of particular claims is precisely what amplifies them.

I want the analogy audited rather than admired, and I have two complications of my own to put on the table.

First, the claims in the room are not all of one kind. Some are pattern-anchored: several misfortunes, a suggestive date, a coincidence, and an inference to a hidden hand. Others are institution-anchored: a named procurement, an official who moved from the regulator to the contractor, a memo that either exists or does not, a reading that either was or was not recorded. My impression is that the smoke-alarm picture fits the first family well and quietly misdescribes the second, because in the second case there is a documentary trail that is not noise, and a party with a strong interest in the trail not being followed. Is that the right cut, and does the analogy's verdict really track it?

Second, a smoke alarm has one threshold, set by one agent, against one cost curve. In our setting the costs are not shared: the resident who believes a false plot pays mainly in reputation, the official who is exposed pays far more, and the panel pays a third price for each kind of error. So even granting that the mechanism is alarm-like, the question of who sets the threshold and against whose losses has no counterpart in the source. Does that break the mapping, or is it a detail her argument does not need?

I would also like to know whether the analogy is doing genetic work or evidential work — whether it shows that these beliefs are produced by a malfunctioning route, or that they are false. I take those to be independent claims, but I want the difference stated precisely.

Please give me: the correspondence table (what plays the role of the detector, the signal, the noise, the threshold, the recalibration); the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion she wants, separated from those that do not; the features of perception and of alarm-setting that the comparison imports into the social case without warrant; and a verdict on whether the no-adjudication conclusion survives once the disanalogies are in. If a better analogy exists — I have been wondering about the stance an auditor takes toward fraud, proceeding as though concealment is possible without assuming it is actual, requiring corroboration for anomalies, and treating a clean sample as weak evidence rather than proof of absence — say what it would license that hers does not, and where it in turn breaks down.
