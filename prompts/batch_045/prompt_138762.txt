I got roped onto the conduct committee of my housing co-op last fall (28 units, we rotate, two-year terms, and I somehow ended up chairing it this spring) and I need something I can actually run, not theory. Here's the setup: members file written complaints against other members about shared-life stuff — noise, missed chore shifts, common-room hogging, dishes, the group chat, bikes in the hallway. We meet one evening a month, read the pile, and we either dismiss, mediate, or sanction (a formal letter, and in bad cases loss of common privileges). Any sanction needs a two-thirds vote plus a one-page written rationale that goes to both parties and gets read out at the next house meeting, and anyone can appeal to the whole co-op at the AGM. Nobody on the committee has any philosophy background, we've got about three hours and no budget, and whatever we write has to be readable by an angry neighbour at 10pm.

The thing I keep geting stuck on is hypocrisy, and specifically whether someone who does the same thing themselves has any standing to complain at all. We've been informally applying a "clean hands" thing — if you've done it too, your complaint gets tossed — and its obviously doing damage. It's become the first move for anyone who wants their case dropped, the members who are held to the highest standard most often lose their complaints, and last month we dismissed a noise complaint against a guy who has been making everyone's life miserable for a year because the complainant once threw a loud party. Then there are the versions I can't handle at all: half the time the person didn't do the same thing, they did a neighbouring thing (they hoard the common room but are complaining about dishes); or they did it two years ago and genuinely stopped; or they openly admit what they did versus being in total denial about it; or they're complaining on behalf of someone else who is completely clean; or the complaint is about how they personally were treated versus about how the house is running. And then there's us — the committee — which has ignored this exact category of behaviour when the person doing it was popular. I want to know whether that's even the same question or whether I'm confusing two different things.

So: please write me a decision procedure, in plain language, that my committee can run on a complaint in about fifteen minutes and that tells us when a complainant's own conduct defeats or weakens their complaint and when it's just noise we should ignore. It should say what order to ask things in, what counts as an adequate answer to each question, where we stop and dismiss, and where the procedure has to hand the decision to a person because it's a question about what kind of house we want to be rather than a matter of fact. Please be explicit about which distinctions it has to preserve — I suspect "they don't really believe the norm," "they have no right to hold you to it," and "the complaint is true and apt but they shouldn't be the one saying it" come apart, and I suspect "did the same thing" is the wrong test in at least three different ways, but you tell me which ones. Also tell me what to do when we can't tell whether two bits of conduct are the "same" norm violation, and whether a complainant can get standing back by owning what they did.

Then run it on three cases, and I want at least one to come out in a way I will hate, because I'd rather find that out now than at a house meeting. One: a member with four months of missed chore shifts files against a member who missed two. Two: a member who is a notorious common-room hoarder files a documented complaint about someone leaving dirty dishes in the sink for a week — different rule, same family of sloppiness. Three, the one I expect to dislike: a founding member who was repeatedly noisy in 2023 and was never sanctioned, because the committee that year let it slide, now files a strong and well-documented noise complaint against a newer member, and the target's entire defense is "look at what she did." For that one I want to know both whether her complaint stands and whether we as a committee have any business sanctioning anyone for noise given our own record, and if those are two separate questions please say so and say which one we should answer first. Finally, be honest about where the procedure is philosophy dressed up as a checklist, which steps are really us choosing what to value, and what it just can't settle.
