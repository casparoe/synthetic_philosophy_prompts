I have been turning over a question on my own, with no training in philosophy, and only later found out that it has a name: the special composition question. Given some things, when is there also a single further thing that they make up? I am not asking you to settle it. I want the specification first — the list of things any answer has to get right before it deserves to be taken seriously — because the only rule I have ever been able to believe in is a negative one, and I would like to know what test it has to pass.

That rule is this: some things compose something only if that something can be broken. Not merely scattered or divided up, but damaged: the arrangement itself can go wrong while the parts that make it up go on existing. I keep the collection database for a small natural history museum and restore mechanical clocks on weekends, so this is not idle for me. A clock that has been dropped is spoiled in a way its gears are not; a bench covered with the same gears is not spoiled at all, only disassembled. That difference feels like the whole thing to me, and I would rather find out now whether it is a genuine constraint or just a prejudice of the workshop. So please treat the purpose as fixed: I want a criterion good enough to apply — one that tells a working person when a bunch of pieces has become one item with one record, one condition report, and one line on the insurance schedule, and when it has merely become a set of pieces — not a verdict I could only repeat.

Here is what I would like from you.

First, the cases: which situations must an answer handle, and, for each, what exactly it is about the case that makes it a requirement rather than an intuition someone happens to have. I have my own candidates and I want them stress-tested and supplemented: an ordinary artifact; a living thing and a colony of living things; a scattered collection, such as a specimen whose parts sit in two different buildings; a temporary pattern like a whirlpool or a traffic jam; something whose parts get replaced over time, like a river or a clock I have rebuilt piece by piece; something that looks like two things occupying one place at one time, like a restored pot and the pot before restoration; a plainly borderline case like a heap of gravel or a cloud; and nested cases — a cell, an organ, an animal, a colony — where I especially want to know whether an answer is required to be "nesting-closed," or whether it may say that composition stops somewhere on the way up.

Second, the distinctions an answer must respect rather than collapse: composing something versus being nothing over and above the parts; existing versus doing any causal work of one's own, given that a composite's causal powers can look entirely inherited from its parts; composing at a time versus persisting through change; there being one thing here versus there being exactly one thing of a given kind here; a real answer versus a merely linguistic, conceptual, or interest-relative one; and the difference between the question of when things compose something and the question of what the parts of a thing are.

Third, the constraints from outside philosophy: an answer should not imply that the inventories of ordinary life, of chemistry, and of ecology all systematically misdescribe the world, and it should not make facts about composition unknowable in principle or a matter of brute accident — though I want you to tell me whether that last demand is a real desideratum or only a rationalist preference, and likewise for the demand that the answer not shift with what anyone happens to care about that afternoon.

Fourth, and this is the part I most want: take each requirement on your list and sort it into what any adequate answer genuinely owes us and what is only the preference of one camp. Is it a real requirement that the answer be nontrivial, falling between "nothing ever composes" and "everything always composes"? That it take the form of one general condition rather than a list of cases? That it contain no brute facts about which arrangements compose? That it be statable without reference to minds, interests, or language? That it vindicate most of what ordinary people and working scientists say? That it explain why coming apart seems to destroy things so much more readily than going together creates them?

Fifth, if some subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, say so plainly and show me the tension. My suspicion is that the demand for a single general condition, the demand for no brute facts, and the demand to draw the line somewhere between everything and nothing cannot all be met at once, which would make the list itself the interesting result. Tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates, and which one I should abandon, and what abandoning it costs me. Does my breakability rule survive your list, and if it fails, which requirement kills it?

If you have room at the end, one or two lines each on how the three standard families of answer — nothing ever composes, everything always composes, and composition only under some condition, whether a general one or brute — fare against the list; keep that brief, since the criteria are what I am paying for. Please write in American English, reason from the cases rather than from what anyone has written, and give me nothing in the way of reading lists, citations, or author names: I am working this out on my own in the evenings, and a table is welcome if it helps.
