I should say at the outset that I have no academic background in either philosophy or machine learning — I work in a hospital laboratory, and I've arrived at this on my own after a long-running argument in an online comment section — so please don't assume I know the technical vocabulary; where you need it, gloss it. I'd also be grateful if you could write your answer in British English.

The disagreement is about whether the words a large language model uses are *about* anything. Take its use of "red". Does that word latch onto the property, or is the model just shuffling symbols in a way that would work exactly as well if nothing in the world were red at all — the complaint I've seen called the symbol grounding problem?

My opponent's favourite move is an analogy. She says a language model is *exactly* like the famous thought experiment of the colour scientist raised in a black-and-white room — "Mary's room", I believe it's called — who has every book, every physics and neuroscience paper, every novel, every paint chart: everything ever written about colour — but has never seen a coloured thing. When she is finally let out and sees a red tomato, she learns something new. A model trained on all that text, my opponent says, is Mary before the door opens. Therefore its talk of red is not about red; no quantity of further text will ever fix that; and the only thing that would fix it is giving it eyes and hands, real causal contact with the world instead of more words.

I'd like an audit of this analogy, in the following order rather than all at once.

**1. The mapping.** Please set out the correspondences explicitly — a table is fine. What plays the part of the room, of the library, of the door, of the tomato, of the moment of learning? One mapping I can't get straight: Mary is doing two jobs at once, since she is both the one who lacks the experience and the one who *judges* that she has gained something. Who or what plays the judging role in the model's case, given that we cannot ask it?

**2. What the analogy is being made to carry.** I've since gathered that the thought experiment was originally aimed at a rather different target — whether a complete physical description of the world leaves anything out — and that this is contested. I can't work out which direction that cuts, and I suspect I have been conflating two separate complaints: "the model doesn't mean anything by its words" and "the model doesn't experience anything". Please separate them and say whether the Mary analogy supports either, both, or neither — and specifically whether it quietly imports a requirement (conscious experience) that the grounding complaint neither needs nor could be satisfied by.

**3. Where it breaks.** The candidates I can see: Mary has a body, a working visual system, decades of acting in the world, and a community who will hand her a tomato and say "red"; the model has none of these. More importantly, there may be no "outside the room" for the model at all, since text is not a deprivation it suffers but simply the medium it exists in. And Mary is a single creature with one continuous life, whereas the model is a programme that can be copied, run a million times in parallel, and is trained rather than taught. For each: does it actually bear on the conclusion my opponent wants, or is it incidental?

**4. A variation of my own, which I'd like you to take seriously and push back on if it's confused.** I tried to find the middle ground by imagining three grades of "contact with the world" for a Mary who is never let out:

&nbsp;&nbsp;(a) A bare cable is run into the room: a camera feed, unlabelled, no colour vocabulary attached to it, no teacher, and nothing she can do with the images. She still has her library, and now also a stream of pictures whose statistical patterns line up with the words in it.

&nbsp;&nbsp;(b) The same, except that she can act on the feed — turn a dial, sort the images into piles — and someone tells her when she has got it right.

&nbsp;&nbsp;(c) The same again, except that the camera is one she can point, and getting it right matters to her in the way things matter to someone with a life to lead.

My rough thought is that (a) is clearly not enough — it looks like just another text in a different notation — that (c) is clearly enough, and that the live question is whether (b) is enough; and that if (b) is enough, then a system trained to describe and sort images may already be in case (b) rather than case (a). Is that a fair way to cut it up? Does mere statistical correlation between two streams count as grounding when one of the streams is pixels? And if (b) *is* enough, has the Mary analogy overstated things by making "actually seeing a red thing" sound like the essential ingredient, when what is doing the real work is having to use the word successfully?

A diagnostic I invented for case (a), which may or may not be any good: suppose the library Mary was given had been written in a world where the words "red" and "blue" were swapped everywhere. Would Mary, the moment of (a), or the model, ever be in a position to notice the swap? And would noticing — or being unable to notice — actually tell us anything about grounding, or only about internal consistency?

**5. A rival analogy, and a test.** People also argue for the same conclusion from the observation that a dictionary defines every word in terms of other words, so that someone learning a language from a dictionary alone could never get off the ground — and that this, too, is the model's situation. Please tell me whether that analogy does the job better or worse than Mary's room, or whether both fail in the same place; and if there is a better analogy for what an ungrounded symbol system is like, give it to me. Finally, I'd like one concrete test you would accept — one that could in principle be run on something that is not a person — for whether a system's use of a colour word is grounded. I keep going in circles, because every test I can think of seems to be passable by something I still want to call a pattern-matcher.
