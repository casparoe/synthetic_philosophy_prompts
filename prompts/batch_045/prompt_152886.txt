I'm working through decision theory with two people from my philosophy cohort — one is a convinced causal decision theorist, the other holds some kind of functional, procedure-first view and thinks causal decision theory throws away the million in Newcomb's problem — and between us I have built an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept. I would like to know which step to reject, why that step looked fine, and what rejecting it costs me. Three cases first, stated exactly as I intend them.

(A) The mugging. Omega, whose predictions you have overwhelming reason to trust, predicts, before flipping a fair coin, whether you would hand over $100 if it asked you to. It then flips. Heads: it asks you for the $100 and, once you pay, hands you $10,000 (it hands you nothing if you refuse). Tails: it asks you for the $100 and hands you nothing either way. The coin lands tails. Omega shows you the coin, tells you the prediction is now fixed and beyond your influence, and asks for the $100 with nothing in return. Evaluated as a policy in advance, paying when asked is worth roughly $4,900 against $0 for refusing; evaluated by what your handing over the money now causes, it is worth minus $100.

(B) The blackmail. A letter arrives from a stranger whose predictions you have equally overwhelming reason to trust. The letter states its own sending condition: it was sent if and only if exactly one of the following holds — (i) you have condition C, (ii) you will send the stranger $1,000 after reading. C is symptomless, affects about one person in two hundred, kills within two years, and is causally unaffected by anything you can do after reading; the $1,000 buys nothing and the stranger does not claim otherwise; and being told you have C would not change anything you would subsequently do, so the information has no further value to you.

(C) The deposit case, which I invented as a control. Omega predicts whether you would pay $100 on reading a certain letter. If and only if it predicts that you would refuse, it deposits $10,000 in your account before sending anything. It then sends you the letter if and only if exactly one of the following holds — (i) the deposit was made, (ii) you will pay the $100. The letter says this, demands the $100, and you cannot check your balance until tomorrow. Here refusing is plainly correct: a refuser gets the deposit and the letter; a payer gets the letter and loses $100.

My argument:

1. In (A) you should pay. That is the claim I most want to keep.
2. What (1) shows is that the object of assessment is not the act-token but the output of a decision procedure. You ask what the world would be like on the supposition that your procedure produced this output, and you count everything that subjunctively depends on that output — an earlier prediction by a perfect predictor, and whatever lies causally downstream of that prediction — since that is exactly what puts (A)'s $10,000 within reach.
3. Dependence principle: if some fact you care about would have been different had your procedure output differently, then that fact is decision-relevant in the same way regardless of whether the dependence runs through a prediction made minutes ago or before you were born, regardless of whether the branch in which it pays off is one you could ever have observed, and regardless of whether what depends on your output is a transfer of money, as in (A), or a state of the world, such as your health.
4. In (B): had your procedure output pay, the prediction would have been pay, and the letter — which did arrive — would have been sent only if you lack C. Had it output refuse, the letter would have been sent only if you have C. So whether you have C is counterfactually dependent on your procedure's output in the sense of (3): on one output you are healthy, on the other you are dying.
5. By (3), pay. Healthy and $1,000 poorer beats dying, and the dependence is of the same kind that makes paying right in (A).
6. And (3) is confirmed rather than undermined by (C), which has the identical surface form — a letter sent iff exactly one of: something you value, your paying — and in which (3) yields the answer everyone wants, namely refuse and collect the $10,000. So this is not simply the old evidential habit of managing the news in new vocabulary; in (C) the news and the money really do track each other.
7. But (5) is unacceptable. The money does nothing, C is already fixed, and the payer-policy loses $1,000 with near-certainty while the refuser-policy loses nothing; in the version I actually care about the predictor has been dead for thirty years and the letter is delivered by her executors. The same principle also appears to tell me not to smoke in the smoking-lesion case, on the ground that had my procedure output smoke I would probably carry the lesion, which is supposed to be the textbook example of a mistake. My causal-decision-theory friend adds that in (B) the ex ante comparison condemns paying, but I do not see why that comparison should be decisive in (B) and yet be exactly what I appeal to in (A) after the coin has already landed tails.
8. So one of (1) through (6) must go, or (3) needs a restriction I cannot see how to state.

Which step do you reject, and why did it look all right when I wrote it down? Please price the alternatives: can I still pay in (A); can I still explain why (C) turns out the way it does; and is what remains anything more than causal decision theory with a procedure-shaped hat on? If the fix is to stop conditioning the counterfactual on my own evidence, tell me why that is not just ignoring what I have learned. And if the fix is a restriction on (3), state it precisely enough to apply it to this variant: my doctor has already told me, on impeccable evidence, that I do not have C, and the letter arrives anyway — where (4) now seems to say that paying is the only output compatible with the letter's having been sent, i.e. that I should hand over $1,000 so that a past event makes sense.
