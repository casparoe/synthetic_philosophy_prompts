I'm a philosophy professor, and I'm on a university committee deciding whether to cancel tomorrow's classes because of a possible snowstorm. The weather service won't give us a single number; they say the chance of enough snow is somewhere between 30% and 60%. One of my colleagues says this is useless and we should just pick the midpoint, 45%, and plug it into our decision like adults. I definately feel like the range is doing real work: our evidence is messy and doesn't determine an exact number. Here's the weird part: whenever we get an update—say, a new satellite image—the range seems to get wider, not narrower, whichever way the image goes (say 25% to 70%). My colleague says that's absurd: learning something should never make our uncertainty mushier, so we should go back to demanding exact probabilities and forget ranges. I'm not sure he's right, but I cant quite put my finger on why. Is he right? Should I just use the midpoint? And what should I make of the fact that the range widens no matter what we learn?
