I work in machine learning research, mostly on generalization and interpretability, and I have been reading about the rule-following discussion that grows out of Wittgenstein's later work — the skeptical argument that nothing about a speaker determines which rule they meant, given that any finite run of past examples is compatible with endlessly many rules, and the reading of it developed in Kripke's 1982 book on rules and private language, with the invented predicate quus that coincides with addition below some threshold and returns a fixed constant above it, and with the skeptical solution that replaces truth conditions with assertibility conditions and leans on agreement within a community. I can follow expositions of this material well enough, but I keep being unable to tell whether it is a problem my own field already has a name for, a problem my field's vocabulary quietly changes into something easier, or a problem my vocabulary cannot formulate at all.

Here is what I want translated, one by one: (i) the skeptical argument that no fact about me settles whether I meant addition or quaddition; (ii) the familiar objection to the dispositional reply — that my dispositions are finite, and that what I am disposed to answer cannot itself make my actual answer wrong, so dispositions cannot supply the normativity of meaning; (iii) the skeptical solution in terms of assertibility conditions and community agreement. Render each as precisely as you can into the vocabulary I actually use: hypothesis class, training distribution, learner selected by an optimizer, held-out and out-of-distribution evaluation, identifiability and underdetermination of a target function by finitely many samples, no-free-lunch and inductive bias, a loss function whose labels come from outside the learner, and distribution shift at deployment time. To pin the translation down, use this setup: a learner is trained by empirical risk minimization on samples of a target function drawn from a distribution whose support covers only a small region of the input space, reaches zero error in distribution, and then diverges from the target once inputs leave that region. Call the gap between the functions the training data is consistent with and the function the learner will actually apply off distribution the compliance gap. In that setup, is the claim that the learner means addition rather than quaddition anything over and above a claim about which values it would output on the counterfactual inputs, and if it is something more, what in my vocabulary would have to carry the extra weight? I have also been worrying at what I call the trainer's regress: the correction signal that is supposed to fix what the learner means is itself just more behavior emitted by another system whose outputs are, from the learner's point of view, unlabeled, so the appeal to a community of correctors seems to postpone rather than answer the question — and I would like to know whether that is the same regress the philosophical discussion is worried about or a different one wearing similar clothes.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which of the three claims above translate cleanly, which translate only by being bent, and which have no expression in the ML vocabulary at all? In particular: does the notion of the correct answer have a real counterpart, given that in my framework error is defined against an external label-generating process — and does that dissolve the normative element of the paradox, or merely change the subject from what the learner means to how a third party scores it? Is community agreement, once translated, just the deployment distribution plus a stipulation about who counts as a corrector, and if so is that an answer or a restatement? Does the private-language strand of the discussion have any grip on the case of a learner trained only on its own outputs with no external labels, where there is no one to disagree with it? And how should I read whatever is left over: does the residue show that my vocabulary lacks the intentional and normative notions the argument needs, or does it show that the skeptical claim was thinner than it looks — that the no-fact-of-the-matter conclusion is underdetermination of theory by data with a first-personal flourish attached, so that what presents itself as a metaphysical conclusion is really only epistemic? Finally, I have a nagging suspicion that interpretability research, when it asks which algorithm a trained network implements, is the same problem under a new name; tell me whether you think that is right, or whether there is a principled difference between asking which rule a system implements and asking which rule I meant.
