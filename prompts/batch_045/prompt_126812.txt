I work on the evaluation and post-training of a forecasting system built on top of a language model, and I have been leaning hard on an analogy that a colleague has started to resist. I would like a proper audit of it rather than a verdict.

The analogy, as I use it. An incoherent set of degrees of belief is like a bookmaker who posts a set of odds against which a clever client can place a combination of bets that loses money no matter what happens. Incoherence is not merely unattractive; it is exploitable, and the existence of the exploiting combination is supposed to be the reason coherence is a requirement of rationality rather than a stylistic preference. In my setting the incoherence I care about is not the textbook kind within a single reported distribution but across elicitations: the system returns one number for rain when asked directly and a different, non-complementary number for no rain when asked on its own; it returns numbers for the cells of a three-way partition that do not sum to the number it returns for the disjunction of two of them; and it shifts all of these depending on whether the question arrives with a menu of alternatives attached, with the base rate stated first, or with a different ordering of the same evidence.

What I am using the analogy to license. Three things, in ascending order of importance to me. First, that this kind of cross-framing inconsistency is a defect in its own right and not merely a symptom of inaccuracy, so that it deserves to be measured and reported separately from calibration and resolution. Second, that it is worth spending training signal on directly — a coherence term in the objective, applied to outputs sampled across paraphrases and partition choices — even in the regime where enforcing it makes the system no more accurate on our benchmarks and possibly slightly less. Third, and this is the one my funders care about: a downstream user or an adversary who can choose the framing, choose the stakes, and query the system cheaply and repeatedly can extract a guaranteed loss from the party who acts on those numbers, which makes incoherence a security property of the deployed system and not an aesthetic one.

The resistance. My colleague makes three points, and I cannot tell how much of my case each one touches. The model is not an agent: it has no money, no preferences, and no disposition to accept either side of a bet at the rate it quotes, so the thing the Ramsey–de Finetti style argument is about — a pattern in what a single agent is willing to do — does not obviously have a counterpart here. The loss lands on the operator, not on the thing whose numbers are incoherent, so even a successful book does not show that the incoherent party is being irrational in the way the original argument shows. And the loss is denominated in whatever the operator is actually trading, while the quantity we optimize is a strictly proper score; the classical argument gets its force from assumptions about stakes being scalable and about the value of money to the agent being linear across the relevant range, neither of which has a clean image in a system whose loss is a benchmark number.

What I want from you.

Lay the mapping out explicitly, item by item, as a table of correspondences: each element of the betting set-up and its counterpart in my system, with an honest flag where the counterpart is missing or has to be constructed by hand. I suspect the two hardest items are the agent's standing disposition to take either side of a bet at the quoted rate, and the settlement currency whose value to the agent is additive across the separate bets; tell me if I have picked the wrong problem cases.

Then separate the disanalogies that matter from the ones that do not, and do this separately for each of my three conclusions. Some breaks will sink the security argument while leaving the training-objective argument untouched, or the reverse; I want that said per-conclusion rather than globally.

Take the adversarial version seriously and stress it. A real adversary does not know the system's quotients for free but has to pay to elicit them; can query only finitely many times; faces an operator who can decline trades at framings that look strange, or cap stakes, or route the question through a fixed template; and gets stochastic rather than deterministic responses. Under those restrictions, does guaranteed loss survive as guarantee, or does it degrade into positive expected loss against a repeated-query strategy? What is the right analogue of a Dutch book when the counterparty's answers are sampled from a distribution rather than chosen — is there a coherent notion of a population-level or distributional sure loss, and does it still support the word guaranteed?

Address the diachronic half too. We also care whether the system updates by conditioning when new evidence is appended to its context, and I am told the updating versions of these arguments carry extra assumptions — about the agent anticipating the update, about memory, about there being no free information in the interim — that the synchronic version does not. Say what those are and whether my in-context case has them.

Finally, two things I care more about than the verdict on the analogy. What does the betting picture quietly import that my setting lacks — I have in mind that it makes exploitability look like the whole cost of incoherence, so that an incoherent system nobody ever trades against comes out fine, and that it makes coherence look like a property of a single belief state rather than of a reporting protocol. And if the betting picture is doing no work for me that an accuracy-based, strictly-proper-scoring-rule argument would not do better, say that plainly and tell me what I should be arguing instead of what I have been arguing. If there is a better analogy available — a forecaster paid by score, an advisor whose reports are used by a principal, an instrument that is calibrated rather than a bookmaker who is exploited — sketch it and say which of my three conclusions it carries and which it drops.
