I sit on the board of a regional land trust, and I have been asked to draft the written opinion the board will vote on at its meeting four weeks from now. I would like you to draft that opinion for me.

The case is this. A donor has left us a restricted bequest of approximately $4.3 million to buy and restore a 1,140-acre block of retired farmland adjoining an existing national wildlife refuge. The land is currently tilled row crop under a lease that expires next year; the plan our staff has put forward is to buy the tract, breach the drainage tiles, and re-flood roughly 880 acres as shallow marsh and wet meadow, with the remainder held in perennial grass, plus a small endowment for long-term stewardship. The gift is restricted in its terms to the permanent protection and restoration of native habitat for the benefit of fish and wildlife. The donor's instructions also require that the restored land be managed passively: no supplemental feeding, no culling, no predator or pest control, and no manipulation of water levels except to maintain the target plant communities. A conservation easement reflecting those instructions would be recorded at closing.

The board is split almost evenly, and the disagreement has more substance than I expected.

Our staff ecologist argues that the comparison on offer is not marsh versus nothing, but marsh versus tilled field. Drained cropland already carries its own animal population — voles, ground-nesting birds, deer, an enormous invertebrate biomass — and its yield enters a food system with a welfare footprint of its own. She also notes that restoration delivers flood attenuation, water-quality improvements, carbon retention in the peat, and habitat for listed species, and that the alternative is not that the money goes to some better cause: if we decline a restricted gift, it passes to the residuary beneficiary and the land most likely goes either back into row crop or into a twenty-year solar-and-grazing lease a developer has already offered the owners, which would keep the acreage in grass and pay nothing to us. Finally, she says, we have no way to aggregate welfare across an ecosystem, and declining to act because of an unmeasurable total is itself an action with very large welfare consequences taken on even thinner evidence.

A second member, a retired economist, has circulated a dissent. Her claim is that the relevant comparison is not only between two land uses but between animals that would exist and animals that would not. Her reading of our own projections is that reflooding 880 acres would add breeding populations on the order of tens of thousands of amphibians and waterbirds and many times that in fish, with the overwhelming majority of the young dying within weeks by predation, starvation, disease, or winter kill, and with periodic botulism die-offs in the waterbird colony. Her conclusion is that we would be creating, year after year, a very large quantity of short lives that end badly, and that doing so in the name of benefiting wildlife is confused: the animals in question do not yet exist, so we are not benefiting them by bringing them into being unless their lives are worth living, which is exactly what is in question. She proposes a standing rule — the trust should not fund projects that increase expected wild-animal suffering, with the burden of proof on the project's proponents — and she applies it using a rough accounting that weights each animal-week by a discount for what she calls phenomenal simplicity, on the grounds that a tadpole's or a nestling's experience is simpler and matters less than an adult's, and that counts only increments above the cropland baseline.

A third member accepts much of this but says the honest conclusion, if she is right, is not that we should refrain from restoring this tract but that we should be retiring habitat elsewhere, and that a rule with that implication cannot be stated publicly by an organization that depends on donors and on its standing as a conservation charity. A fourth has proposed a compromise: restore a smaller marsh footprint, and commit a fixed share of the bequest's investment income for ten years to a monitoring program that measures welfare-relevant outcomes directly — survivorship, body condition, disease events — and to research on humane fertility control for overabundant species, on the theory that whether restoration helps or harms is a question our sector is about to spend a great deal of money acting on elsewhere, and information now has value beyond this tract.

What I need is a memo I can circulate ahead of the vote. It should:

1. Give a concrete recommendation — accept the bequest and restore as planned, accept and restore in modified form, seek to have the gift's terms modified by a court or by the donor's family, or decline — with a timeline for whatever steps follow.

2. Take the economist's argument at its strongest and say plainly what would follow if she is right, rather than retreating to the claim that welfare cannot be measured.

3. Address how the restriction to the benefit of fish and wildlife should be read, given that it may mean benefit to species and populations or benefit to individual animals, and say whether the passive-management requirement is a constraint we have discovered or a substantive moral choice we would be making.

4. Recommend whether the trust should adopt a standing welfare screen for future projects and, if not that, what weaker version — if any — it should adopt.

5. Deal with the institutional consideration honestly, without allowing donor opinion or public defensibility to settle the question on its own.

6. State what evidence or argument would change the recommendation, and what we should do while we lack it.

Please argue from the considerations themselves; I don't need citations or reading suggestions. The memo should run roughly 900 to 1,100 words and be written so that a board member who disagrees with its conclusion can still see that their case was understood.
