Me and a mate got into a three-hour argument last night and I need something better than either of us managed. He's read a bit of philosophy and kept saying that picking forecasting models is just the problem of induction in a lab coat: the only reason we trust the model that did well last season is that it did well last season, and there's no non-circular way to justify that, so we might as well pick the winner and stop pretending. I kept saying the no-free-lunch results back him up in a nastier way: average over every possible problem and every method does exactly the same, so a benchmark win can't be evidence that the method is better in general, only that it happens to match the particular distribution of problems the benchmark sampled. Then we realised neither of us could say what should actually happen on Monday morning, and we left it there.

Context: I'm one of three people on the forecasting review panel at a national public health agency. Twice a year we pick which submitted model becomes the official seasonal forecast for respiratory disease — the number that triggers stockpile orders, staffing plans and the briefing to ministers. Two weeks per round, three of us (me, an epidemiologist, and one statistician who is part-time and shared with another team), and we have to produce a one-page justification that a senior civil servant signs. Submissions come with a shared track record: last three seasons, scored at roughly forty locations, but the scoring set was proposed by the submitting teams themselves and a lot of the tuning happened after they'd already seen the outcomes.

What I want is a decision procedure we could actually run on a submission, in the order we'd run it, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step:

- What is the method's inductive bias, stated in one sentence — what does it assume about the world that its competitors don't? If the team can't say, that's a stop.
- Does the benchmark actually discriminate that bias? My idea is a mirror test: ask for the same model with that assumption flipped (smoothness turned into jumpiness, the seasonal trend reversed, the contact structure randomised) and see whether the mirror does worse. If the mirror does about as well, the win isn't coming from the bias we're being asked to bet on. Is that test sound as I've put it, and is there a cheaper version we can run in two weeks without asking the submitters to build another model?
- Was the record earned prospectively — forecasts lodged before outcomes were known, on units the panel chose rather than the team — and how do we score a submission that is part prospective and part not?
- Is the structure the method exploits still there this season? New dominant strain, changed reporting lag, moved school terms, behaviour after the last bad season. What counts as a sufficient answer to the world hasn't drifted?
- Selection effects: with around forty submissions the top score is inflated just by being the maximum. How do we compare a submission against the whole pool rather than against the winner, and how large a gap has to be before it means anything?
- Where does the procedure stop with a straight no, and where does it hand the question back to us as a values call — e.g. under-predicting a severe season being far worse than over-predicting?

Then run it on three cases: (a) Meridian, a mechanistic compartmental model, won the last two seasons, submitted by the team that also runs the surveillance system, but only ever scored at locations they chose, and it's the model the minister already likes; (b) Halcyon, a new machine-learning ensemble that topped the table last season, with a margin that comes almost entirely from the fortnight around the holidays when reporting collapses, and it was retuned on the same seasons it was scored on; (c) the boring baseline, last season's curve shifted by the average trend, never wins, never fails badly, and nobody can state what it assumes. I want at least one answer I won't like. I suspect it will tell us to publish a pooled spread instead of picking a winner, which will go down terribly with the people who sign off.

Also, as you go: separate what is actually settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and flag it when you're leaning on a contested reading — I don't want something like real-world problems being structured enough to escape the no-free-lunch result, or there being a pragmatic vindication of induction, presented as the consensus. And say plainly what the procedure can't do for us.
