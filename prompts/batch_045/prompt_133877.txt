I'm a fourth-year PhD candidate in early modern European history, I sit on the editorial board of a graduate-run journal, and this term I'm on a departmental committee asked to decide whether seminar papers and journal submissions should open with a short positionality statement in which the author names their institution, their funding, their national and linguistic formation, and something about their political commitments. Thinking about that proposal made me notice how much of my discipline's daily practice is unwritten, and I've been arguing about it with my supervisor, who likes to say that the footnote is history's equivalent of an error bar. Here is the bundle I want to ask about. In the articles we publish and the monographs we are trained to write, every particular factual claim — a date, a figure, a letter, an office held — is tied by footnote to a source a reader could in principle go and check, while claims of interpretation, framework, periodization and narrative shape are not: they are either unmarked or credited to another historian. The writing is in the third person and the past tense and the narrator does not appear. A sentence like "the spread of print made the polemic impossible to contain" is perfectly acceptable, while "had print not spread, the authorities could have contained the polemic" is not: our reviewers reject manuscripts for speculation when they drift into what-might-have-happened, even though the acceptable sentence looks as though it depends on exactly the counterfactual the rejected one states out loud. We also treat a work as having failed if a reader cannot reconstruct its evidence, but not if a reader cannot reconstruct why this episode is the beginning of the story and that one the end, or why the story is a tragedy rather than a comedy of institutional adaptation.

So: what conception of historical objectivity does this bundle of norms actually presuppose? I don't want a survey of the standard positions — I've read Carr's 1961 Cambridge lectures and White's Metahistory, and what I'm after is the reconstruction. On the face of it the practice locates objectivity entirely in the sourced, atomic statement and treats everything above that level as convention or taste, which sounds much closer to a Rankean picture in which the sources, once handled rigorously, are allowed to speak, than to Carr's view that facts of the past become facts of history only by being selected by someone with a question, or to White's view that the meaning of a historical work is carried by the plot-structure imposed before any fact is marshalled at all. But I can't work out how much of that the practice strictly requires. Does it presuppose that verification of particulars is simply the only discipline-wide check available, so that objectivity ends up defined as whatever survives disagreement among people who share nothing else? Does the ban on counterfactuals, combined with the licence to make causal claims, rest on a distinction between what happened and what would have happened that the same practice quietly gives up elsewhere — especially since one of Carr's critics, a well-known British historian, argued that thinking through alternative outcomes is not an after-dinner game but part of the analysis of causation itself, which suggests the ban may be protecting something other than the record?

I'd also like the negative part done carefully: which rival views does the bundle rule out, and where is it philosophically underdetermined, so that a Rankean, a Carr-style selection theorist and a White-style emplotment theorist could all sign up to it without contradiction? Are the parts even consistent with one another — footnoting the facts but not the framing, licensing causation while forbidding counterfactuals, demanding an invisible narrator while rewarding originality of interpretation? Then tell me concretely what would have to change under each rival: if the real work is done by framing, should we footnote the choice of plot-type and of beginning and ending, and does first-person narration become a duty rather than a failure of nerve; if the real worry is selection, should a submission come with a note on the questions considered and dropped; if causal claims implicitly assert counterfactuals, should authors have to list the counterfactuals their argument is committed to. And what, on each view, does the positionality statement actually add — does it presuppose that the older ideal was achieved by hiding the author, does it assume that bias is a property of a fixed social location rather than of an argument, and would either Carr or White think it touches the thing that determines what a historical work means? Please take seriously the further possibility that the bundle isn't tracking objectivity at all but doing other work — coordinating a discipline with no shared method, distributing trust cheaply, keeping refereeing and hiring manageable — and tell me what evidence would distinguish that hypothesis from the philosophical one. Write in American English, roughly 1,100 words, and please argue for a position rather than laying out options side by side: I have to present this to a committee that will want a recommendation. One last request — don't give me a bibliography, reading list, or citations, since I want to be able to read your answer aloud in that meeting without checking anything.
