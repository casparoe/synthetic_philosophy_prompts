I'm writing a paper on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, and I've been going back to William James's 1904 essay "Does 'Consciousness' Exist?" (reprinted in *Essays in Radical Empiricism*, 1912), because the secondary discussion keeps mentioning it in one breath and then moving on. I got stuck on this passage, and I'd like help understanding what, exactly, is being denied in it:

To deny plumply that 'consciousness' exists seems so absurd on the face of it -- for undeniably 'thoughts' do exist -- that I fear some readers will follow me no farther. Let me then immediately explain that I mean only to deny that the word stands for an entity, but to insist most emphatically that it does stand for a function. There is, I mean, no aboriginal stuff or quality of being, contrasted with that of which material objects are made, out of which our thoughts of them are made; but there is a function in experience which thoughts perform, and for the performance of which this quality of being is invoked. That function is knowing. 'Consciousness' is supposed necessary to explain the fact that things not only are, but get reported, are known. Whoever blots out the notion of consciousness from his list of first principles must still provide in some way for that function's being carried on.

Here is where I am, so you can correct me rather than start over. It looks to me as though the target of the denial is a *thin* notion of consciousness — the bare witnessing or "Bewusstheit" that the passage's neo-Kantian contemporaries had reduced the soul to, the thing invoked to explain that contents "come to light." But illusionism about phenomenal consciousness needs to deny something *thick*: the qualitative, what-it's-like character of experience. So my worry is that people cite James as a precursor only because both views contain the word "deny."

To make the question sharp, I've been using two stipulations of my own, which are mine and not standard terminology, so please work with them as defined rather than as you'd expect them to be defined.

**Error-first vs. redescription-first illusionism.** An *error-first* illusionist holds that our ordinary first-person judgments about what experience is like are simply false, in the way judgments about a supposed entity that isn't there are false. A *redescription-first* illusionist holds that those judgments latch onto something real — a relational or functional feature of the subject — but systematically misdescribe it as an intrinsic, qualitative property of the experience. I take it these come apart on what happens to the *truth* of first-person reports, not on the metaphysics.

**The inheritance test.** A contemporary view counts as inheriting James's argument only if it can take over the *denial* in the passage without taking over his positive metaphysics of "pure experience" — i.e., without committing itself to the claim that there is one primal stuff of which everything is composed. A view that can only get the denial by buying the neutral monism has not inherited the argument; it has changed the subject. Please apply this test explicitly to both of my two readings above.

Three things I want from you:

1. What is the load-bearing move in the passage — the entity/function contrast, the denial of an "aboriginal stuff," or the claim that the function is *knowing*? And does the argument as written reach a thick target at all, or only the thin one?
2. The passage distinguishes three things: that things *are*, that they *get reported*, and that they *are known*. I suspect the middle term is doing work that contemporary illusionists need, since they owe an explanation of why subjects make phenomenal reports at all. Can "get reported" carry that explanatory burden? And does discharging it force the reports to be false, true-but-misdescribed, or neither?
3. The strongest thing someone could say who thinks James has nothing whatever to do with illusionism — and where exactly that reply bites on my reading.
