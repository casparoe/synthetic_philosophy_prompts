I host a fortnightly podcast on the philosophy of cognitive science; most listeners are graduate students and postdocs in psychology, AI and philosophy, but about a third are interested laypeople who have read one popular book on evolution and minds. Next week's episode is a forty-five-minute interview with Daniel Dennett that I recorded a couple of years ago and am only now broadcasting, built around his four-fold taxonomy of minds — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian and Gregorian creatures — and the image of stacked generate-and-test cycles that goes with it. I already have the easy episode: I can get him to walk through the levels and to recount the digger-wasp story and the one about hypotheses dying in your stead. What I don't have is a sequence of questions that makes him work. So please approach this as both a philosopher of mind and a seasoned long-form interviewer. Before you write the questions, spend a short paragraph or two brainstorming where his framing is most exposed: where the boundaries between the levels do argumentative work without being defended, and where his characteristic moves — refusing necessary and sufficient conditions, appealing to the intentional stance, diagnosing an objection as covert Cartesianism, reclassifying a puzzle as an empirical research programme rather than an objection — would let him off the hook.

Then give me eight to ten questions, ordered as you would sequence them across the forty-five minutes, each with (a) a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give, and (b) a brief note on what would count as his actually answering rather than restating the view. I want at least one question on each of the following, and I want them to be questions, not invitations to lecture: where the joints between the levels really fall, and whether the Baldwin effect — selection for plasticity itself — collapses the Darwinian/Skinnerian boundary rather than sitting neatly above it; whether the Popperian level is a claim about a mechanism or about a behavioural profile, and what could distinguish a Popperian creature observationally from a very flexible Skinnerian one (I fully expect the answer that there is no bright line and that he doesn't do necessary and sufficient conditions, so build me the question that survives that answer); whether the Gregorian level marks a difference in kind of mind or merely a difference in the environment a mind is embedded in — if the thinking tools are imported rather than innate, is the mind partly outside the skull, and what does he say about the enculturated apes, or about an invented case of mine, a species whose brains can be substantially reconfigured by the vocalisations of conspecifics but which accumulates nothing across generations; what the taxonomy says about systems that have never been alive, since a large language model is trained on the accumulated output of Gregorian minds, deploys words at scale, and can generate and discard candidate answers without acting in the world — is it Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian or a fifth thing, and is the real reason for saying none of the above that nothing is at stake for it; and whether the taxonomy does any ethical work at all, since I'd like one question here that he cannot answer by saying that everything is a matter of degree.

Finally, one of my listeners wrote in to say that the digger-wasp anecdote which opens the whole discussion has a shakier empirical basis than textbooks suggest, and I'd like to raise it without the episode turning into a fact-checking digression: give me a question that uses it to test whether the Darwinian-creature level does real explanatory work or merely labels behaviour that isn't flexible. Keep each question under about forty words, don't open any of them with "so" or by asking him to define his terms, and don't add any suggested reading or citations — I've read the book and I only want questions. Please write the questions themselves in British English.
