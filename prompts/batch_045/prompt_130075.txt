I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and in the autumn of 2016 I was on the record telling readers that the election-forecasting models were being unfairly maligned and that a roughly one-in-three chance for the eventual winner was not a small number. I then watched that number come in, and I am now trying to write something better than the column everyone in my trade wrote afterwards, the one explaining that unlikely events happen sometimes and that the models were vindicated. So I want to run the episode through the philosophy of probability properly rather than gesturally. Here is the episode as I will stipulate it, and please do not fuss over my figures, which are approximate: in the weeks before the vote the public forecasting models gave the eventual winner somewhere between about thirty percent and about two percent depending on the shop; they were largely drawing on the same state polling averages; Clinton won the national popular vote by a couple of points; the national polling error turned out to be within its historical range while the state-level errors in a handful of Rust Belt states were correlated and all ran the same direction; and the public reaction afterwards treated the models as having made a prediction and as having been refuted by the result.

What I want from you is a disciplined mapping. Take the three standard interpretations in turn — frequentism, propensity, and subjectivism in its Bayesian form — and tell me, for each, what the forecaster was actually asserting when she published a number, what her critics were asserting when they said she had been wrong, and which features of the episode the interpretation handles cleanly and which it cannot account for at all. I can see the first move for each of them, so go past it. The frequentist has to name a sequence or a reference class for an event that happens once, and saying that it is a class of elections like this one is doing more work than it appears to; the propensity theorist has to say what the chancy setup is supposed to be for a causally messy human event whose outcome, at the level of individual voters, is in some sense settled before anyone forecasts it; the subjectivist gets a clean story about what the number meant and a much harder story about why the whole exercise is not unfalsifiable, since if the number was only her credence and the event happens once, it is unclear what she learned when the long shot came in or what she would have learned if it had not. Two complications I keep circling. The first is what I have started calling the reflexivity problem: these numbers were published, widely read, and plausibly fed back into the thing they were about, through donations, campaign resource allocation, and complacent or panicked turnout. If the probability of the event depends on the publication of the probability, does that damage one interpretation more than the others, or does it show that all three tacitly assume a fixed target and owe us an account of what happens when the target moves in response to being measured? The second is the apparent consensus: the models looked like independent confirmation of one another but were largely the same data in different clothes, and the errors that sank them were correlated across states rather than independent. For each interpretation, was the agreement of the models evidence at all, and how would each have a forecaster reason about a correlated-error tail that her model was too thin on?

Then turn it around and tell me what the episode does to the interpretations themselves. My working thought is that the live question is not which interpretation is true but which one can underwrite what I call the audit requirement: whatever probability means, a forecaster who publishes numbers is answerable after the fact, in public, in a way that no single outcome can settle, and I want to know which interpretation gives her the resources to be answerable and which quietly collapses into the claim that she said what she believed. Does 2016 count as evidence for or against any of the three, or is it a case that all three absorb without cost, which would itself be worth saying? And given your diagnosis, what would count as a real test — a large track record of scored forecasts, a reference class fixed in advance, some fact about how the models have calibrated since? Where one of the interpretations simply has nothing to say about the practice of public forecasting, say so rather than stretching it to speak.
