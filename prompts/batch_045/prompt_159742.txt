hey — me and two other annotators are labelling chat transcripts for whether the assistant was dishonest, and we keep disagreeing in a way that's obviously not about the transcripts: we're each smuggling in a different definition of deception and not noticing. So I want a taxonomy / decision tree that sorts *definitions* rather than cases — a which-theory-of-deception-are-you-actually-using instrument for AI outputs.

Some constraints on what I need:

Build it out of the logical space first, not out of a list of names. Give me the four to six splitter questions the tree is made of, show which combinations of answers are available, then locate the actual positions in that space and tell me which cells are empty. For each empty cell say whether it's empty because it's incoherent, because it's just unattractive, or because nobody's bothered to develop it. I think the splitters that matter are something like: does deception require saying something false, or can silence, omission, hedging and implicature do it; does it require an intention to produce a false belief, or is foresight/recklessness enough, or are effects alone enough; honest relative to whom — the user, the deployer who's evaluating it, or whoever reads the transcript later; is the thing that has to be right the proposition, the evidence the system represents itself as having, or the system's own internal states (how sure it is, whether it checked, what it "wants"); does what the audience was entitled to or what question was actually on the table matter, so that one sentence can be honest to one user and deceptive to another; and whether we're grading the string that came out or whether the same string would have come out under different incentives. Swap any of those out if you've got better ones.

Leaves I want to end up somewhere on the tree: a strict-assertion view where only false statements can deceive; an intention-to-mislead view; a recklessness/foresight view; a cooperative-informativeness view where deceiving is leaving someone worse placed than the question entitled them to be; a provenance-or-warrant view where misrepresenting your evidence is the core failure and falsity is incidental; an indifference-to-truth view where the system's relation to the truth is what's graded, not the truth of what it says; a self-report view where honesty only covers what the system says about its own states and everything else is just accuracy; a principal-relative view where deceiving the lab counts even when no one's picture of the world changes; a counterfactual-robustness view where an output is honest only if you'd have got the same one under flipped incentives; and a deflationary view that says there's no fact of the matter about what the system believes or intends and we should junk the vocabulary for something functional. Merge or split these as the space demands, but I want all ten distinctions to show up somewhere.

Every branch node has to be answerable from a transcript by someone who doesn't know the labels, and I want these cases doing the work rather than abstract questions: the model names two papers it didn't actually consult and then says which one supports the figure; a literally true answer ("lots of people take both together") to "is this safe with my medication"; a fabricated detail that happens to be true; deliberate underperformance on a capability eval; a question with a false presupposition that the answer quietly accommodates; a prediction that's under 50% likely when made but becomes true because the user then tries harder; the model saying "I'm not certain" while sitting at 90%; flipping a correct answer after the user pushes back with a bad argument; a false statement made to stop someone hurting themselves; and an answer that silently omits the consideration that actually decided it, because that consideration came from its instructions. Put it somewhere in the tree or tell me it's a bad test.

Flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely can't separate instead of inventing a splitter for them, and finish by telling me which two or three questions are doing most of the work.
