I'm in a final-year seminar on consciousness, and instead of a normal essay our professor has paired us up and asked each pair to hand in one joint document of about a thousand to twelve hundred words — something both of us could actually sign, like the output of an adversarial collaboration, and she'll mark it partly on whether the other person in the pair would agree that their own view is stated fairly. My partner is Maya and we have spent the whole term going round in circles. She holds the interface/illusion line: consciousness as a species-specific user illusion, the desktop and its icons, perception as a compressed display of what matters for action rather than a window onto how the world is, the evolutionary point that selection rewards fitness not accuracy, and the claim that what we call the feel of experience is just the interface running and not some further fact about it. I'm on the other side: I hold that experience is a real, structured, causally efficacious process going on in the organism, and that illusion talk quietly helps itself to the very thing it wants to demote — you can only say the icon isn't how things are by relying on how things look. What I need from you is a draft I can shove across the table to her and ask, honestly, is this fair to you?

So here's what the document has to do. First, the claims we can both actually sign, including the unglamorous empirical ones: that there are real dissociations between what people report and what they can discriminate; that neither of us believes in a little inner theater with a homunculus watching it; that we both think the explanatory gap between a functional or neural story and the phenomenology is a genuine puzzle and not just confusion, though we'll disagree in a second about whether the gap is in the world or in our concepts. Second, the places we still differ, each one phrased the way its own side would phrase it — no strawmen, because if Maya reads her position and doesn't recognise it the whole exercise has failed. Third, the cruxes, and for each one the test, case, or argument we'd both accept as bearing on it, with both of our predictions written down now, in advance. Please include these three cruxes at least, plus anything else you think is really load-bearing. One: what the interface is made of and who it's for — I keep pushing that her view either needs a user, which is the homunculus back again, or has to identify the user with some downstream consumer subsystem, and I want her side's actual answer rather than a dodge. Two: a case I made up in section, which I'm calling the fading icons case — subjects under a heavy dual-task load report over a few seconds that the color of a patch has gone flat, that they've swapped from seeing blue to merely knowing it's the blue one, while their forced-choice discrimination of exactly those patches stays up in the high eighties or nineties; she'll say that's precisely what you'd predict if experience is a display mode, and I want to know what my own best response is and where it's weakest. Three: a hypothetical closed-loop stimulation that reliably makes people say their pain has gone symbolic — they still withdraw, still rate it as bad, still learn to avoid the stimulus, but insist it no longer feels like anything to them; I think this one cuts differently from the first and I'd like both predictions pinned down before we know what we'd say. Also, we suspect part of our fight is just about words, maybe about whether illusion means misrepresentation or merely compression, and maybe about whether we're even using consciousness for the same thing — please say plainly which parts you think are verbal and why, and don't manufacture agreement where there isn't any.

Two practical things. Our handout suggested a numbered agreed-claims block followed by a disagreements table, but our professor wants the whole thing as one continuous piece of prose, like an email we wrote together, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables at all — so any structure has to be carried by ordinary sentences and transitions. And it's a closed-book take-home, we're not allowed to look anything up, so please don't include any citations, references, or reading suggestions and don't let anything rest on a particular author, paper, or year: describe positions and arguments in plain words instead. It also shouldn't need anything a model can't reason out — no bibliographies, no quotes, no fact-checking — because half the point is whether this disagreement can be made exact just by thinking carefully about it.
