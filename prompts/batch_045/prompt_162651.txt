I'm writing a short paper on whether the lottery paradox and the preface paradox are really two faces of one problem about justified belief, or two structurally different problems that happen to look alike. A colleague thinks they are the same problem in different costumes; a referee wrote that the preface is just the lottery with vaguer numbers. I think both are wrong, but I keep failing to say crisply what the difference is, so what I want from you is a careful comparison rather than a survey of positions, and no reading list at the end.

Set both cases up in whatever form you find most useful for comparing them, and then compare them along the axes that actually do work. The ones I have in mind: what kind of evidence supports the individual claims (one homogeneous statistical distribution versus local, claim-by-claim evidence); whether the existential claim — that some ticket wins, that somewhere in the book there is a mistake — is certain or merely probable, and whether it is derived from the same evidence as the individual claims or from a separate, higher-order consideration; whether the route to inconsistency runs through closure under conjunction, that is, through aggregating many individually justified beliefs into a set that contradicts something else the subject knows, or through a direct conflict between a body of first-order beliefs and one global belief about that body; whether any particular claim is singled out as the likely culprit; and what the subject is supposed to do in response, since in one case there appears to be a definite instruction that cannot be followed — withdraw something, although there is nothing you could rationally withdraw — while in the other it is less clear that any instruction is even in the offing. For each axis, tell me whether the contrast is real and load-bearing or merely presentational, and tell me which of the familiar families of response depends on which way that axis goes: raising the threshold for justified belief or for knowledge, refusing to let high statistical probability count as justification at all, splitting the attitude into credence versus outright belief, or simply tolerating an inconsistent but individually impeccable belief set.

Then run the following case as a diagnostic. An internal auditor at a large hospital system reviews ten thousand expense reports. For each report she performs a genuine local check against receipts and policy — she is not relying on base rates for any individual file — and each check comes back clean, leaving her about 0.99 confident that that report is compliant. She also knows, from the system's own audited track record, that first-pass checks of this kind miss something in roughly one report in two hundred, so she is confident that about fifty of these ten thousand reports do contain an error; but no report is more suspect than any other, and nothing in her local evidence points anywhere in particular. So: local and roughly equal evidence for each individual claim, a population-level statistical ground for the existential claim, no distinguished culprit, an existential claim that is probable rather than certain, and a practical upshot, since at the end of the quarter she must sign a statement asserting that the reports are compliant. Which of the two paradoxes is this closer to, and what does the answer show? Concretely: if your account of what separates the lottery from the preface survives this case — if it still sorts the case to one side and can explain why it is not a case of the other — I want to know which feature it was really tracking. If it does not survive, and the case lands in the middle or on the side you would have predicted against, then tell me which of the two paradoxes you now take to be the more basic one and which the special case, and what has to be added to the taxonomy — some third variable beyond evidence-source and certainty — to get it right.
