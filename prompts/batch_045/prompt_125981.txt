I'm about two hundred pages into a novel set in a broke river town where the chemical plant's contamination got covered up, and I've written myself into a corner: two of my characters are supposed to produce a joint statement, and I can't draft the scene until I know what's actually in it.

Merrill is a retired philosophy lecturer who now runs the town archive. Her side: conspiracy theories as a class carry a prima facie epistemic defect — there's something about the shape of these explanations (hidden intentional coordination that is cheap to posit and expensive to rule out, the way each piece of counterevidence gets absorbed rather than answered, the escalation when a prediction fails) that earns them suspicion before you get to the details. Dee is a reporter who has covered the plant for fifteen years. Her side: that's prejudice with a degree attached. Each theory stands or falls on its own evidence, the label does no epistemic work, and the official story deserves no friendly default either. They have been going back and foward for eleven chapters and the argument is now boring even me, which is a bad sign.

So write me the document. Call it the joint statement of a generalist and a particularist about conspiracy theories, and make it something both of them could sign without wincing, with the leftover disagreement stated exactly enough that a reader can see where the fault line runs.

What I need in it:

A numbered agreed-claims section first. Real claims, not filler — I don't want 'both agree evidence is important'. Ten or so. Things like what both concede about real conspiracies being discovered, about the social life of conspiracy theorising, about where the pejorative force of the phrase actually comes from (or doesn't).

Then a disagreements table: the crux, the generalist's formulation of her own position in wording she'd accept, the particularist's in wording she'd accept, the case or argument both agree would bear on that crux, and what each of them predicts it will show. Cruxes I think are in there: whether the defect, if there is one, attaches to the proposition or to the community that holds it — Merrill keeps pointing at people who don't update after a failed dated prediction, Dee says that's a fact about people and not about the claim; whether the official story gets a symmetric default or not; and whether there is any such thing as inductive support from the base rate of past conspiracy theories.

And then the thing I actually care about, which is my own invention and which both of them have to respond to in the statement. I've started calling it the third-act tell: the moment in a conspiracy account when a culprit and a motive arrive together and suddenly organise everything, while the evidence base barely moves. In a novel that's a sign the material has been designed. In an explanation of real events I read it as a sign the story was fitted to the facts instead of extracted from them. Is that a genuine epistemic tell, or is Merrill just generalising from the handful of theories that got famous? Where does each of them file it?

Give them one shared test case, since they argue about the town: five years before the book opens, the council rezoned the barrel field, and within three years three of the five councillors who voted yes had jobs with the company that bought it. There's a memo that reads like coordination and no proof, plus a perfectly ordinary alternative story in which those three were simply the competent ones available. Both characters agree this case bears on the crux; they disagree about what it shows. Put their predictions down before anything is resolved.

If part of their fight turns out to be about words — about what 'conspiracy theory' should be used to pick out, or about what 'prima facie' is doing — say which part and what's genuinely left once the wording is fixed. And tell me what each of them would count as evidence against their own view, because neither of my characters is supposed to be a fanatic.

Last thing, and I mean it practically: these lines get spoken out loud by people who are meant to sound like they know what they're on about. So at the end tell me how confident you actually are in each part of what you've drafted, and single out the one claim you're least sure of — the spot where, if you've got it wrong, a reader who works on this stuff for a living will feel their teeth itch.
