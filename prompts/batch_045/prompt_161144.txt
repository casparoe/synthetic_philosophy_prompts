I'm a retired process chemist who's started going to a philosophy group at the public library, and we've got onto dispositions — fragility, solubility, that sort of thing. I have to admit the finks-and-masks business looks to me like much ado about nothing, and I'd like to be told where I'm going wrong.

As I understand it, both cases come to the same thing: you've got the disposition, the stimulus arrives, and the manifestation doesn't happen. Whether the disposition got switched off on the way in, or the result got blocked on the way out, reads to me as a difference in the story you tell, not a difference in the logic. And every fix I've seen offered is just a more careful conditional — add ceteris paribus, or "in ideal conditions", or that trick of Lewis's about the object retaining its basis — and apparently that trick is reckoned to handle one of the two but not the other, which is exactly the pedantry I mean. If they're the same counterexample, surely any conditional that survives one survives both?

Here are three cases, from the sort of stuff I actually worked with:

1. A finely divided nickel catalyst, a perfectly good hydrogenation catalyst sitting there in the reactor. But the feed carries a trace of something that poisons the active sites the moment the feed hits it, quicker than the reaction could ever get going. Stimulus arrives, nothing happens.

2. A flammable solvent in a tank under a nitrogen blanket. You put a spark in, no fire. Pump the same solvent into air and it burns beautifully. Nothing about the solvent changed; the nitrogen is just in the way.

3. A case I made up, which I'v been calling a "late bloomer". A solid that won't burn at any temperature you care to try — but heat it in air and somewhere around 300 C it decomposes into a gas that does burn, and off it goes. So at the moment you apply the stimulus there's no disposition there at all, and yet you get the manifestation. To me that's the fink with the sign reversed, and I'd have thought any conditional patched up to get the fink right will call this stuff flammable, which its plainly not.

So: is the fink/mask distinction real, or am I right that it's pedantry? Which of 1 and 2 is which, and what test do you actually use to tell them apart? Does my third case do any damage the other two don't, or is it the same complaint wearing a hat? And while I'm at it — why can't we simply say that soluble means has-the-molecular-structure-that-makes-it-dissolve, identify the disposition with its categorical basis, and be done with the whole business? In chemistry that's more or less what we meant by it.

Tell me how confident you are in each part of what you say, and flag the single bit you're least sure about. American English, please — I've been over here thirty years and I still write colour.
