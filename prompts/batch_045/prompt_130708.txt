I've been puzzling over the asymmetry people are said to show when asked about the chairman who starts a programme that will raise profits and, as a side effect, either harms or helps the environment, while saying that he doesn't care about the environment either way. As I understand the state of play, roughly two kinds of story are on offer. On the first, the result tells us something about the folk concept of intentional action itself: whether a foreseen side effect counts as something the agent did *intentionally* is partly a matter of its moral valence, or of the agent's attitude of indifference towards it, so the concept isn't the morally neutral tool philosophers took it to be when they used it in action theory. On the second, nothing about the concept moves; what moves is what respondents take themselves to be doing in answering the question — in the harm case the only evident point of asking about intentionality is to fix blame, so the word is heard as doing blame-marking work, and the answer shifts to suit. If I've got the dialectic wrong, please correct me.

What I want explained is what is really at stake between these two stories, and specifically how one would tell them apart. Is this a genuine disagreement about the psychology, or two ways of describing one mechanism that differ only in what they choose to call "the concept" and what they choose to call "pragmatics"? What would have to be true of our use of the word for the first story, and not the second, to be right?

To pin that down, here are three variants I invented while turning it over, and I'd like to know what each story predicts for them, and why:

1. Swap the word. Keep the vignette identical, but ask whether the chairman *knew* the programme would affect the environment, or whether he *foresaw* it, instead of asking whether he brought it about intentionally. Does the blame-marking story have to predict that the asymmetry shrinks or disappears here, while the story that puts moral valence into the concept predicts it spreads to any mental-state word near enough to intentionality? Or is the second story perfectly free to say the effect is local to one word and nothing more?

2. Change the format. Drop the yes-or-no question and instead ask people to rate their agreement with a written statement — the chairman harmed the environment intentionally — in a context where nobody is asking, and nothing hangs on the answer. If the effect is a matter of what respondents take a question to be *for*, format should matter; if the effect is in the concept, it shouldn't. Is that a fair test, or does the first story survive by saying that the pragmatic pressure just migrates into the new format?

3. Pull norm-violation apart from disapproval. Suppose the side effect breaks a company rule that the respondents themselves think is a silly or unjust rule, or suppose the chairman will be praised and promoted for the side effect rather than blamed for it. Does the effect track the breaking of a norm, or does it track whether the person doing the attributing wants to condemn? Which, if either, of my two stories is committed to which answer?

Finally: please flag plainly where you are confident and where you are guessing, and say so outright if the distinction I've set up is an artefact of how I've framed it, if my three variants don't in fact discriminate between the stories, or if the question as I've posed it can't be answered well — I'd much rather be told that than be given a polite answer to a bad question. Please write in British English, and aim for roughly 600–900 words.
