I chair the clinical ethics consultation service at a three-hospital network, and I want to pilot a protocol for a recurring decision we make badly: whether a proposed intervention actually improves a patient's welfare, when the only thing we are allowed to count is the reduction of craving and suffering.

Background: the theory I want operationalized is tranquilism, the view that a person's welfare is improved only by the absence of craving and suffering, and that nothing contributes positively on its own side of the ledger — pleasure, achievement, relationships, knowledge and satisfied preferences matter, if at all, only because their absence typically brings craving or suffering with it. I am not interested in being talked out of the view for the pilot; I am interested in what it takes to run it at the bedside. I already know where it bites: on this view extinguishing a craving and satisfying it come out exactly the same, a heavily sedated patient can be doing extremely well, and a patient who has died scores best of all. That is the material I need the procedure to handle honestly rather than quietly avoid.

Constraints on the deliverable. My consultants are nurses, junior doctors and social workers with no philosophy training; the procedure has to be runnable in about fifteen minutes from a chart review and one conversation, has to produce a written rationale that a family and our quality committee can read, and has to end in one of three outputs: approve, decline, or refer to me. I need the questions in a fixed order, a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, explicit stop rules for when the answer is no, explicit hand-off points where the remaining question is one of values or of clinical fact rather than of the theory, and a one-page version for the ward. Where a step looks like a procedure but is really a judgment call, say so on the page rather than burying it.

There are distinctions I need you to settle explicitly, with a test my staff could apply, because the whole thing collapses if they are left vague:

First, craving versus a want that does not hurt to have. Give my staff a way to tell an aversive, attention-grabbing pull from a standing preference that the patient reports without distress, and tell them what to do when the patient cannot or will not report.

Second, unfelt and dispositional craving. Our long-stay patients include people who are tube-fed, cognitively impaired, or heavily medicated and report no wants at all, but who would be distressed under some counterfactual condition — if food were placed in front of them, if a delusion were challenged, if a medication were withdrawn. Does that count, and under what evidential standard, given that we cannot run the experiment?

Third, craving under full information and the adaptation problem. Several of our patients have adjusted their expectations downward over years of illness and now want very little; on a counterfactual test they look tranquil, on a full-information test they do not. Pick a handling and defend it.

Fourth, what suffering ranges over. I need it to include at least dread, nausea, breathlessness, grief, boredom and the feeling of being trapped, not only pain, and I need a rule for anticipatory distress that never arrives.

Fifth, displacement and time window. Some interventions remove a craving and reliably grow another — treating one compulsion, starting a drug that blunts appetite and replaces it with restlessness. Tell my staff whether they are netting over a shift, a week, or a decade, and what to enter when they have no data.

Sixth, comparative versus absolute judgement. I suspect we can only ever rank two options by expected net craving and suffering, and never assign a welfare level to a single patient; confirm or correct this, and build the procedure around whichever is true.

Seventh, behavioural evidence in patients who cannot speak: pacing, vocalisation, withdrawal, autonomic markers. Tell me whether these are evidence of suffering or criteria for it, because the difference changes what my staff are allowed to conclude.

Then apply the finished procedure to three cases. (1) A resident with advanced dementia who paces the corridor and calls out for much of the day, where the team is split between an antipsychotic and a much more expensive increase in one-to-one contact and stimulation. (2) A man in his forties with treatment-resistant depression requesting continuous palliative sedation, whose reported distress is largely dread about the future rather than present pain, and whose wife supports the request. (3) A case where I expect the procedure to give me an answer I will not like: a patient rendered inert and indifferent by a dopamine-blocking medication, who reports no wants and no distress and now sits all day, alongside another patient whose cravings are being comprehensively and expensively satisfied, and whom everyone on the ward takes to be flourishing. Run both through the protocol and tell me what it ranks higher and why, and where the protocol's answer and my own judgement should part company.

Finally, be explicit about the epistemic status of each part of what you give me. Mark which steps follow analytically from the definition of the view and which depend on a contested reading of craving or suffering — whether the two can even be specified independently, whether dispositional states can count in a purely experiential theory, whether a wholly negative account of welfare is coherent. Do not present a contestable reading as what the view commits its defenders to, and do not present the view itself as anything close to a consensus position in the literature. And state plainly what the procedure cannot do: I particularly need it to stop short of any recommendation that depends on the welfare comparison between a sedated or anaesthetised patient and one who no longer exists, and to refer that to me and to our committee as a values question the theory does not settle.
