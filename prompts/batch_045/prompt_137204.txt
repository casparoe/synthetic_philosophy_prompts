I teach a final-year seminar on cultural evolution, and I am considering using Gore Verbinski's The Ring (2002) as the case study for our unit on memes. Before I commit to it I would like a careful reading of the film through the Darwinian theory of culture, and I would rather find out now that it will mislead the students than discover it halfway through the session.

The film's mechanism, as I will present it: a Seattle journalist, Rachel Keller, investigating a cluster of teenage deaths watches a videotape of disconnected, disturbing images; the phone rings immediately afterwards and a voice tells her she has seven days. Viewers die a week later. The escape hatch the film supplies is the one Rachel discovers and then uses: make a copy of the tape and get someone else to watch it, which spares you and dooms them. The images turn out to originate with Samara, an adopted child who etched pictures onto surfaces and into people's minds, and who was sealed in a well, where she survived a week. The closing scene has Rachel helping her son Aidan dub another copy and declining to answer his question about what will happen to whoever watches it.

What I want is an assessment of four claims I have been turning over, plus whatever a sharper reader than me thinks I have missed.

One. Is the tape a replicator at all? Test it against Dawkins's three conditions and against the further Darwinian requirement that variation be blind with respect to fitness. On the surface it scores well: it is copied, the copies are faithful, and the chain continues. But its content is not the product of copying plus error; it is authored by an agent with a purpose, and the curse, the phone call and the deaths are enforced by Samara rather than built out of the host's behaviour. My worry is that I would be showing students a designed artefact with one lineage, no variation, and no competition among variants, and calling it Darwinian. Is that fatal, or does the reading survive if what the film models is selection among hosts rather than among variants?

Two. A student proposed a distinction I cannot find in the literature: between carrier-borne replicators, whose copying is performed by a host and whose extended phenotype is built out of host labour, and self-executing replicators, which generate their own content and enforce their own transmission. On her view the tape is the second kind, and that is precisely why it is a poor model of culture: it deletes the thing cultural evolution exists to explain, namely why a human being copies anything in the first place. I find this persuasive but too quick, since chain letters and other pass-it-on-or-else items are real and their spread is genuinely studied. Is coercion a transmission channel that dual-inheritance models leave out, or is it parasitic on the ordinary ones?

Three. Is the seven-day latency a real transmission-virulence trade-off, long enough for the host to make a copy and short enough to keep the chain moving, or am I reading adaptation into a plot device? What, inside the film's own terms, would settle it?

Four. The film opens with the tape circulating as an urban legend, and Rachel learns of it by hearsay long before she sees it: the item that actually spreads through the population is not a video but a story about a video, and it spreads without anyone copying anything. Is the film, read carefully, a better illustration of Sperber-style cultural attraction than of memetics, and if so is the interesting object the interaction of the two, given that the rumour makes people seek out the tape and the tape's lethality makes the rumour worth repeating?

Finally, the part I care about most. The ending treats passing the copy on as culpable and refuses to let it be costless; the film's moral interest is in whether you hand the thing to someone else. The meme's-eye view is deliberately amoral. Does that mean the film imports a normative framework the theory excludes, or does it show something the theory needs, namely that transmission is something agents do and can be held responsible for?

Please say plainly whether you think this is a bad teaching object, and if you think it is a good one, tell me what it shows about cultural transmission that the formal treatments in Dennett, Sperber, and Boyd and Richerson do not. I do not want the film reduced to an illustration of a thesis, and I do not want the theory bent to fit the film; where the two resist each other, I want that on the record too. I am working from Verbinski's remake, but if you know Hideo Nakata's original or Koji Suzuki's novel well enough to say whether either shifts the picture, I would welcome it.
