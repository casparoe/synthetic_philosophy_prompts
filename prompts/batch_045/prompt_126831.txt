Hey — I've been going in circles on trolley cases and I want to know what I actually think, because I'm pretty sure it isn't the thing people usually say it is. I'll give you my gut verdicts on a bunch of variants and you tell me what principle, if any, they add up to.

1. Five on the main line, one on the spur, I'm a bystander at the lever. I pull. Fine.
2. Same setup but I'm the driver and the brakes are gone. Also fine — I really don't feel any difference between these two, though I gather some people do.
3. Footbridge: a big guy is standing next to me, his body would stop the trolley. I shove him off. Not fine, even though the arithmetic is identical.
4. Loop variant: the spur rejoins the main line further on, so the only thing that stops the trolley before it comes back around to the five is the guy on the spur. His body is what does the saving. I pull, and I think it's fine — this is where I lose the plot, because on the usual way of putting things his death is the means here, not a side effect.
5. Same as 4 except there's a working buffer three metres past him, so his body does no stopping work whatsoever. Obviously fine. I mention it only to show I'm not just being sloppy about the causal details in 4.
6. Same footbridge, same big guy, but instead of pushing him I blow the bridge supports so the whole span drops onto the track. His body ends up doing exactly the work it did in 3. I think this one is fine. I also feel like a complete fraud about it, and I can't tell whether that's a real distinction or just that I've found nicer words for the same killing.
7. Surgeon, five patients who'll die without organs, one healthy guy in for a checkup. Not fine. (Calibration case, I assume we agree.)
8. The big guy on the footbridge has already slipped and is falling; left alone he lands two centimetres short of the track and the trolley kills the five. I reach out mid-fall and nudge him those two centimetres. I think that's fine. But I can't see how it differs from 3 except that the lethal trajectory already existed.
9. I pull the lever, the five are saved, and the one on the spur is crushed but alive and dies over about three hours where nobody can get to him. I want to say still fine, but I'm noticeably less comfortable than in 1 — and if I do say it's less fine then I've quietly stopped talking about killing and started talking about something else.
10. The one on the spur is my kid. I don't pull, I don't think I'm required to, and I don't think I'm wrong. Maybe that's a separate partiality axis and the principle can just be silent — but include it in case it constrains things.

The thing I keep falling back on is two rules stuck together: (a) moving an already-existing threat onto someone is okay, creating a new threat that hits them is not; (b) their death being a foreseen side effect is okay, their death being what does the saving is not. But (b) gets case 4 wrong by my lights, and (a) seems to get 6 wrong, and 8 is a coin flip under both. So which clause goes, and what replaces it? If you think the honest answer is that the real principle is about rights rather than about what I'm intending, say so and show it buys my verdicts at least as cheaply.

The one I actually can't settle: the guy on the spur is only standing there because ten minutes earlier I asked him to wait there for me, for totally innocent reasons, and the buffer's broken so his body is what saves the five. Sometimes that reads as "he was already in it" and sometimes as "I put him there." I go back and forth hourly.

Don't just tell me it's the intention/foresight distinction — I've heard that and it doesn't fit 4 and 6. And please don't appeal to what any particular author said; I want the principle, not the pedigree.
