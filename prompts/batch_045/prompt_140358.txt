I want a diagnosis of the debate about the doing/allowing distinction, and in particular about its most contested instance, the moral difference between killing and letting die. Not a verdict, and not a survey: I already know the standard positions and the standard cases, and that is partly my problem.

Here is what I mean by stalled. Both sides agree on the paradigm data. Nearly everyone who thinks there is a distinction thinks the surgeon who cuts up the healthy visitor is doing something worse, and harder to justify, than the official who fails to divert the trolley; nearly everyone who denies the distinction has a stock of matched pairs in which every candidate explanatory difference has been controlled away and the intuitive asymmetry survives anyway. The asymmetry is reported as one of the most robust and cross-culturally widespread moral intuitions we have, and also as one of the hardest to state in a form that does not collapse under the next cleverly engineered case. That combination — maximum intuitive grip, maximum theoretical fragility, decades of clever people, no convergence — is what I want explained.

I have two hypotheses, which I offer as hypotheses. The first is that this is not one disagreement but three stacked inside one label: a question about where the extension of the distinction falls (which cases count as doing, which as allowing), a separate question about whether falling on one side of that line has any independent moral significance, and a third question about what would explain the significance if it exists — causation, counterfactual dependence, the agent's intentions, the rights of the patient, the cost to the agent, what the agent's will expresses about her character. My suspicion is that the most famous exchanges look like failures of argument but are actually participants answering different questions from this list, and that a paper which settles one of them is read as having failed to settle the others. If that is right, I would like the three questions prised apart and told which of them is actually load-bearing.

The second hypothesis concerns the extensional question, and this is where I would like you to work hardest. I think a large family of cases shows that the extension of the distinction is hostage to something that looks morally inert. Consider a matched pair. In the first, a patient's infusion pump is built to shut off at a fixed time unless a nurse resets it; a nurse who wants the patient dead does nothing, the pump stops, the patient dies. In the second, a structurally identical pump is built to run continuously unless someone presses a stop button; a structurally identical nurse with the same motive presses it at the same moment, and the patient dies. Hold fixed what each agent foresees, what each desires, the certainty of the outcome, the triviality of the cost of preventing it, and the causal sequence from that moment on. The only remaining difference is which physical state of the device is its resting state — which state it occupies if nobody does anything. Call these default-flip pairs, and note how many real cases have this shape: do-not-resuscitate orders and their inverse, fail-safe versus fail-deadly engineering, opt-out versus opt-in consent regimes, the difference between a life-support machine the hospital switches off and one it declines to restart.

If the doing/allowing verdict flips on that difference alone, then either the distinction is tracking something far thinner than its defenders claim — an artifact of engineering convention, institutional default, or statistical normality — or it is tracking something real about baselines and intervention, and the defender should say what that is and why a designer's choice of default can carry moral weight. There is a third possibility: the moral bookkeeping is being done at the wrong time, and the real doing happened earlier, at the point where someone chose the default, which would make the distinction an artifact of looking at a single time slice of a longer agency. And there is a fourth: these cases reveal that the distinction has no stable extension at all, only a family of dimensions along which cases grade continuously, so that the appearance of a binary line is an artifact of the paradigm cases.

I would also like you to test the normative question against aggregation. Ten thousand agents each fail to send a cheap text message, and ten thousand distinguishable strangers die, one per agent; one agent kills ten thousand. Where does the distinction go when the omissions are collected and the killings are collected? My sense is that the answer depends on whether the asymmetry is being understood as an agent-relative constraint on what each of us may do, which resists aggregation, or as a claim about how bad outcomes are, which does not — and that these are different questions being asked under one heading.

So: is this a verbal dispute, a substantive one, several substantive ones with a shared name, a distinction that is real but doing pluralistic and ineliminable work, or an ill-posed question that has survived because it is useful in law, medicine, and moral psychology for reasons independent of its philosophical defensibility? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the work — about the provenance of the intuition, about whether verdicts in default-flip pairs really do flip, about whether the distinction's extension is learned from local institutional practice — and if so, would answering it settle anything or only relocate the argument? I want the diagnosis argued from what each side actually claims and from the case families above rather than asserted, and I want you to say what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a distinction that dissolves one of the three questions, a no-go result showing that no assignment of cases to doing and allowing can satisfy the desiderata both sides accept, an experiment on default-flip pairs, a replacement taxonomy, or an argument that no progress should be expected and that the trench warfare is the appropriate response to the question.
