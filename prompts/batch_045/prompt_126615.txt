My little brother Dev signed up for the Army last month — he's nineteen, he's not a British citizen yet, and the recruiter more or less hinted that a few years' service would smooth his settlement application. I drove him to the recruiting office and I've felt a bit odd about it ever since, and last night two friends got into a proper argument about whether he'd done something morally dodgy and I couldn't referee it at all.

Nadia went first. She said enlisting is writing a blank cheque: he's promising in advance to shoot whoever he's told to shoot, and if the war he ends up in turns out to be one of the bad ones, the people on the other side are entirely within their rights to resist him. So he's taken on a risk of becoming someone who kills people he has no good reason to kill, in exchange for pay and now for a visa. She said the whole point is that you *can't* know in advance whether the next war will be just, and that's precisely what's wrong with agreeing in advance.

Rob said that's holding a nineteen-year-old to a standard nobody could meet. No individual soldier can work out whether a war has a just cause; that judgement has to be made at the political level, and ordinary soldiers are entitled to defer to it. He also made a practical point: every side in every war already believes it's in the right, so telling the soldiers on the unjust side that they're murderers won't make wars more discriminate, it just hands each side a licence to call the other lot criminals and fight harder.

Nadia said he'd answered a question she never asked. She isn't claiming Dev is to blame — she's claiming the killing might be impermissible, and those are different things. If someone is hypnotised and attacks me, I can still stop them, and they've no complaint, even though they're not blameworthy. So even if Rob wins everything about uncertainty, all he's established is that both sides are equally blameless, which isn't the interesting claim. The interesting claim is whether both sides have the same right to be there at all.

Rob came back saying that distinction blows up in her face. If blameless soldiers on the wrong side are still liable to be killed, her view permits killing the innocent, which is exactly what the old line about soldiers on both sides being in the same position was meant to stop. And the hypnotised man isn't a soldier anyway; the reason you can kill a soldier is that he's a threat, and threats needn't be culpable — but then the soldier on the just side is also a threat to the one on the unjust side, so if liability is just about being a threat, symmetry walks straight back in.

Nadia said threats come in kinds. If I'm permitted to stop you, you can't coherently describe your resistance to me as self-defence — self-defence against something you have no right to resist isn't self-defence — so the combatant on the wrong side has no right to resist, and that's where the asymmetry actually sits. She added that Rob's 'everyone thinks they're right' point proves too much: it would rule out condemning anything anyone is sincerely convinced of.

Rob's last go was the one I've been turning over since. He said: fine, then tell me which act of Dev's is supposed to be the wrong one. He isn't deciding anything, he's one of tens of thousands, and the injustice — if there is one — is produced by ministers and voters and a long chain of people most of whom will never fire a shot. If Dev had sat it out at home, the war would have gone ahead exactly the same. So either the wrongness is in his individual pulling of the trigger, which seems to depend on facts completely outside his control, or it's in the collective enterprise, and then it's spread so thin that calling him a murderer is a category mistake: it's the political community that's at fault and he's just the bit holding the rifle.

What I definately can't work out is whether they're even arguing about the same thing. It sounded to me like three separate questions got tangled together — whether the killing is allowed, whether he's to blame for it, and whether he can be forcibly stopped — and I've no idea which one actually carries the weight. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right about what at each step, not just who wins overall, and tell me what, if anything, they're both missing? One thing they never got onto is whether the visa-and-settlement motive makes it better or worse, so if that's relevant, say where it fits.
