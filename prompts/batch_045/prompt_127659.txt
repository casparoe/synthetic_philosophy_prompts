So I somehow let myself get talked into doing a 30-minute brown-bag talk at work next week — I'm a backend engineer at a logistics company, the audience will be maybe 12 to 18 other engineers, nobody there has any philosophy background, and there's a whiteboard plus a projector I probably won't bother with. The topic got chosen because of an argument I couldn't finish with a friend at a bar a few weeks ago: she said that pain asymbolia — the thing where, after certain kinds of brain damage, people can apparently still tell you exactly where the pinprick is, how strong it is, whether it's burning or stabbing, but they don't seem to mind it, don't pull away, and can sometimes look amused by it — shows that pain is really two separate components bolted together, a damage-report channel and an alarm-or-valence channel, and that only the second one is the part that's bad. I said that felt like it was doing to much work with the word "component," and we left it there. Now I have to make that disagreement interesting to a room of engineers for half an hour, and I don't want to just read a lecture at them, so what I actually want is a plan for building it.

Don't write the talk for me and don't explain the philosophy to me either, and definately don't hand me papers or a reading list — I won't read them. What I want is the sequence: what has to be in place before the disagreement is even visible to that room. My guess is that people will be quietly sliding between three different things — the tissue damage and nerve signaling, the felt sensation with its location and intensity, and the minding-it — and that if I don't get them to see that slide first, the whole thing will look like a word game, but tell me if that's the wrong opening move. Then: the single case that should carry the whole thirty minutes, and what it has to have and what it has to lack in order to do that job. I've been playing with a variant of my own that I think might beat the textbook asymbolia case — call it "mute but motivated": someone with the full sensory side, localizes and rates intensity perfectly, reports zero unpleasantness, insists they don't mind it at all, and yet still withdraws reliably, still learns to avoid the stimulus after a single trial, and will still press a button to switch it off if doing so is cheap. Is that a better carrier than straight asymbolia, because it splits the "alarm" into a dislike part and a get-me-out-of-here part, or does it make things worse by being so hypothetical that nobody in the room will buy it? Pick whichever case you think is right and say what it must contain and what it must NOT contain — I suspect it matters a lot whether the person is calm versus amused versus merely indifferent, and whether any of it is visible from the outside.

The part I'm most worried about is the misconceptions: which ones will actually form, in the order they'll form in a room of engineers, and where in the sequence I should head each one off. The ones I can already see coming are (a) treating it as an architecture lesson — obviously there's a signal and a separate alarm module, next slide; (b) "so they're not really in pain, that's just nociception," which I think dissolves the question by definition but I don't know how to show that without getting into a fight about words; (c) "so pain isn't intrinsically bad, the badness is a judgment we layer on top," which smells like it's one step away from concluding that hurting people is fine as long as they don't complain; and (d) confusing "this doesn't feel bad to them" with "there's nothing bad happening to them." Add the ones I'm missing, especially any that my own examples invite — if the signal/alarm analogy is going to come back and bite me later in the talk, tell me where, and tell me what I should openly refuse to settle in thirty minutes and say out loud that I'm refusing. Finally, one closing question that isn't "so what is asymbolia" — something that would show me they'd grasped the structure of the disagreement rather than remembered the case; my instinct is to make it about who gets to decide that someone is suffering once behavior and report come apart, or about what you'd even build if you had to detect suffering in a system where all you can observe is behavior, but you pick. End with a single last line beginning "Carrying case:" that names, in one sentence, the case you'd actually build the talk around.
