I've been asked to run a sealed-bid tender for the organisation I work for, and I've been spending what spare time I have on the mechanics of how such things are supposed to work. Our format is the ordinary one: suppliers submit sealed bids, the lowest bid wins, and the winner is paid its own bid. There is an alternative format that textbooks like, in which the lowest bidder still wins but is paid the runner-up's price; there, quoting your true cost is the sensible thing to do whatever you happen to believe about your rivals, whereas in our format a supplier has to bid some way above its own cost, and by how much depends on how many rivals it faces and on what it expects those rivals to do. Real tenders almost never use the other format.

What I can't get straight is what our format presupposes about the bidders. Taken at face value it seems to need a great deal: not merely that each supplier knows its own costs, but that each has a view about the others' costs, a view about the others' views, and so on upward — and, further, that they all in some sense share a single picture of the spread of costs they are drawing from, so that whatever disagreement exists among them is really just a difference in private information. It also strikes me as odd that we seal the bids and forbid the bidders from talking to one another, thereby destroying mutual knowledge, while the predictions we rely on seem to need that mutual knowledge; and that our own rules let me reject a bid for being implausibly cheap, as though a supplier offering a very low price must be mistaken about its own costs, even though finding out who genuinely has the lowest costs was the whole purpose of the exercise.

I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of rationality and mutual knowledge that this practice rests on, and to tell me which parts the practice strictly presupposes and which it merely sits comfortably with. Could the same practice be licensed by a view on which suppliers are risk-averse, or one on which they reason only a step or two about each other rather than reasoning to a fixed point, or one on which they learn across repeated tenders instead of working anything out in advance? Where is the choice of format philosophically underdetermined — where would several quite different theories of bidder rationality all tell me to carry on exactly as we do? And what would concretely have to change in how we run the tender if I stopped assuming common knowledge of rationality: for instance, if I announced in advance that every bid would be published afterwards, or if I knew that one regular supplier always bids its true cost?

Please argue from the structure of the situation rather than from the literature — I want your own reconstruction, not references — and write the answer in British English.
