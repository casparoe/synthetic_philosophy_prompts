OK so I just spent something like three hours arguing with my friend Priya at the pub about whether it's actually good to be as morally good as you can possibly be, and I came away realising I've got a pile of gut reactions I can't fit together. Her line was that the whole idea is repellent: a person who maximised moral goodness would be a bore, no fun to be around, no real interests of her own, and the rest of us are right not to want to be that or to marry that. (She kept invoking that famous paper about moral saints everyone trots out, and I kept saying that's a debating move, not an argument.) But once we started going through actual people instead of the abstract idea, I found myself defending some saints and being weirded out by others, and I can't tell you what the rule is.

My verdicts, more or less as I blurted them out:

1. Guy donates a kidney to a complete stranger, anonymously. Great. I'd want to be that person, I'd want my kid to grow up to be that person, nothing creepy about it.

2. Woman gives away half her income for thirty years, lives in a small flat, keeps bees, plays in a ceilidh band. I admire her a lot and she seems *fine* — like someone you'd actually want at dinner.

3. Woman gives away 90%, sleeps on a mattress, never takes a break, and describes people who don't as "complicit". I admire her from a distance, I don't want to be her and I don't want her at dinner, and — this is the bit Priya jumped on — I think she's somehow less good, not just less fun, than #2. Not worse at producing good outcomes. Worse as a person.

4. Someone who has engineered her whole life so that being good is easy: no car, no smartphone, no social media, lives in a co-op, has quietly stripped every temptation out of her environment. She probably does more good than #2. I'm unimpressed. Which feels wrong and I can't defend it.

5. A guy who spent two years of his life on a campaign that would have happened anyway without him — there was a backup, someone else lined up to do it just as well. I still admire him about as much as #1. Real sacrifice, zero outcome.

6. Same guy, same two years, same sacrifice, same motives, except this time the campaign slightly backfires and makes things a bit worse. I don't admire him at all. Which is nuts, right? Because if I genuinely don't care about outcomes the backfire shouldn't matter, and if I do care about outcomes then #5 shouldn't get full credit for achieving nothing.

7. A woman who's cared for her mother full time for fifteen years with no real alternative, who couldn't have walked away without catastrophe, who never exactly chose it in any ongoing sense. Everyone calls her a saint. I feel sad about her and I don't really admire her, which makes me feel like a jerk. She's sacrificed more than anyone else on this list.

8. Someone who does an enormous amount of good for a rotten reason — to impress somebody, or out of guilt, or because he likes being the guy who does that. I don't admire *him*, but I don't think what he did is worth less. Those feel seperate to me and Priya said that was just having it both ways.

9. Someone who is a saint about animals and basically cold to her own family. I think she's less good, not merely lopsided — like the shape of the whole life matters, not just the sum of it.

During the argument I invented three things to try to sort this, and I want to know whether any of them do work or whether they're just my prejudices in a costume:

- the photocopy test: clone the person N times into N relevantly similar situations and ask whether the world improves roughly N times over. The kidney donor passes. The second whistleblower at the same company doesn't. The daughter looking after her own mother doesn't. But #7 is exactly the case where the test says "no marginal value" and my reaction isn't about marginal value at all, so maybe the test is tracking something other than what I thought.

- the exit-was-open clause: admiration only fires if the person could have walked away, at real cost to themselves and not much cost to anyone else, and didn't. This handles #7 nicely, but it also seems to make #4 *more* admirable rather than less, since she rebuilt her life on purpose.

- moral ergonomics: the thought that arranging your life so virtue is cheap is itself an achievement and I'm being unfair to #4. I don't believe this but I can't say why not.

So: is there a principle about what makes a maximally morally good life admirable — or desirable, or just plain good — that gets all nine, or nearly? I'd much rather have something that drops one or two of my reactions than something with a separate clause bolted on per case, so tell me which of them it can't accommodate and which you'd overturn. Constraints: nothing that quietly collapses into "produce the most good", since that's Priya's side and it clearly isn't what I think — outcomes only seem to move my reactions in one direction, and I want to know what that asymmetry is. And please don't give me a reading list or tell me who argued what first; I don't care about the bibliography, I want the shape of the thing.

One case I keep going back and forth on that isn't above: someone who was pretty selfish and careless for thirty years and then, after a scare, becomes completely devoted and gives away everything for the last five. I definately find myself admiring that more than #2, and I can't tell whether that's a real moral judgement or just a good story. What does your principle say about it, and what else does it predict that I haven't thought to ask about?
