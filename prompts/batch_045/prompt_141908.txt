I'm a philosophy professor, and for the last couple of years I've been the token ethicist on our teaching hospital's clinical ethics committee. We get about thirty consults a year, and we have to log a single recommended course of action in the chart within 48 hours or the consult never gets signed off by the attending — which means the form more or less forces us to pretend every case has an answer. Plenty of them don't, and I want us to stop deciding that case by case.

So: give me a decision procedure the committee can actually run — the nurses, the chaplain and the two residents on it have no philosophy training — that sorts a consult into four kinds before we write anything: conflicts of prima facie duties that weighing genuinely settles; cases that look like dilemmas but are really ignorance or uncertainty about the facts or about what the patient wanted; cases where weighing does settle what must be done but leaves real moral residue that we ought to name and document; and cases where there is no permissible option at all, so that whatever gets done is in some sense a wrong. I want the questions in the order to ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because what's left is a question of values rather than facts. It should also tell us what to write when we land in each box — in particular when we should refuse to tick a single recommendation and say instead that the choice belongs to the family, or that no available option is clean.

Then run it on three real-shaped cases. (1) Two patients, one ventilator, bad flu week; the hospital has a written allocation policy but it runs out at exactly the point where the two patients score identically. (2) A comatose man on pressors whose wife insists on full support because he'd never want to miss their daughter's wedding next month, and whose adult son says his father told him last year he'd never want to live like this — no written directive, and the two are evenly credible. (3) A ten-year-old with relapsed leukemia whose mother wants to stop chemo and whose father wants to continue; both are legal custodians, both are loving, and the oncologist says either path is medically defensible. The third is the one I expect to dislike the answer on: the residents treat it as a tragedy, and I suspect any honest procedure is going to tell us it isn't our decision at all — I want to see wether that's right or whether the procedure is deflating something it shouldn't.

Be straight about where the procedure misfires, and about whether a tie-breaker like first-come-first-served really resolves a conflict or just relocates it. Lead with the bottom line in your first sentence — what the procedure is, in one sentence — then give the reasoning; no preamble, and don't restate the question back at me.
