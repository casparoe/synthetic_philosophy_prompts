hey — I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background beyond stackexchange flamewars, and I somehow ended up on our incident review board. We run blameless postmortems (good), but there's a second step afterwards where we write an "accountability note" that goes into the person's file and feeds promo and comp. So we absolve blame and then assign it anyway, which feels incoherent but nobody can say exactly why.

The thing that keeps biting us: two people make basically the same call with the same information, one gets a bad outcome and one doesn't, and the two notes come out completely different. Someone told me this has a name — "moral luck"? apparently there are a few flavors: luck in how things turn out, luck in what situation you landed in, luck in what kind of person you are. That sounds like our problem, except we can't just shrug. We meet weekly, we have about an hour per incident, there's an EM and someone from HR in the room, and the output goes in someone's permanent record.

What I want is a procedure, not an essay. Something my team can literally run:

- a fixed order of questions to answer from the incident writeup
- what counts as a sufficient answer to each, in normal-person language (my EM will not read a sentence with "counterfactual" in it)
- where the procedure stops and says no accountability
- where it hands off to a human because the remaining question is about values, not facts

Specific things it has to handle, because these are the ones we actually argue about:

First, we already know the outcome when we write the note, and I don't think we can un-know it. So give me a concrete trick for separating "was this decision reasonable given what was knowable at the time" from "did it turn out badly." My idea was to write two notes — one with the outcome redacted, one with it — and if they disagree, that disagreement is the luck, and we have to say out loud which one we're filing. Tell me if that's workable or if it's self-deception.

Second, the inverse case: someone who skipped review, pushed to prod on a Friday, and got away with it — no incident, and it cut p99 latency in half so they looked great. My intuition is he should get the same note as the person who skipped review and caused a six hour outage. Make that fall out of the procedure rather than being an extra rule bolted on.

Third, symmetry: whatever strips blame for lucky outcomes should also strip credit. Our "heroes" are often just people whose risky hotfix happened to work, and HR really does not want to hear that, so flag where that bites.

Fourth, the temperament thing. Some of our engineers are wired cautious and never cause incidents; some are cowboys and sometimes cause them and sometimes ship the thing that saves us. If part of what we're rating is just personality, that's the third kind of luck and I don't know what to do with it procedurally — you can't put "be more cautious" in a performance note.

Fifth, near misses and latent bugs that never fired, and the related case where the badness depends on when we happened to discover it. And a case we hit last month: the person who owned the service for three weeks versus the person who wrote the code four years ago.

Two more constraints. I can't read academic stuff — no citations, no reading lists, no paywalled papers; if you're leaning on a distinction from the literature, explain it in one sentence in your own words. And please mark honestly the steps where we're not really doing desert at all, we're doing incentives or deterrence or politics — I'd rather have that labeled as a values call than have it smuggled in as if it were a finding.

Run the finished procedure on three of the cases above, including at least one where it gives an answer the board will push back on hard. Then tell me which single step is the weakest and where the whole thing will definately misfire on us.
