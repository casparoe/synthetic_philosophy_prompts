I've been going round on voting methods again (yes, I know, far too many blog posts about this), and I've noticed I don't actually have a principle. I know the Gibbard–Satterthwaite thing: with three or more outcomes and no restrictions on preferences, the only strategy-proof rules are dictatorial ones. So "it can be gamed" can't be the objection, because that's every rule that isn't a dictatorship. But I still have fairly strong reactions to particular cases, and I want to know what I'm implicitly running.

My verdicts:

1. Plurality, three candidates, my guy on 8%. I vote my second choice. Doesn't count against plurality. Everyone can see it coming, it's stable, it settles into blocs, and the move is the same move every time — you don't need to be clever or well-informed to find it.

2. Instant runoff, where there are profiles in which a candidate would have done better if some of their own supporters had ranked them lower, and where ranking a hopeless candidate first can knock out a strong rival. This has apparently happened in real elections, though I'm hazy on the details. Does count against IRV — but only mildly. Oddly it feels worse to me than 1, despite needing much better information to pull off.

3. Borda: bury your main rival at the bottom of your ranking, or get your side to run near-clones of your candidate. Serious strike. The incentive isn't a corner case — there's basically always something to gain by exaggerating your ranking, whatever the numbers look like.

4. Approval: where I put my approval threshold. Barely bothers me at all. It feels less like strategy and more like calibrating how generous I'm feeling, and I can do it without knowing the polls.

5. Random dictator — draw one ballot at random and do whatever it says. Strategy-proof, since you only matter if you're drawn. I reject it anyway. It's strategy-proof by being indifferent to almost every ballot; it isn't aggregating anything.

6. A rule where turning up and voting sincerely can cause your own favourite to lose (the no-show thing). This is the worst defect on my list. Worse than being manipulable, because it punishes the exact behaviour I least want a rule to punish.

7. Thirty per cent of voters coordinate through an app; no single one of them could gain by misreporting alone, but the bloc can. Counts against the rule — and it counts more than I'm comfortable with, given that the theorem I keep citing is about lone voters.

8. The single-peaked escape: put everyone on a left–right line and take the median, and it's strategy-proof. Doesn't impress me. Real electorates aren't single-peaked on one dimension, and if you try to infer the dimension from the ballots instead of assuming it, misreporting your whole ranking in order to move the axis is back on the table.

Here's my stab at a principle, which I suspect is wrong: a manipulation is a genuine defect only if it's poll-robust — meaning a voter who knows only their own preferences plus public polling to within a few points could still expect to gain by misreporting — and only if the rule is responsive, i.e. sincere ballots can actually determine the winner; and defects that punish sincerity or participation are worse than defects that merely reward guile.

So: test that against the eight cases, tell me where it breaks, and repair it rather than replacing it wholesale unless it's hopeless. I want one principle, stated precisely enough that someone else could apply it, with a note on which case motivates each clause. Then give me its verdicts on cases I haven't listed — at least three, and I'd like you to include: STAR (score everything, then automatic runoff between the top two), a grading rule like majority judgement where people grade candidates rather than ranking them and the median grade wins, and a made-up one I invented this morning: approve as many candidates as you like, then hold a lottery between the top two approval-getters with the odds tilted by how far apart they are.

Please commit. I don't want a menu of three candidate principles with tradeoffs for me to pick between — pick one and defend it, say plainly which of my verdicts you'd overturn and why, and be honest about any verdict of mine your principle just can't accommodate. Also flag where you're least confident, particularly on whether the poll-robustness clause is doing real work or whether I've just stuck a number on a vibe, and whether the responsiveness clause is secretly doing all the labour. And if you think what I'm really tracking isn't manipulability at all but something else — say the rule being legible to the voters it governs — say so and tell me which of my verdicts gave it away.
