I work in pharmacoepidemiology, mostly with observational claims data, and on two separate projects I have run into a configuration that I cannot name. I would like to know whether it has an established label, and, more than that, whether what I am looking at is really one phenomenon or two that I am conflating.

The configuration, stated for the simpler of the two cases. Comparing a treated group with an untreated group on a binary adverse outcome, the crude comparison favours the untreated group: treated patients have, as I recall, a few percentage points more events. Adjusting for a pre-treatment covariate with four levels reverses the comparison: within every one of the four levels the treated group does better, by margins that are not trivially small. Adjusting further, for a second pre-treatment covariate crossed with the first, reverses it again: in fifteen of the sixteen finer cells the treated group does worse. In the second dataset the same thing happened at three levels of aggregation before the cells became too thin to estimate anything, and each reversal was in the same direction relative to the level above it. I have since satisfied myself with a small constructed example that nothing arithmetical forbids this, so I am not asking whether it can occur; I am asking what it is.

Three things I think I can rule out. It is not Simpson's paradox proper as usually stated, which is a single reversal between an aggregate and one partition of it; here the reversal recurs, and the object that reverses at the third level is not the grand aggregate but the stratum one level up. It is not qualitative interaction in the clinical-trials sense, which is a difference in the sign of the effect between subgroups at the same level of analysis, not between levels of aggregation. And it is not non-collapsibility, which as I understand it is a shift in magnitude between a marginal and a conditional estimand that occurs even in the absence of confounding, and which is not a claim about the sign. I have also come across the regression-textbook talk of suppressor variables, and epidemiologists' talk of negative confounding, for the case where a coefficient changes sign when a covariate is added, but these seem to me to describe one step of what I am seeing rather than the pattern, and they carry no implication that the flip will recur if you keep going.

So I want the carving, not the label. Specifically: does the phenomenon require the reversal to be strict at every cell at the deeper level, or does one contrary cell still count; does it require the deeper estimates to be individually significant, given that the cells thin out as one descends; can the alternation continue indefinitely if the covariates are continuous and finer partitions are always available, or must it terminate; and does the fact that the sign alternates impose any constraint at all on the sign of the estimate one gets from adjusting for everything? I also want to know whether I am describing two separable things — a purely algebraic fact about nested partitions, and a distinct epistemic predicament, namely that every variable in these hierarchies is an ordinary pre-treatment confounder, so the standard causal advice is to adjust for all of them, and yet the sign I would report then depends on how many confounders happen to have been measured, with nothing in the data telling me whether I have finished.

If there is a name for this, give it and say how it is demarcated from Simpson's paradox and from the near-misses above. If there is not, say so plainly, and propose a term with a definition tight enough to settle the boundary cases I listed, rather than a name alone.
