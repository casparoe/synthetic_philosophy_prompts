I convene a graduate reading group that has spent two years on Marx and machinery, and it has hardened into two readings. The first, call it the forces-first reading, takes the passage on fixed capital and machinery in the Grundrisse known as the Fragment on Machines as the decisive text: there Marx argues roughly that the development of fixed capital indicates the degree to which general social knowledge has become a direct productive force, that labour time ceases thereby to be the measure of wealth, and that capital's own drive to replace living labour with machinery brings into being the material conditions for a society in which that knowledge could be collectively reappropriated. On this reading automation is a real historical tendency toward the reduction of necessary labour time, and the contradiction between the development of the productive forces and the value relation does the explanatory work. The second, the relation-first reading, treats that passage as a speculative excursus that Capital supersedes: machinery is never a neutral productive force whose effects can be read off it, the same machine that would shorten the working day lengthens it and intensifies labour under capital precisely because of amortisation and the competitive struggle for relative surplus value, and to speak of productive forces developing independently of the capital relation is to smuggle back a teleology Marx gave up.

Write the document the two camps should have produced at the end of that year: the adversarial-collaboration statement both could sign. No introduction and no closing paragraph.

1. Exactly five numbered agreed claims, each one or two sentences, that both readings can sign — claims about what machinery does under capitalism, about what the Fragment asserts, and about what neither side is maintaining (neither, for instance, holds that machinery was introduced in order to lighten labour). Phrase them so that neither camp would call them slanted or empty.

2. A table of exactly five rows, one per crux, with these columns: (a) the crux stated neutrally; (b) the formulation of its own position the forces-first side would accept; (c) the formulation of its own position the relation-first side would accept; (d) one concrete case or piece of evidence both would accept as bearing on the crux; (e) each side's prediction about that case, recorded in advance. If you judge a crux to be verbal rather than substantive, say so in column (a) and add one clause on what the substantive disagreement behind the words is.

The cruxes I want covered, unless you have a better fifth: whether the reduction of necessary labour time is a tendency of automation as such or is systematically offset within the capital relation; whether general intellect names a productive force that capital harnesses and that could be reappropriated, or is only a description of capital's command over knowledge with no subject corresponding to it; whether the contradiction between forces and relations of production does explanatory work or is a teleological leftover; whether automation in fact lowers capital's dependence on living labour once maintenance, repair and software work are counted; and whether machinery that absorbs workers' know-how into its own operation — a model trained on the shopfloor's own records, say — is better described as real subsumption or as expropriation of skill, and whether the choice of description makes any difference to the wage.

Prefer stipulated test cases over empirical claims you cannot verify without a library: for example, the same automated plant run as a worker-owned cooperative competing in the same market, or a fleet of machines whose profitability depends on continuous maintenance by a small, highly skilled workforce. If you reach for a historical episode, keep to one broad enough to be safe, such as the nineteenth-century British factory acts limiting the working day. Cite nothing: no sources, no bibliographies, no block quotations. I want a text the two camps could sign offline, in which anything from Marx or his readers is paraphrase rather than wording anyone would have to look up.
