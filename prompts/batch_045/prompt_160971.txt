Hey — hoping you can help with something that's been bugging me. I'm not a philosopher; I read about animal cognition as a hobby (kept a reef tank for years and got nerdy about it), so I'm coming at this from the outside.

Here's the pattern I keep running into. Someone points at a behaviour and says this is what a creature that actually feels things looks like: a fish that keeps rubbing the side of its body against the gravel for hours after being injected with something that stings; a bee that gets shaken the way a predator would shake it and then won't go back to the flowers with a certain smell, for days afterwards; a crayfish that got zapped repeatedly and afterwards just sits in the dark part of the tank even on days when nothing is zapping it. And then someone else says: fine, but that's a reflex, or a fixed action pattern, or learning wired up by evolution — none of it needs anybody to be home inside. So the first person escalates: the animal trades off a shock against a food it really wants, or it transfers what it learned in one setup to a totally different one. And the second person says: sure, more elaborate wiring. And it never stops. However fancy the behaviour gets, the second person can always posit an unconscious mechanism that outputs exactly that behaviour, and the first person never acquires a new *kind* of evidence, because behaviour is all there is to point at, and every piece of it has already been absorbed.

The bit I want a name for is the structural thing: the "no mind needed" explanation doesn't get any more expensive as it gets more complicated, so it can soak up evidence forever without ever paying for it, while the other side pays full price every time and can never get ahead. I don't think it's just the problem of other minds, because that one is usually framed as a general puzzle about knowing any mind at all, including human ones, and nobody here is doubting other humans. And I've seen people invoke a rule of thumb — don't reach for a fancy mental explanation when a humbler mechanism covers the data — which somebody told me is named after some long-dead psychologist. That feels close but wrong: it's advice about which explanation to prefer, not a description of why one side's explanations cost nothing.

One more wrinkle, so you don't hand me something too simple. Whatever I'm describing, it isn't plain stinginess about animal minds, because it seems to switch off around animals that are very unlike us. Show people a bee doing something half as elaborate as the fish thing and plenty of them will say yes, that one's conscious — the discount seems to track how much the creature resembles us, not how much evidence there is.

So: does this have an established name, and if not, coin one and define it tightly enough that I can tell it apart from ordinary scepticism about other minds, from the humbler-mechanism rule, and from just being wrong about a particular species? I'm after the carving, not the label — if the nearest established concept only half fits, tell me exactly where it stops fitting. And where are the edges: does the thing survive if you switch from behavioural evidence to evidence about mechanisms and brain structures, or does it just move house — and if it moves, is that the same phenomenon wearing a hat or a second one? Please don't point me at papers or reading lists, I've got no library access from here and couldn't follow up anyway; just give me the shape of it in your own words. Also say how confident you are in what you're claiming and flag the part you're least sure of — I'd rather have one honest wobble than a tidy answer. Last thing: write it as continuous prose, no headings, bullets, bold text or tables, just paragraphs like you were writing me an email.
