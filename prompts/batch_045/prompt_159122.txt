Hey — I host a small politics-and-philosophy podcast called *Unforced Error*, and next weeks guest is a political theorist named Miriam Ashgrove. Her new paper tries to rebuild Chesterton's fence as an actual decision rule rather than a bumper sticker: roughly, before you dismantle an inherited practice you owe an account of what it was *doing*, because social practices carry tacit, dispersed knowledge that nobody has in their head, and because removal is usually irreversible — tearing the fence down destroys the option of putting it back cheaply. I want eight interview questions that make her do more than restate that, and I dont want any of them to be the kind of question she can answer with a slogan.

Here's the territory I want covered, roughly one question each (merge things if it flows better):

1. Why the burden of proof is asymmetric at all. The preservationist also seems to owe something. Expect her to talk about baselines and about the fact that the practice is already paid for and already coordinated around — press on whether "existing" is doing moral work or just causal work.

2. Whether the principle is really about *ignorance* or really about *irreversibility*. If it's irreversibility and option value, then it says nothing about easily reversible arrangements, it can actually favor *removing* things (a fence that itself blocks future options), and it isn't distinctively Burkean — any consequentialist with a real-options model gets there. That reduction worries me and I want it put to her.

3. The self-stultification worry: you often can't learn what an institution is doing except by removing it, the way you learn what a component does by ablating it in an experiment. So the rule seems to demand knowledge it forbids you from acquiring. Her likely move is pilots, small-scale trials, federalism as laboratories of democracy — push on whether that collapses the view into "experiment first," and whether experimentation is exactly what Burkeans are supposed to distrust.

4. Functionalism about practices. Chesterton's parable assumes the fence is there *for a reason*, doing a job. But plenty of practices are there because somebody wanted a fence: enclosure, rent extraction, status hierarchy. Are those load-bearing too? If she says yes-but-unjust, follow up that her view then says keep unjust institutions until replacements are built, which sounds like ordinary reformism with a pause button.

5. Fragile versus self-fulfilling equilibria. Some institutions hold only as long as everyone expects them to, so ablation is unrecoverable; others are bad *only* because everyone believes in them, so removal cures itself. Ask how she tells those apart in advance.

6. Who knows, and what kind of knowing. The builder may be dead or may have lied, users can't articulate what they're doing, the knowledge may be in the practice rather than in any head. Follow up: if it's tacit, no amount of interviewing surfaces it, so we're back to removal being the only test.

7. Self-application. Is the fence principle itself an inherited practice that owes us an account of what it's for? And who grades the answer — what stops "understand it first" from being an unmeetable bar, given how little anyone understands about complex social systems?

8. The error tally and the democratic objection. Make her name a specific reform that fence-style caution predicted would go badly and that went fine, and say what kind of evidence would actually count against the principle. Then put Burke's partnership-across-generations idea to her: why do the dead get a vote at all, and does the principle give them one in cases where the practice was imposed on their contemporaries rather than chosen by them?

For each question give me: (a) how I should actually say it out loud, one or two sentences, in plain speech; (b) the answer she is most likely to give, in her own terms and as charitably as you can make it; (c) the follow up keyed to *that specific answer*, not a generic "but what about X."
