After forty years in experimental physics I am used to attaching error bars to a claim rather than saying that it depends, and I have lately been reading on paternalism and the ethics of nudging. Consider a piece of choice architecture I have not seen discussed, which I will call a stake-gated default: an institution sets a default for a class of decisions, but the default's force is engineered to decay as a measurable proxy for what is at stake for the chooser rises — below a threshold the choice is made for you unless you intervene, while above it the default is suspended, the question is presented as genuinely open, and the earlier existence of the default is disclosed at that moment. Assume the proxy is imperfect but not arbitrary.

Give me a single number: your credence that a stake-gated default, compared with an ordinary default of the same content, is not wrong in the way that makes paradigm nudges morally suspect. Then decompose that number — how much turns on whether the objectionable feature of a standard nudge is the bypassing or slighting of the chooser's deliberative capacity rather than the manipulation of outcomes or the bare fact of influence; how much on whether any stakes proxy could track deliberative engagement well enough for the gating to do real work; how much on whether undisclosed influence is independently wrong-making; and whatever further term you think belongs. Give a number and a one-line justification for each, show how they combine, and tell me which term your final figure is most sensitive to and what evidence would move it most.
