I am putting together a seminar module on automated decision-making for final-year philosophy and public policy students, built around a screening of Gattaca (Andrew Niccol, 1997). I want the session to do more than use the film as a cautionary illustration of "bias in algorithms", so I need a reading of it that takes a specific technical literature seriously and is willing to say where the film and the literature do not fit.

The literature I want it read against is the family of impossibility results for algorithmic fairness. I will state them in my own terms so that you do not have to reconstruct or cite anything: when the outcome being predicted has a different prevalence in two populations, a single predictor cannot in general be calibrated in both populations — a given score cannot carry the same expected outcome for members of each — while also having equal false-positive and false-negative rates across them; and a third natural demand, that a given decision threshold carry the same predictive value regardless of population, is likewise not jointly satisfiable with those. There are also well-known tensions between group-level criteria of that kind and "treat like cases alike" individual fairness, which turns everything on the choice of a similarity metric. Assume these results as background; I know the papers and do not want references, a bibliography, or suggestions for further reading — only the reading of the film.

Here is what I want. Work out what Gattaca's regime is actually doing in those terms: the genetic registry classifies everyone, in-valids are excluded from professional employment despite discrimination being nominally illegal, and the profile is used as a proxy for fitness. Then take the scenes I would show: the childhood game of chicken in the open sea and its nocturnal reprisal; Vincent's daily grooming and the borrowed samples by which he passes as a valid; Jerome's silver medal and the paralysis he inflicted on himself; Irene's undisclosed cardiac risk; Anton's investigation of the murder and the genetic sweep that follows an eyelash; Lamar's decision at the launch to falsify a result he knows is being gamed; Director Josef's killing; and Jerome's death with his medal. Say what each of these dramatizes, if anything, about calibration, error rates, proxy use, or the harms of being accurately ranked, and be explicit about scenes that illustrate nothing in the literature and would mislead the class if I presented them as illustrations.

I also want you to work through a counterfactual inside the film's world, since I plan to hand it to students as a written exercise. Suppose reformers abolish exclusion by faith-birth status and replace it with a regulated risk score: Gattaca may decline any applicant whose predicted probability of mission-critical failure exceeds a published threshold, provided the score is calibrated in both populations and rejects capable valid and capable in-valid applicants at equal rates. Assume, as the film's world suggests, that mission-relevant failure really is more common among in-valids, and that seats on a launch are scarce and governed by a hard safety constraint. Which of those requirements cannot be met together, which would Gattaca's board quietly abandon and with what justification, and would Vincent be admitted, rejected, or admitted only by luck under each surviving version? Say too what becomes of the fairness question once the problem is one of allocating a fixed number of seats rather than classifying individuals.

Finally, be an honest critic in both directions. I can see two ways the film resists this reading, and I want you to develop them or replace them with better ones: first, that mode of conception is not merely correlated with the predicted trait but close to identical with it, so this is a limiting case in which the usual trade-off — give up some accuracy to buy fairness — may not have the shape the results describe; second, that the film's objection is not fundamentally about error rates at all but about a caste order, unequal access to enhancement, and the expressive and dignitary harm of being told what you are for, none of which a fairness criterion registers. Vincent's own claim, that he got where he is on his merits, is an individual-fairness claim rather than a group one, and the ending vindicates him through a single official's discretionary act of rule-breaking — which the fairness literature would normally treat as the problem rather than the solution. Tell me how to present that tension without resolving it dishonestly, and close by saying what the film shows about statistical discrimination that the formal results and the policy papers cannot show.
