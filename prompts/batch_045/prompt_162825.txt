I am a novelist, and I am stuck on two pages of dialogue. Two brothers, both obstinate, end up bidding against each other at a charity auction where — by a rule I invented for plot reasons and later discovered is a known parlor game — the highest bidder wins the lot but the second-highest bidder also pays his final bid and gets nothing. They are soon bidding well past the item's value, locked in, each convinced the other will blink first. At the top of the scene one brother says to the other: you are throwing good money after bad. I put the line in because it is what someone would actually say, but as I rewrite it I realize I do not know what it asserts. It is being used to establish two things at once: that continuing to bid is irrational, and that the brother who continues is culpable rather than merely unlucky — foolish in a way that licenses contempt. And I have a nagging suspicion that in this particular game the line is close to inverted: once you are already in deep, raising your own bid is often the loss-minimizing move, so the fellow who escalates may be reasoning perfectly well and simply be trapped. Nothing I say about the scene will land until I know which precise theses the proverb could be expressing and which of them are true here.

The readings I can half-formulate and want you to sharpen and adjudicate include: a purely forward-looking expected-value claim (do not spend where the marginal return is negative); a stronger normative claim about sunk costs, namely that facts about what you have already spent are not merely irrelevant but positively apt to mislead you; a debiasing claim, that the proverb is aimed at a known psychological mechanism rather than at a principle of rationality; a claim about the strategic landscape, since money already spent can change what is rational later by altering other people's expectations or by making a threat credible; a claim about the framing of a sure loss rather than a risky one, where continuing looks like the only gamble left; and a final cluster of readings that are not about money at all — about character, about not compounding a mistake, about refusing to be the sort of person who quits. I also want you to test three presuppositions I think the proverb quietly makes and that I suspect are doing most of the work: that the bad money is already and irreversibly gone while the good money is still intactly yours, so that the two are separable; that the loss is certain rather than contingent on what you do next; and that the situation is non-strategic, so that what you do now does not change what anyone else will do later. Where those fail — as I think all three fail in my auction — say whether the proverb becomes false, or merely inapplicable, and whether the accusation of culpability survives when the rationality charge does not. Finally, one more thing for the menu: the same line gets used about marriages and about manuscripts people have spent a decade on, and I want to know whether anything in the analysis depends on the losses being measurable in a single currency, or whether the proverb smuggles in a precision about value that those cases do not have.
