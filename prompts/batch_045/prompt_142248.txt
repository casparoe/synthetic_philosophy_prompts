THE NOTES (they are notes, sorry, not prose)

Working title: "Parity isn't justice: what the gender-equality paradox doesn't show"

The datum. Across countries, the more gender-equal the society — on the usual aggregate indices, the ones that bundle political representation, health and survival, educational attainment and economic participation into a single score — and the richer it is, the *smaller* the share of women among STEM graduates. Scandinavia is the usual exhibit. Two readings dominate: (a) it's nature — strip away the obstacles and women's real preferences finally show up in the data; (b) it's culture — those indices measure formal equality, not the informal hierarchy that actually does the work.

§1. I want to say both readings share a mistake: they treat the revealed distribution as if it were a measurement of one thing, when it's a joint product of at least two. Call them the preference term and the conversion term. The conversion term is how strongly a given profile of preferences gets converted into an occupational distribution — think of it as the derivative of the outcome distribution with respect to the preference distribution, holding the institutional environment fixed. Penalties for career breaks, the cost of part-time work, whether caring is socialised or privatised, how much a given field punishes a non-linear CV: all of that lives in the conversion term, not in anyone's preferences. If preferences were literally identical across two countries and only conversion differed, you'd still see different gendered outcomes. So the observed gap identifies only the product, and nothing about the preference term can be read off it without a model of the conversion term. The paradox is, at bottom, an underidentification result, not a discovery about nature or about culture.

Corollary, which I quite like: there's a mechanism that generates the paradox out of a justice *improvement*. Where the welfare state de-penalises care work — socialises its cost, makes exits and re-entries cheap, doesn't wreck your pension — female-typed careers become less costly to choose. That raises their uptake. It lowers the female share of STEM. And it's a straightforward improvement in the option sets of the people concerned. Call it the de-penalisation hypothesis. It predicts the paradox with zero change in preferences and zero appeal to nature.

§2. Second worry, about the aggregate indices themselves. An axis of equality can be saturated: past some point it isn't the thing binding on the choice you're studying, so more of it changes nothing. And different locally-binding axes can push in opposite directions — de-penalisation of care widens the gap, removal of informal exclusion in hiring and promotion narrows it. Regress a gap on an aggregate score in that situation and the coefficient is an artefact of the weights in the index. So I want to propose a sign-flip diagnostic: look for episodes where one axis moves while the others are roughly held fixed, and record the sign. If the signs are heterogeneous, the cross-national correlation is telling you about index construction, not about human beings.

§3. The normative bit, and the bit I'm least sure of. What justice cares about, I want to claim, is the shape of individual option sets — what a person could have done without paying a cost that a relevantly similar person of another gender wouldn't have paid for the same choice — and not the shape of the population distribution. And these can come apart in both directions: parity can fall while justice rises (de-penalisation), and parity can rise while justice falls (if everyone is squeezed into the same few survivable careers). If that's right, cross-national parity rankings have no justice-theoretic interpretation until you bolt on a model of conversion, and neither the "it's nature" camp nor the "it's still oppression" camp gets to read its conclusion straight off the graph. What we should be measuring instead is within-country variation in penalties — say, how much a field punishes a career break — rather than national league tables of representation.
