Book club I'm in (mostly rationalist-blog types) shuts down every argument about whether a film or novel is actually any good with the same move: beauty is just taste, it's exactly like wine — you can train your palate, but there's no arguing, so there's no fact of the matter and the whole argument is pointless. One guy even said this is basically Hume's own view, which I doubt.

Can you audit the analogy properly? Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the wine, the palate, the training, the tasting conditions, the sommelier's verdict, the person who just likes the sweet stuff — then where the mapping breaks, and seperate the breaks that actually matter for the conclusion they want from the ones that are just noise.

Two things I specifically want dealt with. (1) Wine tasting has contrast and adaptation effects: the same glass tastes different depending on what you had before it, and aesthetic judging seems to do the same thing (a mediocre poem looks brilliant after a terrible one). Does that support their side or Hume's? (2) I half-remember a study where a white wine dyed red got described by tasters in red-wine terms. If training doesn't buy you reliability even there, what happens to the 'trained palate gives you a standard' move — and does it matter that what got corrupted was a sensory channel rather than a judgement?

Then: does 'no fact of the matter, so arguing is pointless' survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the whole conclusion? And is there a better analogy for beauty than wine — colour, yumminess, something else entirely — one that doesn't smuggle in the bit they need? Please answer in British English.
