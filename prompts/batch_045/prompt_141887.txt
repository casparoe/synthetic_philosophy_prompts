I was grading a stack of undergrad papers on Moore and got into a bit of an arguement with a colleage in the lounge about whether Moore is just a hedonist with extra steps, and it made me want a cleaner case for my early analytic ethics seminar next term. Here is the background I want you to assume: in Principia Ethica (1903) Moore holds that goodness is simple and indefinable, he uses the method of isolation to test intrinsic value (imagine a thing alone in an otherwise empty world; if that world is better than one without it, the thing is intrinsically good), and this is how he argues that beauty is intrinsically good, since a world with just a beautiful landscape and no one there to enjoy it is still better than an ugly world. He also treats personal affection, including friendship, as one of the greatest intrinsic goods, and he accepts the doctrine of organic unities, where the value of a whole need not be the sum of the values of its parts. Please build me a case where Moore's axiology and hedonism come apart in the direction people find hard to construct: a friendship that Moore says has intrinsic value even though neither friend is getting any pleasure from it at the time, with hedonism saying it has no intrinsic value at all. Constraints: realistic, ordinary-life scenario (no sci-fi, no demons, no perfect predictors), no longer than a paragraph, and the Moorean verdict must not come from the friends enjoying pleasant memories or expecting future pleasure; it should fall out of the friendship itself as an organic unity of loving cognition. After the scenario, walk through what each theory says and why, say which feature of the case is doing the work, what happens if that feature is removed, and how a hedonist would attack the case to stop it from counting. Please give the whole thing as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
