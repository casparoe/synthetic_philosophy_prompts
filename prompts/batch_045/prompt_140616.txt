I convene a small graduate reading group that sits between comparative economic history and the philosophy of social science, and we have now spent two sessions going in circles on whether the question "why did sustained, energy-intensive industrial growth happen in Britain rather than in Qing China?" is even well posed. Half the group thinks it is a perfectly ordinary explanatory request that happens to be empirically hard; the other half thinks the form itself carries a mistake — that once the contrast class is made explicit, the question either mutates into a different one ("why anywhere?", "why then?", "why first in one place rather than another?") or turns out to be unanswerable in principle rather than for want of evidence. I have decided that we will not settle this with more historiography, so I want a pair of constructed cases to argue over instead.

Please build me two cases to specification. Assume the usual contrastive account of why-questions: a request for explanation has the form "why P rather than Q?", where Q is the foil, and an adequate answer cites a difference-making factor between the actual case and the foil. I further want you to use the following three-part test for whether such a request is well posed. (1) Common comparison class: the actual case and the foil must be drawn from a class specifiable without reference to which of them won. (2) Non-collapse: the factor that does the explanatory work must not be the very factor that makes the foil a live candidate in the first place; if it is, the contrast is doing no independent work and the question has quietly become a different question. (3) Grain stability: the verdict about what explains P rather than Q must survive small re-descriptions of P and of Q, so that the answer is not an artifact of how finely the relata are individuated. Now design two cases, each about which of two comparable regions or organisations first adopted some new technique or practice, such that in Case A the contrastive question is well posed and genuinely answerable, and in Case B the question, despite looking exactly like the question in Case A, is ill posed. The defect in Case B must be structural and diagnosable by the test above, and it must not be any of the cheap defects: not scarcity of evidence, not chanciness or indeterminism, not chaos or sensitivity to initial conditions, not general scepticism about historical explanation. It must also be invisible from the surface: a reader given only the two narratives, without the test, should be inclined to answer both questions in the same way and should not be able to tell which case is the broken one. Constraints on the cases: realistic macrosocial material, stylised but historically plausible (two textile districts, two river-valley polities, two firms in one industry — your choice), no science fiction and no philosophical apparatus such as demons, perfect predictors or counterfactual oracles, one paragraph each, and the two stories recognisably the same kind of story so that it is the structural difference rather than the subject matter that separates them. One further specification, because otherwise the exercise is too easy: in Case A the difference-maker must be relational rather than intrinsic — a fact about how the two candidates stood to each other, or to a shared environment, rather than a property of the winner that the loser simply lacked.

After both cases, give me the demonstration that they meet the specification. For each: state the question, name the foil explicitly, and run all three conditions, saying which passes, which fails and why. Then identify the single structural feature that differs between A and B, and show the swap test — that importing that feature into A breaks A, and removing it from B repairs B — so that it is clear the feature and not the storytelling is doing the work. Then tell me how a member of my group who insists that contrastive questions of this shape are always well posed would try to escape: what re-individuation of the relata, or what reformulation of the question, would they propose, and does that manoeuvre actually restore the original question or silently change its subject? Finally, bring it back to the case we started from: on your own account, is "why Britain and not China?" well posed, ill posed, or well posed only after a specific repair? Say exactly what the repair consists in, and say what it costs — what the repaired question can no longer tell us that the original one seemed to promise. Please argue it out rather than pointing me at literature; I have no interest in a reading list here, and I would rather have your reasoning than your sources.
