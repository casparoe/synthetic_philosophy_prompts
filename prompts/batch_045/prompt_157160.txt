I host a small philosophy podcast, and on Wednesday I'm recording an episode with a moral philosopher I'll call 'M.' — she has spent several years defending a thesis that my friend and I argued about until well past midnight on Saturday and never settled: that hypocrisy never deprives a blamer of standing, because standing to blame is just the authority to invoke a norm that genuinely binds its target, and everything people treat as a defect in the hypocrite's blaming is really one of two other things — either an epistemic defeater (her own conduct is evidence that she does not sincerely accept the norm, or that she is an unreliable detector of violations of it) or a free-standing vice of unfairness, self-righteousness or bad faith, which makes her blaming unattractive, even ugly, without making it unauthorized. She also insists on a sharp line between standing and the all-things-considered question of whether one should blame, and she is very quick on her feet: nearly every objection we threw at her on Saturday got reclassified within a sentence as 'that's sincerity, not standing' or 'that's unfairness, not standing.' My friend took the opposite side: blame is a form of moral address that presupposes the blamer's own acceptance of the norm as binding on her, so a hypocrite's blaming misfires structurally, rather in the way a demand misfires when the speaker would not accept it being made of her.

Three cases carried most of the argument, and I want the episode to press on them. The first was a mother who smoked for thirty years and now tells her seventeen-year-old daughter not to vape: my friend said the mother can say plenty of useful things to her daughter but cannot blame her, I said that sounds like confusing the value of the warning with the question of authority, and M. would presumably say the mother's record is at most evidence about her sincerity. The second, which neither of us could handle, was a university that issues a public statement condemning fossil-fuel lobbying while its endowment holds fossil-fuel assets: here the individual-psychological story M. leans on seems to run out, since an institution is not exactly sincere or insincere, and yet the 'you're one to talk' reaction is if anything louder than in the interpersonal case. The third was one I invented on the drive home and liked better than either: a person who blames you today for something she fully intends to do herself tomorrow, explicitly as a device for binding herself — prospective hypocrisy, where there is no past violation to be evidence of anything, and where the blaming seems to do real normative work. Its mirror image is the case where the blamer's own violation was far harder for her to avoid than the target's was, which I started calling asymmetric exposure, and which made me suspect that what actually tracks our judgments is not compliance at all but something like whether the blamer is demanding more of others than she is willing to demand of herself under comparable pressure.

What I'd like from you is eight to ten interview questions, in the order you'd ask them, none of which let her simply restate the thesis or tell me what drew her to the topic. I want at least one question pressing the moral-address picture of blame as a speech act that can misfire; at least one on the fact that ordinary blame practice treats 'you're one to talk' as a move that shifts something back onto the blamer, and on what the practice would look like if it were tracking a real standing condition rather than etiquette; at least one on whether the epistemic-defeater story can be neutralized by avowed self-blame (someone who says, sincerely and with self-reproach, 'I do exactly this, and you should not'), and if so whether introspective avowal is really enough; at least one on the institutional or collective case; at least one on prospective and self-binding hypocrisy; and at least one on asymmetric exposure and on whether difficulty of compliance, rather than compliance itself, is what is doing the work. Please build the sequence so that her two stock reclassifications get progressively harder to reach for — anticipate the reply she is most likely to make to each question and give me the follow-up that closes it off or forces her to pay a price I can name on air — and close with one question about what would count as evidence against her view: what feature of our blaming practice, or of the concept of standing, she would have to concede she cannot explain.
