For the past eighteen months I have been reading my way into the predictive processing and active inference literature, in preparation for a short piece I am drafting for a reading group I convene here in retirement. My own training was in experimental physical chemistry, and I find that I read these papers the way I once read papers reporting a fit to a kinetic model: I look for how many quantities the data actually pin down, and how many were free to move. I have noticed a recurring pattern in how findings get accommodated, and I would like to know whether it has a name, or whether I am running together several different things that the literature keeps apart.

The pattern, described without the vocabulary that I suspect would settle the question: a finding arrives, and it is accommodated by adjusting the strength of what the system expects against the weight given to what the senses deliver. When the adjustment in one direction looks strained — the expectation would have to be implausibly rigid, or the incoming signal implausibly faint — the very same finding is re-accommodated by shifting the weight to the other term. This seems possible because only some combination of the two shows up in the observable behaviour, so a change in one can always be compensated by a change in the other. And the choice is made after the fact, without a measurement that fixes either term on its own.

Two cases that set me off. The first is the disagreement I have encountered over certain perceptual differences in autistic subjects: one account explains them by saying that the expectations are unusually weak, so experience is dominated by the data, while another explains them by saying that the expectations are intact and the incoming signal carries unusually high weight. These strike me as mirror images, and I cannot see what observation would decide between them. The second is the fate of the midbrain dopaminergic response to unexpected reward: within this framework I have seen it read as the magnitude of an error signal, and elsewhere as something like the confidence or expected reliability attaching to that error signal. Whichever reading is adopted seems to depend on which anomaly is currently being absorbed. It is possible I have misrepresented both debates; if so, say so.

My own instinct is to describe this as a redundancy of description rather than a dispute between theories: the two accounts are not rivals so much as the same formal description with the explanatory load slid along a free direction, and the sliding is what lets the framework accommodate a result it did not predict. In my old field we would have called the analogous thing a choice of gauge, and we would not have treated the choice as a discovery. There is also, I believe, a literature in systems biology about models whose parameters are barely constrained by the data they are fitted to, which looks like the same complaint in different clothes.

So my question: is there an established concept for exactly this, and if the nearest concepts only partly fit, what does my description differ from in each case, and which fits best? I am less interested in a label than in the carving — what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie. In particular I would like to know what would distinguish a legitimate use of this kind of adjustment from a degenerate one; whether the reply that the framework states a principle rather than a model, with the substantive content supplied elsewhere, actually answers the worry or changes the subject; and where my gauge analogy holds and where it breaks. I should add that I have no library access from here, so please argue the point rather than direct me to sources.
