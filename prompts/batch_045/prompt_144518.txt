I should say first that I have no formal training in philosophy — I read a little of it in the evenings for my own amusement — and I work in technical compliance for a small firm that supplies own-brand clothing to high-street shops, so a large part of my week is spent with the little pictograms on care labels: the washtub, the triangle, the iron, the circle, the bar or double bar underneath, and the cross struck through any of them. What puzzles me is the gap between how the system presents itself and how it actually works. It presents itself as a language without words: the same label goes into garments sold in a dozen countries, and the whole selling point is that nobody has to read anything. But the quantity that matters most — temperature — is not pictured at all, it is printed as a numeral in the middle of the tub, whereas I gather the North Americans use dots instead, so the picture itself carries only the rough ordering and the exact value is handed back to a written convention. The symbols are owned, licensed and legally protected, and the standard that defines them is revised every few years by a committee; and strictly, as I understand it, the label states only the harshest treatment the garment will tolerate, not what is needed or sufficient, though everyone I ask reads a crossed-out iron as an instruction. Meanwhile the same design world that trusts pictures to explain themselves keeps putting words back in:EXIT signs, captions, the little booklet that ships with the garment.

I got interested in all this because I recently came across Otto Neurath and his picture language, and I would like to know what the people who designed my care labels are implicitly committed to. My understanding, which you should correct if it is wrong, is roughly this: that Neurath and his collaborators showed larger quantities by repeating the same small figure rather than by drawing a bigger one, on the ground that you cannot tell whether a bigger shape is meant to be compared by height or by area; that they kept the figures flat and unperspective; that a person was given the job of turning raw statistics into a self-explanatory chart, and that this shaping was understood to involve judgement; that Neurath nonetheless insisted his system was only a helping thing that always travelled with words, and called it a technique rather than a full language, even while he had a slogan pinned up in his office to the effect that words divide people and pictures bring them together. The thing I cannot fit together is that the same man is famous for a metaphor about sailors who must rebuild their ship plank by plank on the open sea and can never put it into dry dock to start again from the keel — a picture, if I have it right, of there being no clean, given, pre-theoretical starting point for what we say.

So here is what I would like. Take the practice I have described — not just the care label but the wider family it belongs to: textless assembly leaflets, airport pictograms, the red circle with a slash meaning prohibited — and reconstruct the theory of meaning, or of signs, that it only really makes sense under. Does it presuppose that pictures are non-arbitrary in a way words are not, and that this is what makes them cross borders; or can a thoroughly conventionalist account explain the practice just as well, in which case the practice is more underdetermined than it looks? Please separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely suits it, and say whether the parts of it hang together: is a system that crosses things out to negate, underlines things to weaken, and repeats things to count already a grammar rather than a set of likenesses, and if so does the appeal of calling it natural survive? Then tell me what would have to change in practice if the implicit theory were given up — in particular, whether a label that dropped the numerals and went purely ordinal, using only dots and bars, would be more faithful to the picture-language idea or simply less informative, and whether a label could honestly be made to say what is required rather than what is tolerable. And if there is a real tension between the practice's faith in the self-explanatory picture and Neurath's own suspicion of pristine starting points, I would like you to say what it is and whether his calling his system a mere technique dissolves it or merely postpones it. I am not asking for anything you would need to look up: no references, no footnotes, no reading suggestions, just the argument, worked through in ordinary language, in British English.
