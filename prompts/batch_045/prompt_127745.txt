I've been hanging around a tenants' union for a couple of years and I keep running into this line: "the base determines the superstructure." One guy in the group uses it constantly. The way he puts it: law, religion, morality, what people think is normal about gender, what counts as art — all of it is just a reflection of who owns the means of production, so until you change the ownership, the ideas won't budge.

The other week a few of us were arguing about whether it's worth trying to change anyone's mind about anything. I was defending doing outreach on, I don't know, the idea that landlords are parasites, and he basically said it's a waste of breath. His version: the ideology follows the economics automatically, arguments don't do anything, go organize instead. I think that's too strong but I can't say exactly where it goes wrong, because when he says it I sort of believe him and five minutes later I dont.

So: what are the actual precise claims hiding in that one sentence? I want them laid out as a menu from the boring to the wild, with a verdict on each — which ones nearly everyone would accept, which are real and arguable but not obvious, which are just false. And the part I actually care about: which of them would he need in order for "don't bother arguing, change the economy first" to follow? Because I suspect the reading that gets him his conclusion is a different reading from the one that sounds obviously true when he says it, and that the switch happens without anyone noticing.

A few things I keep tripping over, if it helps you see what I'm asking:

- Countries with basically the same economy have completely different moralities about stuff like welfare or guns. That feels like a counterexample, but he says those differences are themselves explained by history that was economic further back. That feels like it could explain anything, which makes me think it explains nothing. Is there a version of the claim you could actually be wrong about, and what would count as being wrong about it?
- Loads of ideas hang around long after the economy that supposedly produced them is gone, and some ideas seem to show up first and change the economy afterward — that protestant work ethic thing everyone's heard of, or the way the "shareholder value" talk of the 80s seems to have rewritten how companies actually behave rather than the other way round. Do those kill the claim or just complicate it?
- I read somewhere that Engels walked it back late in life in a letter, saying the economy is only the ultimately determining factor and that ideas react back on it and sometimes determine the shape things take. If that's right, is that hedge a real qualification or does it make the whole thing too soft to argue with?

Here's my own attempt at a version I can actually believe, and I want to know whether it's just a weaker version of something that already exists or whether it's broken: the base doesn't decide which ideas exist, it decides what ideas cost. An idea that would get you fired or make you a pariah in your town doesn't spread no matter how good the arguements are, and an idea that costs nothing to repeat spreads or doesn't on the strength of its arguments alone. So economics sets the price list, not the beliefs. Does that still count as historical materialism, is it interesting enough to be worth holding, and what does it predict about the cases above that the plain version gets wrong?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — which reading of the slogan his argument actually needs, and whether that reading survives — then give me the menu and the reasoning after. No preamble, don't restate my question back at me, and please skip any "you should read X" stuff, I'm not near a library and it won't help me.
