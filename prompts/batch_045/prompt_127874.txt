I have noticed a pattern about evidence that I cannot name, and I want to know whether anyone has carved it — and if not, how it should be carved.

A friend who once took a philosophy of science course tells me there is a famous puzzle about theories that fit facts you already knew before you met the theory. From the one-paragraph summaries I have been able to find, that puzzle seems to be about the order of discovery in the history of science: the fact was on the books first, the theory was built later, and somehow the fact is supposed to stop counting. That is part of what I have in mind, but only part, and not the part I most want explained. What interests me is a difference between people rather than a difference between dates.

Four situations.

(1) I teach an undergraduate survey in a field where a well-known hypothesis H is standardly supported by a fact F that appears in every textbook. My students, meeting H and F together for the first time, find H strongly confirmed: "of course — that is exactly what you would expect if H were true." I find the same fit unimpressive. Nothing about our evidence differs in any way I can state: I have no counterexample, no competing hypothesis I prefer, no reason to doubt F. What differs is that I know how F became famous. People working on H publicised it, wrote it into the textbooks, and — as far as I can tell — H was to some extent adjusted until it accommodated F.

(2) A student who has never heard of H listens to a podcast in which H and F are presented together and is bowled over. She mentions it to her roommate, who gets the same jolt, and passes it on. None of them has any idea that the fit between H and F is ancient news among specialists. It looks as though a quantity of apparent confirmation has been manufactured out of a fact that every person in the chain already had — and manufactured merely by arranging who hears what in what order.

(3) In a seminar, a colleague who knows nothing of H sketches something very like it, and someone points out that it would explain F. Everyone in the room, specialists included, feels a small genuine shock, and afterwards several people say they now regard H-style hypotheses as more likely than they did that morning. Yet nothing new about the world was learned: F was already known, and the specialists in the room already knew the connection.

(4) When I explicitly tell my students the history — that F owes its fame to H, and that H was partly shaped to accommodate F — the glow fades for most of them, though not for all. For me it never returns. That makes me suspect part of what I am tracking is only a feeling. But it cannot be only a feeling: my confidence in H's predictions in some new domain did not change when I learned the history, but my willingness to cite F as a *reason* did, and that seems like a change in something normative rather than merely in how H strikes me.

So my questions. Is there an established concept for this — the apparent confirmation that a fit between a fact and a theory seems to acquire or lose depending on the route by which it came to a particular person's attention, and the way it can be passed from person to person by testimony? If the nearest established concepts are several, lay them out and say exactly how the thing I have described differs from each, which (if any) fits best, and why. I am not asking for a label. I want to know what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie. In particular:

(a) Is any of this something a Bayesian should care about, or is it entirely a fact about human psychology — a sense of fittingness that tracks nothing rationally relevant? If there is something to care about, is it roughly this: the recipient of the fit is missing a correction for a filtering process (only hypotheses that happened to fit survived long enough to be told to her), the correction is real, and she cannot estimate its size? What, if anything, should such an agent do — and is "suspending judgement" even available to her, given that she must still act on H?

(b) Does it aggregate? If a hundred undergraduates each get the boost, has the community acquired a hundred boosts, one boost, or none? Does the total stock behave like a currency that is divided, so that wide transmission dilutes rather than multiplies?

(c) Can it be regenerated? Suppose a field collectively forgets that F was used in constructing and publicising H, or a new subfield grows up that has genuinely forgotten. Is H confirmed by F again? Is that absurd, or is it simply what novelty ever amounted to?

(d) What should an honest teacher do with the history? Withholding it seems like a species of deception; telling it destroys something that the students had. Is there a difference between learning the history of this particular case and being warned in the abstract that some of the evidence one is about to meet was used in building the theory?
