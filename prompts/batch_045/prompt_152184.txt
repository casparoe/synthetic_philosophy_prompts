I've been reading about algorithmic fairness on my own — no formal training in statistics or philosophy — and I keep hitting the same claim in popular form: that a risk score cannot satisfy several natural fairness requirements at once. As I understand it, the standard example is a score used to make a binary decision (release or detain, lend or refuse), where you want (i) the score to *mean* the same thing in every group — among people given 7 out of 10, the outcome really happens about 70% of the time, whichever group they belong to; and (ii) the false positive rate to be the same across groups; and (iii) the false negative rate to be the same across groups. Every explanation I have found puts the blame on base rates: the outcome simply occurs more often in one group than the other, and that fact alone forces the tradeoff. I follow that argument, but I don't believe it is the whole story, and I want a case that isolates whatever is left over.

Please construct one for me. It should be a realistic setting — a bank, a hospital, a parole board, a hiring pipeline; nothing science-fictional — and short enough to state in a single paragraph, with numbers small enough that a friend with a calculator can check every step (think a hundred people per group, four or five distinct score values). Here is what the case has to do:

1. There are two groups of equal size, and the outcome the score predicts occurs at exactly the same rate in both. No base-rate difference anywhere in the setup, so the usual explanation is unavailable.
2. The score is computed without taking group membership as an input, and isn't secretly a proxy for it. The same cutoff is applied to everyone's score at decision time.
3. The score is honestly calibrated in both groups, in the sense of (i): no miscalibration, no fudging, no deliberate bias. Assume also that the outcome is recorded the same way for both groups and that there is no label bias, so the difference can't be blamed on the target variable being measured badly for one group.
4. Despite 1–3, there is no cutoff — and no pair of different cutoffs, one per group, if you want to consider those — at which the resulting decision rule has both equal false positive rates and equal false negative rates.
5. The escape hatches are closed: the rule has to be a real rule. In each group it must flag some people and not others. "Flag nobody," "flag everybody," and "flag nobody in one group and everybody in the other" are not allowed to count as ways of making the error rates equal, and I want you to say explicitly what happens at every cutoff worth considering, including the degenerate ones, so I can see that nothing is being smuggled in.
6. The conflict must not be a disguised version of the base-rate story. Nothing in the case should make one group worse off in the sense of the outcome being more frequent; whatever does the work has to be a different feature of the setup.

After the scenario, I'd like: the check for each of the three requirements, with the arithmetic shown; a clear statement of which requirement has to be given up and what exactly is lost; an honest answer about which single feature of the case is doing the work; a demonstration of what happens when that feature is removed (for instance, if the scores were distributed identically across the two groups — does the conflict vanish?); and, from both directions, how someone who thinks calibration is the only genuine fairness requirement would attack the case, and how someone who thinks equal error rates are would attack it.

Two more things. First, my own hunch is that the work is being done by how well the scores separate people *within* each group — that in one group the scores are genuinely honest but coarse and uninformative, while in the other they sort people cleanly — so that the tradeoff is about the sharpness of the information rather than about any average level. I can't make that precise; please test it, sharpen it if it's right, and correct it if I'm conflating two different things. Second, I want to know whether this case really shows something the base-rate version doesn't, or whether it turns out to be the same impossibility wearing different clothes.

What I need it for: I'm arguing with a friend who says the whole impossibility literature is just the base-rate point restated in technical language, and if I write about this I want to be able to say plainly what the tradeoff consists in. So the case has to *generate* the conflict from the definitions of the requirements and the arithmetic of the numbers, not stipulate it — and if what I've described can't actually be built, please say so and build the nearest thing that can.
