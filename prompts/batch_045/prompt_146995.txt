Two friends of mine — call them Dev and Miriam — spent most of last night arguing about Kavka's toxin puzzle, and I then spent another hour arguing with each of them separately, so I am not a neutral reporter; my own contributions mostly amounted to restating whichever side I had last heard. I genuinely cannot tell who has the better of it, which is why I'm writing this out.

Here is the case as they framed it. An eccentric billionaire who can detect your mental states with perfect reliability tells you at midday on Monday that he will pay you a very large sum at midnight if, by then, you have formed the intention to drink a dose of toxin at midday on Tuesday. The toxin makes you wretchedly ill for roughly a day and has no lasting effects. The money is paid whether or not you drink. You know now, and know that you will know on Tuesday, that on Tuesday you will have no reason to drink and decisive reason not to, and so you are as close to certain as makes no difference that you will not drink. The question between them was not whether it would be *rational* to collect the money — both granted that — but whether a person in that situation can form the intention at all, and what it would show if they could or couldn't.

Their exchange went roughly as follows. (1) Dev opened by saying the nausea is a red herring: run the case with a harmless but pointless action instead — intending to drink a glass of water at Tuesday midday, say — and you get the same inability, so what is doing the work cannot be aversion. (2) Miriam replied that the substitution doesn't give the same result, and that this is precisely the point: with a harmless action your future performance is genuinely uncertain, since you might simply do it out of inertia or cussedness, whereas with the toxin you are certain you won't; what blocks the intention is certainty of future non-performance, and aversion matters only because it is what makes non-performance certain. (3) Dev then said the whole dispute rests on a conflation: intending is not predicting. An intention is a commitment that settles deliberation and structures further planning, not a forecast, and there is nothing incoherent about acquiring a commitment for reasons that concern the having of it — akratic agents intend all the time while suspecting they won't follow through. (4) Miriam answered that suspicion is not certainty, and that an intention you are certain you will never execute isn't an intention but a wish or a mere resolution; if resolutions counted, the puzzle would be trivially solvable and nearly everyone would collect the money, which she took to be obviously false. (5) Dev's reply was that you can exploit your own psychology: if you know that once the intention is in place there is some real chance you will drink out of stubbornness or out of a desire to be the sort of person who keeps his word to himself, then forming it is partly self-fulfilling and the certainty evaporates. (6) Miriam said that is gaming the setup — an intention that can only be formed by leaning on your own anticipated irrationality is not the state anyone theorizing about intentions cares about, and if that is what the billionaire wanted he should have paid for a prediction rather than an intention. (7) Dev then shifted to what he called structural fixes: if the billionaire repeats the offer weekly for a year, you can rationally cultivate a standing disposition to follow through on formed intentions, and then in later weeks you really would intend; alternatively, at midnight you can irrevocably arrange for a nasty penalty if you fail to drink on Tuesday, which buys you a reason to drink and thereby makes the intention formable. (8) Miriam replied that neither works: the iterated version cannot get off the ground, since the first week requires you to intend to drink on an occasion when you know you won't, and the disposition you would cultivate is money-responsive in exactly the way that makes it dissolve at the moment of action, once the money is already in hand; and the penalty version simply replaces the original case with a different one in which you *do* have a reason at the time of action, which is the ordinary situation and no answer to the puzzle. (9) Dev ended by saying that all of this shows the case is underdescribed: whether the intention is formable depends on contingent facts about techniques of self-management — self-deception, selective attention, getting drunk beforehand, hypnotic suggestion — and varies from person to person, so the confident philosophical verdict that one *cannot* intend is overreach. (10) Miriam ended by saying that if that is all the puzzle amounts to, then it has no bearing on the theoretical work that intentions are supposed to do — planning agency, the resolution of sequential-choice problems, the role of commitment in bargaining or in predictor-style cases — and its celebrity in that neighbourhood is a mistake, which she was happy to accept as a conclusion.

Please adjudicate this for me, and I mean adjudicate it rather than survey it: I don't want a reading list or a summary of what various authors have said, since I can't chase references anyway. Go through the ten moves in order and rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, saying briefly what is right and what is wrong in it, and flagging any place where a move is right for a reason its author didn't give. Then tell me who is more right overall on the three sub-questions that seemed to carry the weight: whether aversion is doing any work or only certainty of non-performance is; whether intending requires believing you will act, and how much the distinction between a weak expectation and near-certainty can bear; and whether the iterated and penalty variants dissolve the puzzle or merely change the subject. Finally, say what both of them were missing — I have a nagging feeling that my own framing of the case (a perfectly reliable intention-detector, near-certainty known in advance, money with no strings) smuggles in more than they noticed, and that something important hangs on the difference between being able to form an intention at will and being able to form it in the way intentions are normally formed, but I can't make the point sharply enough to have used it in the argument.
