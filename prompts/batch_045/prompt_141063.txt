Bit of an odd one. I write novels — literary-ish, family stuff, three books out — and a couple of years ago I published one with a genuinely nasty scene in it that I wrote and rewrote maybe thirty times, and it never once occured to me that I was doing anything morally questionable, because the person in the scene doesn't exist and never did. Meanwhile my fifteen-year-old has been playing an open-world game in which you can, if you feel like it, keep attacking an opponent long after the fight is clearly over, and the character on the ground changes what they do and say depending on exactly what you do — they try to shield themselves, or go still, or drag themselves away. I watched him do it once and it made me physically uncomfortable in a way my own pages have never managed. He thinks I'm being a hypocrite. My agent thinks I should stop moping about video games and get back to the manuscript, and she's probably right, but this one has stuck in my head and I want to write a short essay about it.

Part of what's stuck is something a friend who works in game AI told me. She said that in a lot of these games the response behaviour isn't hand-authored line by line the way dialogue in my books is; it comes out of systems with parameters and random seeds and the like, so the specific way the character begs or flinches is a genuine function of what the player did, and nobody — not the writer, not the designer — decided in advance that this particular string of behaviour would ever happen in the world at all. I genuinely can't tell whether that's meant to make me more comfortable or less. On the one hand it's less like somebody sat down and chose to write a begging scene; on the other hand it starts to feel as if the player is bringing something into existence that wasn't going to be there otherwise, which is more or less the shape of the thing that bothers me about real cruelty.

So I've decided I want my inconsistency to be principled, and I'd like you to make the case for me. Please argue that what's morally different about the interactive case has nothing to do with whether the game character has a welfare, or experiences, or moral status, or any of that — assume for the sake of the argument that it has none at all, that it's just a system with states — and everything to do with the fact that the player is doing something rather than depicting it, and doing it under a description on which the character's response is part of what the player brings about. And I want the argument to actually survive my kid's two best objections, so please deal with both: first, that whatever I'm up to when I write and rewrite a violent scene across thirty drafts involves just as much agency and just as much deliberate choosing as anything he does with a controller, and second, that if the character can't be harmed then there is nobody the player is wronging, and wronging nobody isn't a lesser wrong, it's no wrong at all. Somewhere in there I also want you to say why, on your argument, the fact that the flinching is procedurally generated rather than scripted makes things worse rather than better — or why it makes no difference whatsoever, if that's honestly where the argument goes. And one last thing: if your argument works, does it land harder on him or on the person who built the system?

Just continuous prose, please — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. Write it the way you'd write an email to a friend who happens to be stubborn.
