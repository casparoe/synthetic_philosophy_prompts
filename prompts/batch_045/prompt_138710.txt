You are a metaphysician who works on modality and personal identity. Think the problem through carefully, and start by brainstorming several candidate principles before you commit to any of them.

I am trying to find out what I actually think about the necessity of origins — the Kripkean idea that I could not have come from a different sperm and egg. My working principle has been: I am essentially the person who develops, by an ordinary developmental route, from the very pair of gametes I actually developed from. My gut verdicts keep straining against it.

Here they are. (a) A numerically different sperm of my father's fertilizes the very egg I came from: the resulting person is not me — I am confident. (b) The very sperm and egg I came from, with one base pair chemically altered an hour after fusion: obviously still me. (c) The same gametes, but the zygote's genome is edited to replace a disease-causing allele: still me, I want to say. (d) Mitochondrial replacement: the nuclear material from my mother's egg is transferred into an enucleated donor egg, so cytoplasm and mitochondria come from a third party: still me, even though the egg involved is not numerically the egg I came from. (e) The same gametes, but the zygote splits at the two-cell stage and both cells develop into adults: neither of them is me, or so it seems to me — and this verdict I trust least. (f) In vitro gametogenesis: gamete precursors are grown from my parents' reprogrammed skin cells, so no sperm or egg of theirs ever existed, and the resulting child is genetically my full sibling: not me — yet this case strikes me as much closer to being me than case (a) is, and I cannot say what accounts for the difference in distance. (g) The very gametes I came from, but switched by a clinic mix-up into another couple, gestated in another womb and raised as someone else's child: that person, disturbingly, feels like me — though I suspect I am confusing identity with having my genome.

Cases (a) and (c)/(f) look hard to reconcile, since any alternative sperm of my father's would have differed from the actual one by only a handful of de novo mutations while case (c) involves more changes and still feels like me; and (e) suggests a fission clause that has nothing to do with gametes at all.

Can you give me a principle, or two or three competing ones, stated precisely, that gets as many of these verdicts as possible? For each: say which case motivates each clause, say plainly which of my verdicts it forces me to give up, and give its verdicts on cases I have not listed — the same gametes united a decade later after being frozen; my zygote fusing with another into a single chimera; the zygote's genome rewritten so that it matches my sibling's; gametes derived from my parents' skin cells but selected so the recombined genome matches mine exactly. If no candidate saves every verdict, tell me which one to drop and what it costs.
