I am preparing a seminar session on objectivity in history, and I have been reading the opening chapter of a popular introduction to historiography that I would rather not name. It tells the subject as a three-stage decline from confidence to scepticism, and I want to know how much to trust before I put it in front of students. The chapter runs roughly as follows:

Until the nineteenth century history was a branch of literature. Then Ranke, in the preface to his first book in 1824, announced that the historian's task was to show the past "wie es eigentlich gewesen" — how it actually was — and that slogan turned history into a science: facts dug out of archives, sources weighed against one another, the historian's own convictions left at the door, and the facts permitted to speak for themselves. That naive faith in the bare fact did not survive the twentieth century. Carr's *What is History?* pointed out that the past is indefinitely large, that someone must decide which of its facts count, and that this decision is made by a historian with interests — before you can study history you must study the historian — so that the facts of history are constituted by the questions we bring to them, and no account can claim to be the one true story. Hayden White's *Metahistory* completed the demolition: history is written in language, and a narrative is shaped by a plot-type — romance, tragedy, comedy or satire — and by a governing trope, which means the historian's ordering of events is a literary achievement rather than a discovery. On this view the events are real but the meaning is made, and the line between history and fiction is a convention. The lesson usually drawn is that objectivity in the old sense of a view from nowhere is unrecoverable, and what historians can offer instead is methodological rigour, explicitness about standpoint, and transparency about sources.

My own attempt to sort this out is to distinguish three levels at which a historical account might be objective or not: constraint, whether surviving traces rule some accounts out and others in; selection, which facts about the past become historical facts at all, and whose questions decide that; and configuration, the narrative and figural form by which selected facts are made into a story with a point. On that map it looks as though Carr's argument lives at the second level and White's at the third, and that neither touches the first — so the chapter's conclusion needs a further step that neither of them supplies. But I cannot tell how much of my map is stipulation, and I notice the chapter says nothing about what Ranke himself thought made a fact worth selecting.

Could you audit the chapter's account claim by claim — separating what a careful defender of each of the three positions would accept, what is a familiar simplification, and what each of them explicitly denied — and then say whether the conclusion about transparency replacing objectivity survives the corrections? I would be especially grateful for your judgment on three points: whether the English rendering of the 1824 phrase matters for how Ranke's position is then characterized; whether Carr is really committed to the historian's interests constituting the facts rather than selecting among them; and whether White's narrativism is best read as a claim about truth or as a claim about meaning, and so whether my three levels hold up or collapse into one another under pressure. I should add that I am more suspicious of the chapter's treatment of Ranke than of its treatment of White, which I suspect may be exactly backwards.
