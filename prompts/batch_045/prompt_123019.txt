My family runs a pick-your-own strawberry farm, and every spring we have the same argument about whether to pay extra for a fancy frost-warning service. My brother's position is that the weather is just physics, so all the uncertainty in a forecast is really only us not having measured enough: with enough sensors and a big enough computer you'd know exactly what will happen, and until we have that, a ten-day forecast is worth about as much as last year's calendar. Then he goes one step further and says that if the weather is just physics, then whatever happens to the crop was always going to happen, so all our planning is a kind of theater — last month he used exactly this to argue that we shouldn't have bothered covering the low field, even though the covering pretty clearly saved most of those plants. A friend from uni who did a physics degree once muttered something about tiny measurement errors doubling over and over, but he was a few drinks in and I never really followed it, so I don't know if that helps my side or his.

Both halves feel wrong to me and I can't say why. My own half-formed idea is that maybe we don't need to know what the weather will do ten days out at all — we could just look each morning and re-decide, covering a bit more or a bit less as we go, so small mistakes never get the chance to pile up into a big one. My brother says that's just the same problem chopped into pieces. There's also the money side: about forty thousand dollars rides on two nights in May, covering early costs us in lost picking days, and I notice I care much more about avoiding the bad outcome than about being right on an average day, which makes me suspect "is the forecast accurate?" might be the wrong question entirely.

Whose reasoning is actually right here, and what should we do differently this spring? Please give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no intro and no summary at the end. For each point, tell me how confident you are and say plainly if my question is muddled or if my brother is assuming something false that neither of us has noticed — I'd much rather be corrected straight out than politely agreed with.
