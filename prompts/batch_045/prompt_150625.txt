Writing from a philosophy PhD student's perspective here — 3rd year, mostly perception and consciousness, in a dept that's very cogsci-facing, so I get dragged to a lot of journal club.

Last week we were arguing about the no-report paradigms — the ones where instead of asking people whether they saw the target you measure pupil dilation or optokinetic nystagmus or some EEG marker, in a crowding / inattentional-blindness style setup — and the usual claim was being made that the indirect measure shows the subject consciously perceived something they didn't report. I said: fine, but you've just relocated the report problem. The measure could be tracking post-perceptual processing, or a partial representation that never made it into experience at all, so you still haven't shown that there's a determinate fact of the matter that your measure is latching onto. One of the senior people waved this off with 'consciousness is a user illusion — there's no fact of the matter beyond what's on the interface, so there's nothing for your measure to be missing.' I pushed a bit on who the user is supposed to be if there's nobody behind the interface, and he said something like 'exactly, that's the point.' Which felt like it was doing a lot of work for four words.

So: what are the actual precise claims that slogan could be standing for? I can think of a few in a vague way and I don't trust my own separation of them. There's something trivially true about the interface being a compressed digest — Sperling-style partial report, you dont see all twelve letters, you get a summary — but that reading seems compatible with everything I was worried about and does zero argumentative work for him. Then there's something about introspection only giving you access to the interface and not the machinery. Then something much stronger where the illusion is supposed to be experience itself, or where being conscious just is being made available to the interface. And I half-suspect that the 'there's no user' version just collapses into eliminativism, or into a global workspace / higher-order / self-model view wearing a different hat, but I can't say which.

Can you lay the readings out as a menu from platitude to radical, with verdicts — which are trivial, which are substantive and defensible, which are false or unstable, and which quietly turn into some other position with a name — and say for each whether it would actually get him his conclusion about the crowding case, or whether my 'you've just moved the report problem' worry survives untouched? Two more things I'd want: whether the standard objection that an illusion is a state that seems some way it isn't (so it presupposes experience) actually lands against any of these readings or is just question-begging; and whether any reading gives you a criterion for when a *system* has an interface at all — because if 'is it conscious' becomes 'does it have an interface', I don't know what evidence would settle that either. I've got him again on Thursday and I'd like to know which of these is the one worth pressing on.
