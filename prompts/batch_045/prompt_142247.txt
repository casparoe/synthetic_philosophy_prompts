I'm one of three people on the programme committee of a small, newly endowed institute — five years of core funding, about eight resident fellows a year, mostly philosophers and theoretically inclined cognitive scientists, and effectively no compute budget — whose founding charter takes as its organising idea a proposal of Turing's: instead of trying to write a programme that simulates the adult mind, build something with the organisation of a child's mind, which he compares to a notebook bought from a stationer's, with very little mechanism and a great many blank pages, and then subject it to an appropriate course of education. My two colleagues read that charter very differently. One says the proposal has been vindicated by history, since we now build systems by training rather than by hand-coding, so what remains is engineering and a conceptual institute would be a museum. The other says the vindication is only apparent and that the whole interesting content of the charter sits in a single question: what, precisely, is the difference between teaching a system and building one. I trust neither summary, and the three of us have to hand in an agenda by the end of term.

So what I want is a defended five-year research agenda, not a survey: three or four questions the institute should make its own, in priority order, together with the reasoning that fixes the order. Please don't give me a bibliography or a reading list — I want the dependency structure. Which questions are upstream of which; which are genuinely tractable with conceptual tools and no compute; which are neglected in the sense that almost nobody with our toolkit is working on them; and which should be dropped because they are empirical problems in philosophical costume, or because their answer wouldn't change anything downstream. I also want to know what would move a question up or down the list: which result, argument, or technical development over the next five years would change the ranking, and which questions are stable under any evidence we can realistically expect.

Here are the candidates as the committee has been stating them, so that you can also tell me which of them we have misdescribed. (i) The syllabus objection: education may merely relocate the specification burden, so that the target that would have been written into a programme reappears in the curriculum, the sequence of tasks, the feedback regime and the schedule of rewards and punishments — in which case the child-machine proposal inherits the problem it was supposed to dissolve, only less transparently, and might even leave us worse off. (ii) Teaching versus rewiring: both are interventions that alter a system's dispositions; if the distinction is one of degree, or of which interface we happen to intervene through, does anything philosophically interesting turn on it? (iii) Reason-responsiveness: whether a system's updates track the content of a correction as a reason, rather than merely registering reinforcement, and whether that could be probed from the outside at all. (iv) The examination reading of the imitation game: if the test is closer to a school examination than to a behavioural criterion, then rote learning, training to the test, and vast stored tables of replies become the crux rather than a side worry, and questions about what understanding adds to success become prior to questions about how to produce success. (v) Scaffold-dependence: whether the capacities of an educated system are partly constituted by its school — the teachers, the feedback protocol, the artefacts on the desk — so that the real unit of assessment is machine plus institution, and what it would even mean to examine the machine with the scaffolding removed. Which of these are load-bearing, which are pseudo-problems or already settled in substance, and what have the three of us left out?
