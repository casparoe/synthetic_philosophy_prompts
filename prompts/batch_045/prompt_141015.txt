You are a professional philosopher who specialises in population ethics and who has, at some point in your career, advised a government on how to appraise policy. Take this slowly. Before you commit to a reconstruction, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming: list at least five different ways the practice I describe below might be rationalised, including rationalisations that rule each other out, and note what each would predict about the details. Only then give me your considered answer, structured under the headings I suggest at the end.

Here is the practice, as concretely as I can state it. I work in the appraisal unit of a finance ministry, so I have seen several versions of it from the inside.

A number of wealthy countries with low birth rates — Japan, South Korea, Singapore, Hungary, Italy among them — pay their citizens to have children: cash baby bonuses, per-child tax credits, housing priority, loan forgiveness conditional on childbearing. What interests me is not the spending but the paperwork around it.

First, the way these programmes are justified and scored. The case made in cabinet is almost never that the parents would enjoy the child. It is fiscal and demographic: the worker-to-retiree ratio is projected to worsen, pensions and health spending for the elderly are funded out of current taxation, therefore the country needs more future workers and taxpayers. Long-run fiscal sustainability reports treat a smaller projected workforce as a cost in itself. Migrants are admitted through points systems that partly score applicants on expected lifetime net fiscal contribution, and in the workforce projections a migrant and a newborn are, for these purposes, interchangeable units — nobody in the ministry cares whether the twenty-fifth worker arrives by birth or by plane.

Second, what the scoring leaves out. Every other intervention the same ministry appraises is scored by its effect on the welfare of the population, normally through willingness-to-pay or quality-adjusted life years, i.e. in terms of how well off identifiable people end up. Fertility programmes are the exception. The benefit side is the present discounted value of the marginal child's expected lifetime net tax contribution. The child's own lifetime well-being does not appear anywhere on the benefit side.

Third, a complication from another department. The environment ministry's models treat each additional resident as a lifetime stream of emissions, so in its long-run scenarios a larger future population is a cost. The same marginal child is therefore an asset in one spreadsheet and a liability in another, and is a welfare subject in neither.

Fourth, the episode that prompted this question. Our ministry is considering replacing the universal baby bonus with what it calls a marginal birth payment: the same money, but paid only where the birth can be certified as one that would not have occurred at the current payment rate — in practice, targeted at higher-order births or at parents who declare they would otherwise have stopped. The appraisal keeps the old convention, excluding the child's own lifetime well-being from the benefit side, and gives this reason: the child who exists only because of the payment cannot be said to have been made better off by it, since without the payment there would have been no one to be better off. A member of the committee objected that this is not neutrality but a substantive and controversial claim about whether existence can benefit the person who exists, and that if you are prepared to count the fiscal contributions of a person whose existence you caused, you have already taken a stand on comparing existence with non-existence and cannot then refuse to count that person's welfare. The ministry replied that it is taking no stand at all: it is simply counting effects on people who are already alive, and the child's welfare is excluded for bookkeeping reasons, not philosophical ones.

I have read a little around this and have had two unhelpful friends. One says the whole thing is just the repugnant conclusion in a suit. The other says it is the non-identity problem, and that the targeting reform makes things worse rather than better, which I find counter-intuitive: surely paying only for births that would not otherwise have happened is the efficient thing to do, whatever your ethics. I cannot tell whether either friend is saying anything, and I do not want an answer of the form this is really total utilitarianism, because the refusal to count the child's welfare looks to me like the opposite of that.

So, please tell me:

1. **The implicit theory.** What view of the value of adding people — of whether and in what way it matters that there be more people — does this bundle of practices and scoring conventions presuppose? Reconstruct it in full, including the parts that are not stated.
2. **Strict presupposition versus good fit.** Which parts of the practice strictly require that theory, and which are equally licensed by rival views? In particular, is the ministry's already-alive reply sufficient to reconstruct the whole practice on a person-affecting or asymmetry-friendly basis, so that the practice is philosophically underdetermined? If so, say exactly which observable features of the practice would settle the question, and which never could.
3. **The exclusion.** Is the decision not to score the child's own welfare a genuine substantive commitment about existence and non-existence, or is it a defensible convention about measurability, standing, or double counting? What in the practice would become incoherent if existence *could* benefit the person who exists? Pay attention to the fact that the exclusion is applied only in fertility programmes and not elsewhere.
4. **Internal coherence.** Is it coherent for one part of government to count a marginal person as a fiscal asset and another to count the same person as an emissions liability while no part counts that person's welfare? What, on this practice's own terms, is a person — a welfare subject, a fiscal unit, or something else? Is there a version of the practice that is coherent, and would it still be recognisable as population policy?
5. **The targeting inversion.** Assess my second friend's claim. Does making the payment conditional on the birth's counterfactual non-existence strengthen the justification or undermine it? Does the answer differ depending on which of the theories from (1) is the right reconstruction? This is the part I most want you to reason through carefully rather than settle by assertion.
6. **Replacement.** Suppose the implicit view were replaced, first by a strict asymmetry view on which we have no reason to bring happy people into existence, and then by an averaging view on which what matters is the average welfare level rather than the total. For each: which elements of the current practice survive unchanged, which survive with a different justification, and which would have to be abandoned? I want the concrete institutional consequences, not just the abstract ones.

Finally: is there something philosophically important that both the ministry and the objector are missing — some distinction they are both sliding over — which a better-designed practice would have to confront explicitly?

You do not need to cite anything; reason from the practice as I have described it.
