I write about science and ideas, and I'm working on a feature about how working researchers get away with back-of-the-envelope probabilistic arguments — the Drake-equation habit of multiplying a chain of rough factors, Fermi estimates, the standing default of assuming no correlation in epidemiology and cosmology — and about what, if anything, licenses the step where you treat quantities as independent merely because nobody has handed you a reason not to. Every expert I interview gives me the same answer: the defensible version of that step is its formalization. Don't presume independence; adopt the maximum-entropy distribution over everything you know and let whatever dependence there is fall out of the constraints. I want a constructed case for the piece that puts that answer under pressure, and I want it built so that the formalization is what comes out looking bad — the opposite of the usual moral, in which the practice is sloppy and formalizing it repairs it.

So please build me one small case, set somewhere a reader can picture (a rota, a conveyor, a delivery round, a pick-path through a warehouse — not abstract symbols), with two variants that are identical in every respect except one parameter. Specifications: nothing in the case may be chancy — it must be deterministic and finite, small enough that I can print the enumeration as a table in the magazine, with every "probability" in it a frequency over an explicitly specified window. A competent practitioner estimates some target quantity in the ordinary way, by multiplying single-process frequencies, and the estimate must be one a real analyst would make rather than a strawman. In the first variant that estimate is exactly right, not approximately right, and the case must let you say what vindicates it given that nothing in the story is random and nothing is causally insulated from anything else. In the second variant the same estimate, made in the same way, must fail in the strongest available sense: it assigns positive frequency to combinations that cannot occur at all, while every single-process frequency is unchanged and every check either process affords on its own comes out exactly as before. The difference between the variants has to be knowable in advance from the setup rather than discovered by collecting data, and it must not be a late-revealed hidden common cause: everything needed is on the face of the case from the start. Finally, the maximum-entropy formalization must reproduce the practitioner's error rather than correct it when the agent's entire constraint set is the single-process frequencies, and must reach the right answer only for an agent who already knows the structural fact — in which case the presumption was idle. My own suspicion, from playing with two processes running on fixed cycles of different lengths and their number-theoretic relation, is that something like a resonance between the cycles is the lever here, but I haven't been able to get a case to come out cleanly, so use that only if it works.

After the scenario, give me the demonstration: the events, the target quantity, the practitioner's number and the true number by enumeration in each variant; the number the formalization yields for each of the two epistemic positions; an honest statement of which feature of the case is doing the work; what happens to the verdicts if you vary one specified thing — I'd suggest the length or the grain of the window over which the frequencies are taken, since I suspect the vindication in the first variant is grain-relative and I'd like to watch it break; and the strongest reply available to someone who denies that any of this tells against the presumption at all, together with your assessment of whether that reply is actually good. If you cannot get exact vindication in the first variant, tell me so rather than fudging, and say what the nearest satisfiable version of the case looks like.
