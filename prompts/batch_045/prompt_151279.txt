The standard way of talking about this treats tightness as one dial: a culture is strict or it isn't, and the interesting empirical claim is that the dial gets turned up by threat — population pressure, disease load, scarce resources, exposure to invasion. The东西 I want to argue is that the single dial is hiding at least three separate variables, and that almost every political fight about "how much should we demand here" is really a fight about the third one while everyone thinks it's about the first.

The three:

BREADTH — how many domains of behaviour have a norm attached at all. A tight culture has an opinion about how you queue, how you dress, how loud you are, what you say to your mother-in-law. A loose one shrugs at most of that.

SEVERITY — the expected cost of breaking a norm that does exist. Gossip, ostracism, fines, prison, being quietly not promoted.

LEGIBILITY — how much of the normative landscape a newcomer can learn in advance. Can you read the rules off a document, or do you have to have grown up there?

The claim is that breadth and severity are what people measure and what the literature talks about, but legibility is what people actually argue about. Codified rules feel looser even when they're harsher, because you can comply without having been socialised. Unwritten rules feel tyrannical even when the sanctions are trivial, because you can be in violation without knowing it and there's no way to check.

Which gives the bit I think is genuinely new, or at least I haven't seen it put this way: codification and informal enforcement are substitutes, not complements. When a society loses the machinery of informal sanctioning — mobility, anonymity, heterogeneity, people not knowing each other's families — the demand doesn't go away, it converts into written rules. So the same population can get measurably looser (people care less what the neighbours think, deviance bother them less) and measurably more rule-dense (there's now a policy about everything) at the same time. If that's right then "should we be tighter or looser?" is a badly formed question, roughly as badly formed as asking whether a container should be taller or narrower. You can trade one against the other and the felt experience stays put.

Then a second wrinkle, which is that looseness is partly self-concealing. In loose cultures the strongest surviving norm is often a norm against being seen to enforce norms — visibly caring that someone broke a rule is itself the rudeness. So enforcement doesn't disappear, it gets outsourced and made invisible: to HR, to platform moderation, to insurance, to inspectors, to whatever anonymous process issues the fine. The person who would once have tutted at you now files nothing and thinks themselves tolerant, while a third party does the sanctioning at a remove. Two consequences I like: first, surveys asking people how much deviance bothers them will systematically undercount the enforcement actually happening, because they only ask about the enforcement people are willing to be seen doing. Second, looseness has a running cost — you have to buy enforcement from institutions when you stop getting it free from neighbours — which means tolerence is, at least partly, something a society has to be rich enough to afford.

The normative part, which I'm least sure about: I want to say the value of looseness isn't really tolerance, it's discovery. A loose setting lets people find out what they actually want before locking it in; a tight one hands them a script that was optimised for somebody else's environment. On that framing the tradeoff isn't freedom versus order, it's exploration versus inheritance, and the right amount of tightness depends on how fast the environment is changing — inherited norms are a bargain when conditions are stable and a liability when they aren't. That would predict that looseness should track rate of change, not just level of threat, which is a different empirical prediction from the usual story and I think testable-ish. I'm not at all confident it survives contact with the cases where fast change seems to produce a tight reaction — people get more rule-demanding exactly when things are moving quickly, which on my story is backwards.

Last piece, and it's the closest thing to an answer: maybe the right shape isn't an amount. Loose cultures seem to need a small number of very hard rules and then softness everywhere else — strict about the rules of the rule-making game, about violence, about the handful of things that make the rest of the looseness possible, and indifferent about nearly everything else. Architecture people have a term for this kind of design, a narrow waist with wide ends, and I want to steal it. The punchline would be that the question isn't how much rule-following to demand but where to spend it: you get a fixed budget of enforcement and the interesting variable is the shape of the distribution, not its total.
