I'm a PhD student in atmospheric dynamics — my actual research is on ensemble forecasting and data assimilation — and for about a year I've been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group on determinism, and there's one argument there that has restarted in basically the same shape four or five times now. Half the group (mostly the people who actually do nonlinear dynamics, plus a couple of philosophers with an agency-and-responsibility interest) says chaos finished off the Laplacian picture: if tracking a trajectory requires infinitely many digits of an initial condition and any real measurement gives you finitely many, then the "a sufficiently powerful calculator could foresee everything" story is dead, and with it the specific worry that determinism leaves no room for anything. The other half says this is a non-starter: chaos is a property of a model's equations, sensitive dependence is perfectly compatible with the world being deterministic, and all anyone has shown is that one practical project — long-range point forecasting from approximate data — fails, which nobody serious ever denied anyway. Everyone in the room agrees on the mathematics (Lyapunov exponents, error doubling times, the fact that numerically computed trajectories shadow real ones for a while but not forever), everyone agrees on the empirical facts about forecast horizons, and nobody moves. It feels less like a disagreement than like a loop, and I'm meant to give a short talk to the group next month and would much rather diagnose the loop than add one more turn to it.

My own hypothesis, which I want you to push on rather than flatter, is that there are at least three distinct unpredictabilities being run together. First, the one everybody talks about: the trajectory is a function of an exact point in a continuum and we only ever get a region, so error grows at a roughly fixed rate and there's a hard horizon — but nothing about determinism-as-uniqueness fails here. Second, a model-versus-world gap: we diagnose chaos in the equations, but whether the target system is itself deterministic, as opposed to stochastic in a way that finite-precision data cannot tell apart from deterministic, looks like a different question, and I'm not even sure it's an empirically decidable one, since for any finite run of observations I suspect you can write down both a deterministic system and a stochastic process that fit it equally well. Third, something like computability: it seems possible for a system to be deterministic in the sense that a unique future trajectory exists and yet be such that no algorithm computes where it goes, which would break the "predictable by an ideal agent" claim for reasons having nothing to do with sensitive dependence. And then there's a fourth axis I never see separated out — which predictive project is supposed to matter: point forecasts in the weather style, versus statistics of the sort you use for the response of a system to a changed forcing, where what you want is stability of the qualitative structure under perturbation rather than trackability of individual trajectories.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it mostly verbal, with two groups using "determinism" for different things — a thesis about uniqueness of the future given an exact state, versus a thesis about the availability of some predictive or explanatory method — and simply not noticing? Is it a disagreement about what determinism was ever supposed to do for us, i.e. about whether it's a claim about the world or a claim about the ambitions of a certain kind of science? Is it a hidden formal or empirical question (my second one, or maybe the third) that is really doing all the work, in which case the philosophers are having an argument that will only be settled by a theorem or by nothing at all? Or is it bedrock intuition about "openness" and "room" that neither side can argue for, dressed up as a technical dispute? I'd like the diagnosis to engage what each side actually says rather than survey the options, and then an honest answer about what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a theorem about whether finite-precision data can ever discriminate determinism from stochasticity, an experiment, a change of question — including the honest possibility that no progress should be expected and an explanation of why.
