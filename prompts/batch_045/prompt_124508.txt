I've spent fifteen-odd years doing investigations — first newspapers, now mostly open-source work for an NGO — so the vocabulary I actually think in is source-handling: the two-source rule, reliability and credibility ratings crossed against each other, provenance and chain of custody, corroboration, single-sourcing, on and off the record, and the cold-start problem where you've got no file on someone at all. I've been reading around the epistemology of testimony on my own and bouncing off it. Someone told me the two sides are called reductionism and anti-reductionism and mentioned the names Fricker, Burge and Moran; if that's garbled, never mind — I don't need the labels right, and I can't check anything and don't want to, so no citations or reading lists please.

Here's the job. Translate both positions into my vocabulary as faithfully as you can. The reductionist line — the one that says my warrant for believing what you tell me is nothing over and above my other evidence that people like you, in situations like this, tend to get it right — plus the two versions of it I keep tripping over, the global one (a track record for the source generally) and the local one (reasons bearing on this particular claim or this particular domain). And then the anti-reductionist line, that there's some default entitlement to take what you're told at face value unless something specific defeats it, and the further view that being told something is different in kind from picking up a bit of evidence, because the speaker is answerable for it in a way a footprint isn't.

Two things I want run through the translation, because I think they're where my vocabulary gives out.

First, the ledger. Suppose that from next year every source has a tamper-proof, complete, public track record — every checkable claim they've ever made, scored the same way for everyone, updated live, free to consult. What does each position say once everyone has a ledger? My guess is it settles things for the global reductionist, does nothing at all for the local one, and leaves the anti-reductionist definately unsatisfied for a reason I can't put into words. Is that right, and what's actually still in dispute with a perfect ledger in place?

Second, the farmed source. A handler's asset spends six years feeding you ten thousand true, checkable, low-stakes details — logistics, names, licence plates, all verified — and then spends the whole accumulated rating on one high-stakes claim that's false, and is burned afterwards. The ledger says believe them. Every instinct I have says the trust shouldn't carry across that jump, and I can't say why in my own terms: the track record is real, the domain isn't even changing much. Same puzzle in a smaller form: a source who's spotless on movements and starts telling me about chemistry. And at the other end, the cold start — someone with no record at all who tells me something I have no way of checking. Does either philosophical position do better than mine on the farmed source, in a way that isn't just restating my instinct? Would an assurance-type view treat it differently because the trust has to be given afresh on this occasion rather than banked?

Then the bit I actually care about: the residue report. Which claims translated cleanly, which only went through after you quietly bent them, and which have no expression at all in source-handling terms — the second-personal stuff, presumably, and maybe the difference between being justified and getting the truth, which my vocabulary runs together. For each bit of residue, say whether you think it's my framework that's missing something or the philosophers talking past each other. And be straight with me about two things: where you smuggled in an assumption of mine and called it a translation, and what would change your verdict on the residue — which argument would make you say it's my vocabulary that's defective after all, or the reverse. Where is your reasoning most likely to be wrong?
