Write a dialogue between two philosophers of mind who agree that the meta-problem of consciousness is where the real argument now lies, and disagree about what a solution to it would show.

Set the terms as follows, and keep to them. The meta-problem, as that label is now used, is not the problem of explaining consciousness but the problem of explaining the appearance of a problem: why subjects who have been given the full functional and structural story about their own minds still judge, report and seem to find that something is left over. The first speaker, the phenomenal realist, holds that this leftover insistence is correct — there is something it is like to be a subject — and that a complete explanation of why we say so would leave the hard problem exactly where it was. The second speaker, the meta-problem illusionist, holds that phenomenal consciousness as we naively conceive it does not exist, and that a solution to the meta-problem of the right kind would leave the realist's residual claim with nothing to do: not refuted, but idle.

Build the exchange around two devices, which the speakers are to use rather than merely name. First, explanatory saturation: a theory achieves saturation for a subject when, for every possible introspective probe — every question the subject could be asked, every rephrasing of it, every drug, every redirection of attention, every enlargement of the subject's conceptual repertoire, every reminder of the functional story — the theory predicts the phenomenal judgement the subject will make and explains how that judgement was produced by mechanisms whose description nowhere invokes phenomenal properties. Second, the denial-cost test: a claim says more than a rival only if denying it costs something, whether a difference in what is judged under some probe, a difference in what gets explained, or a difference in what is worth doing. Both speakers must say what saturation does to the hard problem, and what the realist's residual assertion that there is still something it is like scores on the denial-cost test. Give the realist one serious resource: that a claim can be brute, fundamental and undetectable under saturation without being empty, much as the existence of an external world or the obtaining of a fundamental law makes no difference to any judgement we could make and is not for that reason void. Give the illusionist the reply that external-world realism earns its keep through inference to the best explanation, while phenomenal realism is posited precisely where no such inference is on offer. Both must go beyond these moves, not repeat them.

Make the two speakers equally competent and equally well informed: neither is a caricature, and neither prevails through the other's obtuseness. They must answer what the other has actually said, so that by the final exchange each has conceded one point and landed one blow the other has not fully parried. By the end they should have identified, in so many words, the single crux their disagreement reduces to, and said why that crux is not merely verbal. In the fourth exchange, apply the apparatus to a concrete case of your own construction — a completed neuroscience that achieves saturation, a machine that satisfies it, or something sharper — rather than to abstractions. Do not let the dialogue end in a shrug.

Format: exactly five numbered exchanges, each consisting of one speech from each speaker, each speech no more than two sentences; speaker labels only, no stage directions, no narration, no framing introduction and no conclusion afterwards. No citations, no named authors, no references to particular texts or dates. Write in British English.
