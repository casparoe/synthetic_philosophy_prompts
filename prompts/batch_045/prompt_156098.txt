You are a decision theorist who has spent years advising grantmakers, health technology assessment bodies, and government ministries on the value of information, and who is equally fluent in the formal result and in how it gets mangled in practice. Before you write anything, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming what a usable procedure here would have to look like and where the theory will resist being operationalized; then write the deliverable itself. Here is my situation. I direct evidence and measurement at a mid-sized foundation funding health and education programmes in low- and middle-income countries, and two or three times a week a programme officer brings me a decision in the same shape: we are about to commit or re-commit three to eight million dollars to an intervention, we are genuinely uncertain whether it works well enough to be worth that money, and the question is whether to buy more information first — a pilot, a longer baseline, a full impact evaluation, a cheap structured expert elicitation — or to decide now on what we have. My team has to turn that into a half-page written recommendation, produced in about thirty minutes each, that goes to an investment committee of lay trustees who are not statisticians and who want one paragraph explaining why we are or are not spending another $400,000 on a study before spending $4,000,000 on a programme. My staff and I are not statisticians either; we have no econometrician on call, and we can rarely get a defensible number for anything.

I got into a long argument about this with a friend who works in decision theory, and I want the argument settled in a form I can hand to my team. Her position was essentially that information has non-negative value — the result she called Good's theorem, that a decision-maker who is always free to ignore what she learns cannot be made worse off in expectation by information that costs nothing — so, she said, the default should be to gather, and any reluctance on our part is just fear of bad news. I pushed back that the theorem seems to buy almost nothing for us: it says nothing about whether a study is worth its cost or its delay, and in our world the assumptions behind it visibly fail in ways I want named and handled rather than waved at. Specifically: we frequently cannot commit to ignoring a finding, because a null result will be used by our own board, by a ministry co-funder, or by the press as a reason to kill a programme even when the decision-theoretically right response would be to continue; the act of measuring perturbs the system (running a pilot in a district signals to that ministry that we are about to scale there, and changes how local staff behave); a small underpowered study read by a non-Bayesian audience produces a worse decision than no study at all; and there are cases where our own ignorance is doing work for us, in that being visibly uncertain lets us fund on plausibility in a way a precise low estimate would forbid — my friend's phrase for this was the option value of ignorance, and I want it in the procedure if it survives scrutiny. I also want the plain consequence dealt with honestly: if no possible finding would move us across a funding threshold, the study has no decision value to us whatever it is worth to anyone else.

So please write the procedure my team could actually run: the questions, in order, that a programme officer asks about a proposed study, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and — this matters most — where it stops and hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. It must be runnable in thirty minutes by someone with no formal training, it must say what to do when a number cannot be obtained (order-of-magnitude bands, rough thresholds, explicit guesses by named people, whatever you think is defensible), and it must be candid about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Tell me which distinctions from the theory have to survive the simplification and which can be dropped without loss; tell me where the procedure will systematically misfire, including the cases where buying information genuinely makes us worse off and how we would recognise them in advance. Then apply it to three cases and show your work: (A) our eight-year-old flagship school health programme, $6M a year, beloved by the board, for which a $1.2M three-year cluster randomised evaluation is being proposed as a way of confirming what everyone already believes; (B) a cheap, politically unloved variant — a small unconditional cash top-up to households — where our prior sits almost exactly on the cost-effectiveness threshold and where a $60,000 elicitation exercise or administrative-data linkage could plausibly tip the decision either way; (C) a promising district tutoring programme where the only affordable study is a twenty-school pilot that will be statistically weak, where the ministry has said it will read a null as proof of no effect and withdraw its co-funding, and where the pilot itself will be read locally as a promise of scale-up. I expect one of these to come out in a way I will not like, and I want you to say plainly what it is and not soften it.
