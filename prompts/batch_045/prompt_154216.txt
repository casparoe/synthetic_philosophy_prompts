I've been lurking on LessWrong and the Alignment Forum since the old SSC days, and next week I finally have to present something instead of just arguing in comment sections: a 45-minute internal talk (30 talk, 15 Q&A) to our group's model-reliability seminar. The room is roughly five archetypes: two monitoring/infra engineers who built our drift-detection stack and think entirely in terms of population stability index, Mahalanobis distance in embedding space, canary datasets and "just add another eval"; one interpretability person who does activation patching, genuinely likes mechanisms, and will insist that whatever I recover with my probes is a property of my probe suite rather than of the model; a philosopher of science visiting from a phil-sci department who works on mechanistic explanation in biology and will want to know what individuates one mechanism from another; and someone from the evals team who suspects all of this is a roundabout way of saying "write better test sets". I want a prep sheet for the Q&A, not a defense of the view.

My thesis is that anomaly detection should be run over mechanisms rather than over inputs or activations. Sketch of what I'm arguing: (1) for a fixed, deployment-relevant slice of behaviour, define the model's mechanism as the minimal set of internal causal paths — the kind of thing you get out of patching / causal-scrubbing style interventions — that suffices to reproduce the behaviour on that slice; (2) say two systems run the same mechanism iff their minimal sufficient causal paths agree up to reparametrization, so permuting neurons, rescaling, or any other transformation that leaves the input-output function and the loss untouched doesn't count as a new mechanism; (3) after every retrain, fine-tune or data-pipeline change, re-derive the mechanism and alarm when its distance from the validated one crosses a threshold. My demo is a resume-screening model that kept its AUC after a vendor changed how PDFs get converted to text, but switched from content features to a length-and-format artifact: flat metrics, new mechanism, the numbers quietly goodharting their way to looking fine. The new bit I'm adding, and the bit I'm least sure about, is a third category beyond "same behaviour, new mechanism" and "new inputs, same mechanism": the case where there was never one stable mechanism to depart from, because the model was already running a different heuristic on every slice. For that I want to alarm on growth in the shortest description of what the model does on the monitored slice — a description-length / MDL-ish criterion — instead of on distance from a baseline.

What I want from you: the objections I'll actually get from those five people, ordered by likelihood times damage, each phrased the way that particular person would put it (engineering vocabulary for the engineers, not engineering-speak for the philosopher), with a ~90-second reply and an explicit note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede in the first two slides rather than be forced into conceding later, and what I should definately not give up. Two I already know are coming — the infra folks saying this is drift detection with extra steps, and the interp person saying my mechanism is my probe's mechanism rather than the model's — so use those as a starting point but spend most of the sheet on the ones I'm not already ready for, including the objection I'm hoping nobody asks, and the question that, if it does get asked, means I should retreat mid-talk to a weaker fallback version of the claim. And this last part matters as much as the rest: flag clearly which parts of what I'm saying are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who actually work on this — especially whether "same mechanism" is a well-defined relation at all, whether reparametrization-invariance is achievable in practice for anything real, and whether the description-length thing is even measurable. I'd much rather find out now which plank I'm standing on is rotten than walk in there presenting a live controversy as if it were consensus.
