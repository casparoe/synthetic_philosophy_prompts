Last night a friend and I spent the better part of an evening arguing about affirmative action and quotas — the American university cases, the Scheduled Caste and Scheduled Tribe reservations in India, and Norway's statutory rule requiring a fixed share of board seats at large companies to be held by women. Her parting shot was that I am against every one of these schemes in principle and in favor of every one of them in practice. I don't think that is fair, but I couldn't say why in a way that satisfied either of us.

I have been asked to circulate a short statement of my position before a policy workshop on selection procedures next week, and I would like it audited before I send it. Here is the package as I would write it down:

1. A person should not gain or lose access to a competitive good because of ancestry, skin colour, or sex; what should decide is their individual qualification. That is my basic objection to quotas and the reason I am against them in principle.
2. But I support the Indian reservations, at least as originally designed: a remedy for a structure of exclusion that is still operating, not a preference for a group as such.
3. I also support the Norwegian boardroom quota and would like to see it copied elsewhere. I do not care whether it improves firm performance. The point is that a self-selecting network reproduces itself unless something forces it open.
4. On American universities, I think the whole-file, individualized review that selective colleges used to practice was defensible in a way that a fixed bonus for race never was. What I object to is the mechanical version, not the noticing of race.
5. I do not think the mismatch argument — that beneficiaries are placed where they cannot compete and do worse than they otherwise would have — is a good argument against any of this. It rests on contested social science, and if it were sound it would tell far more strongly against legacy and donor preferences, which I assume we are not defending.
6. All of these should carry sunset provisions. A preference that becomes permanent has stopped being a remedy and become an entitlement, and that is where I would withdraw support.

What I want is an audit, not a survey of the debate. Go through these pairwise and tell me which are genuinely in tension and which only look that way, since I would rather not revise something that is already fine. For the real tensions, say exactly what would have to give and how costly that revision would be, and rank them by severity rather than listing them as equally serious. Then name the single revision that would clean up the most, and tell me whether my friend's charge actually lands on any one of the six, or whether it is just a slogan. If I am relying on a distinction that won't bear the weight I am putting on it — between mechanical and holistic selection, say, or between a remedy and an entitlement — that is the most useful thing you could tell me.

Assume I know the standard arguments on all sides; I do not need a literature review or any citations, since I will add references myself if the workshop wants them. Around nine hundred words would be ideal. Please write in American English.
