I'm ops director at a 25-person software consultancy and maybe twice a month a client asks us for a fixed-price bid on a project — meaning if we blow the estimate we eat the difference, which has cost us actual money twice in the last year. I want a written procedure a project lead can run alone, in about 30 minutes, before a bid goes out: the steps in order, what counts as an adequate answer at each step, the points where the procedure just says stop and don't bid, and the points where it has to hand the decision to a partner because what's left is a question about how much risk the firm wants to carry rather than a question of fact. Constraints: the leads are strong engineers with no stats training at all, so nothing with a formula they'd have to look up or a likelihood ratio they'd have to squint at, and I'd much rather have something slightly crude that gets used every time than something elegant that gets skipped. Also, please don't give me papers, reading lists or citations — I don't need them and I can't check them anyway.

The part I keep tripping over, and where I want the real conceptual work, is that the obvious outside-view move (pull the numbers from similar past projects, start there, and let the inside view adjust only for things you can actually point to) has a hole I've never seen handled properly: picking the reference class is itself a judgment made from the inside, and my team is definatly good at gerrymandering it without noticing. Last month someone built a reference class of "Rails upgrades where the client already had a staging environment," got four data points out of it, and announced that as the outside view. So I want the procedure to include a second-order step — a disciplined way of asking how often this team's chosen reference classes have actually held up afterward, what to do when the honest answer is "you've never built one of these and there is no reference class," and how to stop that step from becoming infinite regress. I was arguing with a friend who forecasts as a hobby and she said something like "your outside view is just an inside view wearing nicer clothes," which I can't get out of my head. Related: I want epistemic modesty built into the output rather than just as an attitude — the procedure should be able to end at "here is a range, and here is why it needs to be wider than you want it to be," and it should tell the lead when the range is wide enough that bidding fixed-price at all is the actual mistake.

Please run it on three cases: (1) a data migration very similar to six we've done, except the client's legacy schema is properly documented this time and the lead says "no really, this one is different"; (2) something we've never built before — an LLM-backed support triage tool — with no internal reference class and a client who wants a number by Friday; (3) our biggest client's pet project, a "quick" two-week integration, where I have a feeling the procedure is going to tell me something I don't want to hear, and I want you to not soften it. Be explicit too about what the procedure cannot do, where it will misfire, and which parts are judgment calls dressed up as steps. End with a single final line in the form \answer{case1: VERDICT | case2: VERDICT | case3: VERDICT}, each verdict a short phrase like bid 6 weeks, decline fixed-price, or escalate to partner.
