I've had neuropathic pain down my right leg since a cycling accident three years ago, and the only reason I'm still working is a hospital pain clinic whose patient advisory panel I now sit on. They've given me the twenty minute patient voice slot at their education day in a fortnight, and I don't want to spend it on "listen to patients more". I want to argue that the field's whole endpoint is wrong: at the moment the goal is pain management and, when that fails, teaching us to accept the pain and build a life around it, and I want to say the goal should be abolition, and that a clinic like this one ought in principle to be trying to make severe suffering technically impossible rather than merely tolerable.

The version of abolition I'm defending is what I've started calling the dimmer, not the switch. Pain seems to have a signalling part, where it is, what sort it is, that it makes you stop and protect the tissue, and a nastiness part, the part that is what actually ruins your life. Pain people talk as if those are at least partly seperable, and there are these odd cases I'm probably getting slightly wrong of people who still seem to feel the sensation but aren't upset by it. My claim is that the nastiness part is the thing medicine should be trying to abolish, and that the way to do it is an adjustable ceiling on how bad any experience can get, not by numbing the signal. I also want an answer to what takes suffering's place once it's gone: my answer is urgency without unpleasantness, an alarm that gets you moving without making you miserable. And I need to deal with the opioid thing before anyone else does, my line being that we tried to turn the whole system down with a blunt instrument and got tolerance and addiction, and that a ceiling is different in kind.

The room will be about forty people, nearly all the clinic's own staff: two interventional pain physicians who do blocks and implants, a palliative care consultant, three clinical psychologists who run the acceptance and CBT groups, two physios, a rehab doctor, and maybe six other patient reps. No philosophers, nobody who has read anything about the ethics of this, and several people who have spent their careers telling patients like me that learning to live with it is the realistic goal.

What I need from you is Q and A prep, not a defence of the position. Which objections will I actually get from that room, roughly in the order they'll come and by how much damage they'd do, each one phrased the way that particular person would say it rather than the way a bioethicist would, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and where my reply is soft. Tell me which objection I should give away in the talk itself before anyone can raise it, which one will sound sympathetic and is actually the most damaging, and the one question I'm hoping nobody asks. I'd apreciate it written as one continuous email, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just paragraphs, because I'll be reading it on my phone in the car park ten minutes before I go on.
