My seminar on practical rationality did one week on akrasia and one week on time preference, and the TA used the same picture for both: future goods loom larger the closer they get, the way a building looks bigger as you walk toward it, and acting against your own better judgement is just what happens when you mistake apparent size for real size. He said this is basically Socrates' move in the Protagoras — the power of appearance, with the measuring art as the cure — and that present bias and hyperbolic discounting are the same thought with a formula bolted on. My roommate, who does behavioral econ, then used it to defend a conclusion I don't like: if the hyperbolic discounter is just subject to a perspective effect that isn't up to them, then they aren't irrational in the way blame needs, they're misperceiving, and the only fix is an external aid (deadlines, auto-enrollment, locking the cookie tin) the way the fix for a visual illusion is a pair of glasses. I want that audited rather than either accepted or waved away.

So: give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of distance, of the observer, of the object's unchanging real size, of apparent size, of the measuring stick, of glasses, of walking closer — and then the places the mapping breaks, and honestly I care much more about the breaks than the mapping. Here are the ones I keep coming back to, but please add better ones: (a) a perceiver can't do anything about perspective, whereas an agent can tie themselves to a mast, so the source seems to lack the very resource that does the work in the target, which is odd if the point was to downplay agency; (b) the discounter can foresee today that they'll reverse tomorrow, and "sophisticated" discounters plan around it — there's no analogue of being clever about your own perspective, you can't pre-correct for a tree; (c) I don't think looming and reversal are the same thing, and I've been thinking about two cases that pull them apart — the "cold reversal" case, where someone calmly flips their preference at a scheduled annual review with no change in how vivid the option feels (no apparent-size shift at all, just a reversal), and the "always next year" case, where someone endlessly defers a good thing to a distant date and so seems to overweight the far rather than the near, which on the perspective picture should be like an object getting bigger as you back away. If the analogy can only handle one of those by declaring it impossible, that's a substantive empirical claim it's smuggling in, and I want that flagged.

Then the part I actually need: which disanalogies matter for the conclusion my roommate drew (no irrationality worth blaming, remedy = external aid only) and which are just decoration; what the picture quietly imports into the target that isn't there — e.g. the idea that there is a true rate you're mismeasuring, or that the effect is involuntary in the way perception is; whether a better analogy is available, since I've seen the planner-and-doer model and the "population of temporal selves bargaining" picture offered as replacements, and they seem to me to license a different remedy (internal coalition-building and rules) rather than glasses. Finally, say which conclusions survive once the breaks are on the table: does the perspective picture still explain why hyperbolic discounting produces akratic-looking behavior, does it still get the agent off the hook, and where does that leave the Socratic claim that strict akrasia is impossible? Table is fine, prose is fine, mix them. End with a single last line, machine-readable: Verdict: HOLDS / HOLDS-FOR-THE-DIAGNOSIS-ONLY / BREAKS.
