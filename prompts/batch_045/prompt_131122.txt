Please write a specification for a theory of the commensurability of pleasure and pain, where the purpose the theory has to serve is fixed in advance: it must be good enough to turn lexical threshold negative utilitarianism (LTNU) into an action-guiding view rather than a slogan.

The version of LTNU I have in mind is the two-threshold one. Below some threshold of intensity and duration, pleasant experiences have negligible positive value — zero, or at any rate not enough to count. Above a second threshold, episodes of suffering are not merely weightier than pleasures but lexically dominant over them: no quantity of sub-threshold pleasure, however vast, compensates for a single episode of suffering above the threshold, and no finite exchange rate converts one into the other. My question is what such a view presupposes about pleasure, pain, and the scale on which they are compared, and which of those presuppositions are legitimate demands on a theory and which are merely prejudices of the negative-utilitarian camp.

So, for each requirement: state it precisely, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark it as non-negotiable for this purpose or as a preference of one camp. I would like the list to cover at least the following, though I do not regard this ordering or division as privileged:

1. *What the bearers are.* Whether the items compared are momentary phenomenal states, temporally extended episodes, valence-carrying neural events, or whole lives — and what a theory must say about whether a long unpleasant episode can be divided into parts each of which falls below the threshold (the fragmentation, or "dust," worry), and about whether a lifetime of sub-threshold discomforts can be reassembled into something that matters.

2. *Lexical priority versus incommensurability.* These are different claims and I want them kept apart. A lexical ordering is a relation inside a single scale and presupposes that the ranked items are comparable; incommensurability denies that there is a common scale at all. LTNU is frequently motivated by the second and stated in the vocabulary of the first. Say which one the view actually needs, and what follows if it cannot have it.

3. *Aggregation across persons.* Whether the ban on trade-offs is intra-personal only or interpersonal as well; what must be said about enormous numbers of very small sufferings; and what must be said about the repugnant-conclusion style argument run in reverse, where one severe episode is ranked worse than any number of mildly pleasant lives.

4. *Mixed valence.* Experiences that are pleasant and unpleasant together: relief, the scratching of an itch, effort that is enjoyed, grief one would not give up. Whether valence is separable into a pleasant and an unpleasant component, and what is lost if a theory stipulates that it is.

5. *The zero point, and prudential versus moral significance.* Given that neutral states and sub-threshold pleasures come out equal in value, a theory must say whether it is collapsing the prudential value of an experience into its moral significance, or distinguishing them — and, if it collapses them, what it does with the implication that extinguishing mild pleasure is cost-free, and with the further question whether being permitted to ignore such pleasure is the same as being required to eliminate it.

6. *Vagueness and the cliff edge.* If the threshold is even slightly vague, then states differing by an arbitrarily small amount are ordered as though one mattered infinitely more than the other. What must a theory say about higher-order vagueness here; may it instead treat near-threshold pairs as neither better, worse, nor equal but on a par; and what does that option cost? I would also like your assessment of a proposal I have not seen discussed — giving the threshold a hysteresis or ratchet structure, so that the level at which an episode enters the lexically dominant regime differs from the level at which it leaves it — as to whether this dissolves the cliff-edge problem or merely relocates it.

7. *Prospects and risk.* Since the view rejects the trade-off structure that expected-value reasoning normally presupposes, what must it say about ranking gambles — in particular about whether a small probability of threshold-crossing suffering may be averted at the cost of the certain loss of an enormous quantity of sub-threshold pleasure, and what in the theory would make that verdict follow rather than its opposite.

8. *Locatability.* A view that cannot tell us, even roughly, where the thresholds fall is not action-guiding. What must the scale be connected to — self-report, choice behaviour, neural markers, some hypothetical-choice test — for the theory to be usable, and what is required when those sources disagree?

Then, two results about the list itself, which are the parts I most want. First: is any subset of these requirements jointly unsatisfiable? My suspicion is that no single theory can combine a sharp lexical threshold, the absence of cliff-edge discontinuity in near-threshold comparisons, and a transitive and complete ranking of the relevant states — but argue for or against that, and if the unsatisfiable subset is not the one I have named, say which it is. Second: which single requirement excludes the largest number of otherwise attractive candidate theories, and which requirement on the list is really a partisan demand of the negative-utilitarian camp that a neutral theorist of commensurability could reject?

If it is useful, and briefly, since I intend to do the scoring myself: how a single cardinal scale with a priority clause, a parity-style incomparability model, and a two-dimensional or multi-utility representation each fare against the list. Please stipulate any technical vocabulary you need rather than surveying the literature; I am interested in the shape of the specification, not in who has defended what.
