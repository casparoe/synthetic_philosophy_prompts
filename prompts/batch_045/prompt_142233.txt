I'm TAing an intro metaphysics module this term and keep having the same argument with one of the PhD students, so I'd rather get properly attacked on this than keep winning by stipulation. The view I hold — call it the paraphrase view, I need some label — is that there is no causation by absence, full stop. When we say the guard's not turning up caused the vault to be emptied, or that the missing signature caused the application to be rejected, nothing negative is doing causal work: there's the actual positive history (the guard asleep down the road, the clerk processing the file), some counterfactuals about what would have happened had things gone differently, and a background of norms, plans and expectations that explains why we single out her absence rather than the Queen of England's. On my view causal claims about omissions are in perfectly good order as compressed explanations, they're just not reports of a causal relation with an absence in it. My supervisor says I've defintely just built the conclusion into what I'll count as a cause — causes have to be actual happenings, locatable in time, the sort of thing that can enter into production-like relations, and absences are none of those — and I want to know how much of the view is that definition wearing a coat.

So: what would count against me, ranked from I'd have to give it up through this hurts to this is merely awkward, and for each one say whether a committed paraphrase theorist should concede it or is entitled to shrug. The things I want you to actually work through, with cases rather than name-dropping, are these. (1) Double prevention: someone tries to poison the coffee, a colleague quietly swaps the cups, the victim survives, and the absence of poison upstream seems to be what lets the whole normal causal story run — people treat this as the case where absences inherit causal power, and I've never felt its force, so either show me what I'm missing or agree with me and say why it's not the master case it's made out to be. (2) Symmetric overdetermination by two omissions: either of two guards would have locked the door and would have been enough, neither does, the burglar walks in; my counterfactual machinery breaks here, and I need to know whether my fallback (no cause of the break-in at all, or the two together) is a cost or a feature I can charge for. (3) The timing worry: an omission doesn't seem to have a sharp location or duration in the way causes are supposed to, and the late omission case — the doctor fails to give the antidote at midnight, but the patient was already dead at 11:55 — shows the shape of the problem; is the vagueness a reason to deny absences causal status or just a reason to be careful about event individuation? (4) The case that worries me most, which I made up and haven't seen discussed: the disarmed sprinkler. A fire spreads through a wing of a building because the sprinkler's trigger was never rewired after maintenance months earlier. There's no agent who failed to act at the relevant moment, nobody had a duty, there's no queue of expectations for my story to lean on — and yet the absence of a signal caused the fire to spread sounds completely fine to nearly everyone I try it on. (5) Model-relativity: whether a causal model even contains a variable for the guard locks the door is a modeller's choice, and if it does then the absence comes out as a cause on the usual actual-cause tests, and if it doesn't it can't even be a candidate. Positive causes don't look like they're at the mercy of variable inclusion in the same way — or do they? If they do, I'm not specially exposed and you should say so; if they don't, I owe you something. (6) My own rescue device, which I half suspect is junk: I distinguish thin absences (mere non-occurrences, like the Queen not watering the plant) from thick ones (the non-execution of a standing arrangement — a mechanism that was meant to fire, a plan, a role, a duty), and I use the distinction to explain why some absence-claims are good explanations and others are noise. Is that doing real work, or is it a moves-in-the-night device that lets me dismiss any counterexample as thin? If it's the latter, say so, and tell me what test would expose it.

On epistemics: I want you to seperate what people who work on this genuinely agree on from what's just fashionable, and not hand me a consensus where there's only a majority. Flag every place where my view is insulated by a definition rather than an argument, and be willing to conclude that the whole thing has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and then say whether, for a view of this kind, that's a real problem or just what metaphysics looks like when it's done honestly. Tell me which single defeater you think is most likely to be right and roughly how confident you are in that ranking. And for what it's worth, the thing that would move me most is a case where the absence does explanatory work that no reconstruction out of positive history plus counterfactuals plus norms can replicate; shared intuitions alone won't do much for me, so if that argument is available, make it, and if it isn't, say that too.
