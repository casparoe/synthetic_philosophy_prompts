I am working through Aristotle's *Metaphysics*, Book VIII, chapter 6 (Bekker pages 1045a–1045b), and I cannot tell whether the argument there actually solves a difficulty I care about or merely restates it in other vocabulary. I would like help with the passage itself, and then with what happens when its machinery is pressed on two cases I have constructed.

The passage (two stretches, with the intervening lines omitted; the English is the translation hosted by the Perseus Digital Library).

From 1045a:

> But if, as we maintain, man is part matter and part form—the matter being potentially, and the form actually man—, the point which we are investigating will no longer seem to be a difficulty. For this difficulty is just the same as we should have if the definition of X were "round bronze" (X is the translator's placeholder for a Greek letter used as a variable); for this name would give a clue to the formula, so that the question becomes "what is the cause of the unification of 'round' and 'bronze'?" The difficulty is no longer apparent, because the one is matter and the other form.

From 1045b:

> The same argument, however, applies in every case; for "being healthy" will be the "communion" or "connection" or "combination" of soul and health; and "being a bronze triangle" a "combination" of bronze and triangle; and "being white" a "combination" of surface and whiteness. The reason for this is that people look for a unifying formula, and a difference, between potentiality and actuality. But, as we have said, the proximate matter and the shape are one and the same; the one existing potentially, and the other actually. Therefore to ask the cause of their unity is like asking the cause of unity in general; for each individual thing is one, and the potential and the actual are in a sense one. Thus there is no cause other than whatever initiates the development from potentiality to actuality. And such things as have no matter are all, without qualification, essential unities.

First, the exegesis. Reconstruct the difficulty Aristotle is addressing, including why the "round bronze" and "bronze triangle" examples are supposed to be instructive rather than beside the point. Then explain what work is being done by the qualifier "proximate" in "the proximate matter and the shape are one and the same," and by the hedge "in a sense" in "the potential and the actual are in a sense one" — in particular, what goes wrong if either qualifier is dropped. Finally, say plainly whether Aristotle is offering an *account* of why a portion of matter and a shape make one thing, or instead deflecting the question as ill-posed.

Second, the application. A well-known position about shaped material things runs roughly as follows. Where a figure stands, sculpted from a single lump of clay, there seem to be two things in one place: the figure and the lump. They are held to be two rather than one because they differ in what they can survive: flatten the figure into a ball and the figure is gone while the clay remains, whereas chipping a fleck off its surface is something the figure is said to survive and the lump is said not to. Different survival conditions, hence different things. I want to know whether Aristotle's vocabulary returns a verdict on the following two cases, where that argumentative route is blocked or inverted.

*Case one.* A bequest requires that a work be kept "exactly as it leaves my studio": no clay may be added, removed, or chemically altered, and the shape may not change even microscopically. Suppose also that the sculptor kneaded the clay into existence and shaped it in one continuous act, so that there is no interval during which the lump exists unshaped. Here the figure and the lump appear to have exactly the same persistence conditions, and the lump has no temporal career outside the figure. Does "the one existing potentially, and the other actually" still mark a real distinction between them, and if so, is it a distinction in *number* of things or only in manner of description?

*Case two.* An artwork consists of a quantity of clay whose point is precisely to have no shape: call it *Untitled (Heap)*. If someone sculpts it overnight, the work is destroyed, though the clay persists. Now the asymmetry is reversed — the thing individuated by its form is destroyed by *acquiring* a shape, and the thing individuated by its matter survives. Does the matter/potentiality versus form/actuality pairing have room for a case like this, or does it quietly assume that the form is always the determinate, governing side?

Third, one consequence I cannot assess. If the only cause of unity is "whatever initiates the development from potentiality to actuality" — the sculptor's activity — then the unity of the figure looks like it depends on how it came to be. Would a geometrically identical figure produced by a quite different route (say, erosion, or a machine extruding clay by accident) be the same kind of unity, or a different one, or none at all?
