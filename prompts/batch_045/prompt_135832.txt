I am writing a novel, and I need a number rather than an overview.

In the book, a hospital trustee — a capable, unsentimental woman, not a philosopher — defends spending a large sum now on a modest but certain improvement in care this year rather than banking it for a much larger and equally certain improvement in care thirty years hence. Her argument is not the familiar one. She expressly sets aside the risk that the money or the institution will not survive, the uncertainty about whether the later benefit will arrive, and the fact that a fixed sum buys less when everyone is richer. Even with all of that held constant, she says, the bare fact that the benefit falls at a time she is now further away from is itself a reason to weight it less: distance in time, on her view, is a property of the good, much as distance is for a fire, so that a late benefit is genuinely a weaker reason for action now rather than merely one that contingently turns out to matter less.

A second character replies that this is either incoherent or proves far too much. If temporal distance as such discounts, then the same reasoning should let the trustee weight her own mild inconvenience above a stranger's agony on the ground that the stranger is far away in a different dimension, and it should have licensed discounting yesterday's pain back when that pain was still tomorrow's.

I want to know how much of a case the trustee really has. Please give me your credence, as a number, that some nonzero positive rate of pure time preference is rationally permissible for an individual choosing between her own certain, equally large welfare benefits at different times, once risk of non-receipt, mortality, and diminishing marginal utility have been factored out — and decompose it: how much rides on whether temporal position can be an independent property of a benefit at all, as against welfare being "undated"; how much on whether personal identity over time is a further fact or is constituted by psychological continuity; how much on whether her argument can be stated without also licensing spatial or interpersonal discounting; and whatever other term you think belongs. A number and one line of justification for each, then the combination.

Then tell me where that number is most fragile: which single sub-question would move it furthest if you changed your mind, and what argument or thought experiment would move it. I ask because I need to know which joint the second character should be striking at; if the real weight lies somewhere other than where the trustee's argument sounds strongest, I would rather have her press there.

One last thing, briefly, because it decides the shape of my final chapter, where the same choice is made by a government rather than a person: is the case for a positive pure term in a social discount rate — the term left over once growth and the curvature of the utility function have been accounted for — stronger than, weaker than, or merely different from the trustee's case? A paragraph. I do not need citations; I can find my own reading.
