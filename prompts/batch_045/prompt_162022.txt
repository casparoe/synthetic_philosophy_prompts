I am reshaping a graduate seminar on practical rationality to open with the problem of ties rather than close with it, and before I assign any of the literature I want the specification rather than the survey: what would a theory of rational choice under genuine indifference have to deliver to be worth a week of my students' time?

Fix the purpose as I do. The theory is to serve two uses at once: to evaluate a tie-break from outside (was that agent's selection defensible, and answerable to whom?) and to be followable from inside deliberation by someone who has exhausted her reasons and is running out of time. If those two uses pull the requirements apart, say so.

The cases I treat as non-optional, and each requirement should be tied to at least one: (i) the equidistant-bales case with a clock on it, where suspension is costly and there is a deadline at which the animal starves or acts; (ii) the older variant of a man equally hungry and thirsty between food and drink; (iii) the continuum case, a long chain of options each imperceptibly better than the last but with endpoints that clearly differ, where any demand to find a difference threatens an infinite ascent; (iv) options equal only under a coarse description — two tins alike in every respect the agent can specify — and the question whether rationality requires refining the description before acting; (v) the tie that is epistemic rather than ontic, equal only for all the agent knows; (vi) the tie whose resolution will later be cited as a reason by others, as when a committee chair settles between two candidates of equal merit and the decision is read as precedent; (vii) the recurring tie, the same choice between indistinguishable routes every morning for a year; (viii) the tie delegated to a coin, a colleague, or an algorithm; (ix) the tie that is equal for the agent but unequal for someone else who is affected; (x) the case where the agent's inability to prefer comes from exhaustion or anxiety rather than from equal reasons; (xi) the case where the options are not equal but incomparable.

Two things I want respected rather than collapsed: the distinction between having no reason to prefer and having no reason that settles, and the distinction between a tie's being merely permitted to break and its being required to break. One term I stipulate for your use: explanatory residue, the "why that one?" that survives an arbitrary selection and to which no answer is available. A theory may need to accommodate that residue or to show it was never a genuine demand; I want to know which is a requirement and which is the prejudice of a camp.

Give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion. The first three are the desiderata you take to do the most work, each with its motivating case from my list and a mark of whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of some theoretical temperament. The fourth is the pair of desiderata, from your fuller list or from the three above, that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — or, if you think the list is jointly satisfiable, that claim instead — together with the single requirement that excludes the most otherwise attractive theories and what it excludes. The fifth is not a desideratum: it is what would change your mind about the list, which consideration would make you drop or add a requirement, and where your reasoning about the criteria is most likely to fail. Do not score the existing positions against the list; I will do that with the students.
