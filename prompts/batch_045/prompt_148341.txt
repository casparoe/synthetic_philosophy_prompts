I recently had an MRI for an unrelated reason, and it turned back an incidental finding: my intracranial volume sits in the top percentile for my age and sex, everything structurally unremarkable, no functional implications, nothing to be done about it. When I mentioned it at a family dinner, my brother-in-law — a neuroscientist, and a man who cannot let a fact sit still — said that on some views this makes me count for rather more than the rest of the table. He was mostly joking. The argument afterwards did not resolve, and I would like to know whether his joke can be made to bite or whether it collapses as soon as pressure is applied.

So here is what I am after. Design a thought experiment in which a difference in sheer neural mass — total amount of brain tissue, or total number of neurons, and nothing else — makes a genuine difference to how much a being matters morally, where the difference in size is not doing its work by being *evidence* of something else. Same behavioural repertoire, same discriminations, same functional organisation up to whatever you need to hold fixed, same body, same history, same prospects: only the size of the brain differs. The target verdict is not merely that we would be permitted to weight the larger-brained being more heavily. It is that its pains really do count for more — that the same headache, with the same reported intensity, the same behavioural signature and the same functional role, is a worse thing to inflict on one of them than on the other — and that this is a fact about what matters rather than a fact about what we can find out.

Constraints:

1. A realistic setting — a clinic, a laboratory, a farm, a household. A hypothetical intervention is acceptable if it is the sort of thing a neurosurgeon could in principle perform, but no demons, no duplicated universes, no exotic physics. One paragraph for the scenario itself.
2. The extra tissue must not constitute a separate subject. I do not want the case to win by turning into two minds in one skull: *two matter more than one* is a different and much easier claim than the one I am trying to test.
3. The extra tissue must not be doing any extra work — no additional memory, no additional robustness to damage, no greater speed or finer discrimination, no wider range of states. If the larger brain can do something the smaller one cannot, then size was only ever a proxy, and the case has failed.
4. No appeal to species membership, natural-kind membership, or sheer body mass. The case should not be one that works only because larger animals tend to be larger animals.
5. Size must not be a placeholder for something the scenario leaves unstated. If your case quietly depends on the bigger brain being more likely to be conscious, or on the smaller one not being conscious at all, say so — and it does not count.

Every version I have tried myself has failed in one of those five ways, and I cannot tell whether the failure is mine or the project's. So after the scenario, please do the following. (a) State which principle about moral status, or about the weight of welfare, the case is testing, and show that the principle delivers the verdict from the features of the case rather than from a stipulation that it does. (b) Run the *removal check*: delete the extra tissue, or disable it, or halve it, and say whether the verdict tracks size continuously or switches off all at once — and whether that is the answer your principle should want. (c) Identify the single feature of the case that is carrying the weight. (d) Give the strongest attack available to someone who thinks brain size is simply inert once function is held fixed; I would rather hear it from you than discover it later.

If you think the specification cannot be satisfied at all, say which constraint has to be relaxed and why, and then give me the strongest case you can with that constraint relaxed.
