I had a long argument last night with a friend who teaches in another department of my university, and I came away unable to tell whether the reform I favour is principled or merely self-flattering. I would like a document that settles the question for me and for the body I sit on.

The setting: I am a member of our Senate's Committee on Academic Standards, which must report to the Senate in three weeks with a recommendation on how the University should handle letters of recommendation written on behalf of our undergraduates for graduate study and national fellowships. Two motions are on the table.

Motion A, from the Dean of Graduate Studies, would require that every such letter be accompanied by a one-page structured comparative form. The writer would place the student in fixed percentile bands relative to two reference classes: all students the writer has taught in the preceding ten years, and the writer's own previous nominees for comparable awards. The form would go into the dossier beside the letter; the letter itself would remain uncapped and unedited.

Motion B, from two members of my committee, would drop the letter altogether for participating departments and substitute a verified writing sample: a graded, invigilated piece of a student's coursework from a designated course, submitted with the instructor's marginal comments and the full grade distribution for that course, with no recommendation letter at all.

The background, briefly. Over roughly fifteen years our letters have inflated to the point where a merely excellent student is now described in language that a decade ago was reserved for one student per cohort. Every writer I talk to knows this, and every writer also knows that restraint is not costless: a letter that is scrupulously calibrated costs that writer's own student admission or a fellowship relative to equally qualified candidates whose writers were not restrained. No reader I have spoken to any longer believes the vocabulary, and readers tell me they apply an informal department-specific discount, which they apply unevenly and which they cannot apply at all to writers they have never read before.

My friend is a labour economist, and her position was this. The inflation, she said, is a symptom and not the disease; the payoffs are set downstream, by fellowship and admissions committees that reward superlative density, and while they do so any writer who unilaterally disarms is simply transferring a benefit to writers at other institutions who have not. She also argued that Motion A does not end the arms race but relocates it one box over, where every writer will rediscover that her own student is in the top band; and she thought Motion B relocates it again, into coaching, editing, and the choice of which invigilated course to submit. She conceded one point in my favour: that Motion A would at least help students whose advocates write badly, which is not a random subset of our students.

What I want from you is the Committee's recommendation memo to the Senate. It should have an actionable bottom line — adopt A, adopt B, adopt A with conditions, reject both, or propose a fourth course I have not described — together with reasons the Dean and my two colleagues could actually reply to, an implementation path our registrar's office could carry out without any other university's cooperation, a timeframe including a review or sunset clause, and an honest statement of what would count as evidence that the chosen course had failed. Please do not spend the memo restating the diagnosis of inflation; assume the Senate grants it.

Two things matter to me particularly.

First, I have a half-formed idea that the two motions fail or succeed for different reasons, and that the whole question turns on something prior: what exactly is destroyed when letters inflate. If what a letter carries is essentially a ranking, then readers can in principle rescale and nothing much is lost beyond a shared vocabulary, and the case for either motion becomes much weaker than I assumed. If a letter also carries something non-comparative — a judgement about fit, about what kind of work this student is good at, that no percentile reproduces — then the inflation destroys something that cannot be re-scaled back into existence, and the two motions are aimed at quite different targets. I would like the memo to take a definite position on which of these is right, and to let that position decide the recommendation rather than decorating it.

Second, the memo should give my friend's objection its full weight rather than dismissing it. If the recommendation is to adopt something, it should say plainly what we are asking our students and their writers to risk, and why the risk is worth running; if the recommendation is to reject both motions, it should say what the objection implies we should do instead, including the possibility that a university acting alone should do nothing.
