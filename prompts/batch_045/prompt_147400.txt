I'm in a philosophy-of-history reading group, and last week we spent an unproductive hour on the concept of causal overdetermination — the firing-squad picture where two or more independently sufficient processes each bring about the same outcome, so that deleting any one of them leaves the event standing. The example we kept circling was the opening of the Berlin Wall in November 1989. On the standard account (which is all the detail I think you need, and I'd rather you didn't look anything up — no footnotes, no further reading, just the argument): through that autumn the GDR was haemorrhaging citizens via the corridor that opened when Hungary stopped policing its Austrian border, and via West German embassies in Prague and Warsaw where thousands had camped out; there were large weekly demonstrations, Leipzig's Monday marches being the famous ones; the economy was in visible decay; Soviet forces in the GDR stayed in their barracks and Gorbachev in Moscow had signalled he would not prop up the regime by force; and then, at a televised press conference, a party official appears to have misread his own notes and announced that the new travel regulations took effect immediately rather than the next day, after which crowds gathered at the crossings and a border commander with no orders to open anything opened his gate, and once one gate was up the rest came up within hours. My friend's claim was that this is a textbook overdetermined event: take away any one of those factors and the Wall still comes down, so the interesting question is only why it came down that night.

What I want from you is a disciplined mapping rather than a list of causes, and I want it run in both directions. Set up the outcome at more than one grain — the GDR ceasing to exist, the border regime ending in 1989, that particular crossing on that particular night — and tell me whether the overdetermination claim survives the change of grain or whether the grain is doing all the work. Then apply three distinctions I've been toying with and tell me which are real and which collapse into each other. Call a factor a *displacement* factor if deleting it slides the outcome in time, place, or manner rather than preventing it; a *substitution* factor if deleting it just means the outcome arrives by a different route; and a *deletion* factor if the outcome really doesn't happen without it. Notice separately that a factor can be dispensable for the outcome while being indispensable for the *explanation*, or the reverse, and say which of the 1989 factors fall where. Then the two worries I can't settle. First: a great many of these factors look merely incremental — each raises the probability without any of them being sufficient — in which case the package is nearly certain but nothing in it is redundant, and that is a different animal from a firing squad; does calling both 'overdetermination' hide the interesting difference, and is there a better cut? Second: the press-conference blunder and the border commander's unauthorized decision are causes that work by being *believed* — thousands of people acted on a rule that apparently was not yet in force, and by acting on it made it real — so say what an account built around independent sufficient processes should do with a cause whose efficacy depends on its uptake rather than its truth, and whether that is a case the framework ever anticipated. Finish by asking what would have to be true for the overdetermination claim to be *false* here — what single counterfactual, if you could run it, would refute my friend — because if nothing could, I suspect the claim is functioning as an epistemic hedge about how robust our evidence is rather than as a thesis about the causes.

Where the framework has nothing to say about this case, say so plainly instead of stretching it to cover the gap; I would rather have a short honest answer than a tidy one. And close with a short epistemic note: tell me how confident you are in the overall analysis, and single out the specific claim in it you are least sure of and why.
