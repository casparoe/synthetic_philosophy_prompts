**On being anchored: a naturalist's note on original intentionality**

1. The distinction, as I have met it, runs like this. My belief that there is water in the jug is about water in its own right; the ink marks on this page are about what they are about only because somebody reads them so. The first is original or intrinsic intentionality, the second derived. Derived aboutness is borrowed: it traces back, along a chain of designers, users and interpreters, to someone whose states are about things without borrowing.

2. Dennett's two-bitser is meant to unsettle that picture. The gadget — as I have encountered it in secondary discussion, and I would be glad to be corrected — discriminates twenty-five-cent pieces; it was built by us, for our purposes; and there is, Dennett is said to argue, no further fact hidden inside it that settles whether its internal state means *quarter* or means *slug of such-and-such a mass and diameter*. Its aboutness is whatever it is convenient for us to read into it. And if that is right, the chain never reaches bedrock: the distinction between original and derived intentionality is not a distinction between two kinds of aboutness, but a difference in how much work the intentional stance does for us. Dennett's own version of the argument, if I have it right, runs the point in the other direction too — since our intentionality is itself the output of natural selection, and selection is nobody's interpreter, there is nothing left for originality to be.

3. The obvious reply is not available to me. I do not think brains were designed, and I do not think being made of the right sort of stuff is the kind of property that could do semantic work. Nor, I want to argue, does the standard naturalist substitute help. Suppose we say that a state's content is fixed by what it was selected for doing. The two-bitser was selected as well — by us, in a design office — and so on this account it has proper functions in exactly the sense that I do. Selectional stories buy me a notion of function; they do not yet buy me a notion of originality, and I cannot see how to get the second from the first by piling on more selection. But equally, Dennett's inference from "selection is no interpreter" to "there is no originality" seems to me far too quick: it assumes that the only way to stop the regress is with an agent.

4. Here is my proposal. Say that a state's content is *anchored* in a system when the system itself registers and exploits the mismatch between what that state prepares it for and what then happens — when the system's own error signal, generated by its own commerce with the world, is what keeps the state tuned and what would move the system to revise it. Call a system *self-anchoring* when the anchoring of its states terminates in itself, and *other-anchored* when it terminates in someone else's interpretive practice. Then: original intentionality is self-anchoring, derived intentionality is other-anchored, and the two are different in kind rather than in degree. The two-bitser is other-anchored: nothing inside it registers that it has mistaken a slug for a quarter, and if its discrimination drifts, only we notice. I am, I take it, self-anchoring: my visual calibrations are continually corrected against my own movements, and the comparison of what I expected, given what I did, with what I then saw is internal to me — it is, so far as I can tell, what makes my states track anything at all.

5. The consequence I dislike. Nothing in the criterion mentions neurons, or flesh, or evolution. It is a structural condition on the organisation of a system's error dynamics. So it threatens to hand originality to artefacts. Call it the Crab: a mobile device that maintains a predictive model of its own sensorimotor loop, generates its error signals by comparing what it predicted with what it got, and uses those signals to retune both its sensors and its model; its only objective is to hold one internal variable within limits. We built it once and then left it for a decade in a tidal estuary, during which it retuned itself in ways we neither foresaw nor intended and now reliably discriminates, and acts differently upon, two states of the world that our specification never mentioned — water of two different salinities. Does its registration of salinity mean salinity, originally? My criterion appears to say yes: the anchoring is self-terminating, since it is the Crab's own error signals and not ours that hold the registration in place, and if the registration drifts it is the Crab that notices first. My instinct says no. The Crab's distinction between better and worse is ours at one remove — we fixed the variable it defends, and the notion of misfiring that does all the work was specified in the hardware. But then I worry that the same is true of me: my error signals are only errors relative to a set of variables that evolution, not I, settled.

6. So there seem to be three endings. Accept that the Crab is an original intender, and that originality is cheap and common. Or tighten the criterion until the Crab fails it — but I cannot think of a tightening that is not transparently ad hoc. Or concede the whole distinction to Dennett and say that there is nothing here but a difference in the usefulness of a stance. I currently incline to the first, but I incline to it for the unphilosophical reason that it is the only one of the three that lets me go on saying what I want to say about my own case.
