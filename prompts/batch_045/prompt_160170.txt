Fifteen-odd years of reading this corner of the internet have left me with a habit I have never audited: I take other people's word for what to do far more than I would like to admit, and my discomfort about it is wildly uneven across cases. I want to know whether the unevenness is principled or just squeamishness. Below are my gut verdicts, given as honestly as I can manage. What I want from you is the principle underneath them, if there is one.

Some baselines first, to make clear that deference as such is not what bothers me. My mechanic says the noise is a failing wheel bearing: I believe him, and there is nothing to feel odd about. My doctor says to start a statin: same. An epidemiologist says I should not visit my grandmother during a surge: I do not reconstruct her reasoning, and I do not feel I ought to.

The cases I actually care about:

1. A friend who works on animal-welfare research tells me the cage-free label on eggs is worth paying extra for, though much less than the label implies. I defer, I buy accordingly, and I would call the resulting belief mine. No discomfort at all.

2. My partner tells me a joke I made at a dinner party landed as sexist. I did not see it; she did. Accepting her verdict and apologizing is fine, but only as a stopgap. If I took the verdict and stopped there, never getting why, I would think I had handled it wrongly, and I would be no better at the next joke.

3. A statewide ballot measure would replace cash bail with a risk-assessment tool. I read a writer I trust, whose judgment has tracked mine on many things; I could not have assessed the underlying evidence myself; I voted yes on her say-so. Mild residue, but I would do it again, and I did not come away much better able to judge the next such measure.

4. A philosopher friend says that given my actual risk tolerance it is fine for us to have a child despite the chance that the child's life goes badly. Moderate resistance — not distrust of her exactly, but a sense that this is not hers to settle for me.

5. The same friend says giving ten percent of my income to a bednet charity is obligatory for me. Strong resistance, and I note that the discomfort is much sharper than in case 4 even though the reasoning is cleaner and the stakes are, if anything, more tractable.

6. A former aid worker tells me that short-term volunteering at an orphanage is harmful and that I should cancel the trip. I defer completely: I never read the research, I cannot reconstruct her reasoning, and afterwards I am no better at the next question of that shape. I feel entirely fine about it.

7. A friend who has organized unions for twenty years tells me not to cross the picket line at the café where I buy coffee. I defer and comply. I have not endorsed the claims about solidarity that seem to be doing the work, and I still feel fine.

8. My uncle tells me not to move in with my girlfriend, for reasons that bottom out in his religion. No deference at all, and the absence of deference does not feel like a failure of open-mindedness. I cannot say what distinguishes this from 6 and 7, where I also checked nothing.

9. My oldest friend says I should leave my partner: strong resistance. Another friend says a mutual acquaintance stole from her and that I should cut him off: I defer, and that feels fine. Both come from people who know me well, both concern my own conduct, neither is easy for me to check.

So: 6 and 8 look like the same case with opposite verdicts; 5 and 7 look like the same case with opposite verdicts; 4 and 5 differ in intensity for no reason I can name.

My own stab, to be tested rather than flattered. Deference about what to do is in good standing when (A) what it resolves is something the other person can see and I cannot — evidence, experience, local detail — rather than a weighing of considerations I can see as well as they can; and (B) once I have deferred I am better placed on the next question in the neighborhood, not merely on this one. Case 6 looks like a counterexample to (B) and case 7 to (A), so I suspect one clause is wrong or a third is missing: perhaps (C) the belief is doing coordination work inside a community I belong to rather than truth-tracking work, which would separate 7 from 8 on the grounds that I have left my uncle's community. But (C) makes the permissibility of deference depend on sociological accidents, which worries me. There is also an old blog phrase I half remember about learned helplessness in the epistemic sense — the worry that a policy of deferring atrophies the capacity to judge. Should the principle govern single acts of deference or habits of deference, and if habits, does that change any verdict above?

Three undecided cases, for the principle to earn its keep. (i) I ask an AI assistant whether I should text my ex. It supplies reasons, so it satisfies (B); it has no experience and no community, so it fails (A) and (C). My gut says no. Is my gut wrong? (ii) I put a job decision — whether to take an offer at a frontier AI lab, given what I think about accelerating capabilities — to a forum I have been reading for a decade; several hundred mostly pseudonymous people answer and roughly seventy percent say take it. Is deferring to that aggregate different from deferring to one person, and in which direction? (iii) Someone I take to be morally better than me but not better informed — no evidence I lack, no community we share — tells me I should forgive my father. Is there such a thing as moral expertise of that shape, and does your account permit deferring to it?

What I want back: one principle stated tightly enough that I can apply it myself, or, if the honest answer is a disjunction or a two-factor test, the candidates ranked, with each clause tagged to the case that forces it; verdicts on (i), (ii) and (iii); an explicit list of my verdicts you would overturn and why, steelmanning 4 and 5 before you do; and an explicit statement of where your account falls silent. I am not asking for citations or further reading — I will not follow them up and they would only be noise. I would rather have nine hundred dense words than two thousand loose ones. Please answer in American English.
