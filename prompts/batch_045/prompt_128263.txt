I'm on a tiny municipal review board — we get asked to sign off on things like a new de-icing solvent for the roads depot, a cell antenna on a school roof, a proposed release of sterile mosquitoes for dengue control, a landfill extension — and I keep reaching for "the precautionary principle" and then getting into arguments with my brother-in-law, who's a toxicologist and says it isn't a principle at all, because on pretty much any proposal you can point at some unstudied possible harm and thereby block it, and equally you can point at the harm of not doing the thing and thereby demand it. He's half right and I want to get less muddled. Here are my gut verdicts, which I suspect don't all fit together. New solvent, plausible mechanism for nerve damage from animal data, no human studies, slightly more expensive substitute available: don't use it, precaution applies. A drug for a fatal childhood disease, great early results, theoretical worry about a cancer thirty years out: approve it, and no — I don't think I'm just being inconsistent, because not approving has bodies attached too, but I don't know how to say when inaction counts as a risk. The school antenna, where we've actually looked for decades and found nothing: not a precaution case at all, even though I can't rule anything out. The mosquito release: much stronger pull toward caution than multiplying probability by magnitude would give me, because whatever goes wrong is permanent and I can't even enumerate the ways it could. Landfill extension, small health risk, falls on a poor neighbourhood that gets nothing out of it: precaution, hard. Same size risk borne by the landfill workers who know about it, chose the job, and get paid extra: much weaker pull. And then there's the one I go back and forth on — the same landfill job in a town where there is no other employer, so the "choice" feels fake, consented to but under pressure that isn't anyone's fault exactly. I also notice I want precaution to apply to things we've been doing for sixty years when a better test finds a problem, even though nobody ever *decided* to take that risk, which seems to kill any rule like "precaution is about new things"; and I want it to apply to harms that only land on people who don't exist yet, maybe more strongly than to present harms, though I can't say why "they don't exist" makes it stronger rather than weaker; and I want it to apply much less to something whose entire purpose is to reduce a risk — a flood barrier, a vaccine — even when that thing carries a small novel risk of its own, since banning the vaccine because of a one-in-a-million side effect strikes me as a misuse of the whole idea.

The best principle I've managed to cough up is something like: precaution is owed when (i) the downside is severe and hard to walk back, (ii) it lands on people who didn't take it on and can't realistically opt out, and (iii) the uncertainty is of the "nobody has looked" or "nobody can look" variety rather than the "we looked and got a null result" variety — and when all three hold, you don't get to weigh the benefits you'd forgo as if they were the same kind of item as the harm you might cause. I don't want a label or a reading list; I want to know whether that thing actually holds together, which of my verdicts it gets right, which it forces me to give up if I want it simple, whether there's a cleaner principle that gets more of them (and what it costs me), and how whatever you land on handles the no-other-employer case I keep flip-flopping on. Also, if you think one of my distinctions is bogus — the "we looked vs. nobody looked" one feels suspiciously convenient, like I invented it to save the antenna verdict — say so and tell me what to put in its place.
