I've been going back and forth with a guy in a LessWrong-adjacent Discord about Fitch's paradox of knowability, and I've managed to argue myself into a position I definately don't want. Short version: I now seem to have an argument that concludes (a) verificationism is false, (b) some truths are unknowable *in every possible world*, and (c) no being could know every truth, not even in principle — God included. (c) is where I want off the train, but I can't say which rail is bent.

Setup. Let "Kp" mean: someone knows that p — some agent, some time, possibly a merely possible one with a cognitive architecture nothing like ours. Assume:

1. Knowledge is factive: if Kp then p. Necessarily.
2. Knowing a conjunction means knowing both conjuncts: K(p ∧ q) → Kp ∧ Kq.
3. Unrestricted knowability, stated in the most generous form the anti-realist could ask for: necessarily, for every truth p, it's possible that Kp. I'm letting "possible" be as weak as you like and the knower be anyone at all, so nobody can complain I made the thesis too strong to be theirs.
4. Some truths are in fact unknown — say, whether the number of crows alive at noon on the first day of 2024 was odd. It's a truth either way, and nobody knows it.

Then:

5. Take such a p. "p and nobody knows p" is itself a truth, so by (3) it's possibly known.
6. But suppose someone knows "p and nobody knows p". By (2) they know p, and they know that nobody knows p; by (1), nobody knows p. Contradiction. So it isn't possibly known after all.
7. (5) and (6) collide, so there are no unknown truths: everything true is known. And since (3) was stated as necessary and nothing in (5)–(6) turned on any feature of the actual world, you can rerun the derivation inside any possible world. Necessarily, at every world, every truth true there is known there.
8. My friend's upgrade: you don't need (4) at all. By the same diagonal trick that gives you the liar sentence, there's a sentence F equivalent to "nobody knows F". Given (1) and classical logic, three of the four combinations of truth values for F and K(F) are ruled out at every world — (true, known) and (false, unknown) by the equivalence, (false, known) by factivity — so F comes out true and K(F) false *at every world*. So F is a truth that isn't merely unknown but unknowable, at every world, and no world contains anyone who knows every truth.
9. And the bit I'm most suspicious of: nothing in (5)–(7) used anything about knowledge beyond (1) and (2). Swap in any other notion that is factive and distributes over conjunction — truly believed by someone, provably established, conclusively evidenced — and you get the same collapse. Which means, my friend says, that no "in principle" thesis is safe anywhere: there can't be a cure that's discoverable but undiscovered, or ruins that are findable but unfound. Every "could in principle be X-ed" claim eats itself.

I'm stuck because (8) looks like it ought to be fine — its the same shape as the move Gödel makes, and nobody thinks Gödel cheated — but a modal-logic proof that omniscience is *impossible* is exactly the sort of output that makes me think I've broken something. Meanwhile the guy I'm arguing with says the honest response is to restrict (3) to propositions that don't mention knowledge, and I want to say that's gerrymandered: it carves out exactly the instances that break it and nothing you'd independently want carved out. But I also can't produce a principled test that its fails.

If it helps: I'm not a verificationist, so I'm happy to drop unrestricted (3); I'd much rather keep (1), (2) and (4).

So give me a verdict, not a menu. Either name the step where the reasoning breaks and say what's wrong with it, or — if (1)–(7) is kosher and something just has to go — tell me which premise you'd drop, why that's cheapest, and what you lose. Defend the choice. And if you think the restriction-to-non-knowledge-propositions move is principled rather than ad hoc, say what the principle is and how you'd test it, because "it excludes the bad cases" is the thing I'm objecting to.
