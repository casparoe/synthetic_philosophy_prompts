I write the humour page for my school newspaper, and I'd like a piece for next month's issue built around something our student council actually did to itself. About 600–700 words.

The situation: at the spring fundraiser we ran a "dollar auction" — the variant where the highest bidder takes the prize but both the highest bidder and the runner-up pay their final bids, so the house collects more than the prize is worth. Our prize was a $20 cafeteria gift card. Bidding passed $20 inside a minute and stopped near $70, split between the debating society and the robotics club. Nobody involved can now explain why they didn't walk away.

What I want is a Treasurer's Report on student-council letterhead — addressed to the principal and the council, with a subject line, numbered findings, and an appendix table headed something like "Projected Recovery Schedule" showing the deficit shrinking in percentage terms if we keep going — which reports on the auction and recommends that it be made permanent and expanded.

On the content:

- The jokes have to come from getting the mechanism right, not from anyone being silly. Performed completely straight: each new bid is trivial next to what quitting would cost; the runner-up pays and receives nothing; once the total passes the value of the card, only the house profits; and the one move that was ever safe — never having entered — gets reclassified by the treasurer as a disciplinary matter ("declining to participate is not a budget position"). I'd also like the genuinely counter-intuitive bit somewhere: that the odds of the other side folding don't rise just because you've already spent more, since what you've spent is sunk and frightens nobody. The treasurer should file this as an argument for spending more — if the probability won't rise on its own, the council must raise it.

- The escalation should be recognisably the real thing: the treasurer personally proposed the auction, so stopping now means admitting a mistake in front of the whole school at assembly; there is an audience; the vocabulary is all "seeing it through", "we're past the worst of it", "abandoning the process now would waste everything already committed". And the one intervention that would actually help — a spending ceiling agreed before anyone bid — should be quietly repealed in a late clause, on the grounds that it "constrains the council's flexibility to respond to developments".

- Two invented rules, so this isn't a rerun of the standard example. First, a Re-entry Rule: the runner-up, having paid, may re-enter at any point at ten per cent above the standing bid and receives continuity credit for everything already paid. Second, the House Position: the council, which is also the auctioneer, may bid using student-activity funds, on the reasoning that every dollar bid above the card's face value is revenue for the council's own budget — presented as fiduciary duty. The piece should end with expansion: franchise the format to other schools.

- One councillor should be quoted in dissent, and the objection must be the true one rather than a strawman: that the whole exercise is a transfer from two clubs to the council, and that "if we stop now we lose what we've spent" is not a reason for anything, because that money is gone either way. The treasurer should answer it — badly, but not stupidly.

Deadpan throughout: the treasurer never winks, never notices, is lucid and quietly proud. No moral at the end, no footnote explaining the game to readers, no naming of researchers or papers — I know the standard names, please leave them out; the column should work on someone who has never heard of the auction. The joke should land on the bookkeeping and on the "too much invested to quit" style of reasoning, not on the students who got caught.

Then a note of about 150 words: which parts you invented — specifically the two rules — and whether they change the structure of the game or merely name an incentive that was already present (I'm most unsure about the House Position: is a bidding auctioneer a new mechanism, or just the auctioneer's incentive made visible?); and what would make the satire unfair. I'd like you to say what would change your mind: if escalating is often reasonable in real cases because the person continuing has private information, or because backing down carries reputational and credibility costs genuinely worth paying, then the treasurer is a strawman and the piece is cheap. Tell me honestly whether the strongest version of "we've come too far to stop now" survives your own treatment of it, and where you think your reasoning is most likely to have gone wrong.
