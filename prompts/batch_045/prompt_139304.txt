QUESTION 3 (25 marks; suggested time 40 minutes)

Distinguish the etiological (selected-effect) account of biological function associated with Larry Wright and Ruth Millikan from the causal-role account defended in Cummins's 1975 paper, and assess the following thesis:

(T) If function ascriptions are to do explanatory work in contemporary biology, function must be a property a trait has in virtue of its current causal organization within a containing system, and not in virtue of its history.

Your answer should: (a) state what each account takes a function to be, and what explanatory work it takes function ascriptions to do; (b) discuss at least one case that discriminates between the two accounts; (c) explain what each account can say about malfunction, and say why the capacity to handle malfunction is thought to matter; and (d) assess the suggestion, raised in seminar, that the two accounts are not rivals at all but answers to different questions — stating how that suggestion would reframe the dispute and what it would cost either side to accept it.

Candidates are invited to draw on either or both of the following cases, neither of which was discussed in the seminar.

Case A. A synthetic-biology group assembles a bacterial cell whose genome is chemically synthesized rather than inherited. Into its membrane they insert a transporter of a kind found nowhere in nature, which moves a particular sugar into the cell. They put it there because they want the cell to grow on that sugar, and they then propagate the lineage for many thousands of generations in a medium where that sugar is the sole carbon source.

Case B. In a lineage of soil bacteria, a gene coding for an enzyme that hydrolyses a common sugar is duplicated. The product of the duplicate, as a by-product of its altered molecular shape, also cleaves a synthetic antibiotic that has been present in the soil for roughly two centuries. Cells carrying the duplicate have spread through the population. Population-genetic work suggests that the spread was very nearly neutral for the first several hundred generations after the antibiotic appeared, and became selection-driven only about fifty generations ago, when the ambient concentration rose. Practitioners who study the population now routinely describe the enzyme as functioning to confer antibiotic resistance.

STUDENT ANSWER

The etiological and the causal-role accounts disagree about whether function is a historical property or a current one. On the etiological (selected-effect) account associated with Wright and Millikan, a trait's function is the effect for which tokens of that trait were selected: the heart functions to pump blood because pumping blood is why hearts were favoured by natural selection. Function is thus a relational, backward-looking property, and two organisms that are molecule-for-molecule identical at a time can differ in function if their causal histories differ. On Cummins's causal-role account, by contrast, to ascribe a function to a part is to identify the contribution it makes to some capacity of a containing system whose explanation we are after: functional analysis decomposes a complex capacity such as circulation into the simpler capacities of the parts, and assigns each part the role it plays in producing the whole. The two accounts also disagree about what work function ascriptions do. On the etiological account they answer a why-is-it-there question and carry the normativity that makes talk of a part's proper operation intelligible; on Cummins's account they answer a how-does-the-system-work question, and they do so without any backward-looking metaphysics.

The cases separate the two accounts. In Case A the transporter has no selection history at all, only a designer's intention, so the causal-role account can assign it, straight away, the function the designers built it to perform, whereas the etiological account has no history to appeal to and must apparently wait until the lineage has been propagated for long enough for selection to have acted on the transporter's effects. That is an awkward result for a part that everyone would agree is functional from the moment the cell is assembled. In Case B the contrast is sharper. The enzyme was originally selected for hydrolysing the sugar, and on the etiological account that is what its function is, period: history does not change. Yet it is the resistance-conferring role that does the explanatory work in the population biologist's model of the spread of the duplicate, and it is that role that practitioners cite when they say what the enzyme is for. Since what biology needs from a function ascription is the role that figures in current explanations, the case supports (T): function must be fixed by current causal organization.

The etiological account's best-known advantage is its treatment of malfunction. A heart that cannot pump is still a heart with the function of pumping, precisely because it is a token of a type whose effects were selected for, and that is what makes it defective rather than merely different. Cummins's account, as he originally set it out, seems able to say only that the diseased heart lacks the relevant capacity and therefore lacks the function, which loses the normative dimension of function talk. His account also faces the familiar problem of proliferation: the heart makes sounds, displaces neighbouring tissue and occupies space, and if function is simply causal contribution to a containing system then all of these are functions. The appeal to the investigator's explanatory interests is what is supposed to narrow the set, but it does so at the cost of making function ascriptions depend on what we happen to want explained.

Finally, the suggestion that the two accounts answer different questions offers less than it appears to. Granted, functional analysis tells us how a system currently works while the etiological account tells us why the part is there. But that simply raises the question of which of the two questions biology actually asks, and the answer is not in doubt: working biologists determine a part's function by finding out what it currently does within the containing system, and never by reconstructing a selection history. So the different-questions move does not neutralise the dispute; it resolves it in favour of the causal-role account, and (T) should be accepted.
