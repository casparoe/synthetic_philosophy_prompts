hey, so I'm the undergrad rep on our university's Research Ethics Board, and I'm also taking this astrobiology seminar called Life in the Universe this term where we've spent like three weeks on the Fermi paradox, which is basically the only reason this file ended up on my desk instead of someone else's.

A student society here wants to bolt a transmitter onto the department's dish (it's only ever been a reciever) and beam a narrowly focused, repeating signal at a nearby star that has a rocky planet in its temperate zone — about four light-years, so four years out and four back minimum. The message is a self-decoding primer: counting, then arithmetic, then pictures of people and a greeting. They want to start transmitting in the spring and they've asked for a three-year run of dish time.

The board is split right down the middle. Half think it's speech and science, and that the Great Silence is mostly an artifact of how little of the sky we've actually searched properly, for how short a time, with how narrow a set of assumptions. The other half think an irreversible announcement, made on behalf of the whole species by six people in a meeting room, is exactly the kind of thing that shouldn't get approved because it's cool.

Two things I flagged that nobody had an answer for. First, their main argument is that we're already findable anyway — atmospheric biosignatures, planetary radar, decades of broadcast leakage — so staying quiet buys us nothing. But the beamed signal they're describing is something like a million times easier to detect than our leakage, and I can't work out which way that cuts: either they can already see us (so the beam adds nothing, but also risks nothing) or they can't (so the beam is the actual announcement, and the "we're already loud" line is doing no work at all). Second, the chair floated a compromise — approve it but restrict the content to friendly maths-only stuff, no biology, no coordinates — and the applicants say that's theatre, because you can't beam anything at a star without revealing which direction it came from, and a repeating schedule gives away our rotation and orbit anyway.

Can you draft the decision memo I'll circulate before next week's meeting? It needs a real bottom line — approve, deny, or approve with conditions, and if it's conditions then with a timeline and something the applicants could actually go away and do. Please don't just tell us the Great Silence is evidence we should be careful; that's the stalemate we're already stuck in, and it's the one thing everyone on the board has already said twice. I also want the memo to be honest about the strongest thing on whichever side loses, and to say something about whether six people on a university ethics board are even the right body to be making a call whose downside would land on everyone alive and everyone not yet born, or whether the honest recommendation is to kick it upward somewhere.
