Take your time, think carefully, and before you write the document do a quick brainstorm pass: each side's three strongest premises, and the strongest thing the other side says back.

Here's the setup. At work we have this running argument about bug reports. Someone on eval writes, "the model thinks the user is in Berlin, and that's why it keeps recommending German tax forms." Someone else — usually Dev, who does red-teaming — crosses it out and rewrites it as "the model's continuations are consistent with the hypothesis that the user is in Berlin." Maya, who wrote the original, says the rewrite is longer, less predictive, and doesn't tell the on-call engineer what to actually change. Dev says the original sneaks in a claim nobody has earned. They've been doing this in a shared doc, in front of the whole team, for about a year. Nobody has moved an inch.

So draft the document they should have written instead: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the two camps.

Camp A (Maya's): attributing beliefs, goals, and desires to the system is often the best explanation of what it does; when a prediction strategy is indispensable, has no equally good competitor, and survives arbitrary changes in surface form, the attitudes it posits aren't a convenient fiction — at which point either they're real beliefs or the question of whether they're real has been settled in the affirmative.

Camp B (Dev's): the system was trained to continue text produced by creatures that do have beliefs, so of course its output looks like the output of a believer; content-talk is our projection onto a pattern-completer, and whatever predictive success it has is success at modeling the distribution of human talk, not success at latching onto states of the machine.

What I want in it, roughly:

- An agreed-claims section that isn't mush. They already agree on a lot: that the model tracks facts that appear nowhere in training (give it a story about a made-up town and it'll answer questions about the town), that intentional-level description sometimes beats any purely surface-level model of its behavior, that the stance also fails in distinctive ways (sycophancy, prompt-sensitivity, confident confabulation), and that nothing here turns on consciousness. Find the agreements that are real and load-bearing and put those in.

- The disagreements, each in wording its own side would sign rather than a straw version.

- A hypothesis about the cruxes, which you should use or tell me why it's wrong. I think there are three. (1) What a belief has to be hooked up to: does content require the right kind of causal history — learning from and acting on the world — or is it enough that a state plays the right role in guiding behavior? (2) What the bar is for the stance earning its keep: is it enough that it's our best available prediction strategy for this system, or does it have to be the best strategy in principle, or does it have to beat a competitor somebody actually builds? (3) What to do with the failures: when belief-talk misfires (ask the same thing three ways, recieve three different answers), is that evidence there's no belief there, or just the same messiness you get from a distracted human with inconsistent beliefs?

- For each crux, a case or test both of them would accept as bearing on it, with each of their predictions written down in advance. No hand-waving about future science — I want something runnable next sprint. One to start with, use it if it's any good: early in a long session you tell the model, buried in other material, that the spare key is in the kitchen drawer; forty turns later, after unrelated stuff, you ask something that only has a right answer if the key is in the kitchen drawer, phrased so that it never mentions the key or the drawer. I'll let you write both predictions. Add whatever else earns its place — cross-format or cross-language probes, betting setups where the payoffs are only described in text, activation-level interventions, whatever.

- And be honest about which parts are verbal. If part of this is a fight about what the word "belief" should be reserved for, say exactly where the verbal part stops and the substantive part starts, and don't let the verbal part be an excuse for skipping the substantive one.

Format: a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table (issue / wording Camp A accepts / wording Camp B accepts / crux / agreed test / Maya's prediction / Dev's prediction), then a short section on what each side would count as evidence against itself, and whether either of them should expect to be moved by anything in the next few years.

Keep it tight — under a page of prose plus the table. No literature survey, no citations, no suggested reading; I want the document, not the bibliography.
