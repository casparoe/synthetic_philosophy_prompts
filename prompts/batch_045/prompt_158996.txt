I read the master–slave chapter of Hegel's Phenomenology in a course last spring and it has been rattling around in my head ever since, so now I keep trying to apply it to things and my friends are sick of it. The thing I keep coming back to is university degrees, partly because I'm supposedly applying to grad school and partly because my roommate (first-gen, graduated in May, extremely done with all of it) got into an argument with me about it and I don't think either of us won.

Here's the bit that I can't make consistent. Every university says, more or less out loud, that the degree does not make you educated — the learning does, the diploma is just a record of it, and if you ask around you'll also hear the harder version where the whole thing is just a signal to employers about traits you already had. But then: universities treat degree-granting as a monopoly they guard fiercely, and there's this whole apparatus of accreditation to police who may grant one; a diploma from a degree mill is treated as fraud, and not merely as a bad purchase, even in the case where the buyer genuinely taught themselves the material and would be indistinguishable on the job; and the very same institutions hand out honorary degrees to people who did no coursework whatsoever and nobody treats those as counterfeit. So what is actually doing the work here — the learning, the record, or the stamp? If it's the first two, the mill case and the honorary case shouldn't come apart the way they do.

So: what view of recognition, or of social worth, does the whole credentialing practice presuppose? Does it strictly require something like an institutional theory — a person's standing in this respect isn't theirs until a recognized authority confers it — or is it equally compatible with a signaling story, or with something else I haven't thought of? Which rival views does it quietly rule out, and where is it underdetermined, so that several quite different theories would license the same practice? My roommate's line was that the signal story is basically a cover and the practice only makes sense if you think worth has to be conferred from above; I half believe her but I want it reconstructed properly, including the parts where the practice doesn't need her theory at all.

Two more things I specifically want, since they're where the Hegel thing bites for me. First, the university looks like it's sitting in the master's position: its authority to confer anything seems to depend on the recognition of exactly the people it certifies and excludes — the graduates who have to keep caring, and the non-graduates who have to keep treating the thing as valuable even though it excludes them. Is that one-sided recognition in Hegel's sense, and is it unstable in the way he says the master's is, or am I forcing the analogy? Second, suppose we took mutual recognition seriously as a design constraint rather than a metaphor — say credentials that lapse unless they're periodically renewed by the people they were supposed to impress, or a guild-ish setup where the certified get a real say in whether the certifier still has standing. What would actually have to change in the practice, and what would break?
