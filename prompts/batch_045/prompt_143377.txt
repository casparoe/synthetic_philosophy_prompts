I am assembling the first session of a graduate reading group on the epistemology of thought experiments in the natural sciences, and I want the session's handout to be a classification instrument rather than a survey of views. Please design one for me.

The instrument has to do two jobs at once, and the interesting part is making each job work without collapsing into the other:

(1) It sorts *readers*. Someone who has never heard of the debate should be able to work through a series of concrete cases and come out the other end holding a named account of whether and how a thought experiment can deliver knowledge about nature. That means every question has to be a case or a claim on which the accounts genuinely diverge, phrased so that a reader can answer it without already knowing which account it favours. No question may take the form "do you think thought experiments are really just arguments?" or any other question that hands the reader the label.

(2) It sorts *cases*. The same tree, read top-down from the root, should take a particular thought experiment as input and return a verdict about what kind of epistemic work that thought experiment can do, and for whom. So each branch node must be a question one can ask about a case, not only about a person.

Please deliver five parts, in this order.

**Part A — the questions.** Exactly ten, each a concrete scenario or claim, each with two or three answer options, none of them longer than a short paragraph. At least five of the ten must be built from the four invented cases I describe below, and at least two from well-known historical cases of your choosing (I would like Galileo's two-stone argument against the Aristotelian speed law and Newton's rotating bucket to be among them, but you may substitute others if you say why). The remaining questions should fill in distinctions the cases do not reach. No position name, and no phrase like "the view that", may appear anywhere in Part A.

**Part B — the logical space first, the positions second.** Before naming any leaf, lay out the ten questions as ten slots, say how many answer combinations they formally permit, and then locate the actual accounts inside that space. Do not start from a list of famous names and work backwards to questions. Then pick at least three combinations that no one occupies and, for each, say whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is merely unattractive, or because it is coherent, attractive and simply unoccupied — and, for that last kind, what someone holding it would have to believe.

**Part C — eight leaves.** Each leaf gets a one-sentence statement of the account and a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment. Seven are positions I want named as follows. Use these only as working labels, and describe rather than attribute: no author names, titles, dates or schools anywhere in the handout.

- a Platonist or rationalist account: thought experiments put us in contact with features of nature by a kind of rational intuition, and can yield substantive new knowledge a priori, without new empirical input;
- the argument or "nothing new" account: a thought experiment is a disguised argument whose premises are all already available empirically, so the conclusion is genuinely new only in the sense of being newly noticed, and the drama of the narrative is psychologically useful but epistemically idle;
- a mental-model or simulation account: the reasoner runs a simulation on an internalised model, and the epistemic status of the verdict tracks the fidelity of that model rather than any special faculty;
- a conceptual-revision account: thought experiments characteristically do not disclose facts but expose incoherence between our concepts and a theory's commitments, and their payoff is a forced revision of the conceptual scheme, not discovery;
- anti-exceptionalism about modal judgement: verdicts about imagined cases are ordinary counterfactual judgements of the same fallible kind we make constantly, neither privileged nor specially suspect, and there is no distinctive epistemology of the thought experiment as such;
- the pump or persuasion account: thought experiments change what we find plausible without supporting what they make plausible, and their reliability is a matter of rhetorical efficacy rather than warrant;
- an instability or debunking account: verdicts about imagined cases are sensitive to presentation, framing, order and the demographic and professional profile of the reader to a degree that defeats the claim that they track the facts.

The eighth leaf is one you must construct. I will describe it; you give it a name and a place in the tree. Call it provisionally the *calibrationist* view: a verdict reached by thinking through an imagined scenario counts as knowledge only to the extent that the mechanism producing it has a demonstrable track record of success on independently checkable cases of the same kind, so that extrapolating a verdict beyond the calibrated range yields at most a defeasible expectation and never knowledge. Locate it, say which of its neighbouring cells it might equally have occupied, state whether it collapses into the mental-model leaf or the instability leaf or neither, and hazard one sentence on why it is not defended in the literature — or, if you think it is simply a version of one of the seven, say that instead and supply a different eighth leaf of your own construction.

**Part D — the scoring key and its limits.** Give the mapping from answer patterns to leaves, with explicit tie-breaking rules for inconsistent or mixed patterns. Then give a minimal-move map: for each leaf, name the single answer change that would relocate a reader to a sibling leaf. Finally, list every pair of leaves your instrument cannot separate, together with the additional question that would separate them if it could be asked, and a note on why that question does not fit this format (for instance because answering it already requires knowing the answer, or because it is a question about the metaphysics of the faculty rather than about any case). Do not paper over these pairs.

**Part E — routing the cases.** Push each of the four invented cases and each historical case through the tree from the root, recording the branch taken at each node and the leaf reached. For each case, state which leaves it separates. If any of my four invented cases reaches a cell no named position occupies, say so explicitly and describe the view that lives there. If the tree's routing of a case changes when the case is described differently, say where and why.

The four invented cases, to be used as the spine of the instrument:

- **The unreachable regime.** A scenario concerning a system that can only exist in conditions no experiment can produce and no computer we can build can simulate, because the state space is too large and no shortcut is known. A careful physicist works through the description and reports a confident verdict about what must happen. Ask what that confidence is worth, and what would have to be true for it to count as knowledge of nature rather than knowledge of a model.
- **The framing flip.** One physical scenario, two descriptions: in one a valve opens when a fast molecule approaches, in the other a tiny agent observes and chooses. Verdicts about what happens to the relevant quantity flip depending on which description is given, and afterwards everyone agrees the two descriptions specify the same physical arrangement. Ask what the flip shows, and whether it bears on this particular verdict only or on verdicts about imagined cases generally.
- **The consensus reversal.** Archival work establishes that a century ago every competent physicist who considered scenario S reached the verdict opposite to ours, and that the difference is traceable entirely to a false background assumption they all shared and we do not — they took simultaneity to be absolute, say. Ask whether that history changes what our own confident verdict about S is worth, and in which direction.
- **The surrogate simulation.** For scenario T, a simulation code validated in a hundred adjacent cases but not in T itself returns one verdict, while the trained intuition of everyone we ask returns the other, and no experiment can settle T. Ask which is revised, and what that choice commits the reader to about where the authority of a verdict about an imagined case comes from.

Standing constraints on the whole handout. It will be used by readers with no library access and no internet, so nothing in it may depend on looking anything up: no citations, no bibliography, no footnotes, no further-reading sections, no titles, dates or attributions, and no verbatim quotation from any text — paraphrase and describe instead, even for the historical cases. Assume the reader has never encountered the debate but is philosophically literate, and that the whole instrument, including the routing in Part E, must be completable or followable in about fifteen minutes. Where a distinction you would like to draw requires empirical findings about how people actually respond to scenarios, mark it as such and say how the instrument would need to change were those findings to come in one way rather than another, rather than relying on any result as settled.
