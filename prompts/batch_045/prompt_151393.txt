My friend and I have been arguing about whether "avoid side effects" can be turned into a formal objective for an AI system, and he has produced an argument whose conclusion I reject but whose steps I cannot fault. I would be grateful for a diagnosis of where it goes wrong.

The case we keep returning to: a household robot is told to fetch a mug from a high shelf. The mug sits behind a heavy vase. The candidate behaviours are going around the vase, moving it carefully, knocking it aside and then catching and replacing it before anyone notices, and — the one that matters here — arranging things so that nothing else in the kitchen ever moves again.

The argument, as he writes it:

(1) A side-effect penalty is only worth having if it can be specified without a full specification of value. If we already knew which outcomes are good, we would simply optimise that, and the penalty would add nothing; the whole point of the exercise is to get safety-relevant constraints without first solving the problem the constraints were meant to postpone. Call this independence.

(2) Independence rules out building the penalty out of judgements about which states are better than which. What is left as raw material is only (a) the dynamics of the environment, (b) the agent's own trajectory, and (c) the agent's remaining ability to bring states about.

(3) So any admissible penalty must be either [i] a measure of change in the agent's ability to bring about some set of reference goals — it can still reach states in which the vase is intact, still reach states in which the tea gets made, and so on — or [ii] a measure of divergence between the world that actually unfolds and a counterfactual world in which the agent did something else.

(4) [i] fails. Selecting which abilities to track — the reachability of intact-vase states rather than broken-vase states — just is a judgement about which outcomes matter, which is exactly what independence forbids. A penalty defined over a set of reference goals is a value function with the value smuggled in through the choice of goals.

(5) [ii] fails as well. Its only non-arbitrary version compares the actual future with the future that would have unfolded had the agent done nothing. But that installs one particular future as the reference, and every later departure from it counts as impact — including departures brought about by the people in the house, whose subsequent choices are divergences from that reference just as much as the robot's own actions are. So the penalty rewards suppressing their later agency. It is also blind to whether a change is a rescue or an accident, and it can be satisfied by breaking something and then restoring it, which is not the same as never having disturbed it.

(6) The usual repairs do not help. Substituting the rollout of a careful or a competent agent for the inaction rollout simply relocates the value judgement of (4) inside the baseline. Restricting the penalty to irreversible changes permits anything reversible, however reckless.

(7) I proposed a fourth option of my own: instead of comparing the agent's behaviour with its own inaction, compare it with what the same agent would have done had it been given a different goal drawn from a broad class — a substitution baseline, so to speak. An agent whose conduct is nearly the same whichever goal it was handed is an agent whose mandate is not reshaping the world; an agent that wrecks the kitchen unless it happened to be told to be careful scores badly. My friend replies that drawing the substitute goals requires a distribution over goals, that a distribution over goals is a judgement about which goals matter, and that my proposal therefore collapses back into (4).

(8) Hence: every candidate penalty is either a hidden value function, or blind to the difference between a rescue and a wreck, or positively rewards freezing the world. There is no way to define side-effect avoidance without already possessing the value specification the programme was supposed to replace; the search for impact measures rests on a confusion, and the effort should go into specifying or learning values instead.

I do not want to accept (8). It seems to me to prove far too much, since the style of argument used in (4) and in my friend's reply to (7) would condemn any constraint one might hand an agent, and (5) reads to me like a description of a bug rather than a refutation. But I cannot locate the break.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? If I have to choose, I would rather keep independence as stated in (1) and the conviction that freezing the kitchen is bad than keep the exhaustiveness of the dichotomy in (3) — though I may well be wrong about which claim is really load-bearing, and I would especially like to know whether the substitution baseline in (7) is genuinely a fourth option or merely (4) in disguise. Tell me which step does the work, why that step looks innocent, and what each of the alternatives I might instead give up would cost me in the end.
