I teach an introductory ethics course, and every year I rewrite the answer key for the week we spend on trolley cases and the doctrine of double effect. This year I want the key to derive the verdicts from one statement of the principle rather than from a list of intuitions, and in trying to do that I have found that I cannot tell whether what I hold is a single view or several views that happen not to have met. I would be grateful for an audit.

What I hold, as carefully as I can state it:

(i) The doctrine of double effect, in the form I want: there is a serious moral difference — a difference in what I may do, not merely in what I am to blame for — between a foreseen harm that enters my action as that by which I achieve my end and a foreseen harm that enters it as something that merely happens along the way. The second is governed by proportionality; the first is not something a favourable ratio of lives saved converts into something I may do. So there is, as far as I can see, no number of people on the main track at which it becomes permissible to push the man off the footbridge.

(ii) There is no intrinsic moral difference between doing and allowing, or between killing and letting die, and there is no further wrong of "using" a person over and above what (i) already captures. I have been told that (ii) leaves me unable to explain why the transplant surgeon may not proceed; it seems to me that (i) explains that on its own, and I would like to know whether that is right.

(iii) Verdicts: turning the trolley is permissible; pushing the man off the footbridge is not; the transplant surgeon may not proceed; and the loop variant is permissible — the spur rejoins the main track, so that the man standing on it is what stops the trolley before it comes round to the five, and I take diverting onto the loop to be permissible even so.

(iv) Whether an action is permissible cannot depend on how things actually turn out, only on what the agent had reason to believe: permissibility is settled by the agent's evidence. I hold this for the usual reason — I do not want to say that someone who did everything a reasonable person would do acted impermissibly because of a fact that was beyond her ken.

(v) The distinction (i) turns on is a distinction in the action, not in the agent's head. Whether a death entered my action as a means or only as a consequence is settled by the actual causal structure of what I did and by how my agency was in fact directed — not by what I was attending to, and not by what I took the causal structure to be. If it were settled by occurrent mental states, whether I may act would depend on what I happened to be thinking about while acting, which seems to me morally arbitrary.

(vi) Method: I treat the verdicts in (iii) as data that an adequate principle has to recover, not as noise to be explained away, which is why I am unmoved by arguments that derive those verdicts from emotional response or from features of the cases that are merely presentational. I also want a principle that generates the verdicts rather than one that restates them, so I am suspicious of any repair that amounts to labelling the cases.

Two years ago a student proposed a pair of cases I have never been able to answer, and I now suspect the reason is that (iv) and (v) deliver opposite verdicts on them — though I cannot see what exactly is doing the work, or whether the fault really lies there.

Case A. The loop variant as in (iii). The agent diverts the trolley onto the spur, reasonably believing that the man on it is what will stop the trolley before it reaches the five. In fact — and there was no way for her to have discovered this — a maintenance wagon is parked on the spur beyond where he is standing, so the trolley would have stopped in time anyway; the collision with him makes no causal difference to whether the five live.

Case B. The switch case. The agent diverts onto a spur where a man is working, and her evidence is that he is standing beyond the point at which the trolley will come to rest, so that she foresees at most a small risk to him and certainly does not take his being struck to be part of how the five are saved. In fact the buffer at the end of the spur has been left unbolted: the trolley runs the full length of the spur, kills him, and only his body wedged against the buffer prevents it from breaking through and reaching the five. Had he not been there, they would have died.

By (iv), it seems to me, she acts impermissibly in A and permissibly in B; by (v), the reverse. I cannot see what the difference between the two cases is supposed to consist in, which makes me think I am helping myself to something I have not stated.

So: go through the set and tell me which commitments are genuinely in tension and which only look as though they are, ordered by how severe the tension is rather than listed as equally bad. For each real tension, say what would have to be given up, what the cheapest revision would be, and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set; where two commitments can be kept by drawing a distinction I have not drawn, say what the distinction is. And if the strain is somewhere I have not indicated, I would rather be told.
