Hey — I write a weekly column on behavioural science and ideas for a weekend magazine. I'm working on a piece about procrastination and the whole willpower-app/commitment-device industry, and I keep running into people who say procrastination isn't weakness of will at all, it's just your preferences shifting over time in a way that's perfectly explicable — the present-biased / hyperbolic-ish discounting story. I want to know whether those two stories can actually be pulled apart in an ordinary case, so I need a thought experiment I can drop into the middle of the column.

Build me this.

A single ordinary scenario — one paragraph, under about 120 words, jargon-free and printable as-is, set in a working life a reader would recognise (a freelancer with a commission, a PhD student and a thesis chapter, someone training for a 10k, whatever you like). In it, one agent faces the same pair of options at two different moments: smaller-sooner vs larger-later, offered early, then offered again when the soon option is imminent. The gap between the two options stays fixed; only the distance from now changes.

Specifications, and I want you to show your work on each rather than just assert it:

1. The reversal (early: she prefers larger-later; later: she takes smaller-sooner) has to fall out of the discounting model, not be stipulated. 2. The hard part, and the bit the column turns on: at no point may she act against her own all-things-considered judgement about what's best. No moment where she thinks B is better and does A. No forgetting, no new information, no factual mistake, no ambushing desire, no addiction, no diagnosis, nothing clinical.

3. And yet something has to have gone wrong: she ends up worse off by her own lights, she'd grab a commitment device if you offered her one, and an ordinary reader would call her weak-willed. 4. Name the single feature of the case doing the work — the one that lets (1) and (2) hold while still generating (3). What counts as cheating for my purposes: the piece asks whether procrastination is a failure of will or a failure of timing, so a scenario that just stipulates "she judges B better and does A" begs the question — that's the thing in dispute.
