I'm a junior at a large public high school, and I edit the back page of our student newspaper, which runs one piece of satire each issue. I'd like to commission one for the next issue.

Our student council votes next week on a "Meaning and Belonging" fee — a fifteen-dollar supplement to the activity fee — to be divided evenly among any student organization that registers as a "faith, conscience, or philosophy" group. I have just finished a unit in my comparative politics elective on how England, Germany, France, and the United States arrange religion and the state, and the council debate has been a replay of it: whether handing money out evenly counts as neutral; whether "we don't fund religion, we just don't tax it" is a real distinction; whether requiring a club to register as religious in order to qualify is itself a position on religion; whether an exit procedure that forces you to state your reasons is an exit at all.

What I want is a Swiftian modest proposal: a straight-faced op-ed, 700 to 800 words, headline plus byline identifying the author as the student council treasurer, addressed to the principal and the council, proposing that the school stop pretending to be neutral and establish an official school religion, the Church of the Second Bell. The target is the assumption, held by a lot of earnest people on that council, that a funding mechanism can be made neutral by making it even-handed.

Requirements:

- The proposal must be a workable policy rather than a rant, and it should quietly combine the four arrangements we studied. Automatic enrollment at admission, with withdrawal available only by filing, in person, a Declaration of Withdrawal at the registrar's office during its Tuesday window, paying a processing fee, and naming the creed being rejected — the German pattern, where the state collects the money and leaving requires a formal act before a state office. A voting seat on the council for the Chaplain to the Student Body, who opens each meeting with a scrupulously non-denominational invocation — the English pattern. The school owning the chapel and paying for the roof and the heating while paying nobody who speaks in it, on the ground that it recognizes, pays, or subsidizes no religion — the French pattern. And the established church's own slice being exempt from taxation, with donations to it deductible, so that the money the school declines to collect is money it spends anyway — the American pattern.
- The reductio should be structural, not stated. To get your fifteen dollars back you have to have a religion on file; students who left the registration blank default to the established church; students who were never members of anything are billed anyway; the chess club registers as a conscience community. Every implementation problem the treasurer notices — including the atheist who wants his money back but has no creed to name, and the boy who wants to redirect his fee to the debate team — should be solved in a way that tightens the trap rather than loosens it.
- Include one paragraph in which four exchange students, from England, Germany, France, and the United States, each find a different clause unremarkable and a different clause alarming, and then argue with each other about it. They should not be national caricatures; each reaction should follow from the actual institutions of that student's own country, and at least one of them should be defending a clause the reader has already started to recoil from.
- Keep the irony sustained and deadpan. The author must never name the principles he is dismantling — freedom of conscience, neutrality, entanglement — never break character, never wink, and close by reporting that the council adopted the plan unanimously as its most even-handed option yet. No punchline paragraph.
- It should be funny to a reader who knows the comparative church-state material and legible to one who does not: no invented jargon that needs explaining, no citations or reading lists, and no verbatim quotations from real constitutions, statutes, or court decisions — paraphrase anything legal.
