I sit on an incident-review board, and a surprising amount of our work turns on one distinction: whether some outcome was produced by a *we* — a crew, a team, a committee, a lab — or was merely the aggregate of several individuals each doing something, possibly with the same result. The distinction carries weight, because we only address a recommendation to a group when we think there was a group acting, and we only write "the team failed to" when we think there was something the team did or failed to do. I have fairly firm gut verdicts on the cases below. They do not obviously add up, and I want to know what principle, if any, they are tracking — and, where they conflict, which verdicts I should surrender.

1. Two strangers, one at each end of a fallen tree, no words exchanged, each seeing the other, each wanting the road clear, each easing his own lift to match the other's. They lifted it together. A finding addressed to the two of them is right.

2. Two people each pour concrete into the same mold on successive days, neither knowing the other exists, each intending to build a wall he thinks of as his own. The result is one wall. Not a joint action.

3. As in 1, except one man is shamming: he grips, leans, contributes no force, and intends the other to do the work while he takes the credit. Not a joint action. He is riding on someone else's action, and "they cleared the road" is only loose causal talk.

4. A getaway driver who genuinely does not want the robbery and drives only because he is being threatened. He is causally indispensable and legally implicated, but I do not count him inside the *we*.

5. A committee of eleven votes six to five to adopt a protocol; the six vote for mutually incompatible reasons; two of them later say they were wrong; the protocol is then implemented by staff who had no vote. The committee decided, and the committee acted. A recommendation addressed to the committee is right. Yet I cannot locate any shared intention that the members had — certainly not one they would all avow.

6. Two remote engineers who have never spoken, each keeping his half of a system compatible with a shared written specification, each knowing the other exists and that the other is doing the same thing. Joint action — but, it seems to me, not because of anything either believes about the other. The specification appears to be doing the work.

7. Exactly as in 6, except that each engineer falsely believes the other is a scheduled job that regenerates its half automatically. Nothing about either engineer's own intentions, dispositions or behavior has changed; only a false belief about the counterpart has been introduced. I now want to say they are not acting together, and that a finding addressed to them jointly would be odd. I cannot say why, and this pair is the one I am least able to reconcile.

8. A pilot and an autopilot: the pilot selects a mode, the machine holds an altitude. No *we*; the machine is equipment, like a crowbar. But where the automated component is a learned planner, trained alongside the pilot in simulation, which proposes routes, objects to some of the pilot's choices, and is routinely deferred to — where each side's performance depends on adjustments made to the other — I lean toward saying there is something joint here and not in the autopilot case, and I do not know what the difference consists in. This is the case I keep going back and forth on.

9. A ten-year project in which no person was a member for more than two years, cohorts overlapping, the result universally attributed to the lab. I say the lab did it, but I am shakiest here, and I notice I am more willing to say it when the lab kept a written protocol than when it did not.

The principle I keep falling back on is roughly this: A and B do something together just in case each intends that it come about, each intends that it come about *through the other's contribution*, each is disposed to adjust his own contribution to the other's, and these conditions hold in the open — not one-sidedly, and not on the basis of a false view of what the other is doing. That yields 1, 2, 3 and 4 cleanly. It gets 6 only if I allow "the other" to be satisfied by an artifact such as the specification, which feels like a stretch. It fails outright on 5, where there is no shared intention at all. And it returns the verdict on 7 that the engineers *are* acting together, which is the opposite of what I said.

What I want from you: a principle, or at most two rival candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new incident file, with a note of which case forces each clause; its verdicts on at least two cases I have not listed, one of which should be a case in which one participant is replaced mid-activity by someone the others do not notice; a resolution of the 6/7 pair, or an argument that my verdict on 7 is simply a mistake; a verdict on 8, with the reason it differs from the autopilot case if it does; and an explicit statement of which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and which single verdict I would have to overturn in order to have one unified account. If the best account of joint action comes apart from what our reports ought to say — if accountability should track something other than jointness — say so, and mark the points at which the principle stops and only judgment can settle.
