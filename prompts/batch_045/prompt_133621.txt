I chair the technical review committee for a private foundation's Discovery Engines program, and we have one proposal left to decide at Friday's meeting: a three-year, $2.4M request for what the applicants call Reference-Free Universal Prediction. Their method is a computable approximation to Solomonoff's universal prior — a dovetailing search over short programs under a hard compute budget — with a twist aimed at the standard objection that the prior is defined only up to a choice of reference machine. Rather than pick one, they run the same search over four concretely specified machines (a register machine, a lambda-calculus evaluator, a concatenative language, a combinatory-logic machine), compute the actual translation constants between them, and average the resulting predictions under a prior over machines.

Our two readers split cleanly. One holds that the framework is uncomputable at the core, that averaging over machines merely relocates the arbitrariness into the prior over machines, and that the convergence result — total expected error bounded by the complexity of the true data-generating distribution — is a limit theorem that licenses nothing about a fixed two-hour budget on finite data, where the additive constant from the invariance theorem can be larger than the signal. The other holds that this bound is the only non-circular justification of Occam's razor we have, that the constant is asymptotically irrelevant, and that what survives is an empirical question worth paying to test.

Draft the decision memo I will circulate Thursday. It should recommend one of three things — fund as requested, decline, or fund a twelve-month scoping award with conditions the applicants could actually satisfy — and give reasons they could respond to, rather than a verdict on Solomonoff induction as such. Two questions I want argued rather than asserted: whether the appeal to the universal prior is doing real work in their method or is decoration on a heuristic that stands or falls on benchmark performance, and whether "it's only a constant" answers the reference-machine worry or evades it. Give the strongest version of whichever position you reject. Close by stating what would change the committee's mind and where your own recommendation is most likely to be mistaken.
