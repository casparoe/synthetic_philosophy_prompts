Hi — I'm putting together a lecture on democratic backsliding for a general-audience series, and I keep tripping over the same move from a colleague in political theory (and from roughly half the op-ed pages): "we are late-republican Rome now." He deploys it to license three things at once, which is definately suspicious: (i) that what actually holds a democracy together is norms rather than constitutional rules, so fiddling with statutes, courts and electoral law is beside the point; (ii) that the driver is material — extreme inequality plus a landless urban population living off patronage — so the outcome is structurally overdetermined and not really anyone's fault; and (iii) that once you are on that slope the only open question is which strongman closes it out, so alarmism about any particular figure is misplaced.

I'd rather take the comparison seriously than wave it away, but I don't trust myself to keep the mapping honest. So: can you audit it?

1. A correspondence table first. What is supposed to play the Senate, the annual magistracies, the tribunate with its veto and sacrosanctity, the assemblies, the property-qualified citizen militia, the proconsular command with a private army, clientela and patronage, the grain dole, the Italian allies, the latifundia worked by slave labour, the proscriptions. Where there is no modern counterpart, say so explicitly and say what that does to the argument rather than quietly filling the cell in.

2. Seperate the mechanisms that are doing the real work on the Roman side from the costumes. As I read it the load-bearing part isn't the strongmen; it's that once recruitment from the landless replaced the old property-qualified militia, a general was his soldiers' only route to a land grant on discharge — the army became, in effect, a pension fund with swords, loyal to a man rather than to a constitution. Add to that the absence of any legitimate role for a "loyal opposition": office was a zero-sum race among elites inside a single hierarchy, and after the first proscriptions losing that race could cost you your property and your life. Tell me if I've got the emphasis wrong, or if I'm leaning too hard on the standard version of that story.

3. Where it breaks — and, more usefully, which breaks matter for which of the three conclusions. My own candidate for the biggest one is exit. A Roman senator who lost the race for office couldn't decamp to a law firm, a central bank, a university or a tech company and return richer; the only career was the state, so the stakes of losing were existential and the incentive to burn the system down was enormous. Modern elites have huge exit options, and that cuts both ways: it lowers the temptation to blow the thing up, but it also means the people with the most to lose are the least invested in the system's survival. Does that survive contact with the history, and does it make us more or less fragile than Rome? One more I can't settle: tempo. Rome's breakdown is usually told as two generations, from the Gracchi to Actium, whereas we measure backsliding in single election cycles. Is that mismatch so coarse that the comparison can't do any predictive work, or is tempo the one respect in which we should expect things to move faster?

4. Whether (i), (ii) or (iii) survives in any form, and if only a defensible fragment does, what that fragment is. And if there's a better analogy than Rome for whatever we're actually worried about, give me that instead — I'd rather have something I can defend than something that quotes well.

Write it in British English, please. Assume I can't check anything while I'm standing at the podium, so nothing that depends on looking a source up.
