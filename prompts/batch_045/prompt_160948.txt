My hometown is having the fight that every town seems to be having now: there's a statue of a nineteenth-century industrialist on the main square — big local philanthropist, schools and a hospital named after him, and a fortune made in ways that would get someone prosecuted today — and a committee has to decide whether it stays, gets a plaque, or goes. In the letters page the side that wants it kept keeps reaching for the same move: a statue is a record, taking it down is like burning a book or like Orwell's memory hole, and if you don't like the man you add context rather than delete him. The other side answers with an analogy of its own: a statue isn't a textbook, it's a eulogy — nobody erects one to record that a person existed, they erect it to praise them, so removal is closer to withdrawing an honour than to destroying a document. I've been asked to write a short piece for the local paper laying out where the argument actually stands, and I don't want it to be me picking a side and then decorating it.

So what I want is an audit of the book analogy rather than a verdict on the statue. Give me the mapping first, in plain sentences: who is the author, who the reader, what plays the library, where does the person who commissioned the thing go, what counts as a copy, what counts as burning. Then the places it fails, and — this is the part I care about — a separation between the failures that matter for the conclusion it's being used to carry and the ones that are just decoration. A book exists to be read while a statue exists to be looked at; there are many copies of a book but one statue, and the square is scarce honour-space rather than neutral storage; burning destroys every copy while moving a statue to a museum mostly relocates it and relabels it; and the agent in the picture is doing something different in each case, since book burning is normally an authority suppressing ideas its subjects want, whereas here it's the people who inherited the monument deciding whether to go on praising someone. Say which of those do real work and which don't, and what the analogy quietly imports without anyone noticing — that the statue's function is informational, that keeping it is silence rather than a continuing act by whoever maintains it today, that the square is an archive rather than a list of who this town admires. Then tell me whether the contextualize-don't-remove conclusion survives once those are on the table, or whether the plaque option was doing the work all along, and whether a plaque is even the same speech act as the statue it's attached to or a second, incompatible one bolted onto the first.

And one more thing nobody in the letters page has touched: the committee is also being asked about a street named after the same man, and I don't think the book picture fits that case at all, because you can walk past a statue but you can't avoid a street name — you say it out loud every time you give an address or fill in a form, and you can't hang a plaque on it. Does the analogy break somewhere different there, and is there a better comparison for names specifically, something that captures the being-made-to-say-it part? Also, if it matters for any of this, say who you think should get to decide, and why — but keep that brief and keep it in prose: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just continuous writing the way you'd write a long email to a friend who asked.
