I'm a journalist who covers science, technology and ideas, and for a piece I'm finishing on automated decision systems I spent a day in a decommissioned launch-control facility with a former missile crew commander, talking about how much of the retaliatory sequence ever really rested on a human decision. The visit left me with an argument I can't settle, and a friend who teaches an advanced seminar on war, risk and ethics has asked me to write one essay question for her final. I'd rather write the question I genuinely can't answer than one I already can, so I want to build it around the following move.

Suppose State A replaces its human-in-the-loop second-strike posture with a verified automated arrangement: a launch-on-warning system that, on a confirmed indication of incoming strategic attack, releases retaliation without requiring anyone to decide, and whose existence and logic State A's adversary, State B, has been credibly shown. The naive answer is that this simply dissolves the classic problem of nuclear deterrence — the threat no longer depends on anyone's willingness to do something it would be irrational to do once deterrence has failed, so the threat becomes credible and deterrence is restored. I want the exam question to push past that. It should force students to say what the credibility problem in the standard setup really consists in: a problem about whether retaliation could be rational at the moment of execution; about what the adversary can be brought to expect; about whether it can be permissible to threaten what it would be impermissible to do; or about whether a conditional intention to retaliate is coherent when the condition's arrival removes every reason to act on it. And then it should make them test the automated arrangement against each of those diagnoses separately, where the interesting possibility is that the machine solves one version of the problem and leaves the others untouched or makes them worse. The question should also make them deal with at least three things students will otherwise skip: that an all-or-nothing automatic response covers the worst case while leaving lesser provocations — a demonstrative single strike, a conventional fait accompli, salami tactics at the margin — with no proportionate answer, so that credibility at the top of the escalation ladder may be bought at the cost of it at the bottom; that the same arrangement converts the risk of accidental or misperceived triggering into something the adversary must price into its own crisis behaviour; and that a device that cannot be dissuaded may not be making a threat in the sense coercive bargaining requires at all, which raises the question of whether what is left is deterrence, mere risk, or something else. The question should not be answerable by naming the rationality of irrationality, the stability–instability pattern, or threats that leave something to chance; a student who can only supply those labels should be able to reach roughly ten of twenty-five marks and no further. Aim it at a three-hour paper, a single 1,200–1,500 word answer, 25 marks total, and write it so that no footnote or citation to any particular text is needed.

Please give me the question, a model answer, and the marking scheme as a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

- question_text — the question exactly as it should appear on the paper, including the scenario and any signposting, no more than 300 words.
- model_answer — a string of 1,100–1,300 words written as a real distinction-level answer in continuous prose, in the first person singular, with no citations and no headings. It should be strong rather than perfect: it may leave one implication drawn less fully than an ideal answer would.
- model_answer_moves — an array of 4–6 short strings naming the moves the model answer makes, in order.
- marking_scheme — an array of objects, one per assessable point, each with the fields point_id, what_a_top_answer_does, marks_available, partial_credit_rule, common_failure_mode, and marks_withheld_if_absent. Marks must sum to 25, and at least eight marks should be unavailable to an answer that only restates the standard paradox.
- label_recital_traps — an array of objects with the fields label, what_the_student_says, max_marks_reachable, and why_it_is_capped, covering the three or four moves a well-read student can produce from memory without engaging the crux.
- grade_boundaries — an object with the keys distinction_min, merit_min and pass_min, each an integer out of 25, plus boundary_rationale, a short string explaining what separates a distinction answer from a merit one.
- examiner_notes — a string covering the time and word budget, the two misreadings of the question you expect, and anything you think is wrong with the question: in particular, name any route by which a student could reach top marks without engaging the crux, and say how you would close it.
