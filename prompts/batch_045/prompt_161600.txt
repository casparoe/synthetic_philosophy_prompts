I've been arguing with a friend about the reference class problem for a while now, and I'd rather locate the disagreement precisely than keep trading intuitions. So I want a number from you, and I want to see the machinery that produced it.

First, a concrete case so the question has teeth. A forecasting platform resolves this binary question in 30 months: will a particular seed-stage company either raise a Series B or be acquired? The company is 14 months old, eleven people, B2B software, founded by two engineers who previously worked at large technology companies, currently at about $1.2M ARR, with roughly 60% of revenue coming from a single customer. Stipulate, purely for the sake of argument, that the platform's historical database returns these base rates over the same horizon: all seed-stage software companies, 12%; those that are B2B, 15%; those with two or more ex-large-tech-company technical founders, 22%; those at $1M+ ARR by month 14, 34%; those with more than half of revenue concentrated in one customer, 7%. The intersection of all five features contains four historical cases, one of which succeeded. Add one more feature — the founders' specific previous employer — and there are no historical cases at all.

The proposition I want a credence on is this: for single-case questions of this kind, the choice of reference class is in the end a tractable engineering problem. That is, there exists a defensible canonical procedure — a principled stopping rule for narrowing, a non-question-begging filter on which classes are admissible at all, and a rule for what to do when admissible classes disagree — such that two competent forecasters following it, with the same database and the same scoring rule, would land within a few percentage points of each other on a question like the one above. Call this P. I'm around 0.3. My friend is around 0.75.

Give me your own number, decomposed. I want at least these terms, with a number and one line of justification each, then the combination and a statement of which term the final number is most sensitive to:

1. Whether class-relative frequencies genuinely constrain single-case credences at all, as opposed to single-case probabilities being free-standing degrees of belief answerable only to coherence with the evidence. If the constraint isn't real, the problem dissolves rather than gets solved, and P should be understood accordingly.
2. Conditional on the constraint being real: whether the narrowing regress terminates. Here is my argument that it does not, and I want you to price it. Any rule of the form use the narrowest class for which you have adequate statistics needs a threshold for adequate, and that threshold is itself a bet about how much the narrowing feature matters — which is the very judgment the procedure was supposed to generate for you. Push narrowing far enough and you reach classes with one member, where the base rate is trivially zero or one. So the stopping rule is either arbitrary or circular. How much should that argument move this term down?
3. Whether the set of admissible classes is determinate. Without a way to exclude classes carved out by reference to the outcome itself, or otherwise gerrymandered, any base rate whatsoever can be manufactured — and I don't see how to draw that line without already knowing which features are relevant.
4. Magnitude. Even granting irreducible underdetermination, maybe the residual spread across reasonable classes is small compared with other sources of forecast error, in which case the problem is real but not load-bearing. Give me your read on the case above: what range of probabilities would you consider defensibly admissible there?
5. The recursion. Calibration data are themselves gathered against a reference class of past forecasts — forecasts like this one — so the evidence that would otherwise adjudicate between procedures inherits the same underdetermination. How much does that recursion cost us?

Two more things. First, my friend proposes a meta-rule: don't choose a class, treat all admissible classes as models and take a precision-weighted average of their base rates, weighting by sample size. I can't tell whether this escapes the problem or relocates it one level up, since the set of admissible classes is still what's doing the work; and I'm uneasy about averaging over descriptions of one event rather than over hypotheses about the world. Where do you come down, and does the answer differ if we're minimizing expected Brier rather than maximizing accuracy of a point estimate?

Second, tell me what would actually move your number — ideally something a forecasting platform could run: a preregistration requirement, a tournament condition, an analysis of historical forecast data. And tell me where you'd guess the gap between me at 0.3 and my friend at 0.75 really lives, which of the terms above it attaches to, and which of us is more likely to be wrong about the crux.

Please don't tell me that it depends on one's philosophy of probability. I want the number, with the philosophical disagreement priced as one of the terms.
