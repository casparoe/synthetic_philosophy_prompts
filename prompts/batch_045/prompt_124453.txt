I sit on the Public Realm Design Commission of my city, a seven-member advisory body whose recommendation on capital projects in downtown public spaces goes to the City Council with a staff report attached. The Council votes in six weeks on the reconstruction of Civic Plaza, the square between the central library, the transit concourse, and a 60-bed drop-in shelter. Since the overnight warming program was cut back two winters ago, the plaza has become the de facto night shelter for roughly 30 people, and the dispute that has split our commission is not really about benches. It is about whether the thing we build should settle, or merely manage, who the plaza is for. I need you to draft the decision memo I will circulate to the commission and then, as revised, to Council.

Three options are live.

Option A is the passive or defensive package: fourteen segmented benches with center armrests, flat ledges regraded to a slope, planters narrowing the west arcade alcoves, a publicly staffed restroom locked from 8 p.m. to 8 a.m., overnight shutoff of the drinking fountain. Capital cost $1.6M, forty-year design life, negligible annual cost. The staff report describes this as defensible space and recommends it on cost and durability grounds.

Option B keeps flat benches and open alcoves, but outfits them with a sensor package (load cells plus low-resolution depth sensing; no images retained, per the city privacy ordinance) that classifies reclining, dwelling past a threshold, and nighttime occupancy, and responds on a graduated scale: slats tilt, local lighting rises, a modulated high-frequency tone sounds within about eight metres. The whole system can be placed in open mode by staff during declared heat or cold emergencies, or by commission vote. Thresholds are set and logged on a public dashboard. A vendor offers a three-year pilot at no cost, retains rights to aggregate operational data, and after that would charge about $210k per year with a five-year hardware refresh. The classifier was trained on data from two transit centres in a very different climate; its reported 9 percent false-positive rate for reclining was measured only on adults without mobility aids or bulky clothing, and the data-rights clause would prevent the city from auditing that rate independently after the first year.

Option C is not a capital project at all and is therefore, in the staff report's words, outside the design remit: no physical changes, but extended shelter hours (forty overnight mats), two outreach workers, and enforcement of the existing overnight park-closure ordinance at the plaza only when a bed is actually available. Roughly $480k per year, recurring. The business improvement district says this invites encampments; the outreach providers say it is the only option that addresses a cause rather than a symptom.

I want the memo to treat the choice as a case of the questiondo artifacts have politics, and I want it to be genuinely reasoned rather than a literature survey. Please work with the following distinctions, and feel free to deploy others if they earn their place.

First, distinguish two ways an artifact can be politically loaded: arrangements that settle a matter of common life by technical means whether or not anyone intended them to, and technologies that are inherently political in the stricter sense that they require, or are strongly compatible with, particular organizations of power and authority. The second category has two sub-cases, depending on whether the relevant social condition is internal to the working of the technical system or external to it. Apply these to A and B: does either option require a continuing apparatus of monitoring, credentialed technicians, or a vendor relationship, or merely sit well alongside one? Is that apparatus internal or external to what gets built?

Second, stipulate away intentions. Assume for the memo that the designers of Option A intend nothing but durability, cleanability and safety, and that any exclusionary effect is a by-product, and assume the Option B vendor sincerely believes in dignity-preserving dynamic management. No argument in the memo may rest on anyone's bad faith.

Third, take seriously the social constructivist objection that the artifact itself carries no politics and that the politics lives entirely in surrounding practices and interpretation. If that is right, what follows for the choice between A and B?

Fourth, engage three considerations that have come up in our own deliberations and that I have not seen settled anywhere. (i) Reversibility and visibility come apart. Option A's norm is durable and legible to anyone who tries to lie down; Option B's norm is cheap to revise, but revisions are nearly invisible and are made by whoever has standing to move a slider. It is therefore not obvious which option is the more accountable, and I want the memo to say which asymmetry matters more for legitimacy here and why. (ii) A norm encoded in a thing is governed by whoever can repair, retune or decline to maintain it: in Option A that is Public Works and anyone with a grinder, in Option B it is a vendor release cycle and two staff with dashboard credentials. Which arrangement is more contestable by an ordinary resident? (iii) Option B produces a continuous quantitative record of dwell time, and institutional ends tend to drift toward whatever the instrument records; Option A produces silence, no data, no complaint, no trigger for review. Does the drift make B worse than A even if B is more humane moment to moment, or does A's silence make it the worse of the two?

Finally, address the objection that the commission should refuse to decide at all: that the plaza's nighttime use is a housing failure, and that a design commission asked to choose between armrests and sensors is being invited to launder a political decision as a procurement decision. Relatedly, some members argue Option B escapes the determinism worry altogether because it can be reprogrammed, while others argue reprogrammability only relocates the politics. Both points should be met head-on.

The deliverable: a headed memo addressed to the City Council, roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words, that recommends one option or one explicit, specified combination, and that a commission could adopt without rewriting. It must include the operative decision rule for switching Option B into open mode if that option is recommended or retained; a rule about who has standing to change thresholds, with a notice and quorum requirement; a sunset and review date with pre-registered criteria for what would count as the policy failing; a twelve-month implementation timeline; one honest paragraph on the strongest argument for the option or options you reject; and a short closing paragraph on the limits of the commission's own remit, saying what we should tell Council about what this decision cannot fix. Argue from the concepts rather than from authorities: you may mention Winner's essay in passing, but no footnotes, no citations, no reading list, and no appeals to sources a reader without a library could not check. Do not end on a non-committal note and do not close with a sentence of the form the commission may wish to weigh these considerations.
