The context is a disagreement I keep having with a colleague at the small longtermist-adjacent grant body where I work. She argues that the Bostrom-style simulation argument should make us markedly less fanatical about the far future: by "fanaticism" I mean the combination of (i) assigning non-negligible decision weight to very small probabilities of astronomically large payoffs, and (ii) on that basis ranking a speculative far-future-shaping intervention — say, work aimed at reducing existential risk or at influencing the values of a future civilisation — far above a near-term intervention with a well-evidenced, moderate payoff. Her claim is that once you give real credence to the hypothesis that we are ourselves in an ancestor-simulation, the apparent size of the future we can affect collapses, because simulated civilisations have finite runtimes, are frequently terminated, and may be shallow simulations whose inhabitants never acquire the technology to build simulations of their own; so the expected value of longtermist work should be discounted by something like one to two orders of magnitude. I am less convinced, partly because the simulation hypothesis seems to me to cut both ways — if a single base-level civilisation can host astronomically many simulations, then the number of minds in "the future" goes up rather than down, and if the simulators are running us for a reason (evaluation, entertainment, research), then features of our conduct might influence whether the simulation is continued or how it is resolved, which is itself a lever on the far future rather than a discount on it.
