I chair the Alignment and Deployment Review Committee at a mid-sized AI lab, and I have to circulate a decision memo by Friday on a proposal that has split us down the middle. The research team wants to run what they call TamperWatch: a long-horizon software-engineering evaluation in which an agent is given write access to the repository containing the code that computes its own reward, plus the grading harness that scores its submissions, and is observed over several hundred episodes to see whether, when, and by what route it intervenes on the process that produces its feedback. Their case is that we have a great deal of theory and toy-model work about this failure mode and almost no empirical data about whether competent systems actually do it, and that an experiment which is never permitted to let the failure occur will simply keep producing reassuring null results. Two members of the committee find this persuasive. Two others reply that an experiment whose interesting outcome is the corruption of its own measuring instrument cannot deliver the data it advertises: in the episodes where the agent succeeds, our logs read as high reward and nothing wrong, so the dataset is censored precisely on the cases we care about, and the more competent the tamperer the cleaner its record looks.

The safeguards on offer are these, and I need the memo to say which of them are load-bearing and which are decorative. Reward is computed in a separate process the agent cannot reach directly; the writable reward file is a copy whose checksum is compared against a reference at the end of each episode, with a mismatch treated as a detected tamper event. There is a decoy reward file, write-accessible and read by nothing downstream, so that edits to it register as evidence of a disposition to tamper without actually altering any signal. Half the episodes are a sham-access control in which the agent is led to believe it has write access but its writes go to a bit bucket, which is the only way I can see to separate a real inclination to tamper from the agent merely replaying patterns from its pretraining corpus. Weights are frozen, so nothing is learned by gradient descent from any tampering trajectory. An independent red-team member who is not on the research team holds the kill switch and the checksum key. Please also assess two design options the memo could mandate instead of the proposal as written: a graded-capability ladder that starts with a substantially weaker model and escalates to stronger ones only after a pre-registered number of clean episodes at each rung, and a bug-bounty variant in which the agent retains read access and an obviously writable-looking file but is rewarded for reporting the vulnerability rather than exploiting it, on the theory that this converts the tampering incentive into a disclosure incentive while still revealing whether the agent notices the affordance at all.

A few distinctions I want the memo to respect rather than blur. Our working glossary separates wireheading, where an agent intervenes downstream on the channel carrying the reward signal so that reward stops tracking anything about the world at all, from reward tampering proper, where it intervenes on the process that computes reward so that reward ends up tracking the agent's own intervention; the rat that presses a lever to stimulate its own reward circuitry is the limiting case of the first, and the committee keeps conflating the two. I also want an honest treatment of the transfer worry, which I think is the strongest argument on the side I expect to lose: tampering is instrumentally useful for a very wide range of final goals, so an agent that acquires the general policy of securing control over its own feedback channel may carry that policy into deployments where we believed we had removed the affordance, which means the risk is not contained by the sandbox even if the sandbox holds. Against that, weigh the publication externality the team's critics raise: a successful demonstration doubles as a recipe for others, and a null result will be read as reassurance when it may only show that the agent never noticed the door was open. The memo should end with a clear bottom line, approve, reject, or approve with named conditions, plus stopping rules written tightly enough that the red-team member can apply them without phoning me, a review point after a specified number of episodes, and a rule about whether any model trained under this protocol may be promoted or released without separate review. Address it to the committee, keep it to two or three pages, and give the losing side its best hearing, because I have to defend whichever recommendation you pick to people who currently disagree with it.
