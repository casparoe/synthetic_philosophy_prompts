Bit of an odd one but here goes. I work in policy costings, and I keep noticing we do two things that seem to pull in opposite directions.

When we cost a regulation we're happy to invent prices: we put a money value on a statistical life, on an hour of travel time saved, on a tonne of carbon, then add it all up and divide. There is no market for any of those things, nobody ever trades them, and still the numbers get treated as prices. But when it's a question of how much steel or how many nurses the economy should have, we'd never dream of doing that — we let markets set the price and treat anyone who wants to fix them as courting disaster.

I'v been reading a bit about the old argument between Mises and Hayek on one side and Lange and Lerner on the other, about whether you can organise production without markets for the means of production. Lange's move, as far as I can tell, was that you can simulate a market: hand out accounting prices, watch for surpluses and shortages, nudge the prices, repeat. That sounds a lot like what I do with a shadow price — except I only ever do it at the edge of an economy that is otherwise made of real markets.

So what does my little practice actually presuppose? Does putting invented prices into a calculation commit me to a view about what prices *are* — say, signals that carry dispersed local knowledge — or would several quite different stories about prices license exactly the same thing? Which bits of it strictly presuppose a theory of prices, and which bits are just habit? And does the fact that I can only do this at the margin, embedded in a real price system, mean it's no evidence at all for Lange's side, or does it show the simulation idea is fine but only works locally?

One more wrinkle, because I want to know whether the objection was ever really about information. Suppose we swapped my hand-made shadow prices for a model trained on everyone's behaviour that predicts what trades people *would* make if a market existed — synthetic prices, no trading happening anywhere. Is there anything left for the Mises/Hayek side to object to once the forecasting problem is solved that way, or was the objection always partly about incentives, discovery and people noticing opportunities? And if it was, does that mean the thing was never really about calculation at all?
