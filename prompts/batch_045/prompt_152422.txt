I'm defending a thesis in a philosophy of science reading group next week, and I want a Q&A prep sheet — not a defense of the view, but a map of what this particular room will throw at me.

The thesis: occasionalism is best reconstructed, secularly, as a claim about the direction of explanation rather than about whether causes exist. Call the view coordination occurrentism. The fundamental facts are momentary occurrents; nothing local produces anything else locally; what makes causal claims true is a global constraint — something structurally like al-Ghazālī's habit — linking the total state of one time to the total state of the next, where that constraint is not itself reducible to the occurrents but is also not a productive power. I argue (i) that this handles better than Humean regularity views the question of why there is order at all, which the Humean must treat as brute; (ii) that it dissolves the exclusion and overdetermination worries, since if nothing local does any producing, the mental need not compete with the physical for causal work; and (iii) that its empirical equivalence to standard views is a feature, not a defect: it shows the metaphysics of causation is underdetermined by physics, so the choice between accounts must be made on explanatory rather than evidential grounds.

The room is not medievalists. It is: a Lewis-style Humean about laws; an older-school regularity theorist; a dispositionalist in the Molnar-and-Mumford vein who thinks only powers distinguish genuine causation from coincidence; an interventionist who treats causal claims as claims about what happens under manipulations; a philosopher of physics who holds that causation is a macro-level, non-fundamental notion and that fundamental physics contains no productive causation at all; and a historian of medieval philosophy who will say I have detached occasionalism from the thing that made it occasionalism.

Give me the objections in roughly the order they will arrive, phrased the way each of those people would actually phrase them, ordered by how much damage they would do rather than by how easily I can answer them. For each: a two-minute reply, what I should concede up front, what I must hold, and where my reply is softest. Flag the one objection I should preempt during the talk instead of waiting for it. And give me the question I am hoping nobody asks.
