I write about science and ideas for a newspaper — mostly the history of technology, and the parts of evolutionary biology that end up in the culture pages. For the better part of ten years I have been circling a single question, and I would like a diagnosis of why it never settles rather than a verdict on it: how much of the way things now are is locked in by small, early, contingent events, and how much would have arrived anyway?

It turns up in two places I report on. In technology and institutions it is the familiar lock-in story: a standard, a format, a procurement rule, a funding decision gets adopted early for reasons that were local and reversible, and thereafter shapes decades. In evolutionary biology the same argument has been running for over thirty years: rewind the history of life to some early point, let it run again, and would anything recognisable — intelligence, sight, anything like us — come back? One side says essentially no, that our branch is one draw from a vast space and the tape would not replay; the other says yes, that the tape converges, because the same solutions turn up again and again.

What interests me is that neither side ever moves, and both cite the same material. Camera-type eyes in vertebrates and in cephalopods; echolocation in bats and in toothed whales; flight and warm-bloodedness and grazing machinery arriving several times independently; against that, the apparent single origin of complex cells, the very long stretch of history that stayed microbial, and the fact that in the best-known long-running evolution experiment — a dozen identical bacterial populations founded from one ancestor and left to evolve in parallel — a striking new way of exploiting an untapped food source turned up in just one population, after thirty-odd thousand generations. Each side has a prepared answer for everything the other cites: convergence is convergence only at a coarse functional grain; a single instance does not establish rarity; the repeated solutions keep reusing the same small set of genes and developmental switches, so convergence is not evidence against contingency at all. And each side can protect itself indefinitely — one by saying wait longer, the other by saying you have coarsened the description.

My own hypothesis, such as it is, is that the question as normally posed is under-specified, and that the apparent depth of the disagreement comes from that. "Would X have happened anyway?" seems to me to need at least three free parameters fixed before it has an answer: how deep a perturbation to initial conditions you are allowed (reroll one mutation, reroll one organism, reroll the whole planet); the grain at which the outcome is described (this lineage, this body plan, this function); and the time budget you are allowed to wait. My suspicion is that the convergent side quietly rerolls deeply, waits indefinitely and describes outcomes coarsely, that the contingent side nudges lightly, holds elapsed time fixed and describes finely, and that once the three are pinned the two claims stop contradicting each other — contingent in detail, convergent in outline. There is also a worry I cannot place: we are sampling a single run from inside it, and we could only ever find ourselves on a branch that produced observers, which may bias our one data point in a way neither side accounts for.

I do not want a survey of the literature or a reading list. I want to know what kind of disagreement this actually is: verbal; a hidden empirical question about how rugged the space of possibilities is; a dispute about which cases count as evidence, as between the fossil record, replicate evolution experiments and simulation; two different questions — prediction from initial conditions versus retrospective explanation — sharing one name; or a temperamental difference that argument cannot reach. If my three-parameter diagnosis is right, say so and sharpen it; if it is wrong, say which of those distinctions is doing the real work instead. I would also like to know whether the dispute gets its heat from being load-bearing for something else — for me, whether early choices are worth fighting over, since if outcomes are strongly path-dependent then the boring first decision matters enormously, and if they are not then most of what I write about standards and institutions is noise.

Then, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, a way of pinning the parameters, an experiment, a formal result — or should we expect none, and why?

Two things about how to answer. Please mark your confidence at each step: say which parts of the diagnosis you would defend and which are speculation, and do not smooth over the gaps. And if the request as I have framed it rests on a muddle — if the question has no determinate answer, or my parameters are the wrong ones, or the whole thing is less deep than it looks — say that plainly rather than answering politely.
