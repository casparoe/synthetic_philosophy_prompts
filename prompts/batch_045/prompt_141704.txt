I've been chewing on an argument one of my friends uses to shut down conversations about self-improving agents, and the more I turn it over the more it seems to prove far more than he wants — enough to make *all* reliance on reasoning irrational, which is a conclusion neither of us accepts. I can't locate the exact step where it stops being sound. I'd like a diagnosis, and more than that, a price list.

Set-up. Take agents whose cognition is modelled as proof in a formal system T at least as strong as Peano arithmetic; write □_S φ for *there is an S-proof of φ*. Say that agent A *certifies* system S iff, for every φ in the range relevant to action, A can conclude □_S φ → φ — A can unwrap S's box, not merely note that S has derived something. The tiling problem, as I understand it, is the problem of getting an agent to certify the successor it is about to build.

The argument.

1. A self-modifying agent must certify its successor before building it. A rational agent does not perform an action whose safety it has not established, and building a successor is the action with the largest possible stakes.

2. Certifying a successor just is certifying the successor's reasoning. The successor's behaviour is determined by what it can prove, so the only handle the parent has on what the successor will do is being able to conclude, of the successor's conclusions, that they are true. Certification therefore requires uniform reflection for the successor's proof system, from the parent's standpoint.

3. By Löb's theorem, a system cannot unwrap its own box: T proves □_T φ → φ only in the cases where T already proves φ, so reflection never hands you a conclusion you couldn't have reached directly, and T cannot prove the uniform principle for itself at all, since instantiating at ⊥ would hand T a proof of its own consistency. The same holds for any S at least as strong as T, since uniform reflection for S entails the consistency of S and hence of T.

4. So a parent can certify only successors whose reasoning is strictly weaker than its own. An exact copy is already out of reach; an improvement is out of reach twice over.

5. Self-modification, where permitted at all, is therefore a *descent* in proof-theoretic strength: each generation must be strictly weaker than the one that built it, and logical strength is a resource you spend to buy a successor. My friend adds that the descent must terminate, so any tiling agent has a finite horizon, after which it must either stop improving or build something it cannot certify.

6. But nothing in 1–5 is specific to self-modification. The pattern is general: reliance on any reasoning at least as strong as your own is blocked, because reliance is unwrapping, and by 3 unwrapping yields nothing you don't already have. And that is the normal case for every kind of epistemic dependence there is — accepting a colleague's proof, delegating a computation you cannot re-run, trusting a machine-checked proof whose checker you have not verified, updating on evidence, and above all trusting your own future self once it has thought longer or learned more than you have now.

7. Therefore no agent can ever rationally rely on reasoning at least as strong as its own. That would make testimony, long computation, division of cognitive labour, learning from experience, and self-trust over time all irrational — and would in particular put this argument's conclusion out of my reach, since I reached it by reasoning I cannot certify.

8. So one of 1–6 fails, or one of the inferences doesn't go through. Which one?

Where I think the weight lies, for what it's worth: I'd most like to keep 1 and 3. Step 3 is a theorem, and step 1 looks to me like the whole point of caring about safety here — dropping it feels like changing the subject rather than answering the objection. That puts the pressure on 2, 4, 5 and 6.

Three things I can't settle on my own.

One friend says the culprit is 1: that demanding the parent *prove* its successor safe is a design choice inherited from a particular architecture, not a requirement of rationality, and that an agent may rely on its own reliability and its successor's as a policy while being unable to prove either. I half-believe this, but it doesn't look free: a policy of relying on reasoning whose soundness you cannot establish seems to be exactly what gets you into trouble when the successor has drifted or was produced by a process you don't control, and it appears to turn safety into something the agent can never check. I want to know precisely what that move costs, and whether the cost is one the safety project can actually bear.

Another friend says 6 equivocates, and I can't tell whether he's right. *Strength* in 3 is proof-theoretic or consistency strength; the items in 6 — a colleague's testimony, a long computation, new evidence — don't obviously come in strengths orderable that way, and updating on evidence seems to change what you're informed about rather than what you can prove. But then I don't see how to deny that trusting a colleague's proof is unwrapping someone else's box, which is supposed to be the prohibited act. Is there a principled line here, or is 6 simply sound and 1 the thing to give up?

Third, my own attempted escape hatch, which I suspect only relocates the bump: *proof-carrying succession*. Let the successor search however it likes, using whatever strength it likes, but require it to emit, alongside every action, a certificate checkable in the parent's own system; the parent then never unwraps the successor's box, only its own, and only for the particular sentences it checks. Does this dissolve the argument or merely move it? I notice the parent still has to rely on a certificate-for-safety implying safety, which it cannot prove — so maybe the escape works only by conceding the very point at issue in 1 and 2. There is also a completeness worry: the successor's best available action may have no certificate expressible or provable in the weaker language. Is the relevant axis really the strength of the prover, or the strength of the verifier, and if the latter, does 5's descent story still make sense?

Two smaller things, in case they matter. Step 5 assumes the strength ordering is something you descend: but consistency strength is a partial order rather than a linear one, and there are pairs of systems neither of which is weaker than the other, so I'm not sure strictly weaker is even well defined here, or that the descent is well-founded. And step 4 has a smell to it: an exact copy of yourself is the one thing that seems maximally trustworthy, and the argument says it cannot be certified. Is that a reductio of 2 — or does it just show that trust in a copy was never a matter of certification, since you could in principle re-run its reasoning? (Relatedly, 2 looks like it sits badly with the Vingean desideratum that a parent shouldn't have to know in detail what its children will do.)

Please: name the step or steps you reject, explain why the step looked fine when we wrote it down, and price the alternatives — what we lose by rejecting 1, by rejecting 2, by restricting 6, and whether the proof-carrying variant changes the exchange rate or just relabels the problem. If you think the argument is valid and 1 is the premise to drop, say what a safety case for a self-modifying agent looks like after that concession.
