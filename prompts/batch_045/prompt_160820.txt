I'm a novelist rather than a philosopher, so sorry if I mangle the vocabulary. I got onto Kavka's toxin puzzle — billionaire, vial of stuff that makes you ill for a day, a million paid at midnight if at midnight you intend to drink it tomorrow afternoon, and you know perfectly well you'll have no reason left to actually drink it by then. My protagonist is a stage actor whose entire job is manufacturing mental states on cue, so I built my own version: the director-figure pays her to intend it *in character*, and she's trained to get there by acting as if until the state arrives. Which made me wonder whether the puzzle is even a conceptual one for her.

Anyway, I went looking and found the literature hasn't settled it in forty years. One lot says intention requires the belief that you'll do the thing, so the case is impossible; another says intention is a commitment or plan that carries no such belief with it; a third says the whole setup misfires because nobody forms intentions at will in the first place. Nobody seems to move.

So what kind of stalemate is this actually? A verbal fight about the word 'intention' — folk notion versus a theoretical role in planning agency? A dispute about what counts as data, armchair intuition versus what intention does in deliberation? A hidden empirical question about voluntary control over intention formation, which is what my actor variant is probing? Or is the real work being done by decision theory, i.e. Newcomb's problem in a lab coat? My hunch is the last two, and that the supposedly conceptual claims are smuggling in psychology. Tell me if that's wrong, engaging with what each side really argues, and say what would count as progress — an experiment, a formal result, a distinction, or abandoning the question. In British English please, and keep it out of seminar-speak where you can; some of this is definately going into a character's mouth.
