I spent my career in X-ray crystallography and, since retiring, I have been sitting in on a graduate reading group in the philosophy of history, mostly in the role of the person who asks the question everyone else thinks is naive. That is probably how I ended up spending several months reading the historians' side of the disputes over Holocaust denial, Armenian genocide denial, and the denial of the Nanjing Massacre. I came away less interested in the substantive claims than in one recurring argumentative shape, which I cannot find a name for and would like help carving.

The shape is this. Someone advances a narrow, checkable claim — that a particular figure for how many people could have been in a particular place at a particular time is arithmetically impossible, that a document is a forgery, that a capacity estimate has been misread — and a specialist refutes it publicly and at length, with archives and arithmetic. What follows is not retraction, and it is not retreat either. The refutation itself is recruited as the new datum: if the point were as frivolous as everyone insists, why did three senior people spend two years on it; why does this one small question have an editorial policy attached to it; what does it tell you about a discipline that has to be defended like this. The original claim is quietly dropped — you do not hear it again — but the story about the disciplining of the question survives and attaches itself to the next narrow claim, different in content and identical in function. I have watched the same shape run in the other direction: someone raises an awkward but genuine archival point and, instead of an answer, gets the observation that the interest itself is what needs explaining. And the reason I do not think the right response is simply to deny the premise is that the meta-claim is often partly true: there are real professional costs to certain topics, there are informal taboos, and received accounts have occasionally been revised in ways that vindicated the person who was shouted down. So the honest reply has to take the form yes, but.

I should add that I first noticed this somewhere with no politics in it at all — a long, unpleasant fight in my old department over a retracted paper, where the same conversion happened — and it is the recurrence across such different settings that makes me suspect the thing has a literature and a name. When I described it to the reading group they offered three labels, all of which I think are wrong, though my notes from that evening are garbled and I may have the names slightly off. One was something like the Galileo fallacy, but as I understand that move the bare fact of rejection is what does the work and no actual refutation is required, whereas here the refutation is essential: without a competent public answer there is nothing to convert. One was something like the bullshit asymmetry principle, which seems to me to be a point about relative costs rather than about those costs being made to testify. And one was sealioning, which I take to describe the demand for evidence rather than what happens once evidence has been supplied. Someone also mentioned motte-and-bailey, but that looks like the wrong direction of travel: the speaker is not withdrawing to a defensible core, he is ascending to a different level of claim altogether.

What I actually want is this. Does the pattern have an established name and a literature? If not, give it a name and a definition with boundary cases, so that I can tell which episodes are instances and which merely resemble one. Is the version used by the denier and the version used against the awkward but sincere questioner one phenomenon or two — and if one, is its force symmetric, or does it depend on which side can credibly present itself as the excluded one? Can the good-faith and the bad-faith instance be separated by any structural marker at all — something about what happens after the second or third refutation, say — or only by settling the merits of the underlying claim, in which case I would like that said plainly? And is this best understood as a feature of arguments, of audiences, or of the incentive structure of a field, since that seems to decide whether there is any rational stopping rule for engaging with it. I should say that a bare label will not help me: what I am after is the shape of the thing, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
