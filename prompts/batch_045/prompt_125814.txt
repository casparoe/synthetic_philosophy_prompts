Two mates of mine got properly stuck into each other over Sunday lunch about whether swapping beef for chicken actually helps animals. Both of them agree that animal suffering matters and both think factory farming is grim — they were arguing about the direction of the effect, not about whether to care. Roughly:

(1) Lorna: Switching from beef to chicken is a clear win. Cattle are on the planet for about two years and need something like six to eight kilos of feed per kilo of weight gain, where a broiler needs around two and is slaughtered at six weeks. More feed means more land ploughed, sprayed and harvested, and it's the crop deaths — the mice and voles and insects — where the numbers really pile up. Pasture isn't empty either: it's habitat that's been converted. So chicken means fewer crops and less land per kilo of meat.

(2) Ash: Careful which metric you're using. Per kilo of meat you're swapping roughly two days of a cow's life, most of it on grass, for something like twenty-five days of a chicken's life in a shed, lame, with ammonia burns. And if you'd rather count deaths than days, chicken loses there too: it takes about 0.6 of a bird to make a kilo of chicken and roughly a three-hundredth of a steer to make a kilo of beef. So on both of the metrics people actually use, beef comes out better per kilo, and your whole case has to come out of the crop-death column — which then has to be big enough to overturn a factor of ten.

(3) Lorna: The land column isn't a tiebreaker, it's most of the story, and you're only counting the harvesting. Beef needs maybe twenty times more land per kilo than chicken, and land is where the wild animals are. If — as a lot of people who worry about wild animal suffering do — you think a typical wild mammal's life contains more pain than pleasure, what with starvation and parasites and being eaten alive, then a system that needs twenty times more land isn't just killing more mice at harvest, it's propping up a much bigger population of lives that aren't worth living. Beef is a wild-animal-suffering machine too and nobody ever counts that bit.

(4) Ash: That's a big 'if', and look what it commits you to. If extra habitat is bad because wild lives are net negative, then draining a marsh or tarmacking a meadow is good, and the best farm is the one on the most thoroughly sterilised land. Maybe that's right, but I'd want it argued for, not smuggled in as the move that wins you the argument. You've also quietly switched comparisons: you're now scoring beef against chicken-plus-rewilding-the-freed-pasture, when what's actually on offer is just someone buying chicken instead of beef.

(5) Lorna: Fair on that last bit. But we should compare like with like in another way — nobody eats by the kilo of carcass, they eat by the gram of protein, and chicken breast is denser in protein than beef, so the suffering gap per unit of actual food narrows. And here's the bit nobody counts: you have to amortise the parents. A broiler's mum and dad are kept about a year in confinement and deliberately underfed so they stay fertile, and each pair underwrites a hundred-odd chicks, so their bad year costs you maybe two bad bird-days per bird. But a beef cow rears under one calf a year, and a lot of beef is ex-dairy — culled cows, male dairy calves — so the parent cost per kilo of beef isn't two days, it's a whole extra animal that ought really to be split between the milk and the meat. Do the ledgers properly and chicken's overhead is trivial while beef's isn't.

(6) Ash: Even granting that, it only works if the switch is one-for-one, and it probably isnt. Chicken is cheaper than beef, so a household that switches often eats more meat rather than the same amount, and the extra meat is chicken, which on your own numbers is the suffering-dense bit of the basket — you'd need the elasticities to know which way it goes. And that's the wider problem: every one of these numbers is uncertain by a factor of several and the metrics point in different directions, so the honest verdict is that we dont know whether the swap helps. When that's the state of the evidence, the thing to recommend is what's robust across it — eating less of both — rather than a swap that lets people feel they've done their bit.

They asked me to referee and I genuinely can't tell who's winning, or even whether they're arguing about the same thing by the end. Could you go through the six moves in turn, rate each one from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and say briefly why? Then tell me who's more right overall, and what — if anything — both of them are leaving out of the frame.
