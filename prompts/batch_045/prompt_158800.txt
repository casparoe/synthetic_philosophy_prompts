I've been asked to give the toast at the retirement dinner of Marta Reyes, who ran the finish-grinding line at our plant for thirty-one years and who taught me the job when I came in at nineteen. The complication I should flag at the start: I'm the engineer who specified and installed the automated cell that took over her line last spring, and everyone in the room knows it — her family and grandkids, the sixty-odd people from the floor, and the management team who paid for it, and who would very much like the evening to yield one quotable line about how the new cell "augments rather than replaces" people like Marta. Nine of the people from the floor have redundancy dates in April, and everyone knows that too. Marta herself has told me several times that she doesn't want a fuss, that she doesn't miss the work, that it was a job, and that she has no patience for people who go misty-eyed about skilled manual labour: the dust, the noise and the wrecked shoulders were not ennobling, she says, and she is right.

What I keep going round in circles about with a friend of mine is what exactly is lost in a case like hers, and we've settled on a three-way distinction that I'd like the speech to be built around. Call the first sense of "meaningful work" dependence: there are people who need this done and need you to do it. Call the second authorship: the work calls for judgement that you are answerable for, so that things going right or wrong are, in some real sense, yours. Call the third initiation: you were taken into a practice by people who were already in it, you got better inside a tradition, and you handed it on to someone else. My friend also presses a point I half believe and half resist: that automation very rarely beats a skill head-on — the new cell does not hold a tolerance better than Marta did — but instead redesigns the process so that the skill is no longer called for; the cell doesn't measure better than she did, it is built so that the dimension cannot drift in the first place. If that is right, then some of those three senses survive the automation in her case and some do not, and I would rather the speech be exact about which is which than generous about all three at once.

So: write me a five-minute toast, spoken rather than written, no jargon, roughly six or seven hundred words, that a room like that one can hear. It has to work for three audiences at once — the floor, who will catch any false note about what the job is really like; management, who must not be handed their "augmentation" line; and her grandchildren, who have no idea what a loaded grinding wheel sounds like and should be able to follow everything. There are things I don't want said, and I'd rather you respected the spirit of the list than its letter: not "they'll never replace someone like you," which everyone in the room knows to be false; not "your craft lives on inside the machine," which she would call mystical and which is anyway not true — the cell does not listen to the cut and dress the wheel, it simply never loads up; not any suggestion that the thirty-one years were really only about the paycheque; and not the pious inversion, either, in which the hardship is quietly converted into dignity. I want one concrete shop-floor image doing the work rather than abstractions — I was thinking of the way she could hear a wheel starting to load from the change in pitch and would dress it a good minute before any gauge showed anything, and I'm happy for you to use that or replace it with something better. What the speech needs to arrive at, I think, is a definite claim about what those thirty-one years were for, stated in a way she would not reject as either flattery or pity.

Don't hedge in what you give me: commit to one account of what her work was for, commit to the specific things the speech refuses to say, and give your reasons. Add two short paragraphs after the toast — one explaining where your choices depend on a contested assumption about work or automation, and one in which you tell me, if you think I'm wrong, which of my instructions you're overriding and why.
