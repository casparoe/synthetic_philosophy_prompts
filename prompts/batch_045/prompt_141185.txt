my brother sends one of those long end-of-year letters to about forty people every december -- markets, what he's reading, a paragraph about the kids, the usual -- and this year he's decided it should open with a poem instead of the market commentary. I made the mistake of saying "oh, I could do that," so now it's my problem. He's a finance guy, so anything about cycles he'll read as "the market breathes," and most of the people on his list are cheerful secular-progress types who'll read anything as "things slowly get better." I want something neither of them can just absorb and move on from.

What I have in mind is a sestina, and not as decoration -- the rotation is the argument. Six end-words, standard retrograde permutation (each stanza's end-words run 6-1-5-2-4-3 off the stanza before, so a seventh stanza, if there were one, would land back on the opening order exactly). The return is real and exact, but the words never come back in the same company, so they never quite mean what they meant. I want that to be true of the history too. Roughly where my head is, and I'd rather the poem argued with this than illustrated it: (1) the Polybian sequence -- one-man rule, its spoiled version, the best men, the rich, the people, the mob, and back -- turns because the qualities that found each arrangement are the ones that rot it, and the mixed constitution that's supposed to be the clever answer doesn't break the wheel, it just makes it turn slower. If the poem gets a reform or a golden-age moment, it should be a reprieve, not an escape. (2) In Khaldun's version the thing that lets a group take a state is the same thing comfort dissolves: a stock of solidarity that depletes, and gets replenished only from outside -- the desert, the frontier, whoever doesn't yet have anything to lose. So the cycle isn't closed; it needs a reservoir offstage, and I'd like one stanza where that dependency is visible. (3) In Spengler's version the phases line up across cultures but each culture only gets one turn -- parallel seasons, no recurrence -- plus that business about a young culture trapped inside the dead forms of a older one, speaking a borrowed vocabulary it can't fill. If there's a spring in the poem I'd like it to be a spring that might not turn out to be one. (4) And Toynbee: challenge and response, the creative minority that withdraws and returns, the petrified universal state at the end of the road -- and then, if I've got him right (correct me if I'm mangling it), a late move where the cycle itself gets treated as the vehicle for something that only travels one way, a wheel on a cart rather than a wheel going nowhere.

For the ending: the three-line tornada is where I want it decided. Things I can imagine working -- the six words finally appearing in straight 1-2-3-4-5-6 order for the first time since stanza one, i.e. a line rather than a turn; or one word refusing to come back and a stranger standing in its place; or all six gathered but in an order that reads like rungs. Whichever you pick, it should follow from one of the four accounts rather than from a general wish that things turn out fine, and it shouldn't be either a shrug or a moral. Also: no names, no ruined-statue-in-the-desert business, and no 'Ozymandias' ending. Someone who's read all four should be able to tell roughly which stanza owes what to whom, and someone who hasn't should just find a poem about a city and a family that keep happening. Pick six plain words that shift sense between a cyclical and a linear reading ("spring," "return," "seed," "line," "end," "ash" -- that kind of register, your call), and then give me two or three sentences afterwards on who the poem actually sides with, if anyone, and which of my four points you think I got wrong.
