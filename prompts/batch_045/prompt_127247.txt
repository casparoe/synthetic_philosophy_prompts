I have been trying to get clear about the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis, and I have two sources pulling in different directions. The first is an essay I found linked from a mailing list, written by a computer scientist for a general but technically literate audience: plainly competent, confident, and not obviously careless. The second is my memory of a graduate seminar in which the thesis was described as a peculiar kind of claim — not quite a theorem, not quite an empirical hypothesis, and, on the standard telling, not the sort of thing that can be proved at all. I noted the essay's account down, in substance, like this:

"The point that Church and Turing established in 1936 is that the three great formalisms of computability — Turing machines, general recursive functions and the lambda calculus — carve out exactly the same class of functions. That is the Church–Turing thesis: whatever one of them can do the others can do too, and nothing any machine can do goes beyond them. It is therefore not really a hypothesis at all but a mathematical result, and it is called a thesis only out of deference to the fact that its authors stated it before the equivalence had been fully demonstrated. This is also why Turing's undecidability result is a limit on computation as such rather than a fact about one formalism: no device, however ingeniously built, can decide whether an arbitrary program halts. The live question is whether quantum computing has refuted it. Shor's algorithm factors large integers in polynomial time, which no classical Turing machine can match, so either the thesis is false or it has to be weakened to 'simulable with at most polynomial overhead' — in which case it says nothing we did not already know and can do no philosophical work. The deeper reason to doubt that the thesis is an empirical claim is this: suppose someone built a hypercomputer that settled the halting problem. We could never confirm that it had, since checking a verdict of 'this program never halts' would mean waiting forever. A claim that no possible observation could refute is not a claim about the world; it is a rule about what we are willing to count as a computation. And rules of that sort are not refuted by machines — they are only ever revised by us."

I find that I want to agree with the essay about the mathematics and with the seminar about the status, and I suspect those two attitudes cannot both be right as stated. I also half-remember being told that for restricted settings — finite structures, or devices meeting certain constraints on how they operate — there are provable analogues of the thesis, though I cannot reconstruct the claim and may be garbling it; if something like that is right, it seems to cut against the essay's confident tone.
