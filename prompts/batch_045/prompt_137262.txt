I've been working through the family of puzzle cases that get called counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail, and I've reached the point where I trust my reactions to individual cases more than I trust my ability to say what they add up to. So I want to go the other way: here are my verdicts, and I'd like the principle, if there is one. In every case below, whatever agency does the predicting has an unblemished public track record, and I'll specify how it forms its predictions where I think that matters.

(1) The Predictor simulates me, in full, in exactly the situation I am about to be in — coin tails, asked for $100, nothing gained either way — and records whether the simulation pays. It then flips a fair coin. Heads: it deposits $10,000 in my account iff the record says "would pay." Tails: it asks me for the $100 and pays nothing. The coin lands tails; I am asked. I pay.

(2) Same, except that the simulation ran thirty years ago, the coin landed heads, the $10,000 was deposited and spent decades ago, the Predictor has long since ceased to exist, and an executor acting under its will now asks me for the $100. The payment is now a pure loss: there is no counterfactual version of me waiting to be paid, and nothing I do can influence a machine that no longer exists. I still pay. This is the verdict I am least able to defend, and I suspect it is where my thinking is most likely to be confused.

(3) The Predictor simulates nobody. It holds a psychometric model — a function from an enormous body of data about me to a prediction — trained on a large population and calibrated against a thousand of my own past decisions, on which it has been right 99% of the time. It forms its prediction, seals it in a file, and then flips: heads, $10,000 to me iff the file says "would pay"; tails, I am asked for the $100. Tails. I refuse.

(4) The Predictor cannot simulate me directly, so it distils a decision procedure from two hundred of my recorded choices — a few thousand lines of code that has so far reproduced my answer on every case it has been tested on — and, in the heads branch, runs that, paying $10,000 iff the surrogate says it would pay. Tails; I am asked. I pay. I am aware that the gap between "99% accurate" in (3) and "right on every case so far" in (4) is not obviously a gap in the relevant respect. What moves me is that in (4) the thing being run is a model of my decision procedure, whereas in (3) it is a model of me seen from the outside. I cannot say why that should matter, and I would not be surprised to learn that it does not.

(5) The Predictor does not model me at all. It finds another person and, in the heads branch, asks that person whether they would pay, depositing $10,000 with me iff they say yes; in the tails branch it asks me, and pays them $10,000 iff I say yes. (a) The other person is a stranger, chosen by matching a thousand personality measures, whose answer I have no particular reason to expect to match mine — I would call it even odds. I am asked. I pay, but I pay as one takes a bet: I would stop paying if I thought the chance of our answers matching were one in a thousand. That feels like a different reason from the one I have in (1), where I do not think of myself as betting on anything. (b) If the other person is instead a duplicate of me, made by scanning me last week and told nothing about any of this, I pay for the same reason I pay in (1).

(6) A Letter-Sender learns whether my house has dry rot: $10,000 to put right, already present or already absent, nothing I do now changes it, and I will discover it eventually either way. I put the chance at about 2%. The Sender sends me a demand for $1,000 iff exactly one of the following holds — that my house has dry rot, or that I would pay the $1,000 if asked. If the demand arrives and I do not pay, the Sender carries out a threat that costs me $5,000 and tells me nothing about the rot. The demand arrives. I refuse to pay. I know the argument that, once the letter is in my hand, paying means I am in the branch with no dry rot; I do not find it persuasive, but I cannot say what is wrong with it either. I would, however, have paid $50 beforehand for a device that guaranteed I would refuse, and I cannot articulate why that earlier payment and this later refusal differ in anything but timing.

(7) As in (1), except that the simulation is told, truthfully, that it is the simulation, that the coin came up heads, and that its answer settles whether the real I receives the $10,000. Since it is being asked a question I am not being asked, I have no verdict at all: I cannot see why its answer should track mine, and I cannot see why the case should differ from (1).

The principle I keep reaching for is a veil-of-ignorance one: I should do what I would have bound myself to, before I learned which branch I was in — what an earlier version of me, choosing one policy for the whole affair, would have chosen. That seems to get (1), (2), (5b) and (6) right. But it appears to tell me to pay in (3), which I resist, and to tell me to pay in (7) only by assuming that I and the better-informed simulation are covered by a single policy, which is the very thing I doubt.

So: is there a principle that delivers my verdicts in (1) through (6), or nearly; must it be more than the veil-of-ignorance one, and what clause does each case force; which of my verdicts should I abandon, if any; and what does the account you end up with say about (7)? Tell me also what it says about a case I have not given a verdict to: a version of (1) in which the heads branch pays out iff the simulation refuses. My instinct there is to refuse, and I do not see how that is compatible with paying in (1) unless my policy may be indexed to which mugging I am in — and if it may be, I do not see what stops that indexing from letting me wriggle out of every case at once.

Please answer in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you would write a letter. Take the cases in the order I have given them, saying for each whether my verdict stands and what it costs to preserve it, and close by stating the principle, or the two or three candidate principles, as precisely as you can.
