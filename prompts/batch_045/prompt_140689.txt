I'm on a hiring committee in a physics department right now, and I keep hearing the word "fundamental" used as if it settled an argument. It happened twice in one week. First we passed on a condensed-matter candidate — genuinely strong record, great letters — because a senior colleague said her work was "phenomenology, not fundamental physics." Then at the next meeting the same colleague argued for a string theory hire on the grounds that the Standard Model obviously isn't fundamental, since it's just an effective field theory, so there has to be something deeper underneath it. I did some philosophy as an undergrad and vaguely remember words like grounding and fundamentality, and now I can't work out what any of this presupposes.

So: what view of fundamentality does this kind of practice actually presuppose — physics departments and funders sorting particle physics and cosmology into "fundamental" buckets while condensed matter, materials and biophysics get labelled "applied," and philosophers doing the same thing when they call their work "fundamental metaphysics" and assume mental and social facts are grounded in physical ones rather than the reverse?

As far as I can tell the word is doing at least four different jobs in these meetings, and they come apart: (1) smallest length scale; (2) maximal generality — the theory that quantifies over everything, with no domain restrictions; (3) brute, i.e. not itself explained by anything else, the thing everything else is "nothing over and above"; (4) the level from which the rest could in principle be derived. My problem is that these don't obviously cohere, and I can't tell whether the practice needs any of them or whether it's compatible with several, or with none.

One more wrinkle. A philosopher friend mumbled something at dinner that I half understood: that grounding might not be well-founded at all — maybe the dependence relations descend forever, or levels form a partial order with lots of incomparable peaks rather than a single tidy tower, so "the fundamental layer" fails to refer. If that's right, what happens to a hiring and funding practice that silently assumes one well-ordered tower with a bottom?

Reconstruct the implicit theory for me, and separate the parts the practice strictly presupposes from the parts it merely sits comfortably with. Tell me whether a purely pragmatic story — prestige, expected value of information, path dependence, what's cheap to test right now — could license exactly the same behavior, in which case all the metaphysics is decoration. And be concrete about what would change if we swapped in each reading: my guess is that under the brute/unexplained reading you'd fund whatever is currently unexplained, which would move money to the measurement problem and away from new particles, and that under the derivability reading you'd have to cash out "in principle" and deal with the fact that most macroscopic phenomena seem decoupled from the microscopic details — but I want that spelled out properly, including where my own stab at a criterion (F is more fundamental than G if you have to hold F fixed to explain G but not vice versa) is circular or useless.

Also please say something about the bit that puzzles me most: quantum foundations gets funded under the fundamental heading, despite positing no new scale and no new entities, so fundamentality there seems to mean something like maximal generality — a totally different thing again — and meanwhile nobody in these meetings means "most certain" by "fundamental," since it's usually the most speculative work that gets the label.

Don't give me a reading list; my internet is basically dead this week. I want the argument, and I want to know which commitments are genuinely load-bearing and which are just vibes with a philosophy department stamp on them.
