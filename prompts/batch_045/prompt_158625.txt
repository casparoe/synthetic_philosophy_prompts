Two friends of mine got into it at a barbeque last weekend and I keep turning it over in my head. We'd been talking about pain asymbolia, the condition where people with certain kinds of brain damage can still tell you a pin is sharp or a burn is hot, but don't seem to be bothered by it at all — some of them apparently smile while it's happening. Dana said this settles something: the hurt of pain and the badness of pain can come apart, so badness is an extra ingredient added on top of the feeling itself. Sam said no — if these people really don't mind, then whatever they still have isn't pain, it's just pain's information channel running on its own.

Dana's reply was that this is definitional: if you define pain as whatever is unpleasant, then of course you'll never find pain without badness, and you haven't answered anything. Sam then said we only have their word that they still feel the same thing, and that "it doesn't bother me" is exactly the sort of report you'd expect from someone whose grip on their own distress has been damaged — maybe they're indifferent the way someone with frontal-lobe damage gets indifferent about everything, not indifferent specifically about pain. Dana answered with a reverse case: imagine a drug that left the unpleasantness completely intact but stripped out all the information, so you feel awful constantly with nothing to locate or react to — that's clearly worse, she said, which shows the two really are separate dials. Sam said the reverse case proves his point, not hers: what makes that drug horrible is the feeling, and take away the feeling and there's nothing bad left to be had.

Then Dana brought up a hospital version: a drug for sedated ICU patients that blocks only the unpleasantness and leaves the sensation intact, so the medical team can still use the patient's reactions for monitoring. She said that's a pure win — all the clinical usefulness, none of the suffering. Sam said if the patient can't be bothered by it, then the patient also has no complaint, so it's not obvious whether the drug is a kindness or just a way of making an unconsenting body easier to manage — and that this is exactly why the dissociation matters morally, not just conceptually. Dana said that's a problem with consent, not with the drug.
