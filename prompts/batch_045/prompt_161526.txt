I convene a first-year seminar on free will and moral responsibility, and at the end of term we screen Spielberg's *Minority Report* alongside a session on Libet's experiments and the literature that grew out of them. What I want from you is a reading of the film through that literature: where the analogy to the neuroscience genuinely holds, where it trades on a confusion, and where the film turns out to be raising a question the experimenters never asked. I'd rather you didn't settle for the observation that the film illustrates determinism; my students will have got that far on their own, and I need something that tells them what the comparison actually shows.

The analogy I keep circling is this. PreCrime acts on a pre-agential signal — the PreCogs' vision — that arrives before the agent has done anything, and, on the film's own showing, before he has settled on doing it; the department then intervenes on the strength of it. That is structurally very close to the readiness potential: a slow neural build-up beginning a sizeable fraction of a second before a self-paced movement, with the reported awareness of intending to move coming appreciably later, which on the face of it suggests the movement was under way before the agent knew anything about it. So is Anderton's predicament simply Libet's subject's predicament with the stakes raised? I don't think it is, and I'd like you to say why in detail. Two disanalogies strike me, and I'd rather you pressed hard on both than nodded at them: first, the experimental task is an arbitrary, unmotivated movement made to order in a laboratory, whereas what PreCrime forecasts is a motivated, deliberatively loaded killing, and it isn't obvious that anything like a readiness potential behaves the same way for actions with reasons behind them; second, Libet's own way out, as I understand it, was to concede the initiation and keep a power of withholding — a veto rather than an originating will — which sits badly with the department's procedure, since a pre-crime arrest eliminates exactly the interval in which a veto could be exercised. If there is a third disanalogy I'm missing, I'd like to hear it.

Here is the part I have been turning over myself, which I'd like you to test rather than take on trust. It seems to me there are three quite different things a prediction such as the PreCogs' might be: an inference from statistical regularities; a read-out of something already settled downstream of the agent's deliberation; and a causal contributor to the very outcome it names, because the vision is shown to someone and thereby changes what they do. The film, as I read it, runs on the third — the plot turns on characters who are shown their future and then either avoid it or bring it about precisely because they were shown it — and if that is right, then the film is not illustrating the Libet result at all, but asking what a genuinely reliable advance prediction of one's own action would do to one's deliberating about it. I'd like your view on whether the free-will literature has anything substantive to say about that, or whether it falls into a gap between prediction and agency that nobody has troubled over. The minority report itself — a dissenting second vision, the predictors divided against one another rather than simply authoritative — may be the film's most interesting contribution here, and I'd like to know what you make of it. Two further requests. Please be even-handed in the direction of your charity: where the film is philosophically muddled, say so, and where a scene resists the reading, say that too, rather than forcing every plot point into the Libet frame; the hotel-room sequence where the predicted killing is meant to occur and the final confrontation at the reception are the scenes I most want discussed, but don't treat the whole film evenly on my account. And since my students will read your answer, please calibrate it: indicate, briefly and in your own terms, how confident you are in each of your principal claims, and name explicitly the single claim you are least sure of — flagging plainly if part of your reading depends on how the experimental literature in fact came out, rather than presenting recollection as settled fact.
