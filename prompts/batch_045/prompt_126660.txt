hey — im TAing the classical social theory course this term (my third time, which probably means I've started believing things I used to just summarize) and I've ended up defending something I didn't expect to defend. Here's my position: the protestant ethic thesis is basically right, but only if you read it as a claim about *re-description*, not as a claim about causation by belief. Roughly, what the calvinist doctrine of the calling did was make a certain kind of patient, account-keeping, profit-reinvesting conduct newly intelligible as a duty and newly compulsory as a test of one's own standing — and once conduct gets re-described that way, it can spread without any extra material push behind it. What I want from you is: what would count against this? Give me the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one say whether a sympathetic Weberian should *concede* it as counting against them rather than just say what the marxist or the neoclassical critic shouts from the floor.

Here's what I already have on my list, so you can sharpen it or tell me it's junk rather than repeat it: commercial behavior that looks pretty capitalist shows up in catholic cities and in Reformed communities outside Europe with no calvinist doctrine behind it; Weber himself only claimed an "elective affinity," not a one-way cause, which might be a hedge that eats the claim; and there's the standing worry that the value is being read off exactly the behavior it's supposed to explain, so the explanation is circular. My econ friend's version of this is the one that actually needles me: confession is just a proxy for literacy, legal regime, or guild structure, and once you control for those the difference in conduct is absorbed. So tell me what a matched comparison would have to show to kill that — presence of the doctrine without the conduct, conduct without the doctrine, or something else.

The case I keep getting stuck on is one I made up, and I'd find it the most persuasive if it turns out to be a genuine defeater. Suppose someone digitized a run of 17th-century merchant ledgers from a Reformed town and a matched catholic one — same market access, same guild rules, same tax treatment — and found two things. First, the Reformed merchants really do keep more meticulous books and reinvest more, so the association holds. Second, the most demonstrably pious among them, the ones whose diaries are thickest with calling-language, are also the ones who most often break the guild's price rules and skirt the usury norms their own synod preaches. So the disposition the doctrine supposedly installs shows up as scrupulous rule-following in the ledger and rule-breaking in the marketplace. Is that a falsification, or is any thesis that can predict both outcomes empty? I've been trying out a test I invented to sort this out — call it the detachability test: a value-explanation earns its keep only if you can state the value independently of the conduct it's invoked to explain, and then show the conduct tracks the value rather than the interest it happens to be bundled with. Is that a fair test, or does it rig the game against any explanation that works through meanings? And one last thing: if the honest answer is that my version is insulated — that it's been qualified to the point where no behavioral evidence could refute it — I want to know whether that's a defect, or whether a claim about what made action *make sense* couldn't be exposed to evidence any other way, and whether that's what Weber's switchmen image was quietly conceding all along.
