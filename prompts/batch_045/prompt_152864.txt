So I've been going back to Kierkegaard on the press and the public — the leveling chapters in the book on the present age, the crowd-is-untruth line, the bit where the public is a monstrous nothing that nobody belongs to and everybody serves — and I keep circling back to the Justine Sacco thing from 2013, because it really does look like his nightmare scenario in its purest form. Recap as I remember it, in case your details are fuzzy: she was a communications executive at a big internet company, with only a few hundred followers. Before boarding a long-haul flight (London to South Africa, somewhere around eleven hours) she tweeted a joke in the worst possible taste — roughly, going to Africa and hoping she didn't get AIDS, just kidding, she's white. She turned her phone off. By the time she landed it was a worldwide trending topic, complete with a hashtag counting down to her arrival, and her employer fired her within about a day. I definately don't need the exakt wording, and I don't want a survey of what's been written about online shaming — I've read the standard takes and they all sound alike.

What I want is a real two-way case analysis, and I want it disciplined about the mapping rather than impressionistic.

Theory to case, first. Who, in this episode, is the press, who is the public, who is the crowd, and who (if anyone) is a single individual? His press is an institution with named editors and a political line; the amplifier here is a platform with no editorial will at all, owned by a company whose business model is engagement. Does the substitution hold, or does swapping the polemical editor for a profit-maximising recommendation engine quietly change what the theory says? Same question for leveling. His leveling runs downward: envy drags the eminent down to the same level, and the abstraction of the public makes eminence impossible in the first place. But the target here had no eminence to lose — she was a private person — and the people piling on were, if anything, elevating themselves by a public display of moral correctness. I've started calling that leveling upward, and I want to know whether his machinery can generate it at all or whether it's just outside what he had in mind.

Case to theory, second — where the thing breaks or goes quiet:

1. He says the crowd is untruth. But the content of the crowd's belief here was, as far as I can tell, true: the joke was what they said it was. So what exactly is he claiming, and what does it predict about a mob in which every participant is a named individual who made a traceable, deliberate choice to type something? Is the untruth epistemic, existential, or neither, and does it survive a crowd whose beliefs are correct and whose acts are individually authored?
2. He says the public chatters but does not act. This one got somebody fired inside a day. What actually converted chatter into consequence was an employer — an institution — not the public. Does that refute him, or does it show that his account is missing a mechanism, and what would the patched-up version have to add?
3. The retweet. I want a serious treatment of this, because it seems like the crux. On one hand it looks like the purest possible instance of his category of chatter: an utterance with content but no author, circulating at the speed of the medium. On the other hand a retweet isn't nothing — it's a stance, it's cheap commitment, and people get fired for those too. Which reading is more Kierkegaardian, and does the second one break the category?
4. And the normative question, where I think he's thinnest. His remedy is inwardness, silence, becoming a single individual, holding your relation to the thing yourself instead of renting it from the public. Applied to the people who joined in, that collapses into: don't tweet. Applied to Sacco it seems to give her nothing at all — unless you route it through his category of suffering and martyrdom, which would ennoble her, which seems wrong given what she actually tweeted. Say plainly where the theory has no advice to offer.

Last thing, and don't dodge it: the theory was written by a man who had himself been the target of a sustained campaign of caricature in a Copenhagen satirical weekly, and who more or less invited it — he published something under a pseudonym practically begging the paper to abuse him, and then spent the rest of his life treating the result as a species of martyrdom. Does that origin make the theory better or worse as a tool for thinking about Sacco? I can see both stories: that being burned is precisely what let him see the structure clearly, and that he built a whole metaphysics of the public out of one bruised writer's grievance and then aimed it at everyone. Tell me which one this case supports, or why it doesn't have to pick.
