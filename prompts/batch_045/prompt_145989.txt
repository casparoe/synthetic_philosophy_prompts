You are a professional philosopher who specializes in animal ethics and the moral status of nonhuman animals, and I want a careful, structured answer rather than a survey. Work in stages: first sketch the conceptual apparatus you intend to use — the distinctions you take to be load-bearing, the version of the speciesism critique you think is strongest, and the rival explanations of the same data you'll need to rule out — then apply it to the episode below, then turn it around and say what the episode does to the apparatus. Be disciplined about the mapping: who bears claims here, what their interests or rights are supposed to be, what kind of reason the decision-makers were actually offering, and where your framework gives no verdict at all. Roughly 1,000–1,300 words, no citations or reading list needed; I know the literature, and I want the analysis.

The episode is the killing of Marius at Copenhagen Zoo in February 2014. The facts as I understand them: Marius was a healthy young male reticulated giraffe, born at the zoo in 2012 (press mostly said eighteen months old; the zoo's scientific director Bengt Holst said two). He was culled because his genes were already well represented in the European Endangered Species Programme population, so there was no breeding role for him and no space in the herd; an EAZA spokesman said he could not be considered inbred, so the worry was not his own health but the slots he would occupy and the genes he would spread. Under EAZA rules the zoo manages rather than owns its animals, cannot sell them, and can place them only under equivalent rules; offers from other zoos — Krakow, Yorkshire Wildlife Park, and two non-member parks — were declined, Yorkshire's reportedly because its space would be better used by a genetically more valuable giraffe, Marius's own brother being already there. Sterilization was rejected as harmful to his quality of life. He was shot with a rifle rather than the announced bolt gun, since lethal injection would have made the carcass unsafe for the zoo's carnivores; he was then dissected publicly as an educational event that families attended, and his body was fed to lions, tigers and polar bears, with some parts going to research. There was global outrage, petitions, and death threats against staff — and also support from Denmark's main animal-protection organisation, and the fact that culling healthy surplus animals is ordinary practice in European zoos. Assume all of that is right; I don't need it re-litigated.

What I want is the speciesism lens run through it, and then stress-tested. First, notice that the zoo's justification assigns Marius a value that is not a property of him at all but a function of his genetic profile relative to a managed population: call it the herd-slot conception of an animal's worth. Can any of the standard individualist animal-ethics positions — interest-based utilitarianism, a rights view built on being the subject of a life, a capabilities view — represent that reason as a moral reason, or does it belong to a population-level conservation ethic that the individualist views can only reject as such? If it is the latter, does the speciesism critique even engage the case, or does it simply talk past it? Second: Marius was, on the zoo's own reasoning, replaceable — by a genetically more valuable giraffe. Painless-killing-plus-replacement arguments would seem to license exactly this, while rights views forbid it. Say which of those results you think is correct here and whether the case genuinely discriminates between the two families of view or merely restates their antecedent disagreement. Third, treat the outrage itself as data: a giraffe precipitates international fury while the routine killing of pigs in the same country precipitates nothing, and the feeding of the body to the lions produced a further spike of horror even though the zoo's carnivores have to be fed somehow. Is identity-based prejudice — speciesism in the literal sense — the best explanation of that pattern, or do relational accounts (we owe more to animals we have taken into our care, or to those we have made dependent on us), or charisma and cuteness, or something like betrayal of trust by a custodian institution, explain more of the variance? If a relational explanation wins, say honestly whether that damages the speciesism critique or merely relocates it. Fourth, there is a wrinkle I keep coming back to: Marius existed only because of the breeding programme that killed him, and the individualist views that would forbid the killing are not obviously able to forbid the programme whose by-product he was. Work out what the consistent individualist is committed to here, and whether that commitment is credible or a reductio. Finally, and I care most about this part: does the case confirm the speciesism critique, is it an anomaly the critique never anticipated, or does it reveal that the critique is aimed at a different target entirely? And tell me plainly where the lens is silent — on whether zoos should exist, on who had standing to decide, on the public dissection as opposed to the killing, on whether the conservation goal was worth pursuing at all — instead of manufacturing verdicts. As a constraint on your conclusion: my roommate says anyone who objects to this must also object to culling deer and to eating meat, and my supervisor says the case is the cleanest available reductio of the individualist consensus in animal ethics; tell me how much of each of those reactions survives your analysis.
