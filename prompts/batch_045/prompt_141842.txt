I teach a 75-minute session of an undergraduate seminar on mind and ethics — 18 students, no background in the philosophy of mind assumed, a room with movable chairs and a projector I would rather not use. I want them to feel the attribution problem: every test we could run on a system shows us only its behaviour or its internal structure, never which computation, in the morally relevant sense, that behaviour or structure realizes; and rival attributions fit the very same observations.

Design a game that makes that concrete. Constraints and requirements:

- Nothing but printed materials: cards, envelopes, score sheets. No software, no live system, no real money — points only.
- Small groups must divide a fixed, scarce budget of moral-protection points among five or six candidate systems, described at first only by short transcripts of their behaviour that I will write. They should be betting under uncertainty and should feel the asymmetry between withholding protection from a system that matters and squandering it on one that does not.
- Two staged reveals, each followed by a chance to reallocate. First: one system turns out to be a replay — its outputs were recorded from a run last year and are now retrieved from a table, so almost nothing is being computed. Second: another system's states can be placed in one-to-one correspondence with the states of a system nearly everyone agreed matters, under a mapping described in a paragraph. The second reveal should move allocations without supplying any new behavioural evidence, so that students feel the disagreement was never about the evidence.
- Give me the scoring rule, and say what the transcripts must contain and must avoid so that no system reads as obviously the human one.
- A debrief mapping particular moments of play onto: the gap between behaviour and the computation taken to realize it; the fact that a system can be counted as realizing almost any computation under some mapping or other; and the error asymmetry in allocating concern. Say which of these the game genuinely makes vivid and which it only gestures at.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time — groups hunting for a hidden right answer, or collapsing the whole exercise into the problem of other minds and checking out — and how the rules and the reveal should be written to forestall each.
- State plainly what the activity cannot do. I assume it cannot make them feel that there is a fact of the matter about whether a system is conscious, only that their attributions are underdetermined; if you think it can do more than that, say how.
