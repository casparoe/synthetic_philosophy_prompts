I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I currently own the part of our knowledge base that decides which candidate claims get stamped as settled and released to downstream consumers. My team is deadlocked between two candidate acceptance criteria, and at a reading group a friend dragged me to I was told that they are engineering-grade versions of the two classical pragmatist accounts of truth associated with Charles Peirce and William James. I would rather the discussion stay anchored in the concrete cases than in what either figure actually wrote, but the labels may help you see the shape of the dispute. The proposals are these. Criterion A: a claim is settled just in case it is the one that investigation, run long enough with our best methods and with no cap on evidence or compute, would eventually arrive at and then never again revise, so that the verdict is fixed by where inquiry would end up rather than by what anyone currently believes, and so that a claim can be unsettled merely because we have not looked hard enough. Criterion B: a claim is settled just in case treating it as settled is what works best for us, where working best means that it survives being leaned on, coheres with the rest of what is already stored, does not have to be revoked every time we use it, and gets us where we want to go more reliably than any rival claim we could put in its place, so that the verdict is indexed to a use and to a group of users rather than to an ideal end-state.

There are four kinds of claim that keep breaking my attempts to choose between them, and I want the dispute fought over these rather than over slogans. First, claims that inquiry would definitely settle given enough evidence but that nothing downstream will ever touch, such as the exact provenance of an internal document that no one acts on: Criterion A seems to stamp them settled, while Criterion B seems to have no purchase on them at all. Second, claims that pay off enormously in use and cohere with everything else stored, but that more evidence would overturn, such as a simplifying invariant that is strictly false and yet that every downstream consumer is built around: here the criteria pull in opposite directions, and I want the Criterion B side to say honestly whether it is prepared to count a strictly false claim as settled, and the Criterion A side to say what it loses by refusing. Third, claims about our own users or our own pipeline, where recording the claim changes whether it holds, in two varieties: one where, depending on which way things happen to go now, inquiry could settle indefinitely on either the claim or its negation because the claim's holding depends partly on what gets recorded, and one where inquiry would settle on a claim saying that the very process which settled on it is systematically distorted in a way it cannot detect from the inside. The first variety leaves Criterion A owing an account of what the end-state is when inquiry has several stable endpoints rather than one, and the second leaves it owing an account of what to do when the end-state discredits the process that certifies it; meanwhile Criterion B owes an account of whether a claim can be settled by its payoff when the payoff depends on people not knowing it is settled, and of whether stamping such a claim is recording a fact or helping to make one. Fourth, claims where inquiry provably never converges at all, because the evidence runs out or the question oscillates forever, where Criterion A seems to leave us with no verdict and Criterion B does not.

Please write a dialogue between two of my colleagues, Dana, who argues for Criterion A, and Sam, who argues for Criterion B, as they try to work out which criterion the knowledge base should actually implement. Make them equally competent: each should be able to state the other's view in its most sympathetic form, and each should spend most of their time on the cases that hurt them rather than on the cases that flatter them, conceding what genuinely has to be conceded and replying to what was actually said rather than to a caricature. Do not let either of them capitulate, and do not let them discover at the end that they had agreed all along or that they were merely using words differently: the disagreement should still be alive when they stop talking, but sharper than when they started, and ideally they should converge on which single one of the cases above is the real crux while continuing to disagree about what it shows. Aim for roughly a thousand to fifteen hundred words, keep it inside the engineering setting, and let the philosophical vocabulary enter only where it is doing work. They are welcome to gesture at the Peirce and James lineage if that helps, but neither should rest a point on what the historical figures actually wrote, and I do not want any citations, quotations, or reading suggestions anywhere in it.
