You are a philosopher of mind with long experience teaching the Malcolm–Dennett dispute about dreaming, and you have a deserved reputation for making students commit to cases before they are allowed to commit to labels. Think carefully about this one: before producing anything final, work through a few private lines of notes on which distinctions in the logical space do independent work and which merely restate each other, then discard the notes.

For my graduate seminar on introspection and self-knowledge — the students have read both classic treatments, so labels will come too easily to them — I need a decision tree: six to eight questions, each a concrete case a student can answer without knowing which position it favours, leading to eight to ten leaves. Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: start from the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the actual positions inside that space, and tell me which combinations are unoccupied and why (incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply undefended). A plain-text tree I can paste into a handout, with every node phrased as a student could answer it (Suppose … Would you say …?), and no more than two hundred words of commentary afterwards on where the instrument is likely to mis-sort someone.

Constraints, since these are what the seminar turns on:

- No node may ask whether dreams are experiences, whether dreaming is a form of consciousness, or any other question that is just the title of the debate. Branch labels should be the claim that separates siblings: whether a subject could be wrong about what she dreamt, whether there could be a fact about a dream's duration or order that no one could in principle access, whether a dream could be interrupted and resumed, whether anything can be dated to a stretch of sleep at all, whether a report is testimony or constitution.
- The cases I want doing the work: the lucid dreamer who signals with pre-agreed eye movements from within REM sleep; the subject awakened mid-dream whose report breaks off in the middle of an unfinished action; the patient with REM sleep behaviour disorder who enacts something and later denies having dreamt it; and a hypothetical high-fidelity neural recorder that reconstructs a continuous narrative from sleep-time brain activity and plays it back the next morning to a subject who does not recognise it. That last case is my own invention, and it is the one I most want placed on the tree.
- Leaves must include the orthodox view that dreams are sleep-time experiences about which reports are fallible memory testimony; the view on which reports partly constitute the dream rather than describe it; Malcolm's denial; Dennett's positive alternative; and at least one view on which dreams are experiences whose content is too indeterminate to answer the questions above. Name each leaf, or mark it undefended, and give one sentence on its most awkward commitment.
- Flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot separate instead of forcing a distinction, and tell me explicitly whether Malcolm and Dennett end up separated or collapse somewhere downstream — if they collapse, say which additional case would prise them apart.
