I'm in the final year of a philosophy and economics programme, and this term I'm taking a seminar on rational choice under risk. The tutor has asked each of us to lead one fifty-minute problem session, and mine is on the Pasadena game: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads, and if that takes n tosses you receive (-1)^{n-1}·2^n/n dollars, so the payoffs run +2, -2, +8/3, -4, … and the expected value works out as the alternating harmonic series, which is only conditionally convergent — rearrange the terms and you can make the sum come out to anything you like, which is why people say the game has no expected value at all rather than a large or infinite one. The group is comfortable with expected utility theory and has already seen the St Petersburg game, and they know things like "the harmonic series diverges," but they have no measure theory and I don't want to invoke any. After class a friend of mine said the whole thing is just St Petersburg again with minus signs, and another said the answer is obviously ln 2 since that's what the series sums to in its natural order; I think both are missing the point, but I'm not confident I can show them why in a way that lands.

So please plan the session rather than explain the game to me: the order in which the ideas have to arrive and what depends on what, the one example (or pair of examples) that should carry the whole thing, the misconceptions the group will bring or form and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off, one question I can put to them at the end that would show they've understood the problem rather than just learned the vocabulary, and what I should explicitly decline to get into at this level. My own thought is that the session should turn on a comparison rather than on a single gamble: take the Pasadena game and a companion game whose payoff at every outcome is exactly one dollar more, which intuitively ought to be worth exactly one dollar more and yet is equally undefined in expectation, and then compare the two truncated versions — stop the coin at N tosses, note that the difference of the truncated expectations is exactly one for every N, and let N go to infinity — so that the lesson is that expectation is an instrument for ranking options and not the definition of what an option is worth, and that some comparative judgements survive when the absolute ones do not. Tell me whether that pair is the right thing to build the hour around, whether the group needs to feel the rearrangement result in their bones before the comparison will mean anything, where the "just use bounded utility" and "no one could ever actually offer you this gamble" moves should be placed so that they don't derail the session but also don't get dismissed by fiat, and whether a remark like "use the median instead of the mean" is worth engaging or is a distraction at this level.

One more thing about how I'd like the answer pitched. Please mark clearly which parts of what you're proposing are settled — the convergence mathematics and what follows from it for any rule that ranks gambles by expected value — and which are live disputes among people who actually work on these cases: whether dominance or some surrogate should do the work expectation can't do here, whether the example is a genuine problem for decision theory or an artefact of infinite idealisation, whether unbounded utility is the real culprit underneath. I would much rather say to the group "the literature is genuinely split here" than hand them one camp's repair as the received view. And if the truncation-comparison idea I've sketched is itself a contested move rather than the obvious fix I've been taking it for, say so plainly and tell me what the rivals to it are.
