Next week I'm giving a short work-in-progress talk in my department and I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view. My thesis: the usual standoff between causal and evidential decision theory over Newcomb's problem is mostly a red herring. What's actually load-bearing is a different assumption, which I call copy-robustness: roughly, that had you chosen differently, a functionally identical copy of you in the same situation would have chosen differently too. To isolate it I run a variant with no predictor in it at all: the opaque box was filled by a machine that built a short-lived simulation of you, let it choose, and paid out on the copy's choice; the copy is destroyed before you decide and has no causal influence on you. I claim the pull to take one box survives here, so what's doing the work isn't backward causation or the spooky reliability of a near-perfect predictor, it's whether the copy's choice is the kind of thing you count as up to you.

The room: about twelve people, mostly causal decision theory sympathizers (two of them fairly hardline), two formal epistemologists who think evidential decision theory got a bad rap, and one game theorist who will want to know why I don't just talk about commitment and equilibrium instead. Nobody there works on functionalist or simulation-based views of agents, so the copy setup will be unfamiliar to them.

Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way these people would actually put them -- the game theorist's version shouldn't sound like the CDT people's -- each with a one or two minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Also tell me what I should concede in the first two minutes, the single reply that has to land if the talk is worth anything, and the question I'm hoping nobody asks. American English please.
