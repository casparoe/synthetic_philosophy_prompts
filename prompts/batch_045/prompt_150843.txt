I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training. On a long flight I read Weber's essay on the Protestant ethic and the spirit of capitalism, came home, and got into an argument with a colleague who does causal inference for a living; we have now been circling the same points for months. Please draft the joint statement we should have produced instead.

The two positions to be represented:

- The values-first side: religious ideas — the calling, ascetic self-discipline, the prohibition on enjoying unearned wealth — were themselves a cause of the capitalist spirit, not just the vocabulary in which already-existing material interests were dressed up; Weber's own image is of ideas acting like switchmen, determining the tracks along which material interests push conduct.
- The materialist side: the measured confessional differences in income, savings and schooling are accounted for by literacy, human capital, legal and commercial institutions, and pre-existing regional development; the ethic was a rationalization that happened to spread with those things, not an extra causal factor, and treating culture as a cause is mainly a way of hiding an unidentified mechanism.

Write the document as (1) a numbered section of claims both of us can sign, and (2) a disagreements table. For the agreed claims, I want the non-vacuous ones: what a claim of the form values caused an economic outcome would have to mean to be testable at all; whether the thing to be explained is the origin of the pattern or its persistence; what a statistical association, a natural experiment, and a mechanism story can and cannot each rule out. In the table, state each remaining disagreement in wording the side holding it would accept as their own position, identify the cruxes, and for each crux give one concrete case or piece of evidence both of us would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

To keep it from floating, treat this hypothetical as given (I invented it, so don't check it against anything real): a study exploiting the rule that a ruler's choice of confession fixed his subjects' — villages effectively randomized into Catholic, Lutheran or Reformed observance — finds, 250 years later, that the Reformed villages are richer, but the gap (i) survives controlling for literacy, schooling and pre-1500 wealth, (ii) shrinks by about two thirds once the density of village credit and craft associations is controlled for, (iii) appears fully in the first two generations after conversion and is flat thereafter rather than compounding, and (iv) shows up in savings and bookkeeping practices but not in hours worked.

My own guesses at the cruxes, which you should use or replace as you see fit: whether elective affinity is a causal claim at all or a claim about which material conditions an idea can latch onto; and whether a pattern that appears fast and then stays flat, instead of compounding, is what a values-based explanation should have predicted. If you conclude that some part of our dispute is about words rather than about the world, say which part and why. Keep the whole thing under 900 words.
