I'm a fourth-year PhD student in an epistemology department that is mostly analytic in orientation, and over the last two years I've drifted into classical Indian epistemology. I can work through Sanskrit commentarial prose slowly with a dictionary, but I am not a Sanskritist, and I have roughly two years left plus room for about three papers and one job-market chapter. I want to spend that on work where Nyāya material actually does something for questions my department already argues about, rather than on work where it functions as an exotic illustration of a conclusion analytic epistemology can reach on its own. So what I need from you is an agenda, defended, not a survey, and not a reading list or a map of the secondary literature — I have those already. Let me fix the background I'll be assuming: Nyāya standardly recognizes four pramāṇas — perception, inference, comparison or upamāna (the textbook case: you are told that a gavaya is a wild animal somewhat like a cow, later you encounter one in the forest, and you come to know of it that it is what that sentence meant), and testimony or śabda — treats a pramāṇa as the karaṇa or instrument that brings about a veridical cognition, or pramā, holds that a cognition's validity is established extrinsically (parataḥ: roughly, by inference from the cognition's issuing in successful action) rather than intrinsically as the Mīmāṃsakas hold, and explains our awareness of our own cognitions by a second-order inner perception, anuvyavasāya, rather than by treating cognitions as self-luminous.

Here are the lines of work I find myself circling, in no order. (i) Artifactual testimony, or what I've been calling the instrument problem: śabda standardly requires a speaker who knows the relevant fact and intends to convey it, and the hearer's grasp of a sentence is said to require expectancy, semantic fitness, and contiguity; so what is happening when the proximate source of a true cognition is an artifact or a model rather than a person — a thermometer, a database, the output of a trained system? Is that testimony, perception with an unusual instrument, or is it a source type the framework has no slot for, and does the answer matter beyond bookkeeping? (ii) Whether the pramāṇas have fixed, separate domains or can converge on a single cognition, the old vyavasthā-versus-samplava dispute: if perception can be conceptually loaded, and testimony needs inference to establish the speaker's reliability, and inference needs perception to establish pervasion, then it may not have a determinate answer how many sources any given token cognition has, in which case a lot of the taxonomy debates elsewhere are pseudo-questions. (iii) The parataḥ-versus-svataḥ dispute mapped onto internalism and externalism about justification, or onto default entitlement and phenomenal conservatism. (iv) Anuvyavasāya as an externalist-friendly account of the subject's access to her own knowledge: assuming a second-order inner perception does the work, does it actually answer the complaint that externalism leaves knowledge invisible to the knower, and what does it cost if that second-order cognition needs certification of its own? (v) Vyāpti, or pervasion, and the grounding of inference: co-presence and co-absence, the use of tarka or hypothetical reasoning to remove doubt about deviation, and the question whether pervasion needs a genuinely necessary connection — the sort of tie by identity or causation that Buddhist accounts of the reason demand — or can rest on something weaker; and whether this is the problem of induction in a Sanskrit coat or a real alternative to it. (vi) The irreducibility of upamāna: I have a feeling that what would settle whether comparison is a fifth source rather than perception plus memory plus testimony is a case rather than an argument, but I cannot see what shape the case would have to have in order to be decisive. There may well be a seventh line I'm not seeing.

What I want is a defended ordering of these, or of a better set if my list is wrong, given the constraints: two years, dictionary Sanskrit, a committee of general epistemologists who will read one chapter and two or three area specialists who will read the rest, and a job market that punishes work that looks like it belongs to a subfield nobody in the hiring department recognizes. Please use explicit criteria and be willing to trade them off against each other — importance to live debates, tractability for someone with my preparation, how neglected a line is versus how likely it is to stay neglected, how much of the rest depends on it, and how much of the payoff survives if my Sanskrit never improves past where it is. Tell me which one or two I should drop outright as overrated or as already settled in substance under a different vocabulary. Tell me, if I had to bet the two years on one of them being answerable enough to matter, which one, and say what would count as evidence over the next eighteen months that I backed the wrong horse. And push back on my own hunches where they're wrong: I currently think line (ii) is upstream of everything else in a way that makes it attractive to start with, and I think line (iii) is the most legible to my committee and the least likely to change anyone's mind about anything.
