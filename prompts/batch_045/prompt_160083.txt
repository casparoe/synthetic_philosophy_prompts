I should say at the outset that I have never studied philosophy formally. I read on my own, and I belong to a small discussion group — six of us, mostly retired, no one with any academic background — that meets monthly to argue about something. At our last meeting two of the members got into a long argument about vagueness: the old puzzle in which you build a pile of sand up a grain at a time, or take it apart a grain at a time, and ask at which point it becomes or stops being a heap. It ended with both of them convinced the other had missed the point, and since I was the one who had proposed the topic I said I would try to work out who was right. I cannot, and I would be grateful if you would go through the exchange and adjudicate it.

I will call them Marta and Owen. Their exchange, as close as I can reconstruct it, went like this.

1. Marta opened with the puzzle itself. Each premise looks obviously true — a single grain is not a heap, and if a given collection of grains is not a heap then adding one more grain cannot turn it into one — but the conclusion, that no collection of grains is ever a heap, is obviously false. She took the lesson to concern the word rather than the world: "heap" has no sharp cutoff anywhere along the series, and in the middle range there is no fact of the matter whether a given pile is a heap. She was emphatic that this is not a claim about our ignorance. It is not that we cannot find the line; it is that there is nothing there to find.

2. Owen replied that the argument shows only that we cannot locate the cutoff, not that there is none. Plenty of perfectly definite facts are unknowable in practice: the exact number of grains in the pile, the precise instant at which a candle stops being lit, the exact length of the coastline. On his view vagueness is a name for ignorance. There is some number such that that many grains do not make a heap and one more does; we will never know which number; that is the whole of the paradox.

3. Marta said that ignorance has to be ignorance about something. In the candle case there is a further story to tell — combustion, a flame front, the chemical conditions — such that once every underlying physical fact is fixed, whether the candle is lit is settled, even though no one could ever measure it. With "heap," she said, once you have completely specified how many grains there are and exactly where each one sits, nothing is left over that could make the cutoff fall at one number rather than the next. A line with nothing to determine where it falls is not a hidden fact but a counterfeit one.

4. Owen answered that this demand — show me what makes it true — asks too much. What settles which cutoff is correct could be the entire fine-grained pattern of how the word has actually been used: every utterance, every judgment, every context, across the whole history of the language. That pattern is a definite object even though no one can survey it, and it could single out a particular boundary — perhaps the largest collection anyone has ever been inclined to call a heap, or the point that best fits the total pattern — while leaving every speaker permanently unable to tell where it is. He compared it to water turning out to be a particular chemical compound long before anyone was in a position to know that: what a word means can outrun anything speakers know about it.

5. Marta then raised a regress. If there really is a sharp line between heap and non-heap, there must also be a sharp line between the piles that are clearly heaps and the piles that are merely borderline. But "clearly a heap" is, she said, every bit as vague as "heap" itself. So there must be a sharp line between definitely borderline and not definitely borderline, and another one above that, and so on without end. A theory that needs an infinite stack of invisible, unknowable, ungrounded boundaries in order to preserve one is a theory in which the boundaries are the invention; and the stack does nothing to explain how any of us actually manages to get along with the word. Owen's response was that the stack costs him nothing extra, since each level could in principle be fixed by the same total-usage story, and that an infinity of truths is no more alarming than the infinity of arithmetical truths.

6. Owen then went on the attack. If borderline cases have no truth value, he said, then compound statements built out of them start to misbehave: "either it is a heap or it is not" stops being true, and conditionals about heaps become impossible to assess. Yet we reason with vague words competently all day — in regulations, in disputes between neighbours, in ordinary planning — and we reason as if the usual logical rules held. He added that when we are forced to decide, a judge or an umpire or a bet draws a line and then treats it as if it had been there all along, which suggests our practice commits us to there being something there to get right.

7. This is where I interrupted, because I had been thinking about something on my own and I genuinely do not know whether it helps either of them. Suppose we try to settle the question mechanically. We show a large number of people thousands of piles, record their judgments, train a model that assigns each pile a continuous heapishness score, and then the engineers set the score at which the model flips from no to yes. Two things struck me. First, that flip point is chosen by the engineers for a purpose; it seems strange to say that a decision made for convenience is the fact of the matter about the word. Second, and this is what I actually pressed: the model's comparisons do not line up. It can rate pile A as more heapish than pile B, B as more heapish than C, and C as more heapish than A. Human judgments do this too — with colours and sounds, and plausibly with piles. But if there were one scale with a threshold somewhere on it, "more heapish than" would have to be transitive, and it is not. Owen's answer was that intransitive judgments are simply noise: inaccurate readings of a definite quantity, like a bad pair of kitchen scales, and noise is exactly what you would expect if the truth were sharp but hard to read. My worry is that with weighing there is an independent quantity, mass, for the noise to be noise about, whereas here the judgments look like all there is, so calling them noise seems to assume the very thing in dispute.

8. Marta closed by saying that this is what you should expect if the word's job is not to cut nature at a joint. We need "heap" in order to warn, to plan, to sort, to say roughly how much trouble something is; a word whose whole function is to mark a rough, purpose-relative distinction does not need a boundary, and looking for one is a category mistake. Owen's reply was that a purpose-relative threshold is still a threshold: fix the purpose and the context, and there is a definite number of grains, even if no one knows it.

What I would like from you is a move-by-move adjudication rather than a general essay on vagueness. For each of the eight moves, please say whether it succeeds, fails, or succeeds only partially, and say why in a way that shows what the move was actually relying on. In particular I would like to know: whether Owen's distinction between "we cannot locate the line" and "there is no line" really does all the work he needs it to, or whether Marta's point about there being nothing left over to fix the line undercuts it; whether the regress in move 5 is a genuine difficulty for his view or merely a rhetorical one; whether the logical costs Owen raises in move 6 are as severe as he claims, and whether there is any way of accepting fuzziness while keeping the usual rules of reasoning — I have a vague sense that there is, but I cannot reconstruct it; and whether my cycling-judgments point in move 7 tells against him at all, or whether the comparison with noisy measurement defeats it.

Beyond the individual moves, please tell me what each of them is missing, where if anywhere they are talking past each other, and whether the disagreement is ultimately about words, about the world, or about something else they have not separated out. If the question between them has a determinate answer, say what it is and what would settle it. If it does not, say where exactly it runs out and why. I would also be grateful if you would flag any point at which one of them is using a central word in two different senses without noticing.

One practical request: please do not cite or name the literature. None of us could follow it and we have no access to it anyway. If you need a term of art, use it once and explain it in plain language. Plain prose throughout, I am afraid, is all we can make use of.
