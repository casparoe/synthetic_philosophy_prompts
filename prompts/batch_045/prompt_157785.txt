I'm a PhD student in history and philosophy of science, and the chapter I'm currently drafting asks whether the rapid consolidation of methodological opinion in a research field should be modelled as an information cascade. I'd like a number from you rather than a map of the literature — and please don't reach for citations even where you think they would help. I want your own credence and the reasoning that produces it, because the point of the exercise is to see which part of the argument is load-bearing.

Here is the set-up, stated so that we agree on terms. There are two possible states of the world, equiprobable ex ante. A sequence of agents, in an exogenously fixed order, each choose once between two options; each receives one private signal that is correct with probability q, strictly between one half and one, conditionally independent of everyone else's given the state; each observes the choices but not the signals of all predecessors; each acts once and irreversibly. I'll call a full cascade the state reached when, from some index onwards, every agent makes the same choice whatever her private signal says, so that no further private information enters the public record and the public belief freezes wherever it happened to get to.

My variant adds a single channel. Suppose each agent also, unavoidably, reveals how readily she decided — the latency between receiving her signal and announcing her choice — and that latency is stochastically decreasing in how strongly her signal points one way, with the mapping common knowledge and noisy. Call it a leaky cascade when choices are perfectly uniform but the latency stream keeps carrying information, so the public belief continues to move even though no choice ever does.

The proposition I want your credence on is this: in sequential-adoption settings of that kind, where observers perceive not merely what their predecessors chose but how readily they chose it, the standard cascade diagnosis substantially overstates the epistemic loss — specifically, the asymptotic probability that the sequence locks on to the wrong option is at least an order of magnitude below the figure the pure-action model yields. I would count the matter settled either way by a careful specification of the model with realistic noise in the side channel, so treat that as the resolution condition. My own credence is about 0.55; my supervisor's is about 0.2; and we cannot locate where the disagreement lives.

Please decompose rather than answer directly. I'd suggest these terms, though add or replace as you see fit: (i) how often real observers have usable access to such a side channel at all, as against settings — anonymised ballots, published summary tables, aggregate adoption statistics — where the choice really is all one sees; (ii) given access, how much of the channel is news relative to the public history, bearing in mind that if a cautious agent delays because her signal conflicts with what the record already says, latency partly re-encodes public information and its apparent content is largely an echo; (iii) whether observers actually condition on the channel, and separately whether an outside observer — a historian, a meta-analyst — reading the whole stream ex post would recover more than any participant ever had, since these are different subjects whose information might be tracked, and I want to know which one your number concerns; (iv) whether the pure-action result is itself a knife-edge artefact of restricting observation to actions, so that any side channel whatsoever prevents formation, as distinct from merely overturning a cascade once formed; and (v) before any of this machinery, how much of observed uniformity is cascade-driven at all, as against correlated signals from a common shock or straightforward sanction-based conformity.

Give me a rough probability and a line of justification for each, combine them, and then tell me three things: which single term the final number is most sensitive to; which of the two of us at 0.55 and 0.2 you think is mislocating something and about which sub-question; and how much of your number is really an empirical guess about noise levels and observer sophistication — for which neither of us has data — as opposed to a conceptual judgement about the fragility of the cascade model. I would rather have five honest terms than twelve padded ones, and I would especially like to know if I have dressed up an empirical hunch as a conceptual argument.
