I'm a postdoc in philosophy embedded in a computational neuroscience lab, and in three weeks I'm giving our departmental neurophilosophy seminar — forty minutes plus twenty for questions — on a draft I've been circulating under the title *Structural Pluralism: What Integrated Information Theory Measures, and Why the Max Rule Is the Weak Link*. The talk argues three things. First, that IIT should be split into a formal core and an identity claim: the core is a framework for characterizing the intrinsic, state-dependent cause-effect power of a physical system and unfolding it into distinctions and relations; the identity claim is what adds that a system's consciousness *is* that cause-effect structure, at the grain and over the subset where the integrated information is maximal. Second, that the core is a genuine advance but that the quantity it returns is really a family of quantities indexed by modeling choices — which partition rule you adopt, what spatial and temporal grain you evaluate at, how you handle indeterminism, how you individuate a mechanism in a state — and that the axioms constrain these choices without determining them. Third, that the exclusion/max rule is what collapses the family into a single prediction, and that it does so by discarding most of what the family encodes; so I propose to drop the rule and report the whole profile instead, treating the shape of that profile (where it peaks, how much of it sits at fine versus coarse grain, how it redistributes under perturbation) as the empirical content. The payoff I sketch is that a profile should discriminate wakefulness, REM dreaming, anesthesia, and epileptic loss of consciousness better than a single scalar can, that it gives a cleaner prediction about disconnection cases — the profile should fragment rather than merely halve — and that it lets us stop arguing about whether simple integrated circuits have a trace of consciousness, since the interesting question is whether this is the quantity we should be measuring at all. I also plan to set aside the argument that unfolds the postulates from the essential properties of experience, treating it as rhetoric that does no independent work.

The room matters here, because I want the objections I'll actually get, not the ones I'd get from IIT's online skeptics. Expected attendance: an IIT-sympathetic postdoc who has computed these quantities on small model systems and knows the current formalism cold; two people in the global workspace and predictive-processing camp who think the target phenomenon is access and report and who think calling feedforward processing unconscious begs the question; a senior philosopher of mind who takes the hard problem seriously and who regards the derivation of the postulates from the properties of experience as the strongest part of the theory rather than the weakest; a philosopher of science who works on measurement and will want to know whether I am defending a valid measure or just a quantity with many inequivalent implementations; two machine-learning people from mechanistic interpretability who will press on tractability, since these computations blow up with system size, and on whether anything I say implies a trained transformer could be a complex; and one neurointensivist — the only person present who has patients — who uses perturbational complexity-style indices at the bedside in disorders of consciousness and will want to know what a profile gives her that a single index does not. Several of them were also involved in reading the adversarial-collaboration results as leaving both camps bloodied, so nobody in the room is a neutral.
