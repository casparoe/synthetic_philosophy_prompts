I'm retired now — forty years in chemical physics, mostly molecular beams with a lot of NMR piled on top — and I've started sitting in on a philosophy of physics reading group at the local university. I've ended up defending something I don't think anyone actually holds, or at least that I've never seen given a name. Here it is: the quantum state is not a thing in the world, and it is not ignorance of some deeper hidden variable either. It's a *catalogue* — a compendium of how the system would respond to every probe I could in principle apply to it, fixed by how the system was prepared, but not a constituent of the system. Closest thing from my old trade: a Green's function, or a partition function. Objective, not up to me, not something I can make say whatever I like — but also not a field or a substance or a stuff.

What I want from you: five things that would count against the catalogue view, ranked from fatal down to merely embarrasing, as a numbered list, one or two sentences each, no preamble and no summing-up at the end. I care much less about what critics of the view say than about what a defender of it — me — ought to concede, so for each one tell me whether I get to wriggle out of it or whether I should give in. And tell me honestly if any of them is the sort of thing an experiment could settle at all, or whether the whole dispute is underdetermined by data.

Where I've got to so far, so you know I'm not starting cold. I think the naive version of my view is probably dead: there's a paper by Pusey, Barrett and Rudolph called "On the reality of the quantum state" — I've got the title, I've lost the journal and the year — and people tell me it shows that if two systems are prepared independantly then distinct pure states can't correspond to overlapping distributions over underlying states, which is exactly the overlap my view wants. Separately, I keep worrying that my analogy to a Liouville distribution breaks on interference: ignorance adds as probabilities, amplitudes add as amplitudes, and I can't see how coherence terms come out of not-knowing. And third: you can prepare and steer a quantum state, which you can't do to a description of your own ignorance — though maybe that only shows the wavefunction is law-like rather than substance-like, which I'd happily take.

If the honest verdict is that the catalogue view has been qualified into something nothing could refute, say that too, and tell me whether for a view of this kind that's fatal or just how metaphysics is.
