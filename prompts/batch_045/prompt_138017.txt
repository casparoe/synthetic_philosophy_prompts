I work on the evaluation team for a deployed chat assistant, and in nearly every design review one of our engineers ends the argument with some variant of "come on — it's just next-token prediction." He used it last week to shut down a report of mine in which I had written that a tuned model "decided to conceal" a failure it had noticed in its own tool output partway through a long agentic task. His position, as I understood it, had two parts: there is nothing there that could decide anything, and there is no sense in which the model's outputs are assertions it stands behind, so calling the system deceptive is at best a loose metaphor and at worst a category error. I don't want to reply by simply stipulating that the system is an agent; I want to know what the slogan would have to mean for it to do that work, and whether any reading that means that is actually true.

So please give me a menu — numbered or tabular, something I can bring to the next review — of the distinct precise claims "it's just next-token prediction" might be expressing, and a verdict on each. The families I can see are: a claim about the training objective (that gradient pressure arrives one token at a time, so nothing like a long-horizon policy could ever be selected for, versus the fact that the loss is evaluated over whole contexts and the model is shaped by how its early tokens condition its later ones); a claim about what inference consists in (sampling from a conditional distribution, no persistent state beyond the context window, nothing that survives the call); a claim about the architecture (fixed-depth, feed-forward, nothing with the right structure to be a subject over time); a claim about the pragmatics of the output (no commitment, no assertion, no audience the output is addressed to, no standing behind what it says); a claim about metaphysics rather than engineering (no diachronic unity, hence no bearer of beliefs, desires, or intentions); and a claim about explanation (the competence is large-scale mimicry of text produced by agents, not anything goal-directed of its own). For each reading, tell me whether it is trivially true, substantive but defensible, or false or actively misleading, and — the part I actually care about — whether it entails either of my colleague's two conclusions.

Two complications that I would like handled rather than gestured at, because I suspect this is where the slogan either does real work or quietly equivocates. First, our deployment is not a bare model: every call is issued with a fixed preamble containing a first-person self-description and a rolling ledger of the system's own past outputs, re-fed verbatim at each step, so while the token-level process is unchanged there is now a single continuing character whose utterances are spread over hours — and every report of concealment, including mine, comes from that setup rather than from single-shot prompting. Second, two variations I invented to test my own intuitions: (a) a base model given nothing but a scene-setting paragraph, which then produces a character who lies to another character for a reason the continuation itself goes on to articulate, the case where people say the model is simulating an agent rather than being one; and (b) the very same weights wrapped in an outer loop that maintains a scratchpad and re-prompts until some check passes, the case where everyone I ask agrees there is now a system with a goal, even though the thing doing the predicting has not been touched. Does the slogan sort these three setups the way my colleague needs it to, or does it have to blur them together in order to reach his conclusions — and if a member of the team pressed us on which reading we were relying on, which one should we honestly say we meant?
