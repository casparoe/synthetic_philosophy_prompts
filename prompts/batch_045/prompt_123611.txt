For the past two years, since retiring from a research career in experimental physics, I have been attending a graduate reading group in epistemology, and I am now trying to write up a short paper of my own. Its thesis is that the Gettier phenomenon is best understood as a property not of a single belief-forming method but of a *portfolio* of evidence, and that the quantity doing the work is the hidden correlation between the error terms of two lines of evidence that look, from the inside, to be independent. In my old field we had a name for the corresponding engineering failure — common-mode failure, where two nominally redundant channels go wrong together because they share a component. What I need is a worked case that makes the point, and my own attempts keep collapsing into cases that are either just fake barns or just negligence.

So: build me a Gettier-style case to the following specification.

(i) Realism. Set it in ordinary working science — a laboratory, an observatory, a field campaign — in one paragraph, with no science fiction, no demons or perfect predictors, no stopped clocks, no façades or lookalikes, no lotteries.

(ii) The subject. A competent, careful researcher who forms a true belief in some proposition P (a measured value, a concentration, a date, a classification — your choice). He follows his own protocol, skips no check he was obliged to run, explains away no anomaly, and his confidence is proportionate to what his evidence supports. He is not negligent, and nothing he relies on is false: no false reading, no false entry in the notebook, no false auxiliary assumption, and nothing false that he would offer if asked to justify the claim. There is no false lemma anywhere in the case.

(iii) The evidence. His belief rests on two lines of evidence that are, from his point of view, independent, and each of which is individually highly reliable in the general run of its applications — correct in well over nine cases out of ten.

(iv) The hidden common mode. The two lines share a component the researcher does not know they share (a calibration standard, a sample-preparation step, a software routine, a reference value). The component is right in this instance and right in the great majority of instances, but not infallible: call its failure rate ε, and when it fails, both lines agree on a wrong value. Set ε at roughly one in twenty for the main version of the case.

(v) The verdicts the case must generate, not announce. It must be intuitively true that the researcher does not know P — the Gettier intuition — and this must fall out of the structure of his evidence rather than out of anything he did wrong. It must leave the no-false-lemma analysis powerless: on that analysis the belief qualifies as knowledge. It must also leave a *global* process reliabilism saying knowledge, where I stipulate the operational definition: individuate the process coarsely as "inferring a value from the agreement of two apparently independent methods", and assess its reliability over its actual run of applications, without carving out subclasses. It must leave the safety condition saying no knowledge.

What I want from you:

1. The scenario, in one paragraph, followed by an explicit statement of P, of the two lines of evidence, of their error structure, and of the shared component.
2. The check, worked through separately for the no-false-lemma analysis, global reliabilism as I have defined it, the safety condition, and the sensitivity condition. Tell me honestly whether safety and sensitivity come apart here and in which direction, and whether the verdict of either depends on the grain at which the method is described or on how "nearby" is understood. If the case embarrasses one modal condition and not the other, say so plainly rather than smoothing it over.
3. Which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the verdicts if it is removed — if the lines are made genuinely independent, if the shared component is made visible to a check the researcher could have run, if the case is rebuilt around one line of evidence instead of two.
4. The dial: holding everything else fixed, how low can ε go before the intuitive verdict changes, and where exactly does it change? What does the answer suggest about whether the operative notion here is statistical or modal?
5. The resistance: how would a reliabilist and a safety theorist each push back — in particular, is the reliabilist's re-individuation of the process at a finer grain a principled reply or an ad hoc escape?

I do not want references to the literature or a survey of who has said what: just the case, the derivations, and your own assessment of how well the case survives its own defects.
