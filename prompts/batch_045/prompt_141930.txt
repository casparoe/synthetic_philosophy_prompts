I've drifted into a position on causation that I'd like someone to try to knock me out of, because I can't tell anymore whether it's deep or just insulated.

The position: I think the occasionalist side of the old al-Ghazālī argument basically wins, but not in the version everyone attacks. The cartoon version is that God zaps the cotton into burning rather than letting the fire do it, so anything could happen at any moment and we should all be surprised by breakfast. I don't hold that. What I hold is that the point of the fire-and-cotton discussion is that the observed sequence carries no entailment in it: the contact doesn't produce the burning, and nothing in the earlier event explains why the later one follows, and — this is the part I think gets skipped — the regularity itself is on the same footing as the events, it isn't a second thing laid over them. So the standard objection that occasionalism makes the world unpredictable just misses: the pattern stays exactly as stable as it ever was. It's just that its stability isn't owing to the earlier events. I call it the subtraction reading — you take productive power away from creatures and you lose nothing observable at all.

That last clause is why I'm suspicious of myself. So:

What would count against the subtraction reading, please, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. I especially want the defeaters that a defender of it would concede rather than dismiss out of hand, and I want them sorted by whether they only work if you keep the theology in. I'm not a theist, and "there is no creator, so no problem" is a cheap out — I want the version where you swap the creator for nothing at all and the claim is just that no event is explained by any earlier event, and then ask what breaks.

Two candidate defeaters I keep circling and can't weigh. First, the one I've started calling the calibration problem: if each event is created severally, then the fact that the sequence keeps holding seems to have to be either a further created detail or a fact about the creator's habits, and neither option looks comfortable — the first makes the stability of nature a coincidence sustained one act at a time, the second seems to smuggle back exactly the structure the view was supposed to get rid of, at which point it's hard to see how the position differs from plain regularity-with-a-religious-decoration. Second, a friend's objection (he made it up, I don't think it's a thing in the literature) about counterfactuals: the subtraction reading is supposed to leave everything observable alone, but the practice of intervening — pushing one bit of the world around to see what another bit does, and then taking the result to tell you about a whole class of cases you didn't run — seems to presuppose that the dependence is in the events and not only in the sequence, and I can't tell if that's a genuine commitment the view violates or just a habit of speech it can paraphrase away.

And the meta-question, which is the one I actually care about: on the subtraction reading, is there any possible observation or argument that could count against it? If not, say so plainly, and then tell me whether that's a problem here. Is a thesis about causation that no evidence could touch still a thesis about causation, or has it quietly become a thesis about what explanation would have to be like — and if so, is the right verdict that I haven't discovered something deep, just relocated the question?
