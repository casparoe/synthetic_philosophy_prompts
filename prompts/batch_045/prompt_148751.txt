I'm finishing a term paper on causation by omission, and the topic stopped being purely academic for me a few months ago. An elderly neighbour on our street died after lying on her floor for several days, and my family has been arguing ever since about whether it is true of any of us individually that we let her die — nobody had a duty in any legal sense, nobody's single phone call or visit would obviously have saved her by itself, and yet the sentence "we let her die" keeps feeling both true and unfair. I ended up writing the paper around that discomfort, and I've now got a draft of the central argument that I can't tell apart from nonsense anymore. I'd be very grateful for a careful reading of it.

Below is the argument as it currently stands (minus the introduction and conclusion). I've deliberately kept it free of references because I want comments on the machinery, not on where it sits in the literature — please don't spend your effort on suggestions for what to cite or read; I'll do that part myself. What I would like is the following.

First, the large question: is there a single thesis here, or three unrelated ones? My worry is that §2 and §5 are in tension, and I can't tell whether it's a productive tension or a contradiction. In §2 I want the set of relevant alternative actions to be fixed by facts about the agent's situation and not by anyone's interests; in §5 I concede that the selection is normative, which sounds like a form of interest-relativity by another name. If you think the two can be held together, tell me what reading of "normatively fixed but not interest-relative" does the work; if you think they can't, tell me which section should give way and what the argument looks like afterwards.

Second, the fragmentation problem in §2. Is the deliberative-field move — individuating options by the practical question they answer, with the attentional fallback for agents who never deliberated at all — actually a solution, or does it relocate the vagueness one level down (into what counts as the practical question, or as ordinary effort)? And is the fallback doing too much work: if the field is defined counterfactually, over options that would have been live had the agent paid attention, does it still constrain anything, or can I generate a field for any agent and any outcome? I'd also like a better example than the plant if you have one, since I suspect the plant case makes my view look more intuitive than it is.

Third, the legal section. My claim is that the duty requirement in omission liability is not a moral overlay on a purely factual causal finding but the doctrine's way of fixing which interventions are this agent's business, and hence part of the semantics of the causal claim rather than an extra hurdle. I'm least confident about it, partly because I may be misdescribing the doctrine: I'm working from the rough picture that common-law systems mostly require a recognised duty before a failure to act can ground liability, while a number of civil-law systems impose a general duty of easy rescue. If that picture is wrong, say so, but argue from the structure of the practice rather than from cases you'd have to look up — I'd rather have your reasoning about what the doctrine would have to be like for my claim to hold than a case list. In particular: does the civil-law contrast support my account (wider duty, wider field, more causal omissions) or does it backfire, because a general duty makes the duty requirement look like a variable moral threshold after all, rather than a fixed constraint on option-individuation?

Fourth, the collective case in §4, where no individual contribution would have sufficed but the group's failure to coordinate would have. This is the section that matters most to me personally, since it's the one that decides whether "we let her die" can be true of each of us.

Finally, I'd like prose-level and strategy-level comments: where do I overclaim, which section is weakest relative to the others, what should I cut if I have to lose 300 words, and is there an objection to the whole approach that I simply haven't seen and should address before anyone else raises it?

THE DRAFT

§1. The puzzle. Standard counterfactual-dependence accounts of causation seem to handle omissions too well and too badly at once. If I fail to water your plant while house-sitting and it dies, the death depends on my not watering it, and we happily say I caused the death. But dependence alone over-generates: the death also depends on my not watering it on Tuesday, on my not watering it at 6pm, and on my not watering it with the blue can, and yet "the plant died because I didn't water it with the blue can at 6pm" is at best misleading. Meanwhile the ontology pulls the other way: an omission is not an event, has no location, and cannot be the sort of thing that transfers energy or produces anything. So we need something to select, among all the true dependence claims, the ones that correspond to genuine causal attributions — and we need to say what that selection is tracking, given that it cannot be tracking a causal power of the absence.

§2. Fields. My proposal is that the selection is done by a set of alternative courses of action for the agent, which I call the agent's field. Options in a field are individuated not by their physical description but by the practical question they answer: "water the plant", "call someone who might", and "do nothing about it" are distinct options, whereas watering with the blue can at 6pm is not a distinct option but a specification of the first. Dependence is then assessed against whole options from the field, and the fragmentation in §1 is blocked because the fine-grained candidates were never separate options. For the agent who never deliberated at all — who simply forgot — I add an attentional fallback: the field includes whatever would have been a live option had the agent turned their attention to the matter with ordinary effort. Crucially, I claim the field is fixed by facts about the agent's situation: their abilities, information, role, and the practical predicament they were in. It is not fixed by the interests of whoever is asking the why-question. Causal claims about omissions are thus, on my account, perfectly interest-independent — they just have an extra argument slot that positive causal claims lack.

§3. Duty. This gives me a reading of a doctrinal feature that has always puzzled me: the refusal of criminal law to treat a bare failure to act as causing a death unless the agent stood in a recognised duty relationship. The natural reading is that the court first finds causation as a matter of fact and then imposes a moral filter. I want to say instead that the duty is what fixes the field, and hence that duty enters into whether the causal claim is true at all. An option is in an agent's field only if performing it is the agent's business: rescuing a stranger's child from a paddling pool is something a random passer-by could physically do, but it is not among their options in the relevant sense until something — a relationship, an undertaking, a role, a statute — makes it theirs. The doctrine's duty categories are the list of things that can make an intervention an agent's business. If that is right, the apparent scandal of causation depending on duty dissolves, and the prediction is that jurisdictions with a general duty of easy rescue should display a wider range of causal omissions, which is what one would expect from the wider liability.

§4. Many hands. The harder case is where no individual contribution would have helped. Suppose the plant needed a litre a day and each of ten house-mates could only ever supply a hundred millilitres. Had any one of them acted, the plant would still have died, so dependence fails for each, and yet the explanation of the death that we want cites all of them: they let it die. My solution is to treat the primary causal relatum here as the joint omission of the collective — their failure to coordinate, to set up a rota, to notice that nobody was noticing — since the outcome does depend on that, and to treat singular attributions ("she let it die") as derivative: true in virtue of her membership of a collective whose joint omission caused the outcome, plus a counterfactual about what the collective could have done. This is what makes "we let her die" come out true of each of my neighbours while "she let her die", said of me alone and in isolation, does not — and it is why the sentence feels both true and unfair: true at the collective level, and unfair when heard as a singular claim.

§5. Is this still causation? The obvious objection is that I have made causation by omission into a partly normative notion, and hence collapsed it into responsibility or blame, at which point my thesis is either trivial or no longer about causation. My reply has two parts. First, omissions never had causal powers; all the productive work in any omission case is done by the positive processes that were running, and the only extra ingredient is a selected contrast among actions — so making the selection normative costs no explanatory power that we were ever using. Second, positive causation does not need a field because the causing event is already individuated, so it is no surprise that the omission case looks different, and no argument that I have changed the subject. But I am aware that this reply may concede exactly what §2 denies, since a normatively fixed field looks uncomfortably like an interest-relative one, and I am not sure how to state the difference.
