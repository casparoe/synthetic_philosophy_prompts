I've got 90 minutes with the founding group of a food co-op — about twelve people, grown-ups, sharp, no law or logic background at all — and they're spending next weekend drafting their first written rulebook. I want the session to be about why rules that can change themselves are their own kind of problem, not about what the rules should say. Someone mentioned to me an old game called Nomic where changing the rules is itself a legal move, and that's roughly the register I'm after.

So: don't explain self-amendment to me, plan how I should build it up in the room. Specifically —

- the order the pieces have to land in and what depends on what. My instinct is that they need to actually feel the difference between acting under a rule and changing a rule before anything else, but I can't tell what has to be in place before the recursion (a rule about changing rules, which can itself be changed) does anything other than sound like bureacracy.
- the one example that carries the whole session, and what it has to have and lack. I was going to use: any rule can be changed by a two-thirds vote, including this one. Better vehicle, or does something else do more work? I especially want them to feel the thing where a single vote does two different things at once.
- the misconceptions. I can guess a few: that a paradox here means the rulebook is broken or void, that a rule saying it can never be changed is just a higher threshold in disguise, that this all dissolves if you split things into a constitution and by-laws. Give me the ones I haven't thought of — and, for each, the exact point in the sequence where I should kill it. I don't want to defuse the good ones before anyone has felt the pull.
- one wrinkle I don't know where to put: the case where a rule's text never changes but what it does changes, becuase someone amended a different rule it depends on — you change who counts as a member, or what counts as a meeting, and suddenly the voting rule silently covers a different set of people. Is that the same phenomenon as self-amendment or a different one? And does it belong early, as an intuition pump, or late, as the move that shows the puzzle was never really about self-reference?
- a check at the end that shows they got the structure and not just the vocabulary, and what I should flatly refuse to go into at this level, and say out loud that I'm refusing it.

They'll walk out wanting to write something, so if the last ten minutes should be a small piece of drafting or a diagnostic they run on their own draft rather than a discussion question, tell me that instead.
