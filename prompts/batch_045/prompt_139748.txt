I'm a retired physical chemist — thirty-odd years of calorimetry, and then a career spent staring at equilibrium constants — and I've started sitting in on a philosophy reading group in the evenings, mostly because a friend said I'd enjoy it and, honestly, because retirement is longer then I expected. We've got onto time, and I keep running into the same bloody deck of cards.

The popular book one of the group recommended (I won't name it; the author is very good and I may be misreading him) explains the second law like this: take a new deck, sorted by suit and rank. Shuffle it and it becomes disordered. Keep shuffling and it never comes back into order — not because shuffling prefers disorder, but simply because there are enormously more disordered-looking arrangements than ordered ones. That's entropy. The book then leans on the deck to say that the growth of entropy is really a matter of which variables we happen to keep track of: the shuffled deck only looks messy because suits and ranks are the categories we care about, and there's nothing objectively special about the sorted deck, since it's one arrangement out of 52!, no less probable than any other. And from that it slides to the conclusion that the arrow of time isn't a feature of the world at all, or is at any rate a perspectival, almost anthropical business, and that the difference between past and future is something we project onto a world that doesn't have it.

Now this rubs me the wrong way, and I want to know whether that's just chemist's instinct or whether the analogy is carrying weight it shouldn't. In my old life, entropy differences were things you measured with a calorimeter, and they told you which way a reaction would run and where equilibrium sat. Nobody in a lab ever thought they were taking a reading of somebody's point of view. On the other hand, my instinct is also that 'disorder' was always a rotten word for entropy, and that the deck is probably what keeps it alive.

So: audit the thing. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the part of the microstate, the macrostate, the entropy, the dynamics, the equilibration time, the Past Hypothesis (presumably the fresh deck out of its box?), the agent doing the shuffling, and whatever plays the part of records or memory — and tell me which features of the physics have no counterpart in the deck at all. Then the disanalogies, sorted into the ones that actually matter for the use the book is making of the analogy and the ones that are just noise. The ones I half suspect: that in the deck the initial ordered state is a single permutation, whereas the early universe's low entropy was, I take it, a macrostate with a colossal but still vanishingly small share of the microstates — smoothness rather than one unique arrangement; that the deck's arrow comes from a shuffler who is deliberately randomising, i.e. from time-asymmetric goings-on outside the system, while the gas is supposed to get its arrow from the initial condition plus symmetric dynamics; that 52! is nothing like Avogadro-scale, so recurrence times and the thermodynamic limit don't carry over; and that the deck has no space, so there's nothing answering to a gas spreading into a box, and nothing answering to the way gravity flips the story and makes clumping the high-entropy end. (I half remember a result that about seven riffle shuffles will randomise a deck — don't hold me to the number — and if that's roughly right it gives the deck a single sharp equilibration time, which the universe may not have, or may have several of at once.)

Then the bit I actually care about: does the perspectival conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the deck doing all the work? If it doesn't survive, what's the nearest true thing to it, and can the deck be repaired rather than scrapped?

Because I'd like something to bring back to the group. I've been toying with a variant: two decks, and instead of order being one special arrangement of a single deck, you make the special initial condition a correlation between them — the two decks agreeing card for card, say — and you let the shuffling act on them jointly. My hope is that this gives you a low-entropy state that is a genuine macrostate with a great many microstates rather than a unique permutation, and that the quantity that degrades becomes degree of correlation, which feels closer to what cosmologists mean by smoothness. Does that fix the first disanalogy I listed, or does it just move the bump in the carpet? What does it get right that the one-deck version doesn't? What does it still fail to represent — space, gravity, records, the branch structure where fridges and embryos run backwards locally and pay for it elsewhere? And if you had to design the best possible card-game model of the thermodynamic arrow for a room of retired scientists and one very patient philosopher, what would it look like, and where would you tell us to stop trusting it?
