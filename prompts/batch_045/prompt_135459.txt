I've been asked to give the dedicatory remarks — five or six minutes, call it 750 words — when the city archives opens the reading room they're naming after Ruth Calloway next month, and I'm stuck on a problem that is entirely of her own making. I was her research assistant in 2001 and 2002, mostly driving her to county courthouses in a borrowed car while she read relocation transcripts aloud to me, and I'm now on the archives board, which is why the job fell to me. Ruth is eighty-four, completely sharp, and will be in the front row; she has twice stood up in the middle of a tribute to correct a speaker who said she had "single-handedly" won something, and I would like to get through this without being the third. The room will be half people who were in the Halloway campaign or who teach urban history and know her book — *Four Ways to Win: The Halloway Campaign and the Problem of Overdetermination* — and half her grandchildren and great-grandchildren, neighbours from the block, the mayor, and a local reporter. The genre of a dedication wants me to say that but for her none of this would exist. Her book argues that this is false on its own terms: the March 1975 ordinance that stopped the clearance of the Halloway neighborhood and created the city's first relocation-and-right-of-return law was, she argues, overdetermined, with four independently sufficient causes — the 1974 change in federal highway funding rules that made the spur unfundable, the state supreme court's relocation ruling that spring, the city's inability to bond its matching share after the foundry closed, and a council member facing a primary from his left. I know the philosophical literature this sits in, the preemption cases, the old insufficient-but-non-redundant-part-of-an-unnecessary-but-sufficient-condition formula, and I don't want any of it in the speech: Ruth would wince at the vocabulary and her great-grandchildren would stop listening. What I want is for the structure to be load-bearing but invisible.

The way out, I think, is a distinction she drew in the last chapter: she called it textured overdetermination, the case where the *occurrence* of an outcome has several independently sufficient causes but its *texture* — how fast, how complete, what it cost people, who was named in it, what was left standing afterwards — does not. On her own account Halloway came six months faster than the two comparable campaigns in nearby cities, nobody was hurt (in one of them a man died), the right of return was written into the ordinance and is the reason ninety-odd families actually came back, and the tenants' association she helped build is still meeting fifty years later. That is where I want the praise to live. She also liked to say that a monument is really a set of instructions, and by that standard my speech is itself a small causal intervention: whatever I credit is what the organizers in the room, and the ones who read the reporter's piece, will take to be the kind of thing that works. So I want the credit to fall on the dull infrastructure and on the women in their sixties who started the door-knocking two years before anyone else showed up, and I want the speech to be honest about the role of the funding rules and of luck, without being so modest that it amounts to telling her family she didn't matter.

The hardest part is an anecdote I can't avoid and can't use in the obvious way. Everyone who was there remembers the night of March 12, 1975, when Ruth and two other people ran a mimeograph machine in the basement of Mount Zion until four in the morning, to get nine hundred flyers out for the eight o'clock commission hearing; sixty people came; and the hearing is where the relocation terms were read into the record verbatim, the transcript that the state appellate court quoted from when it upheld the right of return in 1981. I have to include it, because if I don't the room will think I left her out of her own building. But Ruth herself conducted the 1998 oral history in which the commissioner who cast the deciding fourth vote said he had already made up his mind on the funding question before he heard a word of testimony, and she published it — so I cannot let the flyers be the thing that changed the vote, and I can't gesture at that reading either. I need the anecdote to carry whatever weight it can honestly carry and not a gram more. Two more constraints: the mayor is speaking after me and will call her the woman who saved Halloway, and I don't want to contradict him from a podium, but I would like my remarks to have made the room hear his line differently by the time he says it; and the speech has to end somewhere warm and plain, not on a caveat. Write the remarks, and if it's useful, add a short note afterwards on the two or three places a conventional draft would have quietly slipped into a claim she would call false.
