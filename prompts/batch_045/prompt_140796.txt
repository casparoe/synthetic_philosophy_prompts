I've been chewing on this on my own for a couple of years now — no philosophy classes, just a lot of long walks after I turned down a job in another city and couldn't explain to my family why there wasn't a right answer. I finally wrote down what I actually believe about hard choices, and I want to know whether it's a package or just a pile of things I like the sound of.

Here it is:

1. Some choices are genuinely hard: not "I don't know enough yet," but the options aren't ranked. I had two offers — stay near family, or move for work I'd probably find more interesting — and I want to say neither was better, and they wern't equally good either. I've seen people online call this being "on a par," and that sounded right to me.

2. My test for telling "on a par" apart from "equally good": if a small improvement on one side — say a 3% raise — wouldn't settle it, they're not tied, because if they were exactly equal the raise would break the tie. I lean on this test constantly and I trust it.

3. There's still a right way and a wrong way to make such a choice. Deciding by coin flip, or letting the deadline decide for you, or doing whatever is least awkward to explain to people, is getting it wrong. The right way is to decide and then put yourself behind it.

4. But I don't accept the story that deciding manufactures a reason, or that you "create" reasons by committing to something. Reasons aren't the kind of thing you can make by making up your mind. That sounds like magic to me.

5. I'm fairly confident that "better than" is transitive. I don't believe the stories where A is a hair better than B, B a hair better than C, and C comes back around and beats A. I think something has gone wrong in those examples.

6. I also want to say there's a fact of the matter about which option was better for me, even in the hard cases — I just may never know it. That doesn't feel like a contradiction with 1, but I can't really say why.

7. A distinction I invented because I couldn't find one: flat hard choices, where nothing you could learn would settle it, versus crested ones, where something would settle it but it's the sort of thing you can't get at — like how it would actually feel to have been a musician for thirty years. Only flat ones are real hard choices; the crested ones are ignorance in a costume.

8. And a verdict I'm attached to: a friend built a spreadsheet with weights and scores for the same kind of decision and genuinely trusted the output. I think he's making a mistake, but I don't think he's being irrational.

So: is this consistent? Go through the pairs and tell me which ones really pull against each other and which only look like they do. For the real tensions, rank them by how bad they are, say what would have to give in each case, what the cheapest fix is, and whether there's one single revision that cleans up the most. Don't just flag eight tensions — if everything is in tension with everything I haven't learned anything. And tell me at the end what my own view commits me to saying about the spreadsheet friend, since I go back and forth on him.

One more thing: please be clear about which parts of this are settled and which are live fights among people who actually work on it. If a move I'm making is contested, say it's contested rather than telling me the field has sorted it out. And keep the whole thing under 650 words — I'd rather it be tight than complete.
