I'm in the first year of a philosophy PhD and I also sit in on an interpretability reading group in the computer science department, where people like to ask, half seriously, whether a language model believes or knows the things its outputs appear to say. I have been trying to turn that question into something more disciplined, and my current idea is a Moorean test: treat the capacity for Moore-paradoxical incoherence as a constraint on belief ascription — roughly, a system has beliefs only if there is, for it, a difference between committing itself to p and merely reporting that it is in a certain state, such that one and the same system doing both at once generates the kind of tension Moore described (his own examples being the first-person present-tense conjunctions, with the past-tense and second- and third-person versions as the contrast cases that are not absurd at all). To make the test bite I have been working with two invented systems. GATED is a frozen-weight model that has been fine-tuned so that the string *I do not believe that p*, once emitted, causally gates its subsequent behaviour: afterwards it answers questions about p as though p were in doubt, hedges, and revises. The self-report has a real functional role in the system's own operations. FLAT is the same architecture without that fine-tuning: it can be prompted to emit *p, but I do not believe that p* and shows no measurable internal or downstream difference between that and any other continuation; the conflict, such as it is, lives entirely in the text. I am due to give a short talk on this next month and would rather not be ambushed.

When I tried the idea out, someone in the seminar waved it away with a line I have now heard three times in different forms: Moore's paradox shows nothing about belief — the absurdity is in the saying, not in what is said; a text generator is not saying anything, so your test has nothing to get hold of. I think there is something right and something evasive about that, but I cannot get clear on what the line is actually asserting. The saying might mean the act of assertion, the utterance of a sentence type, the avowal of a first-personal state, the performance of a commitment before an audience, or the whole practice-governed move. Absurdity might mean logical inconsistency, pragmatic infelicity, violation of a norm of assertion (belief, knowledge, or evidence), irrationality in the state expressed, or the impossibility of sincerely performing the act with that content. And in might mean located in, manifested only under, or supervenient on. My own hypothesis, offered as one, is that the line is really a claim about the norms governing assertion plus a linking principle from assertion to belief, in which case it begs the question against my test by building is really asserting into the very thing I was hoping Moorean incoherence would detect. But I may be wrong, and I would like the alternatives laid out.

So: give me a menu of precise theses that the absurdity is in the saying, not in what is said could be expressing, ordered roughly from the trivial to the radical, saying for each how it handles the asymmetries Moore himself stressed — present versus past tense, first versus second and third person, omissive versus commissive forms, and the difference between asserting and merely believing a Moorean content. Please set it out as a table with these columns:

- Reading — the thesis stated in one or two sentences, precisely enough that it could turn out to be false.
- Kind of claim — what sort of claim it is: semantic, pragmatic, epistemic-normative, psychological, or metaphysical about the nature of belief.
- Status — whether it is a platitude, substantive and defensible, contested, or false, with a one-line reason.
- Asymmetries — which of Moore's contrasts this reading explains and which it leaves unexplained or gets wrong.
- Verdict on my test — whether, if this reading were right, the seminar objection goes through and the test collapses, or whether the test survives, and if so in what form (for GATED only, for FLAT too, only given an independent account of assertion, and so on); say explicitly if the reading makes the test circular.
- Discriminating probe — one concrete case, manipulation, or prediction that would separate this reading from its nearest neighbour above it in the table; GATED and FLAT, or a third system you describe yourself, are all fine here.

Keep each cell to about forty words, and add beneath the table a bottom line of at most eighty words saying which reading the person repeating the line most likely meant and whether, on your diagnosis, the Moorean test is dead, salvageable, or merely in need of a better formulation.
