The examination question was as follows.

**Question 3 (25 marks). Answer all parts.**

Galen Strawson's "Basic Argument" purports to show that no agent can ever be *truly* morally responsible for anything she does.

(a) Set out the argument's premises, and explain why Strawson holds that the conclusion follows whether or not determinism is true. **[8 marks]**

(b) *The Bootstrapping Case.* Nadia is forty years old. By means of a causal loop whose mechanism need not concern us, she travels back twenty years and, over the following decade, deliberately arranges her own upbringing and education so as to give her twenty-year-old self exactly the character — the desires, motives and deliberative habits — that she has at forty, and that explains why the forty-year-old Nadia arranges things as she does. Assume the case is fully coherent: no contradictions, no failed attempts to "bilk" the loop, and Nadia's action at every point is intentional, deliberate and uncoerced. Nadia is therefore the intentional cause of her own character: every link in the chain that makes her what she is is itself one of her own intentional actions.

Does Nadia escape the Basic Argument? In answering, you should discuss Strawson's treatment of the suggestion that an agent might create herself gradually, over time, and you should say what your answer shows about whether the "regress" formulation and the "luck" formulation of the argument are, as Strawson suggests, two versions of a single argument. **[7 marks]**

(c) Assess the following objection: *"Strawson's demand that an agent be responsible for the way she is is false. What matters for responsibility is not self-creation but whether the agent acts for the reasons she takes herself to have and is responsive to moral reasons; an agent like that is responsible no matter how she came to be that way."* Does this objection refute the Basic Argument? **[6 marks]**

(d) Accuracy of terminology and clarity of expression. **[4 marks]**

Candidates were examined only on material from the lectures: the structure of the Basic Argument; its independence from the determinism question; Strawson's reply to the idea of gradual self-creation; his contrast between "true" (desert-entailing) responsibility and the thinner notions a compatibilist may retain; and his contention that the pessimist conclusion has no practical import. No wider reading was required or expected.

---

**Candidate 0147**

Strawson's Basic Argument has three premises. First: everything an agent does, she does because of the way she is — because of her character, her motives, and her other mental features. Call this P1. Second, P2: if an agent is to be *truly* morally responsible for what she does, she must be truly responsible for the way she is, at least in those mental respects that explain the action. Third, P3: no agent can ever be truly responsible for the way she is. For her to be responsible for being the way she is at some time, she would have had to make herself that way by an earlier intentional action; but that earlier action was itself performed because of the way she was then; and so on backwards. The regress is vicious because it never gets started: at no point does the agent create herself from nothing. Hence no one is ever truly morally responsible, where "truly" means responsible in a way that could make blame genuinely *deserved*, rather than merely useful.

Strawson insists that this conclusion is independent of the truth of determinism. If determinism is true, our nature is simply handed to us by causes we did not choose. But if indeterminism is true, then whatever contribution to action is not settled by our character is a matter of chance, and chance is no better a foundation for desert than determinism is. Either way P2 cannot be satisfied. This makes Strawson a hard determinist of a pessimistic sort, and his conclusion — that we must stop holding people morally responsible, and replace blame and punishment with purely forward-looking measures such as deterrence and quarantine — follows regardless of what physics turns out to be like.

(b) The Bootstrapping Case shows that P3 has an exception. P3 says that an agent cannot be responsible for the way she is, because the series of self-shaping actions never terminates. But Nadia's case shows that the series can terminate, because it closes into a circle. Nadia is, intentionally and deliberately, the cause of her own character; that is just the self-creation, the *causa sui*, that Strawson declares impossible, and the loop makes it possible. Her action at forty is explained by the character she acquired at twenty, and that character is explained by her own action at forty, so there is no vicious regress and no uncreated starting point: every link in the chain has one of her own intentional actions behind it. Strawson's reply to the idea of gradual self-creation — that each act of shaping oneself presupposes an already-existing nature that does the shaping — is answered here, because the nature that does the shaping is itself something she shaped. I conclude that Nadia does escape the Basic Argument, and that P3, though true of creatures like us, is not necessarily true.

(c) The objection from reasons-responsiveness refutes the Basic Argument. P2 is an absurd demand: no one has ever seriously supposed that responsibility requires creating oneself out of nothing. Once we see that what matters is whether the agent acted for the reasons she took herself to have, and would have acted differently had those reasons been different, we can see that Strawson's argument is unsound, since it extracts a dramatic conclusion from a premise that almost nobody accepts. And because determinism is perfectly compatible with reasons-responsiveness, the argument's conclusion collapses. We can therefore go on holding people responsible exactly as we did before.
