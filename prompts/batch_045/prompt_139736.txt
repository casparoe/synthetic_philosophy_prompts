The setup: nine-month fellowship, output is about 8,000 words plus one workshop. Working title is something like "Inevitability and credit: what multiple discovery actually shows". Here's the half-formed thought. Everyone asks whether discoveries were inevitable — if not Newton then Leibniz, if not Darwin then Wallace — and then uses the answer to settle whether it matters who made them. I think two things are being run together and I want to prize them apart.

First, "inevitable" is a claim at a grain, and nobody says which grain. At the coarse grain (natural selection; the calculus) yes, something close was coming. At a finer grain — Darwin's actual package, selection plus divergence plus sexual selection, versus Wallace's, which didn't extend to human minds; or the notational and formal machinery that actually got taken up afterwards — it looks like no, and the fine-grained thing is what shaped the field. So I want a parameter for how much of a result's downstream influence rides on the content and how much on the vehicle it arrived in. I've been calling it "portability" for want of a beter name: low portability means it mattered a lot who got there first, even given that the coarse-grained result was coming anyway.

Second, the slide from "it was going to happen anyway" to "so the discoverer deserves less" assumes credit is basically the cost of the counterfactual delay. That feels like the wrong shape. Ten years of delay in a field with a queue of results waiting on it costs far more than ten years in a field that isn't ready; and a result nobody else was near can cost the most counterfactually while having the least actual uptake, because the field can't absorb it yet. So I want to swap delay for something like readiness-weighted delay — roughly, how much was waiting on the result, integrated over the period it went missing.
