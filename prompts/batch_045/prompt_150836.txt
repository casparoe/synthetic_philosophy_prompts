Next week I'm giving the opening talk at a workshop on expert elicitation: about 25 people, roughly half working forecasters and quant types who live by calibration and Brier scores, a handful of philosophers of science, and two game theorists who know the agreement theorem and the no-trade results cold. I'm the token journalist, invited because I wrote a piece on forecasting a while back.

My thesis: when two well-informed experts keep disagreeing after pooling all there evidence, people reach for Aumann and conclude that one of them is irrational, is sitting on private info, or started from a different prior. I want to argue that's almost never where the action is. The theorem needs each side to have a posterior for the same event, and in in real elicitations the public numbers often aren't that — they're the share of an ensemble crossing a threshold, a number read of a model the person knows is misspecified, or an answer to a question each expert has quietly operationalised differently (what counts as a failure, over what window, by whom). So the theorem is best read as a diagnostic pointing upstream: the disagreement sits in the event or the model, not in the inference. Sketch: three such cases, then the claim that the fix is repairing and aggregating the question rather than averaging the numbers, then a polemical coda — that scoring-rule practice quietly pressures people to present an ensemble share as if it were a probability.
