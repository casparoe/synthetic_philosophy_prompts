So I've been going down a rabbit hole about money for a blog thing I do (poorly maintained, but whatever) and I want help getting straight what our actual money setup *assumes*.

Here's the bundle of practices I mean. In the US, and basically everywhere now: the state declares a currency to be legal tender, makes you accept it in settlement of debts, and requires taxes to be paid in it. Almost none of it is paper — as far as I can tell most of what we call money is entries in commercial-bank databases, and those entries mostly come into existence when a bank makes a loan (correct me if I've got that wrong). The central bank explicitly aims for the unit to lose around 2% of its purchasing power per year, on purpose, indefinitely. And everybody still talks and behaves as though a dollar is a *thing* you can hold, pass on, and charge rent on — as if charging for the use of money were as ordinary as charging for the use of a house.

Now the thing I keep tripping over is Aristotle. As I remember it: money arises because direct barter is clumsy, so a community settles on some common standard that makes a house and a pair of sandles comparable, so that exchange can be reciprocal and fair. It exists by convention rather than by nature — I think he even connects the word for coin to the word for law — which is why whoever controls it can debase or abolish it. And then, the bit I can't get past: he says money is barren. It's for swapping, not for breeding, so lending it at interest and getting back more than you handed over is supposedly the most unnatural way there is to make a living. Which sounds insane to modern ears, because we lend at interest constantly and the entire financial system runs on it.

So: what does the modern bundle actually presuppose about what money is?

What I'd like from you:

Reconstruct the implicit theory of the whole bundle, and be precise about which *part* of the practice depends on which *part* of the theory. Does legal-tender-plus-tax strictly require that money is a creature of the state, or could money have arisen with no state involved at all and still end up looking exactly like this? Does the fact that most money is bank debts created by lending strictly require that money just *is* a credit relation, or is that an accident of history the practice could shrug off?

Then tell me where the parts pull apart. My own suspicion, which I want you to test rather than flatter, is that there are two different jobs here: the at-a-moment job, where money makes different goods comparable right now, like a yardstick; and the across-time job, where money carries a claim into the future, like a ledger entry. Aristotle's story seems to me to handle the first job and have no room whatsoever for the second, while the modern system is almost entirely about the second. If that's right, then the 2% target isn't a small technical detail — it's a flat rejection of the yardstick picture, since nobody would accept a metre that shrank 2% a year. Is that a fair way to put the conflict, or can a friend of Aristotle's account swallow a deliberately depreciating measure?

Then the bit I most want argued. Take this hypothetical seriously: an economy where there is nothing productive left to do with extra money — every worthwhile machine already built, maybe production is basically automated, so lending someone money doesn't let them produce anything they couldn't have produced anyway. On Aristotle's own reasoning, does interest become unnatural *there* but fine in a world full of unused productive opportunities? If so, his real complaint isn't really about money's nature at all; it's about getting a return without there being anything productive for the money to do. And if that's the right reading, does his objection get stronger or weaker in a world of cheap capital, weak investment, and asset prices that mostly track scarcity rather than anything getting built?

Finally: if Aristotle is right that money is a convention whose purpose is to make exchange commensurable, what would a currency built to his specifications look like — say a unit redefined so it always buys the same basket, issued without interest? Would it be what he should have wanted, and would it work? Say plainly why not if it wouldn't.

Don't just tell me that money is philosophically contested and lay out three theories — I've read that, it's boring. Tell me which view the plumbing actually needs, which views it merely gets along with, and where it's genuinely underdetermined and two rival theories would license exactly the same institutions. And please don't point me at sources or build me a reading list; I'm doing this offline and I'd rather you just argue it out from the claims themselves.
