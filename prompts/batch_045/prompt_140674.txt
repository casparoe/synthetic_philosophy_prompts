I was reading an encyclopedia entry online about dispositions (fragility, solubility, that kind of thing) and it summarised the whole finks-and-masks literature in one paragraph. I want to know how much of it to trust. Here it is, roughly as I remember it:

"Dispositions are the 'would' properties — the ones that show up not in what a thing is doing right now but in what it would do if something happened to it. The natural analysis is the conditional one: to be fragile just is to be such that you would break if you were struck. But Martin found two kinds of counterexample. A fink is a case where the very thing that triggers the disposition destroys it: a glass wired to a circuit that, the instant the glass is struck, rearranges its molecules into something unbreakable, so it never breaks. A mask is the same thing in reverse: the disposition is intact, but something else — packing foam, a guardian angel — gets in the way and the manifestation never arrives. In both cases the glass is fragile and it's false that it would break if struck, so the conditional analysis is dead. The moral people drew is that dispositions aren't real properties of the object at all; calling the glass fragile is just a handy shorthand for how it behaves, and the real explanatory work is done by its categorical microstructure. Lewis then patched the analysis by adding a ceteris paribus clause: the conditional only has to hold under normal conditions, with the object's causal basis held fixed — the causal basis being the microstructural cause of the breaking, i.e. the arrangement of its molecules. With that clause in place, finks and masks stop being counterexamples and the amended conditional analysis is the orthodox position today."
