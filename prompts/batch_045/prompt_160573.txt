I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas, mostly the psychology-and-stats beat — and I'm working on a feature about whether the whole "replication crisis" framing has outlived its usefulness. My editor wants an interactive sidebar to go with it: one of those click-through decision trees that drops readers into whichever camp people actually argue for at conferences, but built so readers get sorted by what they think about a concrete case rather than by which label sounds nicest. Here's the fixed setup I want everyone to start from, so please keep it: a medium-sized effect in social psychology with two dozen small published studies behind it finally gets one big preregistered multi-site replication with a sample in the thousands, and it comes back with an estimate indistinguisable from zero and a tight interval; the original author responds that the replicators dropped two unmeasured features of the original protocol. Every reader answers questions about that one scenario.

Constraints, because I have to hand this to a web developer who'll turn it into clickable boxes. Keep it to six-to-eight branch nodes and six-to-nine leaves, aimed at a reader who took one stats class and has read a few of the big magazine pieces, and no node may ask anyone to self-identify methodologically — no "do you prefer Bayesian methods?", no "should we ban p-values?" Each branch should be labeled by the claim that actually does the splitting, and phrased so someone can answer it without knowing where it leads. The tree has to keep apart two things that always get collapsed in this argument: (i) what you think happened — the original was a false positive and was never real, versus the effect is real but tiny and swamped by noise, versus the effect is real but only in that context; and (ii) where you think the fix lives — in the statistical tools, in the institutions and incentives, or in the phenomena and the way they're measured. I also want at least one node built on a stipulation I haven't seen anyone discuss: suppose you could rerun the original experiment in the original lab, with the original experimenters and the original subject pool, the week after the original, and it replicates perfectly every single time, and it has never once replicated anywhere else. Branch on what that should convince you of. A second node on the funder's choice — twenty more big replications of famous effects, or ten years of instrument-building for one construct — would be useful too.

Leaves that have to be findable in your tree, under whatever names you prefer: it's the incentives and publication filter, not the math; the tests were never severe in the first place; switch to priors and Bayes factors; stop testing and just report estimates with intervals; the constructs and measurements are the real culprit; effects are genuinely fragile and context-bound, so what we need is theory and measurement, not more replications; this is just ordinary scientific self-correction and the crisis framing is overblown; and these literatures can't be salvaged, so the field should move on. Build the thing from the logical space rather than from the list: lay out your questions and which combinations of answers are actually available, then place the named positions in that space, and mark the combinations nobody occupies with a line on why (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored). Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate, and flag any node where the evidence genuinely doesn't settle the question instead of inventing a fork — I'd rather print "we don't know" in the sidebar than fake a branch. Each leaf gets one line on the datum it has the hardest time accounting for, and tell me at the end which single node is doing most of the sorting work.
