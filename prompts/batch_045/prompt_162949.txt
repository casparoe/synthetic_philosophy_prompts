hey, I'm in a philosophy of mind course and we're on the free will / neuroscience week, and I brought along a pop-sci book from the library (one of those "neuroscience shows your choices are made for you" ones — I won't name it) to see how it lined up with the seminar. Its version of Libet goes something like this:

"In the early 1980s Libet hooked people up to EEG, asked them to flex a wrist whenever they felt like it, and had them watch a clock so they could report when they first became aware of the urge to move. The recordings showed a slow buildup of electrical activity over motor areas — the readiness potential — beginning roughly half a second before the movement, while the reported urge came only about 200 ms before the movement. So the brain had already initiated the action about 300 ms before the person consciously decided, and what we call deciding is just consciousness receiving a memo after the fact. Libet himself concluded that free will is an illusion. Later brain-scanning work made it worse: researchers can predict which of two buttons you will press up to ten seconds before you press it, so the real unconscious gap is seconds, not milliseconds. The only escape hatch Libet left was a veto in the last 150 milliseconds, but that's just the brakes on a car somebody else is already steering."

The veto line feels snappy in a way I dont trust, but I can't say where it goes wrong. How much of this would someone who actually works on this accept, where is it overselling, and where is it just wrong — especially the step from "there's a readiness potential before the reported urge" to "the decision was already made unconsciously"? Is it fair that Libet drew the illusion conclusion himself, because my vague memory from seminar is that he kind of argued the opposite? And is the "ten seconds" bit even the same measurement, or is a different experiment being quietly mixed in?

Also: in section our TA floated a variant — instead of "move whenever you want," subjects choose between two buttons and report the moment they settled on *which* one, rather than when they felt the urge.
