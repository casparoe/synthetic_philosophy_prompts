I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been asked to give the after-dinner keynote at a two-day invite-only workshop on scalable oversight — specifically on the family of proposals in which a system is trained by having two systems argue a question before a judge (a person, or eventually a model), on the bet that it is cheaper to expose a bad argument than to build one that survives exposure. The room will be roughly 120 people: the researchers who actually run these experiments, six or seven philosophers and social epistemologists who were flown in to poke at the foundations, four program officers from the foundation that funds the workshop and is deciding next spring whether to renew the program, a few graduate students, and three reporters including me. I have fifteen minutes, and I'd like the speech to run about 1,600 to 1,900 words.

Here is the difficulty. My entire professional authority rests on exactly the assumption this workshop is stress-testing: that a literate generalist, given time, documents, and the right to ask hostile questions, can tell a good argument from a bad one in a field she does not work in. If the obfuscated-argument problem is real — if an argument can be defective in a way the available judge cannot find within the attention and knowledge they actually have, so that the asymmetry between making and checking breaks down — then the thing I have been doing for twenty years is a special case that happens to have worked, not a method that generalizes. I want the speech to be honest about that, but I am the keynote speaker and not a penitent: no confession, no self-flagellation, and no conclusion that my trade is obsolete.

To give you something concrete to build on, here is the case I want the speech to open with, from my own reporting. A source handed me a long technical annex — four hundred pages, internally consistent, every citation real, every individual claim defensible. I checked the steps I could check and I published. The document was wrong, and it was wrong in a way I still struggle to describe: no single step was false, but a term shifted its scope somewhere between the second and the seventh section, and the conclusion only looked like it followed because nobody was holding the whole thing in mind at once. There was no line I could have circled.

I'd like the speech to use that case to make a three-way distinction do real structural work, and I'd like the distinction to be yours to sharpen if I've drawn it badly:

- defects that are localizable — a particular step is wrong and can be shown to be wrong on the page, the way a bad line in a proof can be;
- defects that are localizable but expertise-gated — one step is wrong, and a specialist would see it in a minute, but this judge, in these ten minutes, cannot, and the gap between findable and findable-by-this-judge is exactly what a dishonest arguer can aim at;
- defects that are distributed — nothing is wrong anywhere, the wrongness is a property of the whole: a scope shift, a presupposition that does no work until it has been leaned on four times, a conclusion that follows only if you forget what the terms meant at the start.

My sense, which I want you to test rather than ratify, is that the experimental results people are proud of are mostly won on arguments of the first kind, that the second kind is where the training signal gets noisy in ways that are hard to see in the loss curve, and that the third kind is where cross-examination has no move at all, because there is no single step to attack. If that is right, then the inference from 'debate works' to 'debate scales' is resting on an untested step, and the honest version of my speech cannot tell this room either that the method compounds or that it doesn't.

So here is what the speech has to do without:

- No verdict on whether the program works. I don't know, and the program officers are in the room; I don't want the speech to read as either a pitch for renewal or a hint that it should be wound down.
- No reassurance that we would notice if it failed. The whole worry is that the failure is silent — that judges can be wrong in ways they cannot detect from the inside — and any 'we'll see it when it breaks' line would quietly deny the premise I've just asked the room to take seriously.
- No existential crescendo. This audience has heard it, and a board reading the transcript would file it as advocacy.
- No howlers about how the method works, since half the room built it; and no unexplained jargon either, since the program officers' staff and the students are not specialists. Every technical term gets defined in a clause, once.
- No cheap shot at the funders, and no cheap shot at the philosophers, who are guests.
- One moment of actual laughter, which should not be at any of the above.

What I do want is a speech that gives the non-specialists in the room a real handle on why this problem is interesting — a reason to care beyond 'the AI people are worried' — and gives the specialists the sense that someone outside the field has understood the shape of the objection rather than its headline. If there is a version of the buried-defect problem that applies to my own newsroom, and not only to machine debate, I'd like the speech to say so in a way that is affectionate toward my trade rather than prosecutorial.

Please don't give me citations, footnotes, or further reading: I won't be able to verify them and I won't use them. Argue from the structure of the problem.

After the speech, I need two things.

First, a short note — three sentences at most — on where the speech leans on a contested reading of the problem, as opposed to something people who work on this would generally grant.

Second, a table, one row for each load-bearing claim the speech makes about debate and obfuscation, with these columns:

- Claim — the sentence as it appears in the speech, or a close paraphrase if it runs long
- Substance — what the claim actually asserts, in one plain sentence
- Status — exactly one of: widely granted by people who work on this; contested, with reasonable people on both sides; my own extrapolation beyond anything established; rhetorical, with no factual content to defend
- Strongest objection — the best version of what someone who disagrees would say, one or two sentences, not a strawman
- Hedged already — yes or no; and where no, the single line I should change so the passage survives a hostile reader without going flat
- Cut line — one sentence on whether I should drop the passage entirely if I learn a program officer is in the room taking notes

Then three or four sentences on where you are unsure yourself — where you think a reasonable expert would put a materially different probability on something the speech asserts or implies. I would rather have your uncertainty named than have a contested view smoothed into a consensus for the sake of the peroration.
