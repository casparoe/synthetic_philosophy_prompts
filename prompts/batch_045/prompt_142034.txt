I'm a fourth-year PhD student in the programming languages group — I work on dependent types, mostly Coq — and I've been landed with the 80 minute slot on constructive reasoning in our departments "logic bootcamp" for incoming PhDs, in two weeks. Most of the room will be CS students who've written some Coq or Agda but have never had a logic course beyond induction-on-the-derivation, and a couple of them are philosophy-adjacent and dont code, so I can't lean on Curry-Howard as the main vehicle even though it's the thing I'd reach for first. I also know for a fact that a fifth-year has already told them that intuitionistic logic is just classical logic without excluded middle, strictly weaker, nothing to see here, so they will turn up thinking the slot is a formality.

Don't write the lecture for me. I want a plan for how to teach it.

1. The order the pieces have to go in and what depends on what. My instinct is that the Brouwer-Heyting-Kolmogorov style reading of or and exists — a proof of A or B is a choice of a side plus a proof of that side, a proof of exists x. A is a witness plus a proof — has to come before anyone says the words "law of excluded middle", because otherwise "why can't I just split on whether P holds" doesn't sound like a question that needs an answer. But I'm not sure where the real numbers go: I want them to see that you can't in general decide whether two constructive reals are equal (or what the constructive substitute for comparing them even is), and I don't know whether that needs the BHK clauses first or whether it can be what motivates them. And where, if anywhere, does the split between Brouwer-style intuitionism — which adds principles like continuity and bar induction and outright refutes bits of classical maths — and Bishop-style constructive maths, which is deliberately a common subset with no extra axioms, belong? One of the philosophy people will have read something and will say "isn't this all just about meaning being use?", and I want to know if that's a derailment I should absorb into the sequence or head off in advance.

2. The one example to carry the whole thing. I keep going back and forth between the root-two-to-the-root-two proof that there are irrationals a and b with a^b rational, and the intermediate value theorem — the bisection argument looks totally constructive right up to the point where you have to decide whether f at the midpoint is zero. I currently prefer IVT because it hands me the contrast between the exact statement and the approximate one (for every epsilon there's an x with |f(x)| < epsilon), which is provable, and because I've heard there's a computable function that crosses zero but has no computable zero, which would show the exact version really is unprovable rather than just unfound. Tell me which one to use, what the carrying example has to have and what it has to lack — I worry a careless choice makes constructive maths feel like classical maths with theorems deleted, i.e. a loss — and say explicitly why not the other one. If there's a better example I haven't thought of, name it and say why it's better.

3. The misconceptions, in the order they'll form, and the point in the sequence where I should kill each one. The ones I'm expecting: "so intuitionists assert that excluded middle is false" — I want to head this off with the fact that not-not-(A or not-A) is itself constructively provable, so flatly denying A or not-A would be inconsistent, and I want to know whether that's the right tool and the right moment; "constructive maths is just whatever you can get without choice and excluded middle"; "you can always mechanically convert a classical proof into a constructive one" (I think this is only half true and only for certain shapes of statement, but I don't know what the honest two-sentence version is at this level); and "if it's neither proved nor refuted then it has no truth value, or it's a third truth value". Tell me what I'm missing, specifically for an audience that has written `destruct (classic P)` in Coq a hundred times without thinking about it.

4. Where the disjunction and existence properties go. My plan is to use them as the payoff: a theory with the disjunction property can't prove every instance of A or not-A, because then it would have to settle every open problem it can state, and no consistent arithmetic strong enough to encode itself does that. That's the moment the CS people should sit up. Is that right as I've stated it, or does it need a guardrail? I realise there are intuitionistic theories that prove A or not-A for particular A, including for decidable A, so the failure has to attach to the universal claim and not to instances — tell me how to phrase it so I don't get caught out by the one smart question in the room.

5. What to refuse to explain, out loud, and what to name-and-move-on: choice sequences, bar induction and the fan theorem, realizability, Kripke versus Beth semantics, the double-negation translation beyond one sentence, whatever Brouwer's continuity principle is actually doing. My guess is I should say the words so they know the words exist and then decline the details, but I want to know which one of these I'm likely to get a serious question on and should therefore have two honest sentences ready for.

6. One thing for the last slide that tells me whether it landed rather than whether they were entertained. I was going to hand them a short classical proof containing exactly one non-constructive step and ask them to find it and say whether it can be repaired — but I dont have a good candidate: I want something short, whose suspicious step looks innocent, and where the answer to "can it be repaired?" is interestingly different from the near-identical variant next to it. Suggest the pair, or suggest a different check if a written one would just test pattern-matching.
