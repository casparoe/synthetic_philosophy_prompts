I've been chewing on the cluelessness objection to consequentialism and I keep suspecting there are two different problems hiding under the same label, but I can't tell whether the split is real or whether I'm just redescribing ordinary empirical uncertainty with more words. So: can you explain the difference to me — and equally importantly, tell me where my distinction breaks down? I already get the basic objection, so don't spend time re-establishing that our actions have unpredictable flow-through effects. Assume that much.

Here's the split I have in mind. Call it *horizontal* cluelessness: you fix the depth of the evaluation — say, everything that happens in the next two centuries — and the difficulty is just that you have no idea which of an enormous number of branches your action pushes you into. Your uncertainty is over the which-world question, with the time-scale of the aggregation held fixed. That looks to me like plain empirical uncertainty with a very wide, very hard-to-estimate distribution, and I sort of understand why people say it isn't a distinctive normative problem: a fat-tailed distribution over outcomes is still a distribution, and taking expectations is still advice.

Call it *vertical* cluelessness: you don't know how deep the evaluation is supposed to go at all. Whether the effects that matter mostly wash out within a few centuries, or whether the whole decision is effectively about what happens over the next million years, or whether there's any cutoff to speak of. Here it feels like you're not uncertain about which world you're in — you're uncertain about which quantity you're meant to be taking the expectation of, and "just take the expectation" doesn't even seem to be well-formed advice until that's settled. My vague sense is that this isn't the same animal, because you can't resolve it by getting more evidence about the world; it's upstream of the probability assignment rather than inside it.

The thing that made me think about it, and the part I most want your take on: consider the standard move that your ignorance about the long run is roughly symmetric, so the far-future terms wash and you should act on the near-term reasons you can actually see. Take a concrete pair: option A is a large, system-level intervention (something like pushing on the trajectory of a technology), option B is small and local (something like bednets in one district). If the size of your error about flow-through effects scales with the size of the intervention, then symmetric *proportional* ignorance doesn't give you symmetric *absolute* error — and the wash argument, as I understand it, was always a claim about absolute contributions to expected value. So it looks like the symmetry response quietly penalises big interventions, and the bigger the gap in scale between A and B, the more it penalises them, which is a systematic bias rather than a wash. Is that actually right? Does it threaten the cancellation argument, or does it collapse into something boring like "higher-variance options have higher-variance consequences, obviously"?

Three things I'd like, in rough order of how much I care:

1. Whether the horizontal/vertical split is doing real work, or whether vertical cluelessness just reduces to horizontal cluelessness with a mixture over possible horizons — in which case say so and show me the reduction, because I can't see it.
2. If it is real, whether the usual responses people give to cluelessness (symmetry arguments, "fall back on commonsense duty", appeals to the verdicts of near-term reasoning, abandoning expected-value maximisation for something more robust to imprecise credences) are aimed at one of these rather than the other, and whether any of them are aimed at both.
3. The mechanism, in the A-versus-B case above, of whether scale-dependence really is a problem for the wash argument or whether I've made an arithmetic slip somewhere.

Please explain from the armchair — I'm not looking for a survey of who has said what, and I don't want citations. What I want is the conceptual mechanics: what the disagreement is actually about, which step is load-bearing, and where my framing is confused. Concreteness helps me more than generality, so if a toy model or a two-period example makes the point cleaner, use that instead of abstract talk. Definitely flag it if you think I'm smuggling in an assumption I haven't noticed.
