I'm doing one of those self-paced rational choice courses (Open University style, so most of it is me arguing with the notes) and I also help run a forecasting thing at work: we all put probabilities on binary questions — will the data migration finish by end of quarter, will the client renew — everyone gets scored with the Brier score, and there's a leaderboard that goes up on the wall which is apparently taken quite serious by people's managers. A mate on another team says their version is a spreadsheet nobody trusts and that half of them just copy the crowd estimate. There's a couple of things about our setup that I can't tell are deep or just sloppy.

The first is that the only justification I've ever seen for using a proper scoring rule is a coherence one: if the rule is strictly proper, then you maximise your expected score by reporting your honest probabilities, so the story goes that anyone who wants to do well is forced to be probabilistically coherent and to treat the score as something to be maximised in expectation. But isn't that the same shape as the Dutch book argument for having coherent credences in the first place, and doesn't it presuppose the very conclusion — that rationality just is expected-utility maximisation — rather than establishing it? So: what does our leaderboard actually presuppose? Does ranking people by a strictly proper rule commit us to EU maximisation as the theory of rational choice, or could someone who thinks EU is wrong (Allais-type preferences, or a view where risk or regret matters in its own right) still defend the practice with a different story? And can you separate out what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with? Some of us also do continuous questions — how many tickets will land this week — and those get scored with the CRPS, which I gather is the same idea generalised; I'd like to know if that changes anything about the commitments.

Second thing: purely out of curiosity I re-ran last quarter's binary leaderboard under the log score instead, and the top three shuffled, and one guy who was comfortably mid-table is now nearly last because he put 0.99 on something that didn't happen. Both rules are strictly proper, both are supposed to be "the" measure of forecast accuracy, and they disagree about who is good. Does treating them as interchangeable presuppose something false? Is there a fact of the matter about which of two forecasters was better, or does the choice of rule just smuggle in a view about which error is worse — hedging versus overconfidence? Relatedly, there are three changes being floated for next quarter: weight each question by how much the actual business decision hinged on the answer; only score questions that resolved Yes, since "nobody cares about the Nos"; and drop each person's worst question before averaging, like they do in gymnastics. I have a feeling that at least two of those break the whole point of using a proper rule. Which ones, why, and what would we have to change to get the honest-reporting property back?

Please be properly opinionated rather than giving me a survey of the options. Tell me what you think the practice is committed to, which bits are genuinely presupposed and which are only convenient, whether the Brier-versus-log shuffle shows anything philosophically interesting or is just noise, and what you'd actually do with our leaderboard if it were yours. Where you're going out on a limb, say so; where the standard view is settled, say that too; and flag the bits you're unsure about instead of hedging everything equally.
