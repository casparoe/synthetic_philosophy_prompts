My office mate has been sitting on the edge of my desk all week with an argument I can't dent, and I've ran out of ways to push back. He says it shows that nobody knows anything. I obviously don't believe that, but I also can't find the step I'd reject.

Setup: S knows some ordinary empirical proposition p in a case α — a hand in front of her, good light, no tricks. Hold fixed her total evidence E and the method M by which she forms the belief.

(1) Safety. S knows p in α only if there is some margin around α such that in every case inside that margin where S believes p by M, p is true.

(2) The lamp principle. If S knows p, then S is in a position to know, just by reflecting on the grounds she already has, that she knows p. He insists this is only the weak core of the KK principle: not that S actually believes she knows, just that her grounds put knowledge of her own knowledge within reach.

(3) Safety applies to the second-order proposition too. So if S is in a position to know that she knows p in α, then the belief she would form by that reflection — call its method M* — is safe: there is a margin around α within which, in every case where S believes she knows p by M*, she does know p.

(4) No halos. For any case α in which S knows p on E, there are cases arbitrarily close to α — same total evidence E, same belief, same methods M and M*, differing only in some external respect that E doesn't register — in which S does not know p. His example is a clock that reads three and is right, plus a sequence of neighbouring cases in which the clock is broken in ways that make no difference to anything she can tell, converging on the good case.

(5) In those cases, since E is the same, S still believes p by M and still believes she knows p by M*. So there is no margin around α throughout which the second-order belief comes out true.

(6) By (3), S is not in a position to know that she knows p in α.

(7) By (2), S does not know p in α. And p and α were arbitrary — run it again on any knowledge claim you like — so nobody knows anything.

What makes me suspicious is that if I swap 'knows' for 'is justified in believing', the whole thing fizzels: hold E fixed and justification is fixed, so (4) is simply false and there's no bad neighbour to point at. So the argument seems to need knowledge to be an externalist notion before it even gets going. My office mate says that's not a defect, that's the upshot — externalism and luminosity can't both be true, and since safety is what everyone buys these days, luminosity is what has to go, and good riddance to it.

I'd rather keep safety than the lamp principle, but I want to know what I'm actually paying, and whether (4) is the thing to attack instead. Is there a version of 'knowledge has a halo' that survives, or does positing one just move the bump in the carpet somewhere else? If the lamp principle goes, does full KK go with it, and what happens to the thought that knowing p is what licences you to flat-out assert p? And is the step from (1) to (3) as innocent as he makes it look — is safety being asked to do the same work at the second order, or a different job?
