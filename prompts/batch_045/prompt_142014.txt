I'd like a case analysis. The episode is the live-and-let-live system on the Western Front in the First World War. Take the following as given: in quiet sectors, small units facing each other across no man's land largely stopped trying to kill one another, settled into predictable routines — harassing fire at fixed hours, quiet at mealtimes and during burial parties and working parties — and punished units that broke the routines, including newly arrived ones. Take three further features as given: the units holding a given sector were rotated every few days, so the pair of units facing each other was not stable over the long run; the restraint was partial, covering small-arms fire and nuisance shelling but not artillery preparation or set-piece attacks; and both high commands disapproved of it and ordered raids and continuous harassment specifically to break it up.

Analyze this through the lens of the iterated prisoner's dilemma and the reciprocity-based account of the evolution of cooperation, with a disciplined mapping rather than a gesture at one: who the players are (individual soldiers, sections, battalions, armies, high commands — these yield different games, so say which choice your account needs and what it costs you), what the options and the payoff ordering look like on your preferred specification, and what would count as evidence of reciprocity as opposed to common interest or to compliance with orders.

Then three places where I want the mapping pressed rather than asserted. Rotation: the standard account needs a long shadow of the future, but the units in contact changed every few days — so who bears the cost of teaching a newcomer the local rules, and why is that worth doing for them? Noise: much of the violence was stray or ambiguous, and in the repeated game with implementation error a misread defection starts a retaliation chain — is the observed stability closer to tit-for-tat's fragility under noise or to the repair behaviour of contrite or win-stay-lose-shift strategies, and what pattern in the record would distinguish the two? Partial restraint: the truce covered some kinds of violence and not others; can a single repeated game with one move per period represent that, or does it force a multi-level treatment the standard account does not supply?

Then turn the case back on the theory: is mutual restraint in the trenches really a prisoner's dilemma, or is it closer to a coordination problem in which both sides already prefer quiet to mutual violence — and does that make the episode support for the reciprocity account or a problem for it?

Close with the thing that would change your mind: the single piece of historical evidence, or the single argument about the modelling, that would lead you to abandon the reading you have defended.
