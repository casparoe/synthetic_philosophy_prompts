I got into a long argument at dinner the other week about how prostitution is regulated and I've been going round in circles ever since, so I figured I'd ask here. As far as I understand it there are basically three live models, and I might of got some details wrong: the one usually called the Nordic model (Sweden first, then Norway and several others) where selling sex isn't a crime but buying it and most third-party involvement is; the German/Dutch model where it's a legal, registered, taxed occupation with licensed premises, health requirements and zoning; and the New Zealand one where it's decriminalised more or less like any other job, with ordinary employment and health-and-safety law doing the work instead of a special licensing regime. What I can't get past is that all three say they're doing this for the woman involved and they do almost opposite things: in one the client is a criminal and the person who screens her calls or drives her to appointments is a pimp, in another the client is a customer and that same person is an employee or a small business owner. So what I actually want is what each regime has to be assuming, underneath, about what prostitution is — not the stated reasons, but the thing that would have to be true for the rules to hang together.

The Nordic one is where I get stuck, because it seems to need several things at once. Buying and selling look like two halves of a single transaction, so punishing only one half needs an asymmetry principle, something like that the buyer is using and the seller is being used — but that asymmetry gets applied as if it were built into the transaction rather than being an empirical fact about who has money and options in a given case, since the model still criminalises the client of a very well paid escort who is clearly the more powerful of the two. Then there's the consent bit: her agreement is treated as not morally operative (she isn't punished, she's a victim, she gets exit services and support), while his is fully operative, so the same pair of yeses does different work depending on whose it is, which isn't how consent functions anywhere else in law. And I can't tell whether the model strictly requires that prostitution is violence in itself, or only that under present conditions you can't reliably separate the coerced cases from the chosen ones, or only that criminal law is entitled to express a moral judgement about a transaction nobody is complaining about. Those seem like very different commitments that would all produce the same statute, and I don't know which one is actually load-bearing.

My friend's diagnosis is that it all comes down to one thing: the Nordic model doesn't trust what the woman says about herself, the New Zealand model does, and everything else falls out of that. It feels too quick to me but I can't say why. So: reconstruct what each approach strictly presupposes as opposed to what it merely fits comfortably with, tell me where the rules are philosophically underdetermined in the sense that several rival theories about sex, consent, markets or the point of criminal law would license exactly the same regime, and say concretely what would have to change in each one if you swapped in a rival view. I'm especially interested in how each model handles its own worst case, because that's where I suspect the real commitments show: the woman in Sweden who says truthfully that she chose this and that criminalising her clients has made her poorer and less safe, and the trafficked woman in New Zealand who says, untruthfully and out of fear, that she's fine.
