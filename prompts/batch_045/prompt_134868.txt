I'm 72, retired — I spent most of my career as a computational chemist, which in practice meant writing simulation code and arguing with the sysadmin about compute allocations, and now I go to a little discussion group at the public library on Thursday afternoons. About 14 of us, mostly retirees, a few teachers, one person who comes for the coffee; we get a room with a whiteboard for 90 minutes and nobody has read anything in advance. I'm the one who usually proposes the topic. Next month I want to do something on whether digital minds — simulated people, uploaded brains, whatever you want to call them — would matter morally, and I dont want to stand up and talk for an hour, and I really don't want it to turn into a debate about whether the chatbot on my phone is conscious today.

So: design me a game or structured exercise we can actually run in that room in 90 minutes, with no lecture up front and no jargon until the last fifteen minutes, that makes people run into the problem themselves before anybody names it. My own hobbyhorse, which I hope the activity backs them into rather than me telling them, is what I've started calling the clock-speed problem: if you can run a mind at 1x or 100x, then a single afternoon of wall-clock time contains either an afternoon or years of lived experience, and any moral bookkeeping that counts "lives" or "person-years" is quietly picking one currency and hoping nobody notices. The other thing I want in there is what happens when copies get merged back together — two streams of experience go in, one comes out — because I have no idea whether to count that as a gain, a death, or neither, and I suspect everyone in the room will have a strong feeling about it that they can't quite justify.

Concretely, I'd like: rules and materials (nothing fancy — index cards, the whiteboard, maybe a fixed budget of "compute hours" we hand out at the start), a rough timeline, and some accounting step near the end where the group has to write down a single number for how much welfare the setup produced. I want that to be the moment it breaks. Then a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the philosophical moves: whether what matters is the number of minds or the amount of experienced time, whether making a copy creates welfare or just dilutes a claim, whether merging is a death, whether being able to back someone up makes turning them off less bad, and the awkward one — whether any of it changes if the sims only report distress without there being anything it's like to be them. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which part of the problem the game can't reproduce and I'll just have to say out loud myself. Under 800 words total, please — rules can be bullet points, but no filler.
