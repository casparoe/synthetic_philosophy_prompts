Bit of background, since it probably matters for what I need: I spent thirty-odd years as a meteorologist (mostly numerical weather prediction, latterly ocean-atmosphere coupling), and since retiring I've been going to a fortnightly philosophy evening at the library — mostly retired folk plus two PhD students who are kind enough to humour us. Last week's session was on determinism and free will and it ended in a proper muddle, and the sentence doing all the damage was "deterministic but unpredictable" — or, in the version one of the students prefers, "chaos shows determinism doesn't imply predictability". The trouble is that three people used it to do three quite different jobs and each thought it settled their point. One used it to argue that the failure of ten-day forecasts is no evidence at all for indeterminism in the physics — you can have a perfectly clockwork world and still not know what Tuesday will bring. Another used it the other way round: if determinism is compatible with anything whatsoever happening next, then determinism is an empty, uncheckable metaphysical claim that no working scientist should care about, which seems to me to prove far too much. A third wheeled out the butterfly-flap line that was the title of a talk at a 1972 AAAS meeting, about a wingbeat in Brazil and a tornado in Texas, and seemed to think it licensed something like "everything is connected, so nothing is fixed" — which I suspect is just a non sequitur, but I can't say precisely where it goes wrong. So: I want a menu of the precise things that slogan could be claiming, not a single verdict on it.

Here's what I want pinned down, and the bit of apparatus I've invented to try to get a grip on it myself. In my notes I've been separating three grades of "determinism": (i) the world admits exactly one complete gap-free history — there is a single consistent ledger of everything, whether or not anything could ever read it; (ii) the state at one time fixes the state at the next by a rule you could in principle step through; (iii) the state at one time is *computable* from the state at another by something we could actually build. And three grades of "predictability": getting the actual trajectory within a tolerance you can shrink as far as you like; getting the qualitative outcome right (a storm forms in this region in this window, say) without the details; and getting only the statistics right — the climate rather than the weather. The gadget: imagine I own what I've started calling a decimal tap, an instrument that will hand me one more decimal digit of the present state on demand, but where each further digit takes twice as long (or costs twice as much) as the last one, while my uncertainty about the future grows by a fixed factor with each tick of the clock. It seems to me that whether prediction is possible then depends on which of those two rates is larger, and that this is where the real content of the slogan lives — but whether that's a point about our instruments, about the mathematics, or about the world, I genuinely can't tell, and I can't get the group to see it either.

So what I'd like, laid out as a list I can photocopy for the next session, is this: the distinct precise theses hiding in "deterministic but unpredictable" — different sense of determinism, different sense of predictability, different kind of "imply" (conceptual? epistemic? merely practical?) — and for each, whether it's a platitude nobody would deny, a substantive and defensible claim, or actually false, plus which reading each of my three antagonists needed in order to get their conclusion and whether the slogan really delivers it. Please use the decimal tap (or a better gadget of your own) to separate a reading on which prediction fails for want of data from one on which it fails even for a demon handed the entire infinite decimal expansion at once, since I think that's the distinction the group keeps skating over. And one more thing: I'd be grateful if you could say which readings stay neutral on the free-will question, because that's what the session was nominally about and I suspect none of them touch it. American English in the answer, please, and no reading lists or citations — the library closes at seven and I've no patience for chasing references these days.
