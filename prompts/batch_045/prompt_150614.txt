My uncle Ray died in March and I've somehow agreed to give the main eulogy, which is a bad idea for about three reasons at once.

Ray was a moral philosopher — he worked on responsibility and on what people in that literature call the reactive attitudes — and maybe a third of the room will be his old colleagues and former grad student's, people who will hear every move I make and exchange looks if I get one wrong. Another third is family: his wife of forty-one years, his two daughters, four grandkids. Nobody there is a philosopher and everyone there would like to cry without being lectured at. And then there are four or five people from the memory care unit and the hospice team who only ever knew him at the end.

Here's why it's hard. Ray had frontotemporal dementia for about six years, and he was definately not a nice person to be around for a lot of it. There's one afternoon the family now refers to just as "the afternoon": he sat the grandkids down and told them, in detail and very calmly, that their grandmother had been putting something in his food. It wasn't true. She has never really gotten over it, and she's the one who asked me to speak.

Everyone's instinct, including mine, is to stand up and say: that wasn't him, that was the disease. But I've been reading his stuff to prepare, and I think that's the exact sentence that would make his colleagues wince. As I understand it there's a difference between excusing what someone did — he couldn't help it, he didn't mean it, which leaves him fully one of us — and exempting him, deciding he isn't the sort of thing you have that kind of attitude toward at all and you just manage him. And I think a big part of Ray's career was the thought that the second move is its own kind of death, and that you can't live in it for long anyway. Except we did live in it. For years. My sister has a phrase for it — "the long objective stretch" — and I don't know what to do with it, and I need the speech to do something with it.

The other thing I keep turning over: about eight months before he died he had maybe twenty minutes where he was completely himself (his daughters both say it was unmistakable), and he used a good chunk of it to apologize to my aunt, specifically, for the poisoning business and for some worse stuff before that. He knew exactly what he was apologizing for. She accepted. She wants me to say so. Her younger daughter wants me to keep the whole poisoning thing out of it entirely, and thinks that describing the apology at the end quietly makes the six years count as his, which she thinks is both wrong and cruel.

So, please: write me a eulogy, seven or eight minutes, that doesn't cheat.

- It has to survive the philosophers in the room. No bare "that wasn't really him," and no pretending the six years were just some other guy wearing his face. If the excuse/exemption thing is genuinely what's doing the work here, use it — but invisibly, no jargon, no names.
- It has to let my aunt and the grandkids grieve him as him, and not feel like the funeral mostly turned out to be about the bad years.
- It has to say something honest about that apology — what an apology from a lucid twenty minutes can and can't do about harm done in the years around it, when the person who did it and the person who said sorry are, in some way none of us can make precise, not quite the same subject. I genuinely don't know whether the true thing is that it repaired something, or that it was a beautiful category mistake, or that it was the only twenty minutes in six years that actually belonged to him.
- No "he's at peace now" unless you can make it mean something, and no comfort that depends on him still being somewhere. He'd have hated that and so would half the room.
- Don't resolve it into something tidy. I'd rather it be a bit unfinished and true.

One last thing that might help: he used to say — and I'm paraphrasing him badly — that the point of blaming someone isn't that you've checked some fact about them, it's that you're still treating them as someone you're in a relationship with. If that can hold the whole thing up, I think it should.
