For a grad seminar I'm putting together on Zeno, I need the usual escape routes put into tiers — but ranked by how much actual explaining they do for someone who already gets the setup, not by how respectable or safe they sound. Here are the moves I keep running into, described loosely so we don't argue about whose version is whose:

1. Space and time aren't infinitely divisible; there's a smallest unit, so the number of segments was finite all along.
2. They are infinitely divisible, but slicing a journey doesn't manufacture extra tasks — crossing the whole just consists in crossing the parts, and nothing further has to be done.
3. The infinity is only potential, not actual: infinitely many points, but crossing them isn't completing infinitely many acts.
4. Completing infinitely many tasks is fine in principle (the lamp case and friends); the trouble comes from assuming that finishing a series needs a last member.
5. The runner can't get started at all, because read as "must first complete," the series has no first member.
6. The mistake is modeling motion as states plus transitions; motion is primitive and continuous, and the slicing into instants is what produces the illusion.
7. There's no real problem — "must first complete" smuggles in a causal or normative ordering that doesn't track anything metaphysical, so dissolve it and move on.

Put these in three or four tiers with a sentence each on why it lands where it does. Then the part I actually care about: I made up two variants, and I want to know which moves still have something to say and which go quiet.

Variant A, no infinity anywhere. The course is divided into exactly 10^30 equal segements, and each one has a gate that opens only if the runner deliberately taps it before passing. Finite tasks, finite time. I still feel something Zeno-ish about it. Which moves speak to that, and which were only ever about infinity?

Variant B, reversed ordering. The runner must cover 1/2 the course, then 1/4, then 1/8, and so on, but each piece may only be done after all the shorter ones — so there's no first task, though there is a last one. Which of the moves above were secretly about "no last step" and which about "no first step," and does that split reshuffle the tiers?

Last thing: name the pair among these seven that is really one move in two costumes, and say which of the two costumes is doing the work.
