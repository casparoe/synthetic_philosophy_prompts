I am a high school senior putting together an independent project on personal identity, and I have got stuck on a question that is not really about arguments at all but about what the institutions around us already seem to believe.

The thing that prompted it is a dialogue in the 1981 collection The Mind's I, edited by Douglas Hofstadter and Daniel Dennett. In it the brain of Albert Einstein, which was in fact removed and kept after his death, is sliced and its structure recorded, and the recording is printed as an enormous book: each page lays out a state of the brain and ends with instructions telling you which page to turn to next, depending on what has been said to it. Anyone willing to spend years turning pages can carry on a conversation with the volume, and the suggestion is that the book itself, and not the paper and not the person turning the pages, is what is doing the thinking. I cannot tell whether I believe that. But I noticed that I do not need to settle it in order to ask my question, which is about three things that libraries, publishers, scholars and museums already do, and about what each of them presupposes.

First. Libraries that have microfilmed or scanned a long run of newspapers or journals routinely discard the paper and keep the surrogate; once the content has been captured, the physical object is treated as dispensable, and sometimes as trash. Rare book and manuscript libraries do the precise opposite: they hold the object at fixed temperature and humidity, restrict who may touch it, refuse irreversible treatment, and treat a flawless digital surrogate as no substitute at all, on the grounds that a facsimile is not the book.

Second. Publishers re-set texts constantly. The paperback of a novel contains every word of the hardback and almost never the same pagination, and plain-text electronic editions frequently throw away page structure entirely and keep only the words; none of this is thought to destroy anything. Yet scholars in some fields do the reverse: they cite by an address scheme anchored to one edition from centuries ago, so that a passage can be located in any printing, any translation, any page size, any language. There the reference structure is the one thing held fixed while everything else about the vehicle is allowed to change.

Third. Burning a book is treated as a harm to readers, to a culture, to the memory of a dead author: what is lost is access and content. Nobody treats the burning of the last surviving copy as a harm done to the book. And museums that display preserved human tissue, including sliced brain tissue, treat the tissue as though the person were somehow in it, and its destruction as desecration, even though everything anyone might want to say about that person is recorded elsewhere.

So: what theory of what a book is, and in the third case of what a person is, does each of these practices presuppose? For each one, I want to know which parts strictly require the theory you reconstruct and which merely sit comfortably with it, so that some rival theory would license the same practice just as well; and whether the three are consistent with one another, or whether the digitize-and-discard library quietly holds a view that the rare book room and the anatomy museum reject.

Then the part I actually care about, where I think the answer may be uncomfortable. If the dialogue's view were right, and what makes something a mind is the pattern of transitions rather than the stuff that carries them, then as far as I can tell some quite ordinary acts change their moral description, and I want you to check whether this follows and what would have to change in practice. A re-set edition that keeps every word but renumbers the pages, or an unbound copy whose intact pages are simply bound in the wrong order, would have destroyed something that a word-for-word identity test says is perfectly preserved; so re-typesetting would be a kind of death, while photocopying page for page would be a kind of rescue. A complete scan sitting in an inaccessible preservation archive that nobody will ever run, of the sort libraries maintain deliberately and switch on only after some trigger event, would be a mind in permanent suspension; and closing the book halfway through one of its long answers would be, I think, pausing a person rather than killing one, though something looks wrong about saying that. I would like you to work through these, tell me which follow and which do not, and say which of the three practices would have to change and how, if the view were true.

There is an objection I keep hearing from a classmate, and I want you to deal with it rather than let me choose: that the book is a recipe and not a mind, because it does nothing at all on its own, that the person exists only while someone is executing it, and that the reader or the computer running the scan is therefore the thing that thinks — in which case digitizing is innocent, burning the last copy destroys a score rather than a person, and the whole puzzle evaporates. Does the reconstruction of the practices above support that reply or tell against it? Does it matter that our practices do have fairly settled attitudes toward brains that are temporarily inactive, and toward brains that will never be active again?

Finally, the thing I would find most useful: for the main claims you make, tell me how confident you are in each and single out the one you are least sure of. I would rather have one honest hedge than a confident-sounding answer, and if some part of this is genuinely unsettled or beyond what can be settled from the armchair, I would like you to say so and say why.
