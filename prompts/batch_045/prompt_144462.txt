I never studied maths after school; I got into this stuff through board games and puzzle books, and Ive been writing problems for the regulars to have a go at. Last week I set the one below and got this answer back. I made up both games myself, so you wont find them anywhere — everything you need is in the question. And Im not going to tell you what I think of the answer, because Ive argued myself into a circle about it and I dont want to bias you.

THE QUESTION (20 marks, 10 per part)

Ring Nine: nine empty cells in a ring, numbered 1 to 9 going clockwise. Two players take turns writing their mark in an empty cell; the first player starts; you cant pass. The moment your marks include three cells that are consecutive round the ring (1-2-3, 2-3-4, and so on, up to 8-9-1 and 9-1-2) you win and the game stops. If all nine cells fill up and neither player has three consecutive, its a draw.

(a) Set out the strategy-stealing argument as it applies to Ring Nine. Be explicit about (i) the premise about an extra mark that the argument needs, and (ii) exactly what it lets you conclude about Ring Nine — including what it does not let you conclude.

(b) Ring Nine: Fuse has the same board, the same winning condition and the same order of play, plus one extra rule: the moment you place your fourth mark you lose, even if that fourth mark also completes three consecutive cells. Does the argument from (a) still go through? If not, say precisely which premise fails. Does anything follow from that failure about which player is better off, or does it leave the question open?

THE ANSWER (typed up from a handwritten sheet)

(a) You argue by contradiction. Suppose the second player has a winning strategy — call it S. The first player makes any old first move, it genuinely doesnt matter which, and then plays the rest of the game using S, pretending that first stone isnt his. If S ever tells him to play the cell his extra stone is already sitting on, he just plays some other empty cell instead and carries on as before. The bit that makes this work is that in Ring Nine an extra mark can never hurt you: whether you win depends only on whether your marks contain three consecutive cells, and having more marks can only help you get there — it can never take away a win youve already got. So if S really won for the second player, the first player would win as well, which is impossible. So the second player has no winning strategy in Ring Nine, and the first player can force a win. The argument doesnt tell you which opening move to pick or what the rest of the strategy looks like, which is why people call it non-constructive. You couldnt run this in chess, because there an extra move can be a bad thing and you cant just drop a stone onto the board without changing the position.

(b) In Fuse the argument collapses, because the premise from (a) is false. An extra mark isnt a free gift any more — its a liability. The first player has to place a mark on every one of his turns, so on his fourth turn he has four marks and he loses there and then, while the second player is still on three. So the extra mark is the thing that kills you. And because the premise now fails in the opposite direction, the whole argument runs backwards: by the same reasoning, the second player is the one who can force a win in Fuse. The first player is stuck carrying the extra mark and in this version of the game the extra mark is exactly what loses, so Fuse is a second-player win, and the strategy-stealing argument is what proves it, just with the signs flipped.
