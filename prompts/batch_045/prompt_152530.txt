hey — I do a hands-on session every year at the science-writers conference, 75 minutes, around 20 people, mostly reporters plus a few editors, mixed experience levels, and nobody signed up for a philosophy class. This year I want to do it on the precautionary principle, and I want it to be something they actually do rather than me standing at the front defining it.

The thing I want them to feel is that the precautionary principle isn't really a claim about how much risk to take. It's a claim about who has to prove what. Same dossier, same people, and the outcome flips completely depending on whether the default is "you can go ahead unless somebody produces evidence of harm" or "you can't go ahead until somebody produces evidence of safety." I want them walking out able to spot which default a story has quietly already picked. I've started calling it the default leak, because it leaks out of the reporting and into the grammar: "no evidence that X harms" versus "evidence that X doesn't harm," "can't rule out" versus "has ruled out," "scientists warn" versus "scientists reassure." Readers inherit the default and never find out it was a choice.

Second thing: I want them to get burned by the mirror version — precaution pointed at the status quo instead of at the new thing. Blocking the replacement keeps the old thing in place, and sometimes the old thing is what's doing the damage. If they leave thinking precaution just means "be careful," I blew it.

What I have so far is a fake case (I'm inventing the dossier on purpose, I do not want them litigating real science) and a two-round editorial meeting where each table votes. But I can't get the mechanics to bite:

- I want the two-default thing to land as a discovery, not an instruction. My idea is to slip half the tables a card saying the proposal moves forward unless someone produces evidence of harm, and the other half a card saying it's blocked unless someone produces evidence of safety, without telling them the cards differ — so each table thinks that's just how meetings work. Is that too cute? What happens when they compare notes, and how do I keep it from becoming "you rigged this"?
- The dossier has to be built so both defaults are genuinely live. I don't want the evidence packet deciding it behind my back. What has to be in it, and what has to be conspicuously missing?
- I need somthing with actual costs, not real money but a payoff scheme where blocking and approving both cost you something, plus a clock — a lot of the real force of precaution is that the decision has a deadline and the status quo keeps running while you deliberate.
- Round 2 has to flip it so the risky move is the block. How do I stage that so it doesnt feel like I changed the subject?

Then a final round: same dossier, each table writes a hed under their standard, tables swap, and they have to guess which default produced which hed.

Give me rules, materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened in the room onto the parts of the idea. Tell me what will predictably blow up the first time I run it and how to handle it. And be straight with me about what the game can't do — I'm guessing it can't reproduce whatever makes real precautionary arguments feel heavy instead of procedural (irreversibility? the gap between a risk you can price and one you can't?), so tell me which part I just have to say out loud myself.
