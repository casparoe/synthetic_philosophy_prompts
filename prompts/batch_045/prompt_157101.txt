im in a seminar on consciousness and moral status this term and im writing my final paper on whether "sentience comes in degrees" actually does any work once you get down to the boring end of the scale — the insects, and then the thermostats. Everyone in my reading group agrees a chimpanzee matters and a rock doesn't, and everyone agrees that if sentience is scalar then moral status is scalar too, but the moment anyone tries to say something concrete about, idk, a cricket farm or a building full of networked thermostats, the discussion evaporates into "well, it depends what you mean by sentience." So I want a case that forces the issue instead of dissolving into it.

Here's what I need. Two candidate systems, A and B, built out of the same cheap parts: same number of the same kind of simple control unit, each with a couple of setpoints, some error signal, a bit of local memory — no exotic tech, nothing a lab or a well-funded hobbyist couldn't actually build, and short enough that I can describe it in one paragraph without it turning into science fiction. The only interesting difference between them is architectural. In A the units are wired peer-to-peer with no global broadcast, so all the impressive stuff — flexible trade-offs, learning, navigating around obstacles, what looks from outside like persistent goal pursuit — is emergent and local, and no unit has access to what the others are doing. In B the very same units all feed into, and get modulated by, one globally shared state, so there's a single integrated evaluative state at the centre, but the resulting behaviour is simpler and more rigid. What I want is for the verdicts to come out reversed: whoever thinks sentience requires unity or integration (global workspace flavour, or IIT flavour if you'd rather — tell me which you're using and rewire the case if the first one doesn't cooperate) has to say B has a subject and A doesn't, and so has to give B more moral status even though B is dumber in every behavioural respect; while the scalar gradualist, who adds up how much valenced, goal-directed processing there is and aggregates across the system, has to say A matters more, because there's vastly more of it and it's doing more. The reversal has to fall out of the architecture interacting with what each theory says matters — I don't want it smuggled in by describing the swarm in spooky language, or by stipulating that A's units are each tiny subjects, or by making B secretly a person.

Then, after the scenario: walk through what each view says about each system and why, and be explicit about which structural feature is actually doing the work. My guess is it's integration versus quantity-of-valence, but I'd rather you tell me if it's really something else — temporal depth, whether the states are available to the rest of the system, whether anything is at stake for anyone over time. Tell me what happens if I remove that feature: give A a global broadcast, or strip B's, and does the whole thing collapse back into the boring case where everyone just says the insect-flavoured thing beats the thermostat-flavoured thing? And tell me how the unity person will attack the case — I'm assuming they'll say I've built a mere readout, or a mere crowd, and called it a subject — and whether that reply is any good or just a way of not answering. Last thing: someone who refuses to pick a theory of consciousness and just counts behavioural and physiological markers (nociceptors, analgesic modulation, trade-off behaviour, flexible learning, that kind of list) — would they have anything to say about A versus B at all, or is this precisely the case where their method goes quiet? I'm planning to use the case to argue that "degrees of sentience" is doing less work than people in this literature think, so please flag honestly if it can't bear that weight and only shows that two theories disagree.
