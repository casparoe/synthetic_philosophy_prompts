I am writing a novel in which the narrator, a land-use lawyer in her thirties, spends a winter sorting the unpublished papers of her grandmother — a philosophy teacher born into a Jain family in Gujarat, emigrated in the 1960s, and left at her death with an unfinished essay on many-sided truth. I have drafted the central section of that essay, and I need it to be genuinely arguable, because the narrator is going to quarrel with it across the whole book and I do not want the quarrel to be decorative. So I am asking for comments on the argument as argument, not on the background: I have read my way into the basics of anekāntavāda, syādvāda and nayavāda, and I do not need the doctrine explained to me, nor a bibliography or reading list — I will add whatever references I need later. What I need is to know which of these moves are solid and which are bluff, and to be told plainly when a move is wrong as opposed to merely unfashionable. If you are unsure about some point of doctrine, say that you are unsure and proceed on the assumption that my summary is right; do not guess at textual details.

The section begins with a combinatorial observation. The seven modes of the sevenfold scheme — in some respect a thing is; in some respect it is not; in some respect it both is and is not; in some respect it is ineffable; and the three mixed cases combining ineffability with the first three — are, my character points out, exactly the seven non-empty subsets of a set of three atomic verdicts: holds relative to some standpoint, fails relative to some standpoint, and cannot be specified relative to any standpoint. That count is what you would expect if the three atoms were logically independent. But the usual explanation of the fourth mode is a story about generation: the ineffable is what you are left with when you try to assert is and is-not together in the same respect, and speech cannot carry both at once. If that is really a relation of generation rather than independence, then the ineffable entails the conjunction of the other two, the combinations collapse to three rather than seven, and the canonical number is unexplained. Her claim is that the number seven is therefore evidence that the tradition is tacitly treating ineffability as a third independent verdict — a failure of specification, not a specification of a failure — and that this is the most interesting thing in the scheme and the thing the commentators say least about. She then turns this into an objection she calls the flatness objection. Index the verdicts along a second dimension as well as the first: not only the standpoint from which a thing is described but the time-slice of the thing described. Now two cases that the scheme cannot tell apart come apart: the is and the is-not may differ because one standpoint is applied to two slices, or because two standpoints are applied to one slice. The enumeration records that verdicts differ but not along which dimension they differ, and so, she argues, it cannot distinguish thick many-sidedness — several genuinely non-composing dimensions of relativity — from thin many-sidedness, which is just ordinary relativism with one fat index. Her test: the doctrine is substantive only if some two dimensions of relativity fail to compose into a single standpoint. And her suspicion is that for the standard Jain examples they do compose, since a standpoint fixed by an interest plus an aspect just is another standpoint; what blocks the collapse is precisely the ineffable mode, because a failure of specification is not one more specification and so will not compose with the rest. She applies all this to my narrator's own case, a family dispute over whether a house at the edge of town is theirs — yes from the standpoint of the deed, no from the standpoint of the zoning map, yes-and-no from the standpoint of a promise made by a dying uncle, and so on through seven verdicts that leave my narrator exactly as unable to file a single answer as she was before.

The section ends with the reply my character gives herself, and it is the part I am least sure about. Call it the working standpoint. Deliberation, she concedes, requires ranking verdicts, and the scheme provides no ranking; so anyone who must act adopts a standpoint for the purpose of acting, knowing it is one more standpoint among others and not privileged from nowhere. The honest core of the doctrine, on this reading, is not a logic but a discipline: you may commit, but you may not forget that your commitment is indexed. My narrator's objection, which closes the chapter, is that this concedes everything — the working standpoint now does all the work that the absolutism was supposed to do, and the sevenfold machinery is an expensive way of arriving at a modest piece of advice about intellectual manners.

What I would like from you, under three headings and in under eight hundred words: first, whether the reconstruction of the seven modes and the argument from the number seven to the independence of ineffability is fair, and where exactly it would be attacked by someone who knows the material better than I do; second, the strongest reply available to a sympathetic Jain thinker against the flatness objection — I want the version that would actually make my character pause, not a strawman, and if you think the objection is simply question-begging, say why; third, your honest verdict on the working-standpoint ending — is it the real conclusion of the essay or the point at which the essay quietly gives up, and if the latter, what would have to be added for it to earn its place. Please do not rewrite my prose or summarize the doctrine back to me; comment on the moves, tell me which one is weakest, and if one of them is worthless, say so and tell me what to put in its place.
