I'm a journalist — mostly science and ideas features — and right now I'm working on a piece about those pay-out-of-pocket full-body MRI clinics, where you get scanned head to toe and walk out with a list of incidental findings that nobody would ever have gone looking for otherwise. What keeps bugging me is that nobody at these places seems to have a principled answer to what makes something on that list a disease rather than just a fact about a person's body. A philosopher I interviewed for an earlier story told me the whole dispute splits into naturalist and normativist camps, and I sort of get the slogan version: naturalists say disease is a matter of biological fact, roughly a failure of some part to perform the function it was selected or designed to perform, or a statistically species-atypical level of functioning; normativists say disease is an intrinsically value-laden category, basically whatever we — or the person themselves — take to be a bad condition that medicine ought to deal with. But the moment I try to actually run the two slogans through the cases I'm reporting, they stop helping me at all.

So: could you compare the two accounts carefully, and specifically by testing them against three kinds of case I keep running into? (1) 'enhancement-shaped' variants: a small slice of the population has a trait that is statistically unusual and clearly biological, but on every measure anybody has been able to construct they do better — think of the people who genuinely thrive on four hours of sleep, feel fine, and show no downstream costs we can detect. (2) Near-universal conditions: something almost everyone has past a certain age, or that almost everyone had in the environment the species spent most of its history in, so that 'statistically typical' and 'healthy' come completely apart. (3) Environment-relative failures: the trait works fine in one environment and collapses in another, or vice versa — my biologist friend calls this mismatch and claims a huge share of chronic disease looks like this. For each case I want to know not just what each account says, but why it says it, i.e. which part of the account is doing the work.

And here's the bit I definately can't get straight, which you should treat as the spine of your answer: which account, if either, can make sense of the fact that classifying something changes the thing classified? Once a finding gets a label and a billing code, people get anxious about it, start monitoring themselves, report symptoms they never reported before, get treated, and the 'natural' history of the condition shifts underneath everyone. Can the naturalist absorb that without quietly turning into a normativist? Does the normativist have the resources to say that some findings shouldn't have been labelled in the first place, or does it just have to defer to whatever the relevant community happens to disvalue? If both accounts need patching, describe what the patched version looks like and be concrete about where it still breaks. Please don't give me citations, reading lists or names to drop — I can't chase references down anyway, and I'd rather have the argumentative structure. And feel free to tell me if I've set the whole thing up backwards: my editor says I have, but she won't say how.
