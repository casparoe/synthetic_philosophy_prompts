I am assembling the final handout for my upper-level epistemology seminar, where we have just worked through the paired puzzles of rational belief under known inconsistency: the lottery, in which one justifiably takes each ticket to be a loser while knowing that some ticket is not, and the preface, in which an author justifiably stands behind each of her claims while knowing from her own track record that at least one is false. What I want is a classification instrument rather than a survey of the literature: a decision tree of eight questions with six to eight leaves, plus a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto leaves.

Four constraints on how it is built:

First, generate the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of views. Begin from the distinctions that actually do the work — whether the relevant attitude is gradational or categorical; whether rationality requires that justified belief be closed under a conjunction one knows to be inconsistent; whether the statistical base-rate structure of the lottery does any work that the merely empirical track-record structure of the preface does not; and whether the norm at issue governs belief, assertion, or what one is willing to act on. Then locate the positions actually defended in the space of answer combinations, and mark the combinations nobody occupies, with a word on whether each is empty because incoherent, because unattractive, or merely because untried.

Second, make the questions diagnostic. Each node should be a concrete case or claim a student can answer without knowing what it sorts for; no question may ask which theory she accepts, and none may use the words lottery, preface, closure, threshold, or coherence. Include at least one question that separates views which agree on the lottery but come apart on the preface, and at least three that use cases outside the standard repertoire — I have in mind an inspector who certifies two hundred structures while knowing her department's base rate of error; a mixed case in which the agent both knows a base rate and has checked every item individually; and an asymmetric-stakes variant on which acting on the whole set is cheap while acting on any single member is costly.

Third, the leaves must include the closure-preserving views (down to the skeptical one), the degree or threshold views, the views that distinguish belief from acceptance or from what one will assert, and at least one turning on modal closeness, with one sentence on each leaf's most awkward commitment; flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot separate, and tell me which leaf a student who answers yes to everything lands on.

Fourth, no readings, no citations, and no named authors: label each leaf by its standard descriptive name where there is one, and unnamed where there is not.
