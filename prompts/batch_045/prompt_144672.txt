Here is the question as it was set:

**Question 4 (20 marks).** In weeks 4–5 we read Williamson, *Knowledge and Its Limits* (Oxford, 2000), chapter 4, on the anti-luminosity argument. Let K be the condition: *S knows that the last train has left the platform*. S is standing on the platform. Let α0 … α60 be a series of cases that differ only in how thick the fog is: in α0 the air is perfectly clear and S watches the tail lights recede; in α60 the fog is so dense that S cannot tell whether the train has gone at all; consecutive cases differ by a change in visibility too small for anyone to notice. You may assume throughout that whether S knows the train has left is unaffected by whether she is attending to that question — paying attention does not alter the fog.

(a) State what it is for a condition to be luminous, and state the margin-for-error principle. [4 marks]
(b) Using this series, give the argument for the conclusion that no non-trivial condition is luminous. Make clear where the margin-for-error principle is doing work, what the restriction to non-trivial conditions consists in, and what role is played by the fact that luminosity mentions *being in a position to know* rather than *knowing*. [7 marks]
(c) The KK principle says: if S knows that p, then S knows that S knows that p. A classmate says: "The anti-luminosity argument is irrelevant to KK. Luminosity concerns what S is in a position to know; KK concerns what S actually knows. You can give up luminosity and keep KK." Is the classmate right? [5 marks]
(d) Does the argument show that we are never in a position to know that we know? [4 marks]

And here is the answer I have to mark:

(a) A condition C is luminous iff for every case α, if C obtains in α then in α the subject is in a position to know that C obtains. Being in a position to know is weaker than knowing: it means the subject has the evidence and the capacity and could know without any further investigation, whether or not she actually bothers to reflect. The margin-for-error principle says that knowledge cannot be a fluke of the exact circumstances: if S knows that p in case α, then p holds in every case similar enough to α — there is a margin of nearby cases throughout which p is true.

(b) Suppose for reductio that K is luminous. In α0 the platform is clear and S sees the tail lights go, so K obtains: she knows the train has left. By luminosity, in α0 she is in a position to know that K obtains. Because attending makes no difference to whether K obtains, we may take her to have exercised that position, so that she actually knows that K obtains; the margin-for-error principle then applies to that knowledge, and K must hold throughout a margin around α0. Consecutive cases in the series differ by less than a noticeable change in visibility, so α1 lies within that margin, and K obtains in α1. Now repeat: luminosity at α1 puts her in a position to know that K obtains, hence (by the same step) she knows it, hence K holds throughout a margin around α1, hence K obtains in α2 — and so on up the series. But in α60 the fog is too thick for S to tell whether the train has gone, so K does not obtain. Contradiction. So K is not luminous. Nothing here depended on trains: the reasoning needs only that the condition can be arranged in a gradual series running from a case where it plainly obtains to one where it plainly fails. That is what the restriction to non-trivial conditions consists in. A condition that obtains in every case of the series, or in none, is immune, since the margin-for-error principle then guarantees that the subject is in a position to know it in each case — with no nearby case in which the condition fails, there is nothing left for a margin to protect her against. And knowledge of any empirical matter can be degraded by imperceptible steps, so no such condition is luminous.

(c) The classmate is wrong. KK is stronger than the luminosity of knowledge: it says that whenever S knows p she actually knows that she knows p, whereas the luminosity of knowledge requires only that she be in a position to know that she knows p. Since actually knowing something puts you in a position to know it — it would be very odd to deny that — KK entails that knowledge is luminous. So if the argument in (b) shows that knowledge is not luminous, it shows that KK is false as well: there is a case in which S knows p but is not even in a position to know that she knows p, and KK fails in that case. The distinction the classmate draws is real, but it cuts against him: the more KK demands, the more it is exposed by the argument.

(d) Yes. That is the upshot of the whole thing: whenever S knows p, she is not in a position to know that she knows p, and so she does not know that she knows p either. Knowledge is not transparent to itself — for all S can tell by reflecting, she may know or she may not, and no amount of care settles the question. This is not scepticism about first-order knowledge, since S still knows that the train has left in plenty of cases; but it does mean that second-order knowledge is unavailable in general, and that any philosophy built on the idea that the mind is transparent to itself is in trouble.
