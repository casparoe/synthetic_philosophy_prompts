Write an essay on whether the no-free-lunch theorems supply a genuinely new argument for scepticism about induction, or merely a mathematical restatement of Hume's. Take the following three claims as given; you need not prove them, but you should consider how much philosophical work each of them can do.

(i) Averaged over the class of all computable observation-streams, every predictor does exactly as well as every other, so no purely formal rule enjoys an unconditional advantage.
(ii) For any given computable predictor there exists a computable observation-stream, constructible from that predictor's own source code, on which its forecasts converge to no better than chance; yet this stream is in an intuitive sense "adversarial", since it is defined by reference to the very predictor it defeats.
(iii) An ideal Bayesian predictor that mixes over all computable hypotheses with a simplicity-weighted prior dominates every computable predictor on every computable stream, but cannot be implemented by any computationally feasible agent.

Focus your essay on one question: does the rationality of induction rest on the substantive assumption that our world is not adversarially tailored to our own methods and, if so, can that assumption be justified non-circularly — or does the unimplementability noted in (iii) mean that what survives is a practical predicament rather than an epistemic one? You should also consider, briefly, whether an average over all computable streams is the right baseline from which to judge a predictor at all, and what would make some narrower class the right one. Weigh the strongest considerations on each side and close by defending a definite verdict rather than by splitting the difference.
