Bit of an odd one: I help coach a sixth-form debating squad (volunteer thing, so please keep the language plain), and we've got a capital punishment motion coming up on Saturday. Every single time we run it, someone on the abolition side eventually produces the line "better that ten guilty men escape than that one innocent man suffer", and it always works as a conversation-stopper. The other side will have spent five minutes building a deterrence case, the line gets dropped, and everyone behaves as though deterrence has just left the building. It hasn't, obviously — or at least it isn't obvious to me that it has — but I can't get my debaters to say why in a way that survives a rebuttal.

What I want is a precisification menu, not a verdict. What are the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making? I've had a go at sorting them and I think there's (i) something about where the burden of proof sits, (ii) something about the relative badness of two error types — false acquittals vs false convictions, (iii) something about whether we aggregate risk across a whole penal system or look at one trial at a time, and (iv) something relying on a doing/allowing asymmetry: the state kills the innocent man but merely fails to punish the guilty ones. Someone on our side also said there's a fifth reading I'd completely missed, about who counts as "the innocent", and I think she's right. In a capital context that might mean factually innocent; or convicted only because of some procedural screw-up; or guilty of something real but not of the thing that's supposed to get you executed; or guilty enough by the jurisdiction's own rules but just not death-worthy. Those four don't seem to behave the same way in the argument at all, and my guess is the line is devastating for one or two of them and much weaker for the others, but I can't work out which way round it goes.

A few other things I'd like pinned down while you're at it:

- The number. Is "ten" doing real work, or is any ratio greater than one supposed to do the job? Is it a lexical-priority claim — no quantity of escaping guilty people could ever justify one innocent death — wearing a number as a costume, or an actual exchange rate that we could in principle go off and measure?
- Who it's addressed to. Is it advice to a jury deciding one case, or a constraint on what a legislature may write into a penal code? Because the debater who beat us last time was using something that sounds like jury advice to kill a claim about legislation, and that feels like a slide.
- The escalator problem, which is where I personally keep getting stuck: if the ratio holds for punishment generally and not just for execution, and you apply it to each marginal increase in severity, doesn't it eat every punishment there is? And if it only holds for execution, what's supposed to be special about the jump from life imprisonment to death that isn't just more of the quantity the ratio was already trading in? I tried arguing that the gap between twenty-five years and death is a difference in kind — irreversibility, and the fact that you can compensate someone for years but not for an execution — and I got told the compensation move proves far too much, since we don't let people buy their way out of prison either.
- The one that matters most for Saturday: does the ratio count the deaths that occur because the ten escaped? If deterrence is real then letting ten guilty people walk isn't a free lunch even before we get to the innocent person, and I want to know whether the slogan has a reading on which that cost is inside the calculation and a reading on which it's excluded by construction, and which one my opponent has to be committed to if she wants it to defeat a deterrence argument rather than merely sit beside one.

Can you lay it out as a list of readings, each with what it precisely says, whether it's trivial / defensible-but-contentious / actually false, and whether it gets her the conclusion she's using it for? Please don't bother with citations or reading lists — I've got no library access and I won't be able to check anything. And don't write me a speech either; I need the menu, I'll build the case myself. Sorry if I've mangled the phrasing of the line, you know the one I mean.
