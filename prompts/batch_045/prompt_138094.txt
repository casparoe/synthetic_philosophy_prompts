Hi — I volunteer at a community radio station, and I'm one of three people rewriting our language policy. Right now the whole thing is one line: no slurs, context is irrelevant, you get pulled off air. I keep hitting cases where I don't believe that, and I'd rather figure out what I actually think before trying to write something other people can apply. I'll use labels (the n-word, the f-slur, the slur for traveller/gypsy communities) rather than typing the words out.

My verdicts, going on gut:

1. A news reader reporting that someone spray-painted the n-word on a synagogue — fine, obviously.
2. The same news reader saying the whole word in that report when the n-word would have done, apparently just because they like saying it — not fine. I'm a bit surprised I think this, since nothing about the content of what they said changed.
3. A DJ reading out a listener's letter: he shouted [slur] at me on the bus — fine.
4. Playing the original recording of an old track that has the n-word in it — fine, and I don't want it bleeped.
5. A white presenter in the studio singing along to the reclaimed -a form on a track by a Black artist — not fine. This is the pair I can't reconcile: in 4 and 5 nobody is being addressed, nobody is expressing contempt, the words on the record are identical, and I still land in different places.
6. Two Black callers using the -a form to each other affectionately, on air — fine.
7. A white caller using it to a Black caller he's friendly with, imitating 6 exactly — not fine, and not fine by a mile.
8. A gay comedian calling himself the f-slur in his own set — fine. A straight comedian calling that same comic the f-slur in what he insists is affection — not fine.
9. A 12-year-old caller who repeats a slur they heard in a song and clearly has no idea what it means — I don't want to say fine, and I don't want to treat it like 7. It feels like a different thing: not an insult but a mess that needs cleaning up.
10. An exchange student who uses the ordinary word in their language for Roma people, which happens to be the slur in English — same shape as 9 for me: not fine, but a correction, not a ban.
11. A bigot shouts a South Asian slur at someone he's mistaken for South Asian, who's actually Lebanese — I still count that as slurring, and I think it wrongs the person he shouted at, even though they aren't in the group the word targets.
12. Someone typing slurs into a private group chat of fellow bigots, where nobody from any targeted group ever sees it — I still want it to count, and I still want us to be able to kick someone off the schedule for it, even though nobody was addressed and nobody was hurt.
13. A British DJ using spaz to mean clumsy, on a show streamed mostly to the UK but also to the US — the one I go back and forth on. Some days: it's just his dialect, he's insulting nobody. Other days: we're on the internet, and for a chunk of the audience it lands as a slur.
14. Someone using an old slur for the Welsh that I'd bet 90% of the audience has never heard — I basically don't care, which worries me, because on any theory I can think of its the same kind of word as the others.
15. A Black caller using a crude term for white people — not fine under our rules, but I don't think it's the same kind of thing as an anti-Black slur, and I can't say why without either appealing to power and history (which then makes the wrong in the other cases depend on facts about society rather than about the utterance) or just stipulating it.

So: is there one principle that gets these? Or am I obviously tracking three different questions — is that a slur, was it wrong, should we sanction it — and fooling myself by asking only one? Give me the principle, or the smallest set of them, stated precisely enough that I could actually apply it on a Tuesday night, and tell me which of my cases forces each clause. Then tell me which verdicts it can't keep: I'd rather lose 14 than 5, and I'd rather lose 13 in either direction than lose 11, but tell me if that ordering is even coherent. And run it on three I don't have verdicts for: (a) a straight actor playing a gay character in a period drama saying the f-slur in character, in our drama slot; (b) a caller who says the n-word in the middle of explaining why nobody should say it, unprompted; (c) a caller from the group targeted by slur S using S with real contempt about another member of that group.

Also: if the honest answer is that the one-line no-context rule is the right rule for a station even though it's wrong about the phenomenon, say that, and say what the gap between the two is doing.
