My friend and I spent something like two hours last night arguing about whether being dead is bad for the person who dies, and we got nowhere — not because we disagreed about everything, but because we kept hurling examples at each other and neither of us could say what we were sorting them by. He's got the old Epicurus thing lodged in his head: death is nothing to us, when death is there you aren't, so there's nobody left for it to be bad for. He also kept pushing the symmetry point — if the stretch after you die isn't a misfortune for you, then neither is the stretch before you were born — and he says my refusal to swallow that is just fear of dying wearing a philosophy costume. I said the asymmetry is obvious and nobody thinks it's a misfortune for me that I wasn't born in 1750, and he said that I can't just assert the asymmetry, and around then we went in a circle for forty minutes.

So here are my verdicts, assuming every death in the list is sudden, painless, and completely unforeseen, so that the process of dying and the fear beforehand can't do any work. A 32-year-old shot in the back of the head by a sniper mid-sentence, with everything going well: very bad, one of the worst things that can happen to someone. The exact same death at 93, projects finished, ready to be done: barely bad, maybe not bad at all. A composer who dies an hour after finishing the piece she's spent her life on, content: much less bad for her than dying an hour before finishing it, even though the quantity of life lost is identical. A two-and-a-half-year-old who wanders into a pool: very bad, and worse for her than the 93-year-old, even though she has no plans or ambitions or future-directed desires yet — and this is the case my friend says wrecks me if I go desire-based. A man who dies believing his marriage and his work are both fine, when in fact both collapsed years earlier and everyone hid it: something about this seems worse for him, even though nothing happens to him after he dies, and my pull here is weaker than in the sniper case. A man whose remaining decade would have been mostly miserable — untreatable pain, nothing good coming — but who desperately wants to keep going: I say death is bad for him, and my friend says I've quietly switched from counting goods to counting desires and can't help myself to both. Someone who lives entirely in the present, no projects, no ambitions, no desires about the future at all, dead at 45 out of nowhere: I lean toward still bad, but with way less confidence and no idea why. A woman who dies believing her life's central project succeeded when it actually failed years before and her friends staged the evidence: I genuinely don't know whether death is worse for her because the decade she experienced as good wasn't. Dying six months before a cure is announced versus six days before it: I feel the second is worse, but I can't tell if that's just sentimentality about near-misses. And finally, someone who dies and whose organs save three lives, versus someone who thinks about it, decides they'd want to die for a cause, and then shoves a kid out of the way of a truck: I say the organ case does nothing to cancel the badness, and the truck case maybe reduces it a bit, which I admit feels inconsistent.

What I want is the principle underneath, if there is one. I tried writing down something like: death is bad for a person to the extent that it deprives them of the good life they would otherwise have had, with the goods counted impersonally rather than by what they happened to want — and my friend instantly said that gives the wrong answer on the miserable-decade man and makes the composer case come out as barely a loss at all, which it isn't. So: is there a single account that captures these verdicts, or is it really two or three accounts that I'm sliding between and need to choose among? Tell me which of my verdicts any candidate forces me to drop and why I should drop that one rather than the principle, state the principle precisely enough that I can test it, and then run it on a case I haven't listed — say, someone who dies young and would have gone on to have a life that was objectively excellent but that they'd have found miserable — and tell me what it says. Also, honestly: are my friend and I even disagreeing about the same question, or is he answering something like whether it's rational to fear death, or whether anything can be bad for someone who doesn't exist, while I'm answering something else entirely?
