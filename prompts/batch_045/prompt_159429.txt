Two people in my philosophy-of-science reading group — Marta, a physics PhD student, and Owen, who is writing about thought experiments in science — spent a whole session arguing about whether a thought experiment can yield knowledge that isn't already packed into its premises. They built their own example to fight over.

A small ring rests on a frictionless horizontal table, pulled by identical springs stretched to the same tension. With three springs pulling 120° apart, the arrangement is unchanged by a 120° rotation, so no direction is privileged and the ring stays put. With two of them pulling 120° apart, the setup is unchanged by the reflection that swaps the springs, so any motion must be along the bisector — and if forces compose vectorially, the acceleration is exactly what one spring alone would produce: two springs, one spring's worth of pull.

(1) Marta: That result is knowledge, and none of it was measured. One plus one equals one falls out of symmetry plus the fact that a force is cancelled by an equal opposite one; anyone who grants those can see it without touching the table. If thought experiments are only picturesque arguments whose content is nothing but empirical premises, explain how a quantitative prediction dropped out of symmetry.

(2) Owen: Because your premises are empirical, and you assumed more than you said. No direction is privileged is a claim about the isotropy of space; equal opposites cancel is a claim about force. Both come from experience. You also assumed that two springs have one definite combined effect depending only on the two of them, that this combined effect scales when both tensions are scaled together, and that only one magnitude cancels a given force. Remove any one of those and the result no longer follows. Symmetry hands you the direction, not the magnitude.

(3) Marta: I'll grant the premises have empirical content — every argument's do. The point is that the step from premises to conclusion isn't empirical. It is seen a priori, and it corrects a strong untutored intuition, since nearly everyone guesses that two springs must out-pull one. So a thought experiment can change what a reasonable person believes without any new data. If that isn't yielding knowledge, then nothing but measurement is, and that is not a position anyone in the room holds.

(4) Owen: Then I grant the mechanism and withdraw the mystery: thought experiments are superb at making visible the consequences of commitments you already have. But that means they carry no independent authority, and the claim that they yield knowledge of nature reduces to the claim that valid inference preserves truth. The live question is whether a thought experiment ever supplies a premise instead of unpacking one — and your own case answers it: someone who has not accepted that force composition is homogeneous is not moved by the picture at all, and nothing in the picture forces them to accept it. Its power is entirely borrowed.

I was the only other person there, they asked me to settle it, and I genuinely could not. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the four contributions from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating, and then say what — if anything — both of them are missing about how thought experiments earn their keep, and whether their example shows something general or is an unrepresentative case. End with a final line of the form \answer{...} naming who is more right overall: Marta, Owen, or Neither.
