In *A Treatise on Probability* (1921), Keynes distinguishes the probability of an argument from its weight. Roughly, additional relevant evidence may raise or lower the probability of the conclusion, but it always increases the weight, because weight tracks the growing balance of relevant knowledge against relevant ignorance rather than the balance of favourable against unfavourable evidence. In his later economic writings, especially *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money*, he argues that expectations about long-term investment are beset by uncertainty, that numerical probabilities are often unavailable, and that agents' confidence in their forecasts is fragile, which helps to explain phenomena such as liquidity preference.

I should like you to explain a conceptual distinction that I think is needed to connect these two bodies of thought. Please suppose that we separate the Keynesian weight of an argument into two components. The first is *first-order weight*: the extent to which accumulated evidence enlarges our relevant knowledge and reduces relevant ignorance concerning a given hypothesis, exactly in Keynes's sense. The second is *meta-weight*: a stipulative notion for this discussion, meaning the weight of the argument that the evidential situation itself is stable and that our evidence base is not liable to be undermined by unknown unknowns, structural breaks, or the later discovery that apparently relevant evidence was misleading. Two agents might thus assign the very same first-order weight to, say, the proposition that a new railway will be profitable over thirty years, while differing sharply in meta-weight: one has good reason to think that the statistical and institutional environment will remain broadly continuous, the other has equally good reason to think that the environment is prone to abrupt, unmeasurable change.

Please explain, in a way that goes beyond repeating the familiar Keynesian point that many economic expectations involve unmeasurable uncertainty rather than measurable risk, whether Keynes's concept of weight can already accommodate meta-weight, or whether the later notion of confidence in long-term expectation should be understood as tracking meta-weight rather than first-order weight. More concretely, in the railway case, should the agent with lower meta-weight be described, in Keynesian terms, as having a lower weight of argument, a lower probability, or merely a lower degree of confidence? And does the strength of that agent's liquidity preference depend on first-order weight, meta-weight, or some further factor?
