I'm a PhD student in history finishing a comparative chapter on state formation and fiscal capacity in the early modern period, and after two years of arguing with a friend who works on Ottoman tax registers I find I have drifted into a position I can no longer see how to test. Call it the parity thesis: for any explanation of an early-modern global outcome that leans on some putative European distinctiveness — the competitive states system, chartered companies and the joint-stock form, particular property-rights regimes, colonial extraction, the Atlantic slave trade's role in capital accumulation — there exists an account of that same outcome centered on a non-European polity of the same period (Qing, Mughal, Ottoman, Safavid, Tokugawa, or a regional network rather than a state at all) that is no worse supported by the surviving evidence. On this view the appearance of European uniqueness is mostly an artifact of which archives were kept, catalogued, and translated, and of which comparisons get drawn by default, so that what is usually called Eurocentrism is a defect in narrative framing rather than in explanatory content. I have tried to make the thesis testable by attaching three diagnostics to it. The substitution test: take the European feature doing the explanatory work, swap in the closest available non-European analogue, and ask whether the resulting account is actually less well supported rather than merely less familiar — if it isn't, the feature was doing narrative work all along. The reversal test: take a causal arrow that conventionally runs from Europe outward, such as silver from the Americas reshaping Asian monetary systems or European company demand reshaping Indian textile production, and ask whether the evidence supports reversing it or making it reciprocal; count how often the reversal survives. The addition test: when genuinely non-European source bases are mobilized — Ottoman cadastral surveys, Qing granary and price records, Persian and Arabic chronicles, Swahili-coast and Gujarati commercial papers — does the explanation's payoff change, or does the new material only add local color to a story whose causal shape was already settled?

What I want from you is a map of the thesis's exposure, and I want it ranked rather than discussed. What would have to be true for the parity thesis to be false? Which findings would be fatal to it, and which would merely be awkward — things I could absorb with a qualification and go on? Please be explicit about which defeaters a committed parity theorist should concede as counting against them, as opposed to ones they would be entitled to dismiss as begging the question (I can already hear the objection that any apparent European distinctiveness is itself an artifact of Eurocentric source selection, and I don't think I should be allowed to use that move on every occasion). I am especially interested in failure modes internal to the thesis rather than external attacks: does it collapse into a rival position, for instance into the trivial claim that all large historical outcomes are overdetermined, or into a merely normative claim about whose stories deserve to be told rather than a claim about what caused what — and does it have consequences I should find unacceptable? One worries me in particular: if every center is equally adequate, then a resolutely Europe-centered narrative of, say, industrialization is equally adequate too, which is precisely the thing I adopted the thesis to reject, and I would like to know whether that tension is fatal or whether it can be dissolved by distinguishing explanatory adequacy from explanatory completeness. I would also like a discriminating prediction: what does my view forecast about what historians should find in the archives that a mild pluralist or a defender of some version of the great-divergence story does not forecast?

Two last things, and I'd rather have candor than encouragement. First, I suspect I have built myself an escape hatch: whenever the substitution test fails, I can always reply that the relevant non-European archive was destroyed, dispersed, never created, or remains uncatalogued, and that reply is very hard to defeat but also very hard to confirm. Please tell me honestly whether the thesis has thereby been qualified into unfalsifiability, how much of its content depends on that move, and whether a thesis in historiography that is insulated in this way is in trouble, or whether insulation of that shape is just what you should expect from a claim about a partly lost evidentiary base. Second, here is what I would personally find most persuasive, so weight your ranking accordingly: not cases where the parity framing reads strangely or offends historiographical taste, and not cases where a Europe-centered story is simply more familiar, but cases where parity makes a wrong forward-looking prediction — where it says we should expect to find evidence of non-European causal primacy in some body of records and the records, once examined, show the opposite. If there are such cases, or if there could not be, I want to know which, and why.
