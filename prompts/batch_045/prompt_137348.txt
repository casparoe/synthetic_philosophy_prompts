I work on a small team drafting a position paper on differentiated integration for a national European affairs committee. At our last meeting a colleague ended a long disagreement by saying that "ever closer union" was never a destination but a direction — a vector, not a target — and that on that reading the phrase is perfectly compatible with a formally tiered Union: a vanguard group integrates further in a given policy area, the others join later or not at all, and the overall trajectory is still "closer." I do not think the remark is empty; I do not think it does the work he thinks it does. What I want is a disambiguation I can circulate internally.

The phrase occurs twice in the Treaty on European Union. The preamble reads: "RESOLVED to continue the process of creating an ever closer union among the peoples of Europe, in which decisions are taken as closely as possible to the citizen in accordance with the principle of subsidiarity,". Article 1 reads: "This Treaty marks a new stage in the process of creating an ever closer union among the peoples of Europe, in which decisions are taken as openly as possible and as closely as possible to the citizen." The same Article goes on to say that the Union is one "on which the Member States confer competences to attain objectives they have in common."

Three features of that wording seem to me to be doing unacknowledged work, and I want them handled rather than gestured at: both occurrences speak of a *process*, not an end state; the relata are "the peoples of Europe" rather than the member states; and "closer" appears twice in the same breath with opposite-looking directions, once toward union and once toward the citizen, so it is not obvious that the two uses share a metric.

Please lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses the phrase could be expressing. I suggest organising them along at least these axes, but add readings I have not thought of:

1. Whose union. A union of governments and their executives, or a union of peoples; a single relation between many relata or a set of pairwise relations; and what follows if some pairs grow closer while others drift apart. Can an entity be "closer" on average while particular members are further from one another than before?
2. What closeness measures. Candidate dimensions: the range of matters decided at Union level; how binding and how intrusive Union acts are (unanimity against majority voting, hard law against coordination); uniformity of application against variable geometry; how costly and difficult exit or non-participation is; fiscal capacity and risk-sharing; and the density of a shared public sphere — whether citizens argue with one another across borders about the same things. If closeness is multidimensional, "closer" is at best a partial order, and a "direction" may be undefined, trivially satisfiable by motion along one axis, or silent between incomparable states.
3. What "ever" requires. Continuous increase; non-decrease; a ratchet that forbids any return of competences (in which case: does a subsidiarity-driven repatriation breach the clause?); an asymptote approached but never reached; or a disjunctive reading on which it suffices that something, somewhere, is closer at every time.
4. Its force. Description, prediction, aspiration, duty, side-constraint, or permission — and if a duty, on whom, breachable by what act, and justiciable by whom. What would count as evidence for or against a given reading?

For each reading give a verdict: trivial (true but incapable of bearing weight), substantive and defensible, or false or incoherent. And say explicitly which readings would actually support my colleague's inference — "direction, not destination, therefore a two-speed Union is fine" — and which would undercut it.

Test the readings against four constructed cases, and tell me where they disagree and where a reading returns no verdict at all, since a silent reading is itself a finding for a drafter:

A. No competence changes hands and no budget grows, but a decade of thick cross-border programmes — a common university degree, genuinely transnational news outlets, a European civic-service year — produces a real transnational public sphere.
B. Union competences widen sharply, but every act is adopted by unanimity of national governments, national parliaments hold vetoes, and enforcement stays with national authorities.
C. Nine states integrate deeply on taxation, defence and social policy inside a single institutional framework, with a permanent outer tier and a conditional accession mechanism; members are legally stratified.
D. The Union returns three competences to member states on subsidiarity grounds while gaining two, and one member state uses the withdrawal provision and is readmitted on identical terms.

Two further constraints, because both are live in the paper. First, sovereignty: for each reading, say whether it presupposes that member-state sovereignty is a final and indivisible authority — so that pooling is loss, and "closer" means "less sovereign states" — or a capacity exercisable jointly, so that "closer" can mean more joint capacity without anyone's sovereignty diminishing. Which of those two pictures makes the "direction, not destination" formulation attractive, and is that a reason to distrust the formulation? Second, the democratic deficit: distinguish the reading on which the deficit is a *lag* — decisional closeness outrunning the closeness of a demos — from the reading on which it is a structural feature that grows with decisional closeness itself. Which readings imply that further integration could in principle close the deficit, and which imply that it cannot?

Aim for roughly 1,000 to 1,200 words, numbered entries with short labels, no more than a sentence or two per entry before its verdict. Please argue from the wording and the cases rather than from authority: no case law, no citations, no reading suggestions — my committee will not follow up on any of that, and I need the distinctions themselves.
