I work in health-policy analysis and read moral philosophy alongside it, and I keep hitting a configuration I cannot find discussed anywhere.

The interpersonal case is familiar enough. One person is much better off than another, and the only available policy brings the better-off person down to the other's level while benefiting no one at all. Everyone I put this to says the result is not better in any way, notwithstanding that the gap has gone. I gather there is a standard name for the argument that this is decisive, and for the family of views that dodge it by counting a gain for more the worse off the person who gets it is.

What I cannot find is the same configuration drawn inside a single life over time. Suppose someone's life contains thirty good years at well-being 90 and thirty bad years at well-being 30, and an intervention would lower the good years to 30 while leaving the bad years exactly as they were, with no memory, comparison, or habituation effects and no gain at any point. Is the flattened life better? People who reject the interpersonal case without hesitation split here, and the ones who waver say things like that the spiky life is a worse life, or worse shaped, even while conceding that nothing is gained and a great deal is lost. A second, sharper version: the intervention destroys a hundred units in the good years in order to deliver one unit to the bad ones. Once the weighting is steep enough, the view I described looks committed to it, and because the person who pays and the person who benefits are the same, the usual way of stating the interpersonal objection—no one benefits, and someone is made worse off for nothing—does not seem to grip in the same way.

So: does the intrapersonal version have a name of its own, or is it the same argument? Is the pull I find in the first case actually a different implication of the weighting view, namely that with the lifetime total held fixed an even life beats a spiky one, which levels nothing down at all—and does that leave only the second case as the real analogue? Where does the boundary between the interpersonal and the intrapersonal case lie, and does it move if the good stretch and the bad stretch are psychologically discontinuous, with no memory or continuity of concern between them? A label on its own is no use to me; I want the phenomenon carved: what it is, what it is not, and which nearby argument genuinely fits.
