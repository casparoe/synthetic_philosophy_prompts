I'm a genetic counselor at a cancer-genetics clinic, and a lot of my job is sitting with someone who has to pick between two options where one has a solid published risk number behind it and the other basically doesn't — say a variant of uncertain significance that could turn out harmless or could behave like the known pathogenic one. Patients are nearly always more scared of the blurry number, and we have no principled way to tell when that fear is something to design the decision around and when it's something I should push back on. I read about the Ellsberg paradox years ago and it felt relevant, but I never worked out how to actually use it.

So: give me a procedure I can run in a session — the questions in order, what a decent answer to each looks like, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the decision back to the patient because it's a values call and not a facts one. Twenty minutes, me alone, no statistician, patient signs off at the end.

It has to handle a few things. Patients routinely want the known-risk option in both directions at once — surgery for the uncertain variant, and keeping the organ if the risk turns out low — which no single probability assignment can make sense of; tell me whether flagging that inconsistency is my job, or whether it's fine to leave alone given that nobody is going to run a money pump on someone in a clinic. It also has to seperate two kinds of missingness I think matter: numbers nobody can give me because the variant is too rare to have data, versus numbers that exist somewhere and this particular patient could get by paying for more testing or waiting for a reclassification. If the usual arguments come apart on that distinction, say so; if they don't, say that too and tell me what to do instead. And I want a test for whether the fear is tracking something real — an actual asymmetry in who knows what about this case — or just reacting to a blank where a number should go.

Then run it on three cases: a 38-year-old with a BRCA1 VUS deciding about prophylactic surgery whose mother had the cancer; the same patient after a reclassification to likely pathogenic with a wide interval attached; and a patient who refuses reanalysis because she'd rather not know, then asks me to help her decide on the basis of the ambiguity she chose to keep. I expect it to tell me something unwelcome on at least one of those, so don't soften it — and finish by being straight about what the procedure can't do for me.
