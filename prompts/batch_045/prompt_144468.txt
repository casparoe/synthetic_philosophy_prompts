I'm building the belief-state representation for a bounded automated research agent — something an auditor can query ("how confident is it, and on what basis, that this claim holds?") and that I can also use to predict what it will assert and what bets it will take. The obvious starting point, the standard possible-worlds treatment of knowledge and belief, is useless for this: belief there is closed under logical consequence, so my agent counts as believing every tautology and every consequence of what it believes, and the framework can't distinguish an agent that has actually run the computation from one that hasn't, or one that has a proof from one that has only been told the result. So I want the specification before I go shopping for a formalism: what does a theory of a bounded agent's doxastic state have to deliver to be adequate for my purpose (predicting and auditing what it will say, assert, and accept), which cases must it get right, which distinctions must it respect, and which of the things people demand are genuine desiderata rather than reflexes carried over from ideal-agent theory?

Here is my own working list, and I'd like it critiqued as much as extended. (i) Non-collapse: the theory must be able to represent an agent that believes A and A→B while failing to believe B, and must not force every agent to believe every logical truth. (ii) Graded difficulty: it must distinguish the agent's relation to something trivial from its relation to a claim whose status it couldn't settle in its lifetime, and the difference should be a matter of degree rather than a switch. (iii) Verification versus discovery: belief should not be indexed simply to the length of the shortest certificate from the agent's evidence, because a sentence can have a short certificate that the agent has no way of finding within its budget — so the theory needs resources to mark the gap between "a cheap check exists" and "the agent can locate that check," and to say what happens when it does locate it (and that the resulting confidence then sticks). (iv) Derived versus reported: it must separate belief the agent obtained by its own reasoning from belief that came in on trust, since an auditing framework needs to ask on what basis a claim is held. (v) Hyperintensionality along one dimension: two sentences that are arithmetically equivalent but computationally remote must be able to come apart in the agent's state. (vi) Not hyperintensional along another: the theory must still let an auditor detect the case where the agent will buy a bet on one description of an event and sell the same event under a second description it doesn't recognize as equivalent — an error a better-resourced counterparty can exploit. (vii) Exploitability more generally: it should tell me which departures from probabilistic coherence are merely local, among claims the agent has never brought together, and which are genuinely open to a Dutch book. (viii) Finite representability and tractability: the state must be finitely specifiable and cheap for the theorist to evaluate, and it should not require attributing to the agent beliefs about infinitely many sentences it has never considered. (ix) Introspection: the agent has to be able to report its own confidence without the theory generating an infinite regress of states about states. (x) Revision, not just accumulation: it must say what happens when the agent's procedures turn up a contradiction in what it previously accepted. I already suspect a few of these are in direct tension — in particular that any robust no-arbitrage or coherence condition pulls back toward the omniscience I started out rejecting, and that (v) and (vi) may both be non-negotiable in one sense and jointly unsatisfiable in another — and I'd like you to tell me which pairs genuinely can't be had together, which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories, and which items on my list are really just prejudices of some camp that I should drop.

A concrete constraint: the theories I've been sketching all index belief to some notion of cost, so please say what such a cost-indexed family would have to look like to satisfy the list, or why no indexing by cost can satisfy it. Then, briefly — one line each is fine — score three structural families against whatever list survives: the possible-worlds treatment with closure, approaches that simply drop closure and let the belief set be an arbitrary body of sentences, and impossible-world or awareness-style approaches that keep a worlds-like semantics while giving up that all worlds obey logic. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — whether the full list is jointly satisfiable and, if not, which requirement you would drop and why — then give the reasoning, with a one-line "why this is a requirement" per item and a flag on every pair you think conflicts. No preamble, no restatement of my question, no bibliography and no citations: I want the structure of the problem, not the literature on it.
