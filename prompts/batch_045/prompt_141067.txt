Me and my buddy got into a three-hour argument at a cookout Saturday about whether a country can owe anything for what it did three or four generations back, and we kept going in exactly the same circle, which makes me think there's an actual pattern here and not just two stubborn guys. Here's the shape of it. He'll happily grant that nobody alive now did it, and he'll even grant that plenty of people alive now are better off because of it — the land, the money, the institutions. Then he says: pick one. Either it's inherited guilt, which is incoherent because guilt needs an agent and all the agents are dead, or it's a benefit you never asked for and can't hand back, in which case it's really a redistribution argument in moral costume and you should just say so. Whenever I try to say it's a third thing — something that attaches to the group, which outlives its members and isn't just the sum of what they happen to hold — he pulls out this fork that I definately don't have an answer to: if I hand in my passport, move somewhere else and never pay another cent in, does it go away? If yes, you've made morality depend on a piece of paper, so be honest that you're just a consequentialist about it. If no, you're saying it's in the blood, so own that. Neither horn is where I want to be, and I can't tell which premise is doing the damage. When I said maybe the group itself is the kind of thing that can hold an obligation, he said that's just reifying a collective and asked me what makes it the same group across a hundred years, and I didn't have a good answer to that either.

I've since noticed the same fork in two other places, which is why I think it's one thing and not three. One: a university still sitting on an endowment seeded by a bequest whose terms said the money was only for students of a particular race — donor dead eighty-odd years, the condition was never enforceable and never enforced, the money kept compounding, and nobody now working there had anything to do with accepting it. Two: a union local that inherited a hiring hall and a seniority list built on forty years of excluding people, where the current membership includes people who were themselves excluded until fairly recently and who are net worse off than if the exclusion had never happened. In the second case the benefit story is basically dead — there's no enrichment to hand back — and yet it still feels like something is hanging over the local that isn't just forward-looking "let's do better from now on."

So is there an established name and a worked-out account of the middle thing? I half remember from a class that Jaspers split guilt into criminal, political, moral and metaphysical kinds, I have a vague memory of Arendt saying guilt is always personal while responsibility can be shared, and I know there's a view associated with Iris Marion Young about responsibility for structural injustice that's supposed to be non-culpable, plus lawyers talking about unjust enrichment and making the beneficiary pay — but as I understand them, none of them answer my buddy's fork, because they either lean on the living members being connected to the act in the right kind of way or they quietly turn the whole thing into the benefit story. I'm not after a label, I'd be loosing the point if that's all I got: I want to know what exactly this thing is, which of the three stories it collapses into and how it differs from each, where its edges are, and whether the passport question is even the right test. Does it matter whether the wrong is still operating (who owns the houses now) versus completely over and done? Does it survive the group being conquered and reconstituted under a new name? What about people who joined voluntarily, after the fact, knowing the history? And if there's no established concept for it, coin one and define it tightly enough that I could call a boundary case either way. Please don't give me a reading list or citations — I want the carving, not the sources.
