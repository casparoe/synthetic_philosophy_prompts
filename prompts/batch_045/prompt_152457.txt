Someone in my reading group handed me this and I've been turning it over for a week and I can't find the bad step.

(1) Dutch book theorem: if your credences at a time violate the probability axioms, there's a finite set of small-stakes bets, denominated in utils and priced at your own odds, each of which you'd willingly accept, whose net payoff is negative in every world. So rationality requires probabilism. (Stakes small and utils not money, so neither risk aversion nor diminishing marginal utility is doing any work.)

(2) The loss isn't really the point; the point is that you take each bet freely and lose come what may. So generalize: rationality forbids any credence function or policy under which you're willing to accept a package whose net payoff is negative in every world you count as possible. Call this SLA.

(3) SLA can't be restricted to a single time. Suppose your plan for revising isn't conditionalization. Then a bookie who knows your plan sells you, at t1, a conditional bet on H for each cell E of the evidence you might get — priced at your current p(H|E), and called off if that cell doesn't obtain. At t2, whichever E came up, the bookie buys a bet on H back from you at your new odds. Each transaction is acceptable by your own lights at the moment you make it. If your plan isn't conditionalization, the pair loses in that world. Do this for every cell and it loses in every world. So SLA requires conditionalization — not as a useful heuristic, as a requirement of rationality.

(4) But look at what (3) needed: the cells you can now enumerate have to cover every world you count as possible, and the bookie has to know in advance how you'll move.

(5) Nothing in (2) or (3) confines the cells to evidence you can now describe, or the hypotheses to ones you can now formulate. Whatever you might later treat as evidence, whatever you might later take seriously as a hypothesis, has to be in the book now. Same for the messy cells: being drugged, forgetting what you saw, being talked into believing something false, or a theory nobody has stated yet.

(6) So SLA requires that right now you have a sharp credence for every hypothesis you might ever consider and a conditional credence for every experience you might ever have — including ones you can't now describe, and including ones after which your credences are inconsistent and p(·|E) is undefined or makes everything 1 and 0 at once.

(7) Nobody has that and nobody could. So something above is wrong.

What's annoying is the shape of it. (1) is a theorem. (2) looks like exactly whatever makes (1) bite, so rejecting (2) seems to cost me probabilism along with conditionalization. Rejecting (3) means explaining why the two-time construction fails when it looks like (1) with a semicolon in it. And (4) just seems to be an observation about what (3) needs, not a premise I can give up. A friend says (3) is Newcomb's problem with extra steps and that anyone who shrugs at the second box should shrug at the t2 bet; I think that's a dodge, but I can't say why.

Which step do you give up? For each candidate, tell me what giving it up costs and what exactly that step looked like it was buying. I'd keep (1) and (2) if I could, and I'd like to keep conditionalization being rationally required in the ordinary case — I just don't want it required in the drugged-and-unimagined-theory cases. And if the standard repair is to bolt on an extra assumption (that I'm certain now of what I'll believe later, that the bookie knows my plan, that I won't forget), I want to know where that assumption is supposed to come from, and whether a norm that only binds agents who are predictable is a norm of rationality at all.
