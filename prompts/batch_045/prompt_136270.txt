Write a pantoum — five rhymed quatrains in abab, in which the second and fourth lines of each stanza reappear as the first and third of the next, and the opening line returns as the last — that makes the case for a deflationary theory of truth while avoiding the words true, truth, fact, and correspondence until the closing stanza.

Set the poem in a language governed by a stipulation I want honored: its truth predicate is grammatically restricted to reported speech, so a speaker may call another person's assertion, or the record's, true, but never their own present utterance. In that language the only way to stand behind what you have just said is to say it again. Let the pantoum's recurring lines be that community's mechanism of endorsement: each return of a line is an act of re-assertion, and the poem should let a reader feel both that the return adds nothing — it is the same claim arriving a second time — and that something has nonetheless been done, something a deflationist owes us an account of.

Give the recurring lines concrete, small content — weather, a name, a local particular — rather than abstract paraphrase of the theory, and let the final stanza supply the case the recurring line cannot handle: the speaker wants to endorse a whole body of claims whose contents no longer survive, a half-lost archive or a language partly forgotten, so that the deflationist's equivalence, p is true if and only if p, has no sentence left to expand into. The closing return of the opening line should register at once that the deflationist's point survives — there was never more to truth than the claim itself — and what this language, as stipulated, cannot say.
