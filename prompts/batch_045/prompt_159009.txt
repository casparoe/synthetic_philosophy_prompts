Here's the situation. I'm on the governance committee of our city council (city of roughly 180,000). We have a one-time $52 million state infrastructure grant to allocate and we are completely deadlocked and, frankly, unpopular: the options on the table are a bus rapid transit line, two new library branches, a big expansion of subsidized child care slots, and a homeless services campus with permanent supportive housing. Nobody wants to cast the vote that kills three of the four.

So Councillor Reyes has floated handing the whole decision to a "Grant Assembly": 45 residents picked by lottery, stratified by ward, income band and age, meeting over five weekends, paid a $4,000 stipend with childcare and transit provided, supported by a briefing book written by city staff plus expert witnesses they can question. Output would be a binding decision, no council vote.

The part that's blowing up in committee: Reyes doesn't want a plain lottery. He wants the sampling frame to include a survey-measured "deliberative disposition" score — basically openness to changing your mind plus low affective polarization — with high scorers oversampled. His argument is that a plain lottery gets you people who statistically look like the city but who mostly show up with fixed views and zero interest in the other side, and he says an assembly that is descriptively representative *and* deliberation-proof is just "representative of the city as it is, which is exactly what we don't want, because the city as it is can't decide anything." Two other members, Bhatt and Owusu, hate this. Bhatt says it's a competence filter wearing a lab coat and that it sneaks back in the very thing sortition was supposed to get rid of — selecting the right *kind* of person. Owusu says if you oversample on a psychological trait, the assembly no longer represents *her*, it represents a temperament, and that descriptive representation was the entire moral reason for using a lottery in the first place.

I then had a two-hour argument about all this with my friend Dan last night, who is full-on sortition-pilled, and we got nowhere. His three moves: (1) elections select for ambition and for people who enjoy being in charge, and the lot is the only selection method that doesn't reward wanting power — he also had a whole spiel about the ancient Athenians thinking election was aristocratic and the lot was democratic, which I have not verified and don't care about; (2) the stipend, the weekend schedule, the childcare, the venue are all already filters, there is no neutral baseline, so objecting that the disposition score is a filter is special pleading — the only real question is what you want to filter *for*; (3) he wants single-issue assemblies like this one rather than one body doing everything, well paid, with real staff and real research support, and he uses the word lottocracy for it.

The thing I threw at him that he couldn't answer: the briefing book and the expert witness list are put together by city staff who report to the very council that is offloading the decision. So the council still sets the menu, the framing, the facts in the room and the timeline; the assembly only chooses among options someone else cooked. Which makes this not empowerment but 45 randomly picked residents absorbing the blame for a decision the council didn't want to make. He said that's an objection to *this* version of the assembly, not to sortition, and I said that's very convenient for him, since every actual version is going to be somebody's version.

Still unresolved, and I have to take a position on all of it: whether the assembly's decision should be binding or advisory, whether this is a one-off pilot or permanent machinery, and — the thing I keep circling back to — why the other 179,955 residents should accept being bound by 45 strangers when the whole pitch for elections is that everybody gets a say.
