The question was:

It is often said that the fundamental facts are simply those that are ungrounded, everything else being ultimately grounded in them. Discuss whether this characterisation of fundamentality is adequate. Your answer should distinguish grounding from other dependence relations with which it might be confused, explain what work the notion of a fundamental level is meant to do, and say whether that work can be done given only the formal features standardly attributed to grounding. You are encouraged to illustrate your discussion with a substantive case, for instance Schaffer's priority monism.

One answer runs as follows.

Grounding is the relation of non-causal, constitutive determination that holds between a fact and that in virtue of which it obtains: the fact that the glass is fragile holds in virtue of the fact that its molecules are bonded in such-and-such a way. Three contrasts fix the notion. It is not identity: the fragility is not the bonding, any more than a strike is the picketing. It is not causation: the ground does not precede what it grounds, and the bonding does not bring the fragility about at some later time. And it is not merely modal. Grounding is standardly held to entail necessitation — if the bonding grounds the fragility, then necessarily, if the bonding obtains, the glass is fragile — but the converse fails, since a necessary connection can obtain with nothing thereby being explained: a necessary truth is necessitated by every fact whatever, and the truths of arithmetic may be necessitated by anything at all while remaining ungrounded. Grounding is therefore a modal-plus relation, necessitation being necessary but not sufficient for it.

The formal features usually attributed to grounding are irreflexivity, asymmetry and transitivity, each of which has been challenged; Schaffer has argued that once grounding is treated as a contrastive relation, transitivity in particular is in doubt. Fundamentality is then defined negatively: a fact is fundamental just in case nothing distinct from it grounds it, and everything else is ultimately grounded in the fundamental facts.

Given that definition, the existence of a fundamental level follows directly from the formal features. Grounding is irreflexive and asymmetric, so no fact grounds itself and no two facts ground one another. Take any fact, trace its grounds, and then the grounds of those grounds: since the chain cannot double back on itself, it must come to an end, and what it ends in is by definition fundamental. And since grounding is transitive, the facts at the terminus ground everything downstream of them, so the fundamental facts jointly ground all the others. That is just the layered picture of reality the notion was introduced to secure.

Schaffer's priority monism shows why the notion matters. If the cosmos is prior to its proper parts, the whole is basic and the parts derivative: the cosmos grounds the parts, and by asymmetry the parts do not ground the cosmos. But then the parts are not additional items over and above the whole, and the coherent form of the view is existence monism, the doctrine that strictly speaking there is only the cosmos and that what we call its parts are dependent fragments with no being of their own. That is what makes monism a substantive thesis rather than a verbal one: it is a claim about how many things there are.

One worry remains. If fundamentality is only the absence of a ground, the definition says nothing about what the fundamental facts are like, and an ungrounded but idle fact would count as fundamental. But the worry is misplaced. If grounding is what accounts for everything that is not fundamental, then being ungrounded is all there is to being fundamental, and asking for a positive characterisation is asking for something the notion was never meant to provide.
