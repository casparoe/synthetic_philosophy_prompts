I teach an upper-level metaphysics seminar and I am writing this year's exam. I want one 45-minute question on Thomson's lamp that cannot be answered by reciting the line most of my students have settled on: that the description of the switching sequence fails to determine the lamp's state at the limit time, so there is nothing more to say. That remark is compatible with at least four positions they need to be able to prise apart — that the supertask itself is impossible, that the question as posed is ill-formed, that the lamp has no determinate state at the limit, and that there is a perfectly determinate state which the initial description simply does not entail. I want a question that forces a candidate to take a stand on which of these is right, or on what would decide between them.

I have three devices in mind; build the question around them, drop or reorder them if you see a better route to the same crux.

First, persistence: stipulate that no switching occurs after the limit time, and that the lamp is observed some minutes later to be off. Does that later observation settle its state at the limit? If it does, what becomes of the claim that the question has no answer? If it does not, what exactly blocks the inference?

Second, a quantity that does converge: the proportion of elapsed time the lamp has spent switched on is given by a geometric series and tends to a definite limit, although the state itself does not. Several students will reach for this, or for a half-on reading borrowed from summing the alternating series, and take themselves to have answered the question. Make them say precisely what a convergent time-average establishes and what it leaves untouched.

Third, a completion: the lamp is wired to a controller whose programming says that if the input signal has not settled by the limit time, the lamp defaults to off. Must the candidate say whether this stipulation dissolves the original puzzle, and what it would show if it did — in particular whether the puzzle was ever really about the lamp's state rather than about whether infinitely many tasks can be completed in a finite time?

Please give me the question in clearly marked parts with marks assigned (20 total), a model answer of about 700 words that commits to a verdict rather than hedging, and a marking scheme naming the specific moves that earn top marks and the specific errors that should cost them. The errors I want caught: inferring the impossibility of the supertask from the mere failure of the description to entail an answer; treating the convergent time-average as fixing the state; dismissing the later observation as irrelevant because the state at the limit was already one way or another; and treating the stipulation of a default as a solution rather than as a redescription of the problem. Add any further error you think belongs. Finally, a two-line characterisation of the B-grade answer — the one with the vocabulary in place but the structure missing.
