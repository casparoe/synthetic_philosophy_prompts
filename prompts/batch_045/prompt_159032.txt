I work in demand forecasting — long-horizon capacity planning for a regional utility, the kind of thing where you commit to a number fifteen or twenty years out and then live with it. I taught myself the craft on the job; I have no training in philosophy or economics and I have never taken a class in either. Recently I got into an argument with a friend about Malthus that I have not been able to settle, and I would like it restated in the vocabulary I actually use.

My friend's position, as I understood it: the mechanism was right — population tends to outrun food production, and the corrective arrives as famine, disease, or restraint — but the timing was wrong, because the arithmetic was swamped for a couple of centuries by things nobody in the late 1790s could have priced in: mechanized agriculture, synthetic fertilizer, the green revolution, and then the demographic transition, in which affluence rather than misery turned out to be what slows births. So Malthus was *right but early*. My position: that is not a forecast, it is an escape hatch. If a prediction can be rolled forward every time its date comes due, there is no observation that would have counted against it, and I do not know how to score that.

To get sharper about this I have been using a home-made taxonomy, and I would like it tested. Call it the *latency ledger*. A long-run forecast can fail in three ways, I think: the functional form or the sign is wrong (the mechanism does not exist); the mechanism exists but the exogenous inputs moved differently than assumed (the world did not cooperate); or the mechanism exists but I mis-specified a slow-moving variable, so the relation holds while the date slides. Only the third, it seems to me, licenses *right but early*. Alongside it I use a *pre-registered deferral clause*: written down at forecast time, before any data arrives, it says in advance which observations would justify pushing the horizon out and which would refute the model outright. And a distinction I call *dormant versus dead*: a mechanism whose trigger threshold has not yet been crossed is dormant, one whose governing relation has broken is dead, and these look identical in a residual plot over the sample you happen to have.

Here is what I want. Restate the Malthus dispute in my working vocabulary — walk-forward validation, out-of-sample error, calibration and proper scoring rules, concept drift and regime shift, specification search and the degrees of freedom you spend when you patch a model, loss functions, the target variable, and the difference between a forecast you published and one you only fitted afterward. Apply the latency ledger and the deferral clause to the case, and say plainly which failure mode the record supports. Then give me the residue report: which parts of *right but early* translate cleanly into forecasting terms, which translate only if I distort something, and which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all. Two candidates for residue that I suspect matter: my vocabulary assumes a target variable fixed in advance, and subsistence is not that kind of quantity; and my vocabulary has trouble with forecasts that act on what they predict, since Malthus published advice along with his prediction and a published forecast of scarcity can call forth the capacity that refutes it. For each piece of residue, tell me whether it indicts my forecasting framework or whether it shows that *right but early* was never a well-posed claim to begin with. One more thing to translate if it fits: I have half-remembered reading that some twentieth-century economist argued the causation runs the other way, that population pressure *causes* agricultural innovation rather than famine. Please also say whether my vocabulary can even distinguish a mechanism that was suppressed from one that was reversed.

I cannot look anything up from where I am writing, so please do not point me to sources, authors, or things to read, and if you rely on any particular claim about what Malthus actually wrote or what the historical record shows, flag it as an assumption rather than leaning on it. What I want is the translation and, especially, what it fails to carry.
