I have just come away from a long argument at dinner with an old friend — she writes on early modern state archives, I took the other side — about whether the profession's long turn towards history from below and microhistory has come at the expense of explanation. I had put to her that the state-centred narrative is not a neutral baseline against which microhistory is an exotic supplement, but is itself a selection effect: the archive of princes, councils and chanceries is simply the archive that survived, because states are the organisations that keep archives. She replied that microhistory has the mirror-image problem, and worse: her example was Ginzburg's Friulian miller, who is legible to us only because the Inquisition tried him twice and burned him, and whose cosmology reached us through interrogators who had an interest in what he said. On her view the recoverable subjects of microhistory are drawn from the tail of a distribution, not its centre, and a course built on them teaches students to generalise from the prosecuted, the litigious and the literate.

We eventually wrote a formula on the back of a napkin, and I would like an independent verdict both on the numbers it generates and on whether it is the right shape of thought at all. Her rule: a register's claim on the course equals the share of the causal work it does, multiplied by the size of the error the students would otherwise carry away, divided by a legibility penalty for how far the surviving archive distorts that register.

Suppose, then, that I am rebuilding a twenty-four-lecture undergraduate survey, European Society 1500–1800. The candidate registers are: (1) individual lives in the manner of microhistory; (2) household, village, parish and workshop; (3) institutions and organisations — Church, army, company, bureaucracy; (4) elites, cabinets, diplomacy, legislation and war; (5) transnational and systemic processes of the kind Braudel put at the bottom of his layered account of historical time — climate, demography, trade, disease, the slow structures beneath the event.

Please give me the following, in British English, and without bibliographies, reading lists or citations — I want your own reasoning, not sources:

A number per register. Allocate the twenty-four lectures, as whole numbers summing to twenty-four, and say in a line whether you reached that allocation through the napkin formula or in spite of it. For calibration: my friend guessed four lectures for microhistory, I guessed nine.

The intermediate terms, made visible. For each register give a rating out of ten for the share of causal work it does in this period, and a separate rating out of ten for how badly the archive distorts it — and, because the distortion does not run the same way everywhere, say in a clause which direction it runs: towards the heterodox, towards the propertied, towards the settled, towards the victorious, or somewhere else.

A tier list of the five registers by corrective value alone, meaning how much each changes what a student would otherwise get wrong, ranked in tiers with a sentence per register as to why it lands there. This need not track the lecture allocation, and I am interested in where the two orderings disagree.

Your verdict on the formula. Which register does it most over-reward, which does it most under-reward, and which of its three terms does the most damage? If you think the registers are not rival claimants at all but nested, say that instead — treating them as nested would itself be an answer to the allocation question.

Finally, two pressures. If you had to cut one register from the course entirely, which would it be, and what would the course then be unable to say? And if the same material had to be delivered as a ten-lecture module rather than twenty-four, which registers would lose their claim first, and why does compression favour the ones it does?
