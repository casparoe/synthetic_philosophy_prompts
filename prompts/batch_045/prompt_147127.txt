Next term I am teaching a final-year option on modality and identity, and I am still slightly embarrassed about a departmental work-in-progress session last week at which two colleagues talked past one another in front of the graduate students while I, who was supposed to be chairing, could not work out how to intervene. The paper under discussion defended something in the neighbourhood of the Kripkean claim that a human being has its origin essentially, and the argument spilled over into the break. Owen, who works on modality, opened with the standard thought: had a sperm other than the one that in fact fertilised the egg from which I developed done the fertilising, I would never have existed at all, and the child who resulted would have been a sibling of mine rather than me — the counterfactual history in which my parents conceive a different child is not a history in which I appear with different parents, but a history in which I am simply absent. Miriam, who works in philosophy of medicine, replied that the thesis buys its plausibility from cases in which the whole gamete is swapped, and that once we look at actual reproductive technology the line looks stipulative: in mitochondrial donation the resulting child has nuclear DNA from both intended parents but mitochondrial DNA and egg cytoplasm from a donor, and nobody, least of all the parents, is tempted to say that this is a different child from the one they would otherwise have had. So, she said, origin essentialism owes us an account of which features of the gametes are identity-constituting; answering the nuclear genome is a stipulation, not an argument. Owen accepted this, and said the answer is indeed the nuclear genomes of the two gametes, adding that neither the time nor the place of conception matters: a child conceived by ICSI rather than in vivo, or fertilised twelve hours later than in fact, is plainly the same child.

Miriam then pressed on three fronts. First, the gradualist worry: suppose six base pairs of the very sperm that actually fertilised my mother's egg had been corrected with a base editor before injection; now suppose instead that the fertilising sperm was a different sperm whose genome happens to differ from the actual one at just those six base pairs. If the first case is same origin, same person and the second is a different person altogether, the boundary has to fall somewhere, and there is no non-arbitrary number of base pairs at which it falls. Second, a case she invented for the occasion: suppose the fertilising sperm had been grown in vitro from a cell line derived from my father, with a nuclear genome identical to that of the actual sperm but a wholly different cellular history — the genetic criterion says the resulting child is me, a material or historical criterion says it is not, and she cannot see what could settle which criterion is the right one. Third, and she thought this the strongest point, monozygotic twinning shows that one and the same zygote, from that sperm and that egg, can give rise to two people; so sameness of origin does not secure sameness of individual, and the thesis has to be recast as a de re claim about the relation of originating from rather than as a claim of identity — at which point, she said, the intuition that a swapped gamete would have replaced me loses much of its force. Owen conceded the twinning point but said the thesis was always relational in just that way, and that the rest of Miriam's argument confuses two things: the similarity of origins is a matter of degree and our readiness to call a counterfactual child me may well be vague, but identity is not a matter of degree, and vagueness in our counterfactual description is not indeterminacy in the modal facts. Miriam's last move was that this relocates the thesis to a place no possible evidence could reach, and that it is not an idle relocation either: if a gamete swap means that I never exist, then selecting one embryo rather than another, or editing a zygote, wrongs no one, and a metaphysical thesis that quietly disposes of an ethical question in that fashion ought to have to earn its keep. Owen said that this is simply the non-identity problem, that he is perfectly willing to accept the conclusion, and that the metaphysics is not answerable to the ethics.

I told them we should continue by email and, reading my notes afterwards, I found that I genuinely could not referee the exchange. So I should be grateful if you would do so: go through the moves as actually made, in order, and say of each whether it is clearly mistaken, mistaken, defensible, right, or clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification, and then say who is more right overall. I would also value your view on what both of them may be missing — for instance, whether the question whether the counterfactual child would have been me might admit of no determinate answer at all, whether the dispute is really about identity rather than about which description of a counterfactual situation we should adopt, and whether anything in developmental biology about when an organism begins to be an individual should be allowed to constrain a modal thesis. Please do not trouble yourself with citations or a reading list; I know that literature and can supply references myself. A few hundred words of prose, organised around their moves, would suit me better than a general essay on the necessity of origin.
