I'm writing the confirmation-theory chapter of my dissertation and I want my own position audited before my committee does it for me. Here is roughly where I've landed.

(1) Confirmation is credence-relative: E confirms H for a person just in case, and to the degree that, conditionalizing on E raises that person's credence in H. Bayesian conditionalization is the norm for rational updating, and I don't want to appeal to anything outside the probability calculus.

(2) Old evidence really can confirm, and the problem of old evidence is not a pseudo-problem. The anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion was accepted data decades before general relativity, and its falling out of the theory was a genuine confirmation of it. I don't want the dissolutions that say scientists are never really certain, or that they had forgotten the datum, or that the evidence was never fully digested.

(3) The fix I favor is the surrogate-proposition move: one does not conditionalize on E at all, but on something like the proposition that the theory together with the auxiliaries entails E. Since no working scientist is logically omniscient, that proposition can be news, and learning it can raise credence in the theory.

(4) But I also hold that confirmation is not agent-relative in any substantive sense. Whether E supports H, and how strongly, is a fact about the evidential relation; a solution that makes the size of the confirmation depend on how uncertain a particular person happened to be about a piece of mathematics has, I suspect, located the news in the wrong place.

(5) I also think use-novelty matters: a theory that predicts a datum before anyone goes looking deserves more credit for it than one that was constructed to accommodate a datum already in hand, and this is not merely a fact about the sociology or the rhetoric of science.

(6) One more thing, which I genuinely cannot tell is a discovery or a confusion: when I compute degrees of confirmation for a datum the agent is already certain of, the standard measures come apart violently. The difference measure returns zero, while the likelihood-ratio measure explodes, since certainty about E drives the likelihood under the rival to zero. Sometimes this makes me distrust the measures, and sometimes it makes me distrust the framework.

Please go through these and tell me which are actually in tension, which only look like they are, and which is which. Order what you find by severity rather than listing everything as equally serious, and for each genuine tension say exactly what would have to be given up and what that costs. Two in particular: whether (3) can survive (4), given that (3) seems to need agents to be ignorant in a way that (4) says shouldn't matter; and whether (3), if it works, quietly takes (5) down with it, by making accommodation just as much of a surprise at the level of the entailment as prediction was. If one of my commitments is doing more work than I have noticed, or if two of them have incompatible usual motivations even though nothing forces them apart, say which.
