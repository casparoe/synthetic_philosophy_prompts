I keep going back and forth on whether cliodynamics should be treated as a real forecasting enterprise or as curve-fitting with nicer vocabulary, and what I want is an agenda, not a lit review. Picture a funder with maybe $15–20M over ten years — call it eight to twelve researchers plus data infrastructure — whose actual goal isn't "understanding history in the abstract" but getting better-calibrated, earlier warnings about large-scale political instability in wealthy democracies over the next few decades. Which lines of work should they fund, in what order, and why?

Things I want you to actually engage with instead of gesturing at: the too-few-cycles problem (you get on the order of a dozen roughly independent secular cycles per region, and any model with a handful of free parameters can postdict them, so what would count as a genuine out-of-sample win, and can you design something pre-registration-shaped that isn't theater?); thin invariant mechanisms vs thick region-specific models that fit better and travel worse; whether more coded history actually helps or whether the effective sample size is nailed down by history itself; and the much-discussed call that the US was due for instability in the 2020s — partial win, lucky hit, or postdiction dressed up, and what would the next such call have to look like to be informative?

Rank the lines of work and defend the ordering with explicit criteria — how upstream each is, tractability given no experiments and ~a dozen cycles, neglectedness — and say where those criteria trade off against each other. Then tell me which of the alleged practical payoffs are overrated or don't follow at all: should anyone act on a decade-wide instability warning, what would "acting" even consist of, and does any of this change how much weight to put on institutional-collapse scenarios? Finally, what evidence over the next ten years would move items up or down the list, and is there anything you'd deliberately not fund even though it's the field's most prestigious open problem?
