In the law and clinical practice of assisted reproduction, a gamete donor is normally a legal stranger: consent forms, sealed or identity-release records, and official guidance all treat the intending parents as the child's parents, and the accompanying public message is that the people who raise a child are its real family. Yet the same institutions behave as though genetic origin matters profoundly. Clinics and gamete banks maintain voluntary registers that let donor-conceived people identify genetic half-siblings and sometimes the donor; many regulators cap the number of families a single donor's gametes may serve, expressly to limit the risk of unwitting consanguinity; and when a laboratory fertilizes an egg with the wrong sperm or transfers the wrong embryo, the event is universally described as a catastrophe of identity — the parents are said to be raising someone else's child, not merely to have been wronged by a breach of contract.

Three further features of the same practice make the picture harder to read. First, in mitochondrial replacement techniques a child is created with nuclear DNA from two intending parents and the mitochondrial DNA of an egg donor — a few dozen genes out of tens of thousands — and in the jurisdictions that permit this the contributor of the mitochondria is classed as a donor rather than a parent; so the line between "part of this child's origin" and "not" is not drawn at any genetic contribution whatsoever. Second, no one treats a child conceived from a second sample of the same father's sperm as a different child from the one who would have been conceived from the first, although those are numerically different gametes bearing different genomes. Third, an embryo cryopreserved for decades and transferred later is still described as the same embryo and as the child of the gamete providers, whatever year it comes to be born.

I would like the implicit theory reconstructed. What view of personal identity, and specifically of a person's origin, does this bundle of practices presuppose? Does it presuppose origin essentialism in its strict form — the Kripkean thesis that you could not have originated from a different sperm and egg — or only some weaker variant that tracks parents, or genome, or a particular conception event rather than particular gametes? Please say which parts of the practice strictly require a metaphysical claim and which are merely consistent with several: it may be that the registries serve medical history and consanguinity avoidance, that the mix-up judgments vindicate reproductive autonomy rather than identity, and that nothing here is committed on the philosophical question at all. Two test cases should help: under the strict view, the second-sample case yields a numerically different child, and if the zygote had split into monozygotic twins there may be no answer to which of the two you would have been; yet a clone sharing your genome but originating from a somatic cell is plainly not you. Finally, tell me what would have to change in clinic and legal practice if the strict thesis were adopted, and what would have to change if it were abandoned.
