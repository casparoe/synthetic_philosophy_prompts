I spent more than thirty years in experimental physics and have been reading philosophy seriously only since I retired, so please treat my difficulties as a physicist's rather than a philosopher's.

I have been working through Gödel's modal version of the ontological argument — the notes Dana Scott copied out in 1970 — and I have arrived at a position I cannot get out of, which usually means I have misunderstood something basic.

My understanding is this. The argument is a derivation in higher-order modal logic, in S5, from a small number of axioms about a primitive notion of a positive property: positivity is closed under entailment; for every property exactly one of it and its negation is positive; being God-like is itself positive; necessary existence is positive. From these it follows that a God-like being exists necessarily. The derivation has, I gather, been checked mechanically with automated theorem provers, and it is valid. So, as it seems to me, the conclusion is a theorem of the logic together with those axioms, and the only way to resist it is to reject an axiom. But rejecting an axiom is not answering the argument; it is declining to play, and in my own subject we would not call that a refutation. The familiar objection that existence is not a predicate does not seem even to engage, since the argument does not treat existence as a first-order predicate at all but derives necessary existence from the positivity of necessary existence. A philosopher friend raised a worry about quantification across possible worlds, constant versus varying domains and the Barcan formulas; that struck me as a choice of formal apparatus rather than a substantive commitment, so I set it aside. The remark one often sees — that the argument only shows that if a God-like being is possible then one exists necessarily — likewise seems to me no objection, since the possibility is derived inside the argument rather than assumed.

Two further things puzzle me. First, if all this is right, I cannot see why theists rest their case on anything else, or why the wider discussion of divine existence continues at the level of probabilistic and design considerations, which beside a derivation look like guesswork. Second, the one objection that does seem to engage is the modal collapse. If the axioms entail that whatever is true is necessarily true, then they entail something plainly false: that my having toast for breakfast this morning was necessary. But premises that entail a falsehood are inconsistent, and an inconsistent axiom set admits of no repairs; yet I find people proposing exactly that — restricting positivity, or weakening the modal assumptions, in order to block the collapse. Repairing an inconsistent theory looks to me like a category mistake.

Where am I going wrong? I would rather be corrected plainly than accommodated.

I am not asking for references or suggestions for further reading — I want the reasoning itself, and where I have misused a technical term I would like that stated outright.
