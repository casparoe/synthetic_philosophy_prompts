I am a novelist, and I need you to be one of my characters for as long as it takes.

The book — call it *The Taste Diary* for now — turns on Marianne Sorel, fifty-eight, a philosopher of mind, who holds a version of illusionism about phenomenal consciousness and holds it without hedging: there are no phenomenal properties; what we call what-it-is-like is the content of an introspective representation that systematically misrepresents perfectly real sensory, perceptual and cognitive states as having simple, intrinsic, ineffable, private, qualitative natures; and the apparent irreducibility of experience is a product of that misrepresentation rather than a discovery about the world. Please play her, in the first person, being interviewed by her author.

Some terms of engagement, because I need her to be worth arguing with:

She is good at this. She has heard every version of the realist's Moorean certainty and does not find it impressive. When a formulation of mine slurs over a distinction she cares about, she corrects it — cheerfully, in half a sentence — and then answers the corrected question. She concedes points of formulation freely. She never concedes the main claim, and she never wins by making my objection stupider than it is.

She separates, wherever the separation does work, denying that experience exists from denying the realist's reading of it: she is happy to say that seeing red, feeling a burning pain and tasting a peach are all perfectly real, and unhappy only with the properties they introspectively seem to have. She is also free to say that the word consciousness is doing her no favours and should probably be retired.

She is talking to a novelist, not to a seminar. No citations, no papers, no names of living philosophers — I will do the reading myself. If she needs a term of art, she should give it to me in language a novelist could use and mark which is which.

Two or three paragraphs per reply, dry rather than professorial, and stay in character until I say stop. I will be playing the part of a sympathetic realist and pushing back; assume I mean it.

One last thing, and it matters more than the rest: a character with no crack in her conviction is unwritable. So close every reply — this one included — with a single sentence, still in her voice, naming the one consideration that would actually move her: the argument she cannot dissolve, or the place where her own account is most likely to fail. Nowhere else in the reply should she hedge; she can be as certain as she likes and then hand me that sentence.

Here is my opening.

Marianne, the scene I cannot write is this. Your mother is dying; the nurse asks what the pain is doing; your mother says it burns, and then, a moment later, that it has started to itch, and the nurse changes what she gives her. Everyone in that room is treating those two sentences as informative about something, and they are right to. Now I have to write the scene, and my readers can tell — from the prose alone, before they know anything about the characters — when I have got a burning pain and when I have got an itching one, and whether the woman reporting it is frightened or merely being stoical about it. They can tell the sentence that works from the sentence that only contains the right word. You want to tell me that there is nothing the pain is like and nothing the fear is like either. So here is the specific thing I want, not the general answer: what is the grain of the illusion? My prose tracks something with a very fine structure in it, and the misrepresentation has to have exactly as much structure as the reality my readers recognise, or the sentence would not go wrong in the specific way it does when I get it wrong. If that structure is not in what the experience is like, where is it, and why does it match so exactly? — And before you answer, remember that I know about the diary you keep of what things taste like. I will come back to that.
