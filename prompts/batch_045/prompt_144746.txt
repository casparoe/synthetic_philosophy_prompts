I should begin by telling you what I bring to this, because I want a translation and not an introduction. I spent a little under forty years in physical chemistry, most of it on reaction kinetics in systems held far from equilibrium, and the vocabulary I actually think in is that one: state functions as against path functions, quantities that are conserved as against quantities that merely hold steady, detailed balance and the ways of breaking it, free-energy landscapes and how a catalyst reshapes the trajectories available to a system rather than merely speeding them up, hysteresis and path-dependent memory in the substrate, fluctuations around attractors, absorbing states, coarse-graining and what survives a change of resolution. I am fluent in all of that and comfortable being corrected in it. I have been reading philosophy seriously for about three years, mostly with a small reading group, and I know no Sanskrit; use Pali or Sanskrit terms only where the English genuinely fails, and define them when you do.

Our group has lately been working on karma, and the question that keeps dividing us is whether it is best read as a theory of moral desert. By that I mean the claim that an intentional act leaves a normative remainder — that the agent ought to undergo something in consequence, in rough proportion, and that this is what makes the resulting suffering or good fortune fitting rather than merely explanatory or useful. I want you to restate that claim, as faithfully and as unsentimentally as you can, in the vocabulary I listed above, and then to report on what did not survive.

I am not asking for a neutral survey, and I have two suspicions of my own that I would rather you test than flatter. The first is that the usual picture of karma as a ledger — a balance that intention debits or credits and that later experience settles — is the wrong model, and that what the doctrine needs is instead what I have been calling the landscape model: the act does not add to a store, it deforms the space in which the agent's subsequent behaviour moves, so that karma is a path-dependent modification of a propensity field rather than a quantity to be conserved or balanced. I take the discriminating case to be the tradition's notion of exhaustion at liberation, since a conserved quantity cannot be exhausted except by transfer, whereas a propensity field can flatten, or the agent can leave the space it is defined on, which is what an absorbing state would have to mean here. Tell me whether that is a real structural fit or a pun on the word exhaustion, and tell me which of the two models can make sense of the graded weight the doctrine gives to intention, and which, if either, can make sense of the idea that karmic fruits can be redirected or transferred between persons.

My second suspicion is about the persistence problem. Given that there is no continuing self for the remainder to accrue to, the natural object in my vocabulary is a propagating excitation: continuity of pattern through a substrate without conservation of any substance, rather like a soliton. The image our group keeps returning to is a flame passed from one candle to the next. I want to know whether that is a genuine structural correspondence or only a flattering one, and in particular what it cannot carry. The same question applies to the tradition's repeated refusal to specify the mapping from acts to outcomes in detail, treating the fine grain of how acts mature into results as among the things not to be reasoned out: in my vocabulary that looks either like coarse-graining or like confessing that one does not have the Hamiltonian, and I suspect it is neither, but I want you to say which.

What I most want is the residue, and a verdict on it. State plainly which parts of the desert claim translate into kinetics and thermodynamics without loss, which translate only after you have quietly smuggled in an assumption that is not in either framework, and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all — my expectation is that the strictly normative or fitting-making element is exactly what drops out, and I want you to tell me if that is right. Then take a side on what the residue means: is the failure a defect of a vocabulary built to describe systems, or is it a sign that the retributive reading of karma was always less determinate than it sounds? Argue for one and give the reason you find decisive, and say what would have to be true for you to abandon it. I would rather have a shorter answer that commits than a longer one that catalogues the options.

One request about presentation: write it as continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold and no tables. I print these out and read them on paper, and I want something shaped like a letter rather than a handout.
