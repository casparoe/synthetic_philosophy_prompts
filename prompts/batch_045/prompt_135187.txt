Last weekend I went to a science museum with two friends from my debate club, and near the robotics exhibit there was a panel about whether a machine could ever have a mind. It described something called functionalism, which I had never heard of, and since I am trying to teach myself a bit of philosophy of mind before a summer program I have applied to, I copied the text down. It read roughly like this: Functionalism holds that a mental state is defined not by what it is made of but by the job it does — the state that is caused by bodily damage, that produces the belief that something is wrong, the desire to be out of that state, and, absent anything stronger competing with it, wincing and groaning, is pain, whether it happens to be a human neural state, a silicon state in an android, or something we have not thought of yet. Because the same job can be done by entirely different materials, mental states are said to be multiply realizable, and that is why functionalism displaced the older view that each type of mental state is simply a particular type of brain state. The upshot, the panel said, is that the mind is to the brain as software is to hardware: run the right program and the mental states come along automatically, including the conscious, felt ones, so a computer running a detailed enough simulation of a human brain would really feel things. It added that any creature which behaves as if it were in pain but is wired quite differently from us must be in pain, and that a machine which passes for a person in conversation could not be missing anything, because there is nothing more to having a mind than playing the right role. I have since read a little about the multiple realizability argument associated with Hilary Putnam and about objections involving absent or inverted experience, and the panel now strikes me as too smooth, but I cannot tell which sentences are fine as popular summary and which ones a philosopher would wince at.

Two other things make me unsure. First, my friend says the panel is obviously refuted by cases critics have raised, like a whole population organized to mimic a brain or a gigantic lookup table that reproduces someone's conversational dispositions without any of the internal structure: nobody thinks those are conscious, she says, so functionalism is dead. The panel, meanwhile, seems to imply almost the opposite, and I suspect both are overstating something but I do not know where. Second, for a school project I built two toy simulations and I want to know what functionalism says about them. In the first, call it the Chip Twin, every neuron in someone's brain is replaced one by one with a silicon unit that reproduces that neuron's exact input-output behavior in real time. In the second, call it the Coarse Twin, the system tracks the person only at a coarse grain: over periods of about a minute it passes through the same gross states and produces the same behavior, but millisecond by millisecond its internal states do not line up with the person's at all. Does the functionalist have to say that both of these realize the same mental states as the original person, and does the software-not-hardware framing actually help here, or does it hide a question about how finely the program has to be specified?

Please give me a fair reading of the panel text, but take a definite position rather than laying out the options. Go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what you would accept as accurate, what is a simplification you would let a museum get away with, and what is misleading or simply wrong, saying in each case what you would put in its place if you were rewriting the panel. Then give me a straight verdict on my friend's argument — is the lookup-table style case a refutation, a serious objection that most functionalists try to answer, or something else — and a straight yes or no on each of my two simulations, with the reason. If you are genuinely uncertain about any of it, say so, but still tell me which way you would bet and what would change your mind.
