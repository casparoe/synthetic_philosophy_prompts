I'm a PhD student in philosophy writing a chapter on rational choice under imperfect recall, and I have a variant of the absent-minded driver that I want an independent read on before I build an argument on it. I already have the bibliography, so please don't spend words on literature pointers or on who said what: what I need is the reasoning, the algebra, and verdicts.

**The case.** A driver sets off on a road with exactly three turnings, which are indistinguishable from one another and which he cannot count: at each he either turns off or drives on, and he has no recollection, when he reaches a turning, of whether he has reached one before. Payoffs: turning off at the first leads to a dead end in a bad area (0); at the second it gets him home (4); at the third there is an all-night service station where he can sleep (2); if he drives past all three the road is closed at the frontier and he spends the night in the car (0). He receives no feedback until morning. Assume he can implement any mixture that is independent across turnings — there is a randomization app on the dashboard — and that he is an expected-utility maximizer, with the payoffs in utils, before he sets off.

**What I get (please check it).** For a stationary policy "turn off with probability p at every turning," I get expected value 2p(1−p)(3−p), maximized at p\* = (4−√7)/3 ≈ 0.451, with value (28√7 − 40)/27 ≈ 1.262. The visit-frequency belief over which turning he is at — weights proportional to expected visits, i.e. proportional to 1, 1−p\*, (1−p\*)² — comes out around (0.541, 0.297, 0.163). Against that belief, given that after the current decision he reverts to p\*, I get roughly 1.512 for turning off and roughly 1.512 for driving on: exact indifference. If instead he puts weight 1/3 on each turning, I get 2 for turning off against about 1.07 for driving on, so he turns off for sure at every turning and earns 0. I also think that on the 1/3-each belief a bystander who knows which turning it is can Dutch-book him at his own stated odds — he pays about 0.62 per trip in premiums across his expected 1.85 visits and collects about 0.55 in winnings, for a loss near 0.07 — but I'm least sure of that calculation.

**The thing I actually want explained.** In the standard two-turning version (0 / 4 / 1 for first, second, neither) the ex ante optimum is p = 1/3 with value 4/3, the visit-frequency belief is 3/5 versus 2/5, and the driver is again exactly indifferent at the turning. I get the same coincidence in my three-turning case and in every numerical variant I have tried. Is this a theorem — the ex ante optimal policy is always a fixed point of "optimize against the belief your own policy generates over your own visits" — or an artifact of this very small family of cases, or a corollary of something I'm reinventing? If it is a theorem, what is the essential ingredient, and can you describe a case where it fails, using machinery no more exotic than what's above? If it can't fail, tell me what that would show about whether the ex ante and interim standpoints really come apart in imperfect recall.

**What I want you to rank.** Six candidate norms for what the driver should do at a turning:

1. *Resolute planning*: fix p\* before departure and draw from it at every turning; the question of reconsidering does not arise.
2. *Self-consistent interim optimization*: at the turning, hold the belief over your position induced by the policy you are actually running, and require policy and belief to be a fixed point; act on the fixed point.
3. *Naive interim optimization*: distribute credence uniformly over the three positions, on the grounds that you have no idea which you are at, and maximize.
4. *The single-decision reading*: since the choice you make now just is the choice you make at every indistinguishable turning, do not re-randomize independently at each one — pick the best pure action and apply it everywhere. (I get 0 for both pure actions here, which makes this reading look catastrophic in three-turning cases.)
5. *Robustness*: maximize the worst case over which turning you are at, holding fixed that you revert to p\* afterwards.
6. *Maximize the chance of the best outcome*: choose the mixture that maximizes the probability of getting home (p = 1/2), rather than expected value. A referee suggested this and I can't tell whether it is a rival norm or a change of subject.

For each: the ex ante expected payoff it delivers in my case (or "silent"); whether it leaves the driver open to a Dutch book at his own stated odds, and roughly how much that costs per trip; whether, on that norm, forgetting could ever be worth paying for in this case or the nearest variant of it; and then a tier list — tier 1 = you would defend it in print as the correct norm, tier 2 = a serious contender you would argue against, tier 3 = not defensible — with a 0–10 rating inside each tier. Say explicitly which dimension is doing the work in your ordering (ex ante value? betting coherence? robustness?) and why that dimension is the right one to rank by, rather than the others.

**What I want you to flag.** For the norm you put in tier 1, name the single consideration or argument that would move it down a tier, and say where in your own reasoning — the algebra, the treatment of the self-locating belief, the assumption that only i.i.d. mixtures are implementable, or the choice of ranking dimension — you would expect an error to be hiding.
