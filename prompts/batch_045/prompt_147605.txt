I got into reading about art forgery as a hobby a couple of years ago, and there's a pattern I keep tripping over that I cant find the word for. Hoping someone here can tell me whether it already has one.

The basic setup: someone forges a painting by a well known artist. It's good enough to get accepted, and once it's accepted it stops being just an object and starts doing work — it gets reproduced in the books, it fills an awkward gap in the artist's development, and curators and scholars quietly use it to build up their sense of what that hand looks like. Then other pictures get judged against that sense, some promoted, some demoted. Only much later does the forgery get exposed, and by then you can't just delete it, because a sizeable chunk of what everyone now means by "the artist's style" was assembled partly out of it.

The part I actually care about is narrower than that. It isn't that our evidence got contaminated — I know there are standard ways of talking about contaminated or unreliable evidence, and they don't feel like they capture it. It's that the fake seems to stop being merely something the standard got applied to and becomes part of what the standard *is*. Once the fakes have been around long enough, later artists working in that idiom copy them, a forger faking the "style" may in effect be faking an earlier forgery, and if you ask whether some contested picture is genuine, the honest answer looks like: genuine by a standard that the fakes helped build. The ruler wasn't bent; it was partly made out of the stuff it's supposed to measure.

I mentioned this to a friend and she said it's just the hermeneutic circle, which feels both too broad and too kind — that would make every innocent case of learning a style from examples an instance, including ones where every example is genuine. Mine only switches on when some of the examples got in by pretending to be something they aren't, and got used before anyone noticed.

So: does this have a name, or is it handled as a special case of something bigger? If it's the latter I want to know exactly how it differs from the bigger thing. I don't want a label, I want the carving, and I'll push back on a bare name. A few cases I'd want the carve to settle:

- The admired passages in an old painting that turn out to have been added by a nineteenth-century restorer. Same thing, or different? Here nobody made a new object; a later hand modified the original, and what people spent two centuries loving is a physical mixture.
- Studio and workshop pictures, made by people who actually worked in the master's shop and sold under his name. Deceptive in a sense, but not alien to the practice the way an outsider's invention is. In or out?
- The forgery that got exposed and then stayed in the canon anyway, relabeled "imitator of", still reproduced, still shaping what people find typical of the artist. Does relabeling neutralize the effect or just rename it?
- The version people talk about now with image generators trained partly on the output of earlier image generators. Same phenomenon, or only superficially similar?

And one thing I can't work out: if our eye for the real thing was partly trained on fakes, is there something wrong with the pleasure we take in the real thing, or is that a worry that dissolves as soon as you look at it properly?
