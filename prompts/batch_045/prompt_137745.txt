I had a long argument with a friend last night about whether the decipherment of Egyptian hieroglyphs refutes Quine's indeterminacy of translation. Her case: the Rosetta Stone, found in 1799, carries one Ptolemaic decree in hieroglyphic, Demotic, and Greek; Thomas Young extracted phonetic values from the cartouche of a foreign king's name; Champollion announced in 1822 that the script mixes phonetic and ideographic signs, drawing on Coptic as the last stage of Egyptian; and the resulting grammar went on to read inscriptions nobody had read for more than a thousand years, with later scholars filling in much of the vocabulary and grammar. If a translation manual can be that uniquely right, she says, radical translation is determinate after all. I think the argument moves too fast, but I could not say exactly where it breaks, so I want the analysis run in both directions.

Do the mapping carefully. On the theory's side: what precisely is claimed to be indeterminate — the gavagai example, the inscrutability of reference, the claim that manuals can diverge while fitting all the evidence there could be — and what, for a written script, would the evidence base be: the signs, their repetitions and distributions, the cartouches, the accompanying pictures, the bilingual decree, Coptic? Which features of the actual episode does the thesis predict or illuminate — the long failure while the script was assumed to be purely ideographic, the dependence on the assumption that foreign names preserve their sounds, the role of a related descendant language — and which does it misdescribe? On the case's side: is Champollion's success a counterexample, or does the thesis have nothing to say here at all, since there were no speakers to question and no assent and dissent to record — a situation radical translation arguably never anticipated? If my friend's argument fails, locate the failure: does successful prediction on fresh inscriptions break the tie between rival manuals, or could a suitably gerrymandered rival always have predicted as well? And does any of this tell us anything about translating languages that people still speak? Where the theory is genuinely silent, say so rather than pressing it into service.
