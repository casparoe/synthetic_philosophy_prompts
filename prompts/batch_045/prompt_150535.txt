**Question 3 (25 points).** In 2025 a ministry must decide whether to build a coastal barrier. The cost is 4 units now (a unit being a billion baskets of consumption goods); if built, the barrier prevents 700 units of storm damage in 2205. Per-capita consumption is expected to grow at 2% per year, and the ministry's welfare function counts a basket as contributing less to wellbeing the more baskets its recipient already has, with an elasticity of marginal utility of η = 1. Finally, there is a 10% chance that the coast will have been abandoned before 2205, in which case the barrier never does anyone any good.

(a) Distinguish the *pure* rate of time preference from the other reasons a social discount rate might be positive, and explain the argument that a pure rate of time preference is arbitrary — including how the argument's parallel with spatial location is supposed to work. (8 points)

(b) The ministry proposes a *distance-relative* rule: apply the same discount factor to any benefit n years away, whatever the calendar date, so that the 2205 benefit is treated the same whether it is evaluated in 2025 or in 2155. A critic objects that any positive discount rate applied distance-relatively makes the ministry's preferences change as time passes, and so cannot express a temporally neutral ranking of outcomes. Explain this objection and assess how much weight it has. (7 points)

(c) Compute the present value of the benefits using the standard decomposition r = ρ + ηg, first with ρ = 0 and then with ρ = 1%, and say whether the barrier should be built. In the course of doing so: (i) say what each of the three discount rates — the money rate, the consumption rate, and the rate on wellbeing — is supposed to represent, and which of them the philosophical argument in (a) targets; (ii) say how, if at all, the 10% abandonment risk should enter the calculation, and whether taking it into account is itself a form of pure time preference; (iii) assess the claim that because the people of 2205 will be much richer than we are, we should count their gains for less — does that claim support a positive ρ? (10 points)

**Student answer, transcribed verbatim:**

The distinction is easiest to see in the Ramsey decomposition of the social discount rate, r = ρ + ηg. Here ρ is the pure rate of time preference: the rate at which we count a unit of wellbeing as mattering less merely because it falls later. The ηg term is not like that. It reflects the fact that a basket of goods does less for a person who already has many baskets, together with the fact that future people will have more baskets, so that the same basket yields less wellbeing later. The third quantity, the money rate, is the market interest rate: it reflects the productivity of capital and the return available on alternative investments, and it is the rate at which money now converts into money later, not the rate at which anyone's wellbeing is discounted.

The argument that ρ should be zero is the argument from the arbitrariness of location. If two benefits are alike in every respect — same size, same recipient, same certainty — except that one falls in 2025 and the other in 2205, then preferring the earlier one is like preferring a benefit that occurs here to an exactly similar benefit that occurs ten metres away. Bare position, whether in space or in time, is not a respect in which one outcome can be better or worse than another, so a planner who discounts for it is making an arbitrary distinction. Nothing about the intrinsic character of the benefit changes with its date.

Part (b) raises the more interesting objection, and I think it is a good one. A distance-relative rule is indexical: it ranks the very same pair of outcomes differently depending on when the ranking is made. In 2025 the 2205 benefit is 180 years away and is divided by (1 + r)^180; in 2155 it is 50 years away and is divided by (1 + r)^50. A planner who in 2025 would rather have the consumption now will, on arriving in 2155, want the resources to have gone into the barrier after all, and will then want the earlier decision reversed. So a positive distance-relative rate cannot be a temporally neutral ranking of outcomes by date; it is a moving spotlight. The most a temporally neutral planner can do is fix a schedule by calendar date and then decline to revise it, which is what a date-relative rule does. I take this to show that the fate of ρ cannot be settled by asking how a planner's attitudes change over time.

For (c), with ρ = 0 and ηg = 2%, we have r = 2%. Over 180 years, (1.02)^180 ≈ 35, so 700 baskets in 2205 are worth about 700/35 ≈ 20 baskets now. That comfortably exceeds the 4-unit cost, so the barrier should be built. With ρ = 1%, r = 3%, (1.03)^180 ≈ 205, and 700/205 ≈ 3.4, which is less than the cost of 4, so the barrier should not be built.

The 10% abandonment risk should make no difference to the calculation. Temporal neutrality says that the bare fact of a benefit's lying further away cannot count against it, and the abandonment risk is simply the extrinsic guise in which that fact appears: it is only because 2205 is a long way off that there is any chance the coast will be abandoned. So the expected-value calculation should use the full 700, and the risk should be left out of the arithmetic, or at most noted in a footnote. Relatedly, I do not think the point about future generations being richer gives any independent support for a positive ρ. That consideration concerns how much better off they will be, and the Ramsey decomposition already registers it in ηg; adding a further reduction on top of it — the barrier protects rich people, so shade the benefit down again — would be to charge twice for the same fact. But notice that ηg is itself a kind of pure time preference: it is a rate that makes a basket in 2205 count for less than a basket in 2025, and it does so because of *when* the basket arrives. So the philosophical argument against ρ is in tension with itself: if we really thought that location in time was irrelevant we would have to set η to zero as well, and then the case for the barrier would be overwhelming. As it is, temporal neutrality has no practical bite. It fixes one term in a formula and leaves the other free to do all the work, so that almost any policy can be given a temporally neutral justification.
