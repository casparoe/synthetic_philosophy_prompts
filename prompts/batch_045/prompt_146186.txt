I'm writing a paper for school on whether generations are real things or just labels that journalists invented, and I've noticed my gut gives much firmer answers about individual cases than any definition I can come up with. So I'd like to know what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to.

- The students who entered my school in 2023: a real group, but obviously not a generation.
- Boomers: real.
- Millennials: real, more real than I expected when I started.
- The so-called Xennials, born in the late seventies and early eighties and supposedly caught between Gen X and Millennials: fake, a magazine invention — even though I'll grant that those people had analog childhoods and digital adulthoods.
- People who were in elementary school on September 11, 2001: real. This is the one that bothers me, because it's very nearly the same people as the Xennials.
- Gen Z: real, though I feel shakier about it than about Millennials.
- Generation Alpha: probably fake. But "kids who learned to read on a tablet" feels real to me, and that's roughly the same people again.
- The kids who spent all of middle school on Zoom: I go back and forth, and I can't tell why.

The tentative principle I've been playing with is that a cohort is a real kind only if some event or condition struck its members during a particular developmental window and left a lasting mark through an identifiable mechanism — not merely a measurable difference in attitudes or behavior. Call it the scar criterion. It seems to save the 9/11 case and condemn the Xennials, but I can't tell what it says about the tablet kids, and I suspect it makes generationhood come in degrees in a way I find unsatisfying.

Can you say whether anything like the scar criterion can be defended, test it against my verdicts, repair it where it breaks, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts I would have to give up to have a principle at all? I'd also want its verdicts on two cases I haven't listed: a "generation" marked off by something that hit every age group equally, say a decade-long economic collapse that reshaped everyone's expectations in the same way, and two countries whose populations share birth years but not histories, so that the same birth-year slice would pick out different groups in each. And please settle the Zoom case for me, or explain why it can't be settled.
