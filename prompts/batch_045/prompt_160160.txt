we've been stuck on this for four sessions and I need someone to tell me what kind of stuck it actually is

I run a tiny reading group — me (philosophy), two psych people, a novelist, a retired judge, a composer. Six of us, once a fortnight. We've spent four sessions on the question of why anyone voluntarily seeks out art that makes them sad, and it has completely stopped moving. Every position is on the table, everyone has said their piece twice, nobody has shifted, and nobody is even arguing anymore — we just restate.

The positions as they actually show up in the room:

- the conversion story: the distress is real, but awareness that the thing is artfully made and isn't happening to you converts it, so the net valence comes out positive
- the make-believe line: you don't really feel grief or fear, you only quasi-feel it, because you only imagine the threat — so there's no negative state there to explain in the first place
- the being-moved line: the sadness is real and unmixed, and what you like is your own responsiveness to it; the pleasure has your reaction as its object, not the events, so there's no contradiction to dissolve
- the "who says it has to be pleasant" line: the experience isn't pleasant and doesn't need to be; it's worth having for other reasons, and the assumption that a chosen experience must be pleasant is what manufactured the paradox
- the novelist's line: the sadness is part of what makes the artifact good, and people want good artifacts, end of story

We all agree on every descriptive fact anyone offers: people do seek these things out, they do report the emotional episode as unpleasant while reporting the whole thing as worth doing, they sometimes avoid it, they don't seek out the real-life equivalent. Nobody bites on the old catharsis move, and the composer keeps pointing out that music is the hard case because there's no proposition you're imaginally assenting to — no content for the make-believe line to work with. That objection gets nodded at and then ignored, which is part of why I think we're not having one disagreement.

The case that finally broke us: a friend of mine who re-watches one particular film every couple of years, knows exactly what it does to her, describes it afterwards as "that wrecked me", says she's glad she did it, will do it again, would be furious if anyone arranged the same emotional profile for her in real life, and gets visibly annoyed if you describe her as having enjoyed it. Every one of our five positions explains about two thirds of that, and none of them explains all of it, and each of us thinks the leftover third is the unimportant part.

My hypothesis, offered as one: this isn't a single dispute but three questions stacked under one name — why the episode is attractive in advance, whether the episode is pleasant or merely worth having, and whether it's coherent to want it — plus a buried empirical question about the phenomenology (is the affective response genuinely negative-valenced, or a blended state, or negative while occurrent and positive in retrospect), plus a meta-disagreement about what an explanation of a motive is even supposed to deliver: a psychological mechanism, or a rendering of what the person takes herself to be doing. And I suspect part of the impasse is just the word "enjoyment" doing three different jobs.

So: is that roughly right, or is the real structure something else — a genuine substantive clash I'm papering over, or bedrock disagreement about what counts as a reason for wanting something? Please ground the diagnosis in what these positions actually claim rather than giving me a tour of the literature (no reading lists, I don't want citations), and be willing to say if one of the five is just confused.

Sections, in this order, roughly:
- Diagnosis — about 500 words: which parts are verbal, which substantive, which are a hidden empirical or formal question, which are different questions, which are bedrock. Commit; don't hedge into "a bit of everything."
- What each position is answering — about 250 words: map the five onto the questions, and say which pairs are genuinely incompatible and which only look it.
- The rewatch case — about 200 words: run your diagnosis on it and show what it does and doesn't settle.
- What would count as progress — about 300 words: a distinction, a study you could actually run, a formal point, a rephrasing of the question — concrete enough that I could bring it to the group.
- What we should stop asking — about 100 words: the questions worth dropping, and why.
