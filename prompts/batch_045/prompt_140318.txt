I am a fourth-year PhD student and I am defending a thesis chapter on expected value fanaticism in a fortnight, and I would like a Q&A preparation sheet rather than a summary of the literature. The committee is: a decision theorist who works on non-expected-utility frameworks (risk-weighted and related approaches) and thinks the entire fanaticism debate is a symptom of taking expected utility maximization as the baseline in the first place; an ethicist who works on population ethics and longtermism and is, if anything, a fanaticism sympathizer; and a formal epistemologist with a statistics background who is interested in imprecise probabilities and the robustness of probabilistic reasoning under model uncertainty. My supervisor will chair but not ask questions. The format is a twenty-minute talk plus twenty minutes of questions, no slides for the replies, so I need answers I can give standing up in roughly two minutes each. I am less worried about being refuted than about being met with the shrug that my puzzle is an artifact of an implausible decision rule, so I would also like to know what I should concede in the first two minutes to forestall that reaction.

The chapter defends a hybrid position that I have not seen stated in quite this form, and I would like the objections aimed at it specifically. The diagnostic claim is that the force of fanaticism comes from the *probability* side rather than the value side: in mugging-style cases the tiny probability is what I call hypothesis-space-generated, because it is produced by spreading credence over an open-ended space of possible hypotheses of the form some consideration I have not yet thought of; such a probability is reference-class unstable in the sense that whatever procedure assigns it could be run again indefinitely to generate arbitrarily many further disjoint hypotheses with positive probability. The normative claim I call modal scarcity: a probability may enter a decision-theoretic calculation only if it is anchored to an articulable mechanism or reference class whose total probability is bounded from below by something on the order of one over a number of live hypotheses fixed in advance, rather than being the residue left after partitioning an unbounded space. The payoff claim is that this blocks mugging-style fanaticism while leaving ordinary fanaticism about astronomical stakes intact, since in cases like existential-risk mitigation or far-future scenarios the small probability is mechanism-anchored, and the same distinction is supposed to explain why the many-independent-bets defence of fanaticism is legitimate in the second sort of case and circular in the first, where no finite sequence of choices can be specified over which the bets are genuinely independent.

Please give me the seven or eight objections I will actually get from this particular committee, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased the way that person would phrase it rather than in my vocabulary — the decision theorist's formulation should sound like someone who has stopped believing expected utility is the thing to be defending, the formal epistemologist's should sound like someone worried about whether my anchoring condition is even computable or is just re-describing calibration in fancier terms, and the longtermist's should sound like someone who thinks I have invented an excuse to discount the future. For each: a two-minute reply, what I should concede within it, and one sentence on where the reply is soft. Flag which objections are really the same objection in different dress, and end with the question I should hope nobody asks, together with the honest answer if it does get asked.
