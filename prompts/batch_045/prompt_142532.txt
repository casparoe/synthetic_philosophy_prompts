I write about science and ideas for a national magazine, and in six weeks I am giving the keynote at a two-day closed-door conference that a large education funder is convening on what genetics can and cannot tell education policy. The room will be about seventy people: roughly forty-five education researchers, mostly quantitative — value-added modelers, people who run large randomised trials on tutoring and class size, a few psychometricians, and several sociologists of education; about a dozen behavioural geneticists, split between the twin-modelling tradition and the molecular side (polygenic scores, within-family and sibling-difference designs); plus a handful of other journalists, some foundation programme officers, and two representatives from a teachers' union.

The thesis I want to defend is a proposed house rule for science journalists — I have been calling it the Range Rule — together with two companion clauses.

First, the range clause: a heritability estimate is a ratio of variance components estimated in one population over the range of environments that population actually occupied, so the number should be reported only alongside the population and the range, never as a free-floating property of a trait.

Second, the no-leap clause: a within-population heritability figure underwrites neither a claim about what caused a particular individual's outcome nor a claim about the causes of a difference between groups, cohorts, or countries. Both are category errors, not merely risks to be footnoted.

Third, the mean-and-spread clause: when a writer wants to cite a high heritability against an intervention, first ask whether the intervention targets the mean or the spread. Heritability is nearly silent on mean-shifting policies that move a whole population into environments outside the sampled range, but it does bear on policies that aim to compress the distribution by equalising environments the estimate already sampled.

My supporting examples are the familiar ones — the dietary treatment for phenylketonuria, the secular rise in height, the rise in myopia — plus one I take to be less familiar: that the heritability of educational attainment is partly a fact about credentialing institutions rather than about learning, since compulsory-schooling laws and grade inflation change what "years of schooling" measures and therefore change the variance components themselves.

I have fifteen minutes of questions after a thirty-five minute talk, and room to pre-empt perhaps two objections inside the talk. Assume the room knows the technical basics; do not spend the sheet defining heritability or re-litigating the equal-environments assumption unless an objection genuinely turns on it.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually be handed by this room — not the ones I would get from an online audience — ordered by how much damage they would do, each attributed to the bloc most likely to raise it and phrased the way that bloc would phrase it, since I need to hear the sociologist of education's version of the complaint and not the quantitative geneticist's. For each, tell me what I should concede before anyone asks, what I should hold, a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds, and where that reply is soft. Flag any point at which my three clauses are in tension with one another, or at which my own examples prove less than I think they do. Finally, give me the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and what I should say if somebody asks it.
