Hi — hoping you can help me think through something that's stopped being a thought experiment and turned into my actual job.

A few months ago I picked up some contract work rating LLM outputs. Mostly medical and legal-ish questions: we get shown a question and two answers, and we pick the better one. The line we're given in training is that this is fine even though I know far less than the models do, because in an adversarial format errors get dragged into the open — a comparatively ignorant judge plus two competing answers is supposed to beat a single answer on its own. As I understand it that's roughly the same bet the AI-safety-via-debate people make about scalable oversight. But in practice I've noticed I reward whichever answer is longer and uses more confident jargon, and I genuinely can't check most of the specific claims. So I want to know what this whole practice is resting on.

My half-formed worry, which you should feel free to tear apart: maybe what's doing the work isn't truth having an advantage in argument at all, but the tie-break convention. When I really can't tell, I default to whichever answer asserts less, or to whichever one echoes the framing of the question. If that's right, then whatever reliability the format has comes from a procedural default that's been handed to me, not from anything about truth being easier to defend.

So, concretely, I'd like:

1. A reconstruction of the implicit epistemology. What has to be true of argument, evidence, and expertise for weak judge + two adversaries to be a decent route to the truth? And be careful about the difference between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely sits well with — I suspect several quite different theories of testimony or argument would license it, and I want to know where it's underdetermined.
2. The obfuscated-argument problem specifically. There are correct answers whose correctness is expensive to verify, and there are wrong answers that are cheap to dress up as rigorous. Does the setup need the strong claim that correctness is cheap to demonstrate, or only the weaker claim that sustaining an error is costlier than sustaining a truth? What views of justification or understanding does it quietly rule out on the way — holistic ones, say, or views on which competence takes years to acquire?
3. An apparent inconsistency I can't sort out. The same project also has us score single answers on a separate scale, and there we're told explicitly to ignore style and confidence entirely. Do the two halves of the practice presuppose consistent things?
4. My tie-break worry taken seriously: how would you actually tell the difference between truth winning and a default winning, and what would need to change in the protocol to separate them?
5. If we threw out the assumption that truth is easier to defend, what would the evaluation protocol have to look like instead — what would have to change, and what would we lose?

I'm not a philosopher, so please write plainly and don't assume I know the literature; and no need for citations or a reading list, I can't chase references anyway. Please write in British English — the write-up is going to a UK-based client.
