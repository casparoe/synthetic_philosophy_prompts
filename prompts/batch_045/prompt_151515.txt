I should be upfront that I have no academic background — I work in warehouse logistics — but for the past few months I have been reading on my own about a staking rule for favorable games: the claim that when the odds are tilted slightly in your favor and you can play again and again, there is a fraction of your money that makes your pile compound fastest, that staking less than that is merely slow, and that staking more does not simply add risk but actually drags your growth rate down, through zero, and into near-certain ruin. What finally convinced me was not the algebra but a single picture: fill a room with people playing the same favorable game and the room's average wealth can climb while almost every individual in it goes broke. I want a room of adults to feel that in their bones. And then I want them to feel the part I found much harder to accept, which is that the right fraction is not a number you can memorize, because it changes when the same stake can be spread across two independent chances instead of concentrated on one.

The setting: a monthly evening meetup I help organize, about fourteen adults, mixed ages and occupations, none formally trained, at least two of them visibly uncomfortable with anything past basic arithmetic. Ninety minutes, three tables, and I have ten-sided dice, poker chips, index cards, pens and a whiteboard. No projector, no money, and I would rather the prize be symbolic than financial.

What I would like from you is a game I can actually run in that slot, arranged so that both lessons arrive as things that happened to people at the tables rather than as things I announce afterwards.

Constraints I care about:

1. Nobody in the room should hear the name of the rule, or the words log, geometric mean, growth rate, or expected value, before the debrief. If a participant reaches the idea in their own words, so much the better — the naming happens at the end or not at all.
2. The game should be played in two phases. Phase one is a single favorable bet repeated: a die that wins six times in ten and pays even money, with the winning chance stated plainly to everyone, and each player committing privately in advance to one fixed fraction of their bankroll to stake on every round, written on a card and unchangeable for the phase. Phase two hands each player two independent versions of the same bet per round and lets them commit to a total fraction plus a split between the two — including the option of putting everything on one. The point of phase two is that the fraction the group felt its way toward in phase one should now turn out to be too timid, and that two people who stake the same reckless total should come to different fates depending only on whether they split it.
3. My instinct is that phase one should have every player rolling separately, so that one reckless person gets lucky and the gap between the room's average result and the room's typical result becomes visible in the data on the board; and that phase two should use a single shared roll per round for the whole table, so that the remaining differences in outcome can only be coming from the total staked and the split. Tell me if I have that backwards, and what I lose either way.
4. No real money, no calculators beyond a phone's basic arithmetic if you think it necessary, and each round must be resolvable in under a minute by someone who dislikes numbers. Tell me what the bankroll should be denominated in, what it starts at, how rounding works, what happens at zero, and whether eliminated players should stay at zero or be given something to do — my worry is that the reckless players are out by round four and spend an hour bored and defensive.
5. Before phase one, each player writes down the fraction they are committing to, what they expect their own bankroll to be at the end, and what they expect the room's average to be. I want these collected, both to stop hindsight rewriting and to have something to read back during the debrief.

Deliverables: the rules and materials; a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes including setup and the transition between phases; the numbers I will need as facilitator — in particular the growth-maximizing stake in each phase, roughly where the growth rate crosses zero, and what each of the plausible committed fractions should be expected to produce over the number of rounds you recommend, so that I can tell a real signal from noise; the debrief, mapped onto the specific moments in the game that each part of the idea showed up in, including how to handle the player who bet everything and got lucky and now has the strongest argument in the room; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each; and an honest note on what the game cannot show and I will have to say out loud myself — I already suspect the honest answers include something about the result being a long-run claim that a twenty-minute game can only illustrate, and something about the fact that in the game everyone knows the odds, whereas in life the odds have to be estimated and overestimating them is the ordinary way to overbet.
