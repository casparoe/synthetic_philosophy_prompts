I teach a third-year undergraduate seminar in moral philosophy — eighteen students, one ninety-minute session per topic — and the week on care ethics has gone badly the last two times I ran it. Students arrive able to recite that impartialist theories abstract away from relationships and that care ethics puts dependency and particular relationships back at the centre, they nod along, and then they hand in essays in which "care ethics" functions as a synonym for being nice to people you happen to know. What they never feel is the thing that makes the position genuinely difficult: that from inside a care relation the claim this particular person makes on you is not experienced as one weight among others to be balanced, but as something that resists being traded off at all, and that this resistance is precisely what makes care ungovernable by any rule, rota, or standard anyone could write down. I want a session in which they collide with that themselves rather than hear it from me.

The seed of it is a conversation with a friend who manages a small residential care home of about twenty older residents, which has just been put under a new inspection regime: her staff must now log, against a checklist, that each resident was offered a choice at each meal, was addressed by name, and had a documented five-minute social interaction on each shift. She says two things at once, and I think both are true: that before the checklist existed, some residents were being neglected in ways nobody noticed, and that since the checklist arrived her staff have become markedly better at producing the record and no better — in at least two cases worse — at noticing what those two residents actually needed. I have started calling the two features I care about by the names second-personal irreplaceability (the cared-for person is not substitutable from inside the relation, whatever an allocator's spreadsheet implies) and proxy capture (once a measurable stand-in is installed, the people being measured learn to produce the stand-in, and over time the stand-in displaces what it was standing for). There is a third thing I want present in the room: an inverted epistemic asymmetry, since the resident is the person best placed to know whether she has been cared for and also the person least able to make that judgement stick.

So: design me a runnable session. Give me the roles and their secret briefings, a timeline with minute marks, whatever materials the room needs, and the rules under which participants actually make choices — small real stakes are fine, but the pressure should come out of the setup rather than out of anything I announce in advance. I would like participants to reach the collision on their own: first the pull of attending to this particular person, then the discovery that any instrument you build to make that attention reliable across a rota of carers systematically rewards something else, then the uncomfortable thought that a care ethics which cannot be scaled has nothing to say to an institution while a care ethics which can be scaled has stopped being about care. The debrief should map specific moments I can watch for onto each of those three features, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — I expect at least one student to play the whole thing as satire and one to treat the checklist as the point — and how to handle each, and be honest about which part of the problem the exercise cannot reproduce and I will simply have to say out loud. Please write it as continuous prose, the way you would in an email to a colleague: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
