I work on a digital humanities project that assigns period labels to a large corpus of early printed books, and I keep running into something I do not have a word for.

Take the pair medieval/Renaissance. Pretty much everyone who works on the material now agrees that the older picture — a long sleep, then a sudden waking — is wrong: the continuities are real, the break is at best a matter of degree and at worst an invention of much later writers who wanted to praise themselves by contrasting themselves with their predecessors. And yet the labels go on doing work. Once we tag a 1470 edition as Renaissance rather than late medieval, the tag follows the book around. It lands on a different reading list, it is shelved and catalogued next to different neighbours, it is cited by different people, and the corpus we end up with looks more sharply divided at that date than the material looked before we started tagging it. I see the same shape around the Scientific Revolution: once the name is in place, the decades before it get described as a prelude full of precursors and the decades after as consolidation, so that the boundary manufactures the very discontinuity it was introduced to record.

The part I cannot name is the second-order part. The older picture has been refuted, and the refutation has not touched the labels. People will say in the same breath that the Renaissance was a later invention and that this manuscript is Renaissance. The picture is dead; the word that asserts the picture walks around anyway, because the things that run on the word — departments, journals, survey courses, cataloguing rules, my own tag set — are alive, and they keep regenerating the evidence that makes the word look earned. A friend suggested essentially contested concept, but as I understand it that phrase is for terms everybody values and fights over the extension of. Here nobody is fighting, everyone agrees the picture is false, and the term keeps asserting it regardless. It is not quite a self-fulfilling prophecy either, since nothing is making the fifteenth century do anything.

So: does this have an established name? If it does, I would want more than the name — I want to know what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie. If it does not, coin a term and define it tightly enough that it does not collapse into its neighbours. Either way, please say how it differs from ordinary theory-ladenness in description, from a term's merely being vague or politically loaded, and from the case where a classification changes the things it classifies, which is what I expect someone to reach for first and which cannot be what is going on here, since the objects are past and cannot be pushed around. And if I am conflating two or three separable things, separate them for me.

Two more things I would want handled. First, the boundary cases: some period terms seem to get away with it. The long nineteenth century is openly a convenience with arbitrary endpoints and nobody takes it to be reporting a discovery; a stratigraphic boundary is conventional in the same sort of way and no one mistakes it for a finding. But late antiquity was introduced, as I understand it, as a deliberately thin name for an interval, and it seems to have quietly acquired a thick content it was never licensed to have. Is there a principled line between the labels that get away with it and the ones that do not, or is it only a matter of degree? Second: is there any way of telling from the inside whether a label has been successfully laundered, whether its current content is genuinely independent of the discredited claim it was born carrying, or is the honest answer that we cannot tell?
