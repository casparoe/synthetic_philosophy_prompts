I sit on the Evolutionary Systems panel of our national research council — I was co-opted two years ago, nominally because I did a doctorate on developmental constraint and then drifted into conservation genomics, in practice because I am the member who will not stop invoking Gould and Lewontin's 1979 spandrels paper in meetings. On Thursday we decide on a five-year, £8m programme grant, provisionally titled A Functional Atlas for the Balkan Cave Beetle Radiation: the applicants will run selection scans and genotype–environment association tests across roughly forty populations of an endemic karst radiation, and then, for every trait that carries a selection signal, write what they call a selective scenario, naming the ecological agent and the function. The output is a public catalogue of some six hundred trait annotations, each assigned a confidence tier — Tier A where there is direct experimental manipulation, Tier B where there is comparative correlation across independent lineages, Tier C where the annotation rests on narrative plausibility — and it is to be released as open data. Two downstream users are already attached: the national conservation agency, which wants to feed the catalogue into forecasts of which populations can tolerate the aquifer's drying, and a commercial breeding partner. Their response to referees is brisk about the old objection: the critique of the adaptationist programme was fair, they say, when adaptive hypotheses were conjured from natural history alone, but an independent genomic signal of selection now does the evidential work the story used to do, so the worry about just-so storytelling is obsolete.

Half of me believes that, and half of me does not. My specific worry is what I have started calling the loss trap: the great majority of scored traits in this clade are losses — eyes, pigment, wings, the metathoracic flight musculature, a whole set of sensory structures — and every such loss admits both an adaptive story (energy economy, reallocation) and a non-adaptive one (relaxed selection plus drift), and no signature in the applicants' pipeline, as far as I can tell, discriminates between them. The same goes for the one trait in the pilot data that nobody has a story for at all, a midline groove on the seventh abdominal sternite, which the pipeline will nonetheless annotate within about a fortnight of launch. And behind that sits a second worry I find harder to shake: a Tier C narrative that lives in a database with a confidence number attached to it gets read by a conservation modeller, or by a partner with a commercial interest in the traits being adaptive, as though it were data. The panel is split three ways — the chair wants to fund with conditions, another member wants to decline outright on the ground that this is Panglossian storytelling industrialised, and a third says we have no business requiring a working research programme to settle a methodological quarrel before it begins, that this is armchair paralysis, and that five more years of argument while the water table drops is itself a decision. I also do not want conditions that merely invert the default: a drift-of-the-gaps would be as lazy as an adaptation-of-the-gaps.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate on Wednesday evening, about 1,100–1,300 words, with a short header block and addressed to the panel, with the applicants as named recipients of the decision. Do not spend it rehearsing the 1979 paper as such — we have all read it — apply it. Give me one recommendation and commit to it with reasons, not a menu of options for us to choose between: fund as submitted, fund with named conditions, or decline. If you recommend conditions, they should be the sort the applicants could actually satisfy and a reviewer could actually check — what must be true of a Tier C entry before it may be published at all, who if anyone may promote an entry between tiers and on what evidence, what is to be done with the hundred-odd regressive traits, whether the commercial partner's access should be restricted. Then give whichever option you reject the strongest and fairest statement you can manage, not a flattened version of it: I want the losing argument to survive my memo. Close with a short paragraph on which of your own assumptions is shakiest and what finding would force you to change the recommendation. British English throughout, please.
