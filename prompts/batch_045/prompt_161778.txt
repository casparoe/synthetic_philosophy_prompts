Last night I argued for hours with a friend about the gender-equality paradox: the cross-national pattern in which the countries that score highest on gender-equality and development indices show *more*, not less, gender-typed sorting in education and occupation — more women in nursing, teaching and care work, fewer in engineering and the physical sciences. My friend's position was the evolutionary one: relax material constraint and legal exclusion, and what remains is what people genuinely want, so the rich egalitarian countries reveal a preference structure that poorer countries suppress. I don't accept that. But I also don't accept the reply my side usually reaches for — that the national equality indices are really proxies for GDP or "development," that the paradox is therefore a measurement artifact, and that there is nothing here to explain. I think the pattern is real, robust enough to be worth explaining, and interesting precisely because neither camp's story is right.

The view I want to defend at a workshop next week is what I've been calling the *expressive surplus* account. The sketch: occupational choice is pushed around by two things, the material payoff of the option and the expressive load it carries — how much of a statement about who you are the job is, to you and to others. In high-welfare, high-security societies the material channel goes quiet: a nurse and an engineer can both expect a decent, insured life, so material payoff stops doing the selecting. When that happens, the expressive channel carries more of the weight, and gender is the most readily available, most socially legible identity for a job to express. Sorting therefore intensifies with equality, and it should intensify most where work is identity-loaded ("I am a nurse") and least where it is a pure income source. That predicts a graded pattern across occupations, a lag between equality reforms and increased sorting, and a greater sensitivity of choice to how occupations are *narrated* than to information about earnings. The justice upshot I want to end on: the paradox is evidence about the normative meaning work carries in rich societies, not about the intrinsic content of preferences, and the question for justice is not "did she choose it" but whether the meanings attached to the options — care as self-sacrifice, engineering as mastery — are ones people could jointly endorse as equals, and who bears the cost of that division.

The room will be about twelve people: an evolutionary psychologist who reads the pattern as her side's best evidence; two quantitative sociologists who think the indices are development proxies and will want to know why I'm not just doing bad measurement; a labour economist committed to revealed-preference welfare analysis who wants a stated welfare loss before he'll call any of this a problem; two feminist political philosophers who think "choice" frameworks already concede too much; and one science-studies person who will go straight at whatever coding scheme I use for identity-loaded occupations.
