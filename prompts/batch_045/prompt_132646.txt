I'm on the grants committee of a small foundation — five of us, we move maybe $2M a year, mostly global health with a bit of animal welfare and one weird meta-science thing — and I've become the person who keeps pushing importance/tractability/neglectedness on everyone else. Except I've pushed it further than the version I was introduced to. I don't think neglectedness, as usually stated (how few dollars or people are already working on this), is doing any real work. What matters, I argue, is what I've started calling the washout rate: of the good you appear to buy with your marginal dollar, how much would have happened anyway within, say, a decade — because other funders would have arrived later, because a government would have backfilled, because the people you hired would have done something comparably good in another job, or because you just moved the money around inside a system that was going to spend roughly that much regardless. Neglectedness is a proxy for a low washout rate and I think it's a bad one: a cause can look crowded and still have near-zero washout (everyone's money is already committed, so none of it responds to you; or it's a coordination failure where each funder waits for the others), and a cause can look starved and have a washout rate near one (nothing is there because nothing sticks — it's a swamp that absorbs resources and gives little back, which is partly *why* it's neglected). So my position: keep importance and tractability, drop neglectedness, put washout in its place, keep multiplying.

We have a vote coming up that turns on exactly this — one proposal is a well-evidenced, fairly crowded intervention where I think the washout is surprisingly low, the other is a starved and speculative area where I suspect our money would mostly evaporate. So: what would count against my framework? I already know the two obvious moves — that washout is even harder to estimate than neglectedness, so I've just relocated the measurement problem somewhere with worse data, and that I now need a model of the funding ecosystem and not just of the cause, which is a big ask for a five-person committee. I think I have answers to both, so give me the objections I don't have, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which ones a committed ITN person would themselves concede as counting against them rather than just what a critic would say. Two things I especially want. First: am I insulated? It looks like I can always say the washout estimate was wrong, or that washout is high in principle but not in this case — so tell me whether I've qualified this into something unfalsifiable, and if I have, whether that's actually a problem for a thing of this kind or just what allocation heuristics are like. Second: don't lean on specific historical cases I can't check to adjudicate this — if there are patterns in how philanthropy or aid has actually gone that I should already have updated on, argue from the general shape of them and say what the retrospective audit would have to show to count against me. Our newest committee member also says the whole framework quietly assumes importance is measurable on a single scale and that we should just deliberate case by case; I think that's defeatism, but tell me if it's secretly the strongest objection of all.

Please be committed rather than even-handed: tell me whether you think I'm right, whether the multiplication survives, what you'd put in its place if not, and what you'd personally find most decisive if you were sitting in my seat on Thursday.
