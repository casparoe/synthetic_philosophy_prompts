I am putting together the last unit of an upper-level aesthetics course on the epistemology of aesthetic judgment, and I would like to finish with a literary text rather than another stack of papers. The philosophical spine of the unit is the puzzle of aesthetic testimony: the acquaintance principle in its strong form, the weaker compromise view that testimony can give you the verdict but not what it is like to experience the work, and the worry that deferring to a critic is a failure of something like aesthetic autonomy. My plan is to set Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go alongside this material — the 2005 novel, with the 2010 film used for a couple of scenes — because it stages aesthetic testimony in an institutional setting I have not seen discussed. At Hailsham the students' best work is collected by a woman they call Madame for a gallery no student ever sees; Tommy theorises that the gallery exists to identify couples who are truly in love, on the grounds that the students' art reveals their souls; and late in the book Miss Emily gives the adults' version, which is that the art was taken to prove to the outside world that the students had souls at all. What draws me to it is that the content being transmitted by aesthetic testimony here is not a judgment of beauty or quality but a claim about whether these beings have inner lives, that the people best placed to look are the ones who decline to, and that the students are never told what was concluded about them.

The reading I want is in three moves. First, map the novel onto the philosophical structure: where do we actually see deference, secondhand verdicts, expert judgment, and the gap between being told and seeing for yourself, and which scenes would you put on the syllabus? I have in mind the students' own uncertainty about the quality of their work, Tommy's creative failure and his conviction that it disqualifies him, the competing folk theories about the gallery, the moment Madame watches Kathy dance and cries, and the final visit in which the drawings are offered to the one authority who could certify them. Second, and this is the part I cannot find anyone doing, press on two variations the literature does not seem to handle cleanly. Call the first backwards aesthetic testimony: testimony about the value of a work you made yourself, where first-hand acquaintance is arguably maximal and yet the maker still defers to an absent expert. Does that break the acquaintance principle, or merely relocate it — and does it matter that what Tommy is deferring about is not the quality of the drawing but what it supposedly shows about him? Call the second delegated looking: a verdict deliberately transmitted without its evidential basis, so that the hearer can act on it while never having to look at the thing it concerns. The standard discussion asks what the receiver of aesthetic testimony loses; here the transmission seems designed so that nobody is put in a position to lose anything, and I want to know whether the epistemology of aesthetic testimony has the resources to say what goes wrong in that case, or whether what goes wrong is moral and institutional rather than epistemic — and, if so, what that implies about whether aesthetic judgment can bear moral weight at all once it travels as testimony.

Third, be honest about the limits of the reading, because I would rather assign the novel with its resistance in view than use it as decoration for a position. Where does the book refuse to be about the epistemology of aesthetic judgment — where is it about institutional cruelty, about Kathy's narration being angled or incomplete, about the deferral rumour doing the work that aesthetics appears to be doing — and where would the testimony framing actively mislead a student? Does anything in the novel tell against the acquaintance principle rather than merely illustrating it, and is there a point at which the cost of deferring is shown to fall on the deferrer rather than on the maker? If you think the unit would be better served by a different novel, or by pairing this one with a shorter text that makes the philosophical contrast sharper, say so and say why. I will supply the reading list myself, so no citations or bibliography; I want argument, close reading, and a verdict on whether this belongs on the syllabus, and I would like it to close by saying what the novel adds to the debate that the arguments on their own do not.
