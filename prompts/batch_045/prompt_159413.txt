Right, so I've been asked to run a 45-minute session next week for a group of policy advisors at an education ministry. Smart people, no stats beyond reading a regression table, and someone has just put a report in front of them saying that around half the variation in their country's school test scores is "heritable". Half the room has already concluded there's no point funding a reading programme, the other half has concluded the word is nonsense and the report should be binned. I dont need you to explain heritability to them — I need a plan for how I explain it, and I'd rather you not write the session itself.

What I want from you: first, the order the ideas have to arrive in and why — what has to be in place before the word "heritability" does any work at all (the idea of variation within a group? of a defined population? of an environment being held fixed? of splitting variance rather than splitting a single person?), and whether putting an actual number like 0.5 on the whiteboard early on wrecks the sequencing or helps it.

Second, the carrying example. I was going to use two trays of the same tomato variety, one on a sunny windowsill and one in a cupboard: within each tray the height differences are all genetic, and the difference between the trays is all environment. I have a feeling that example is quietly doing something wrong, but I cant put my finger on what. Tell me whether it carries the idea or whether it smuggles in the exact mistake I'm trying to kill, and say what a carrying example here has to have and has to lack.

Third, the misconceptions, in the order they'll actually arrive, and where in the sequence to head each off. The ones I'm expecting: that heritable means unchangeable (I want to use the phenylketonuria-style case, where something is almost entirely genetic and still completely preventable by an environmental tweak); that heritability is a property of a trait rather than of a population in an environment; that 0.5 means half of any given child's score comes from their genes; and the big one — that high heritability within a group licenses saying a gap between two groups is genetic. There's probably also one coming about "how much genes matter" versus "how much genes predict differences between people" that I don't have a grip on yet.

Fourth, what I should openly refuse to get into at this level and say I'm refusing: twin and adoption designs, GWAS, the additive-versus-total-genetic-variance distinction, anything about specific genes. And finally one question I can put to them at the end that tells me they've got it rather than just learned to say "correlation isn't causation" — ideally something where they have to say what would change their mind about whether a particular programme could work.
