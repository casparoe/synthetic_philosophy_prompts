I am a retired marine geophysicist — forty years of seismic fieldwork and reflection-profile interpretation — who has spent the last two years auditing graduate seminars in the history and philosophy of science at the university I retired from. In three weeks I am giving a talk in that department's joint colloquium with the philosophy department, and I would like your help preparing for the question period.

The talk defends two claims about incommensurability, and defends them together, which is the part I expect to be attacked.

First: incommensurability is real but local. What changes across a revolution is not the whole language of a science but the taxonomic structure of the kind-terms in a particular research domain — the partition of the world into kinds that those terms presuppose — so that some claims expressible in the old vocabulary have no translation into the new one, and conversely. This is compatible with the rationality of theory choice. But it does entail genuine Kuhn loss: some questions the abandoned framework could answer are no longer answerable, and our having ceased to care about them is evidence that the field's sense of what makes a good question changed, not evidence that nothing was lost.

Second: comparability across the change — whatever licenses us in saying the new framework is *better* rather than merely *later* — is carried neither by shared meanings nor by reference-preserving causal chains running from the old term to the world. It is carried by what I call *instrumental anchoring*: the continuity of measurement practice, of instruments, and of the calibration hierarchies that tie field readings to laboratory standards. The theory changes; the magnetometer, the travel-time curve, and the standard reference material do not. Field and laboratory practice, not the lexicon, is what makes two successive theories theories of the same world.

My case study is the one I know first-hand: the replacement of geosyncline theory by plate tectonics. I argue that the pre-revolutionary vocabulary distinguished sediment-filled troughs by their facies and their volcanic content, and read the ophiolite sequences — serpentinite, pillow basalt, chert — as deposits laid down inside such a trough; that plate tectonics reads the same rocks as obducted oceanic crust; and that in the transition a question to which geosynclinal theory had a serviceable answer — why tens of kilometres of shallow-water sediment accumulate in a single linear belt — was not answered so much as dissolved and reassigned, at some real explanatory cost that we have since forgotten because we stopped asking.

The room will be: two senior philosophers of science in the formal/Bayesian tradition, who treat theory choice as credence revision over a shared space of possibilities and will regard incommensurability as either trivially true or plainly false; one philosopher of language who works on reference and conceptual change; two people from science and technology studies, one in the symmetry tradition who will hear my "same world" as smuggled realism, and one ethnographer of scientific practice who will say calibration conventions are themselves negotiated and theory-laden and so cannot do the anchoring work I assign them; a structural realist who thinks continuity lives in the mathematics rather than in the instruments; a historian of science who will suspect my geosyncline story is a textbook caricature; and two working earth scientists — a tectonics professor and one of her graduate students — who think the whole vocabulary of incommensurability is obscurantist decoration on the plain fact that the data improved.

What I want is a prep sheet, not a defence of my position. Give me roughly eight objections, ordered by how much damage they would actually do if I fumbled them, each attributed to a specific member of that audience and phrased the way that person would phrase it, with a note on which of my two claims it targets. For each: a reply I could deliver in about ninety seconds, an indication of whether the objection has real force or is merely fashionable, and a candid note on where my reply is softest. Tell me what I should concede in the first two minutes of the talk to take the sting out of the questions, and what I must hold if the talk is to be worth giving. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Where an objection turns on a technical term a retired field scientist would not know, gloss it in a single clause in parentheses. I would rather be told that an objection is damaging than be reassured.

My own sense of the weakest joint is this: I have never been able to say what makes a calibration hierarchy *the same* hierarchy across a theory change without appealing to the very theory whose discontinuity I am arguing for, and I suspect the philosopher of language will find it in about four minutes. Please say so if you agree, and tell me what it costs me.

Please write your answer in American English.
