I need a document, not an essay. Two people I know have been going at each other for months about Dennett's tower of kinds of minds — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian — the idea being that each new kind is a new way of not having to learn things the hard way: hardwired by selection over generations, then individual trial and error with reinforcement, then testing candidate actions inside your head before you act, then importing other creatures' already-tested designs through language and imitation.

Camp One (call them the Architecturals) says the tower has four floors, full stop. Each floor is a change in *what does the prescreening*, and once cultural transmission is in place there's no further change in that respect. Writing, notation, maps, spreadsheets, code are just better memes riding on Gregorian machinery; take them away and you get a worse thinker, not a different kind of mind.

Camp Two (the Scaffolds) says there's a fifth floor they want to call "Turingian" creatures: creatures whose cognitive operations are actually performed by persistent external artifacts, in the sense that no single brain of theirs can execute the operation at all without the prop — long division, a formal proof, double-entry bookkeeping, navigating by chart. On their view the thing that does the thinking is the person plus the prop, and the props themselves improve across generations in ways no individual could rederive, so the competence isn't even in principle located in the individual.

Draft the joint statement the two camps could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want:

- a numbered section of agreed claims — about what the four kinds are and what would make something a distinct kind rather than a souped-up version of the previous one, about what human adults uncontroversially are, and, if you can get them to agree on it, about whether a large language model trained only on human text counts as a Gregorian creature, a fifth-kind thing, or neither, and why that's a real test case here rather than a distraction;
- a disagreements table, where each row is one residual disagreement with the Architectural formulation and the Scaffold formulation side by side, each phrased so that side would actually sign it — no strawmen, and no wording that quietly concedes the point;
- the cruxes, and for each crux a case or argument both sides accept as bearing on it, plus what each side predicts in advance, written down before the outcome. The one I most want pinned down is an artifact-deprivation test: raise a cohort with full language and imitation but zero external notation or measuring devices, and see which competences come back on their own and which never do. Also useful: whether removing the props degrades performance continuously or produces a cliff where the operation becomes impossible; and whether the fight about the "unit" of cognition (individual vs. individual-plus-artifact) is verbal or substantive. If you think part of the dispute is only about words, say which part and why.

End the document with a short note on how confident it is in each of its big claims, and single out the one point it's least sure about. I'd rather know where the collaboration is papering over a gap than have it read smooth.
