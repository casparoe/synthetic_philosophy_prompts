My supervisor has published on Descartes and animal minds, and over the past month she has answered a string of hypotheticals from me, always in the form "what does Descartes actually commit himself to here". Her verdicts so far, compressed:

- an ordinary mammal: no thought and no mind; everything it does is to be explained from the disposition of its organs and the course of the animal spirits;
- a bird taught to utter words: still no mind, since it does not testify that it thinks what it says;
- a machine built with the organs and outward shape of an ape: no mind, and by his own admission we would have no way of telling that it was not of the same nature as the ape;
- a machine that imitates our actions as well as we do: still no mind, because it would be found out in some cases and would act only from the disposition of its organs, whereas reason is a universal instrument that serves in all sorts of encounters;
- a human being who is severely cognitively impaired, or raving mad: mind, because a human body of the right sort is enough;
- and, contentiously, she is committed to the reading of the correspondence on which he withholds from beasts thought alone, and not life or feeling, so that on her Descartes a dog can be in pain while having no thoughts at all.

I have to present a case to her seminar in two weeks, with her in the room, and the case assigned to me is one she has never ruled on: a system built out of human text rather than flesh that responds aptly, and in novel combinations, to whatever is said to it in ordinary language — better than many people manage — but that has no body, no sense organs, no history of acting in the world, and does nothing except produce text.

What will she say Descartes is committed to here? I can think of two or three rules that fit her earlier verdicts equally well and that plainly diverge on this one, so I'm less interested in a bare answer than in which rule she is most likely running, why that rule beats the others given the record above, and which further case would actually separate the rules that agree on this one. The two candidates I have in mind are a human being who permanently fails both of the behavioural tests, and a non-human machine that passes both; tell me which of them does the discriminating work, or what third case would do it better.
