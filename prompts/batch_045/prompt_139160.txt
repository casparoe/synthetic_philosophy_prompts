I'm a high school student doing an independent project on paradoxes in decision theory, and I have gotten stuck on what looks to me like a manufactured problem: the Pasadena game.

The game, as I understand it: you toss a fair coin until it lands heads for the first time. If the first heads comes on the nth toss, you win (-1)^(n+1) · 2^n / n dollars — so +2 if it lands heads immediately, -2 if it takes two tosses, +8/3 if three, -4 if four, and so on. Each of those outcomes has probability 1/2^n, so the expected value is 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., the alternating harmonic series, which converges to ln 2, or about 69 cents.

My teacher says the game is famous because its expected value is undefined, and I don't see how that can be right, for two reasons.

First, the series does converge, to ln 2. The only argument I have heard for calling it undefined is that the series is merely conditionally convergent, so by the Riemann rearrangement theorem you can reorder the terms to sum to anything you like — 5, negative 100, a million. But reordering looks like sheer cheating to me. The outcomes have a natural order: the payoff from the game ending on the first toss, then the second, then the third. Sum them in that order and you get ln 2. A rearrangement is just a different way of writing down the very same game, and bookkeeping shouldn't change what a game is worth. So it seems to me the correct answer is that this game is worth about 69 cents, a rational person should pay up to 69 cents to play it, and that is the end of it.

Second, I don't see why this case is supposed to be interestingly different from the St. Petersburg game, where everyone agrees the expected value is infinite and where the usual lesson is that you should be willing to pay any finite amount to play. Both look like cases where the naive rule "pay up to the expected value" delivers an answer people find strange. If anything, Pasadena is the tamer one, since at least its value is a finite number. So aren't these the same puzzle in different clothes?

The one reply I have encountered and can't evaluate is that in real life nobody can actually pay out 2^n dollars for large n, so you should just cut the series off at some huge number of tosses; then the expected value is an ordinary finite number and the whole controversy evaporates.

Where am I going wrong? Please don't send me to papers, authors, or reading lists — I have no access to them and I would rather have the reasoning than the references. Give me your bottom line in the very first sentence, naming the single biggest mistake I am making, then work through the reasoning without restating my question back to me. If you can also say something about why the "just truncate the series" move does or does not settle the matter, that is the part I am most confused about.
