I am writing a lecture on Sartrean authenticity for third-year undergraduates, and I need an original case that separates the Sartrean diagnosis from two neighbouring diagnoses it is routinely conflated with. Please construct it to the following specification.

**The three views, characterised so that no reconstruction of the literature is needed:**

(i) *The Sartrean view.* An agent is in bad faith when their relation to their own freedom misdescribes it: they treat as settled by facticity what they are in fact choosing, or treat as freely chosen what is in fact merely given. Bad faith is a structure of the agent's relation to their own transcendence, not a false belief and not an inner conflict.

(ii) *A motivational account of self-deception.* The agent counts as self-deceived only if there is some belief-like state they hold against their own evidence, retain because of a desire, and which sits in tension with what their behaviour expresses.

(iii) *A wholeheartedness account of authenticity.* The agent is authentic just in case they identify with the desire that moves them, are not ambivalent about it, and would not trade it away on reflection.

**What the case must deliver.** One scenario in which (i) returns *bad faith*, (ii) returns *not self-deceived*, and (iii) returns *authentic* — all three generated from the views rather than stipulated by the story.

**Constraints.**

- Ordinary contemporary setting, a single scene, one paragraph of prose (roughly 150–200 words). No character may use philosophical vocabulary, and nothing about the agent's bad faith may be asserted by the narrator: it has to be legible in what the agent says and does.
- The agent must be genuinely wholehearted in sense (iii): no ambivalence, no divided will, no regret. The case must not work by making them unhappy, failing, or conflicted, since the point of the lecture is precisely that Sartre's notion is not a psychological-health notion or a self-knowledge notion.
- The route to bad faith cannot be the familiar one. The agent must not claim inability or necessity, must not say or imply anything like "that is just the sort of person I am," must not be offering an excuse, and must not be disavowing anything they believe.
- The agent's project must be one a third-year audience would find genuinely worthwhile, and the bad-faith verdict must not be carried by the content of the project — no one is administering anything monstrous. What does the work must be the agent's relation to the project, not the project.
- No other character in the scene should be able to see anything wrong. The case may not cheat by making the agent's self-description visibly false to their interlocutors.

**My working idea — use it, sharpen it, or replace it, but tell me which structure you ended up relying on.** Build the case out of an excess of *avowal* rather than an excess of disavowal. The agent fully owns what they do, denies nothing, claims no necessity — but treats the value they serve as something they found in the world and themselves as that value's executor, so that the moment at which the value was conferred by their own choice, and could have been otherwise, is not something they stand in any relation to at all. If that structure cannot bear the weight of all three verdicts at once, say so and say why, then use another.

**Deliverables after the scenario:**

1. Three verdicts, each argued from its own view: what (i) sees and why it says bad faith; why (ii) says no self-deception; why (iii) says authentic.
2. The single feature doing the work, plus a perturbation: alter that feature alone and show the Sartrean verdict flipping while the (ii) and (iii) verdicts stay put — and say what it flips *to*. I would like it to land on authenticity or anguish rather than on a flat "not bad faith," but tell me if it does not.
3. The strongest attack available to a defender of (ii) and (iii). I expect roughly this: that the Sartrean is simply counting a metaphysical false belief about the source of value as bad faith, in which case (ii) ought to apply after all. Answer that objection, and state what you would concede to it.
4. One sentence confirming what the case is not: that it is not solved by a false belief, an inner conflict, or a disreputable project.

Keep the whole response under 700 words.
