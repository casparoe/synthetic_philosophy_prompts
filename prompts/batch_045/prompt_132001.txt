Read the jury room of the 1957 film 12 Angry Men through the common prior assumption and the agreement theorem that goes with it. As I understand the two, the Harsanyi doctrine holds that any difference between two people's subjective probabilities should be traced to a difference in the information they have rather than to a difference in their priors; Aumann's theorem then says that agents who begin from a common prior and whose current posteriors are common knowledge cannot remain in disagreement. The film looks like a staged demonstration of both: twelve jurors sit through the same trial, announce their votes in repeated roll-calls, and end unanimous. Push that reading as far as it will go, and then audit it.

Three things I want handled. First, the roll-calls appear to satisfy the common-knowledge-of-posteriors condition while the jurors plainly still disagree; by the theorem that must mean their priors, or their rationality, or something else is not common. Which holdouts are genuine differences of prior — the bigot, the grieving father — and which are not differences of belief at all? Second, the verdict is a decision under asymmetric loss, not a probability estimate: the architect is explicit that he is not claiming the boy is innocent, only that there is a doubt. Does that mean unanimity on the verdict is compatible with persisting disagreement about the facts, so that the film's convergence is weaker evidence for the pooling story than it looks? Third, a wrinkle I will call the reporting problem: a vote is informative to the others only if they can take it as an honest report of a posterior, and the film keeps giving them reason not to — impatience, animus, personal grief all ride along with the hand-raising. Are the staged demonstrations the film's own answer to that problem, a way of turning one juror's private simulation into evidence that nobody has to take on trust? And if so, is the thesis of the film that what deliberation needs is not more votes but more demonstrations?

Be honest about where the work resists the reading. The last two holdouts are not argued into line: one is shouted down and abandoned, the other breaks down over a photograph, and the closing exchange of names implies that nothing much is shared afterwards. I want a reading that takes both the film and the formalism seriously, says where each illuminates the other, and says what the film shows about rational disagreement that the theorem cannot.

Close with a one-sentence bottom line, as the final line of your answer, in this form: Verdict: whether the film vindicates, complicates, or refutes the common prior picture of disagreement, and in which of those senses.
