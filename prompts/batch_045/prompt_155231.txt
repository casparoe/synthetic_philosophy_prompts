I'm giving a talk next month in a joint seminar on historical epistemology and philosophy of language, forty minutes plus twenty for questions, and I want to be ready for the question period. My title is something like "Decipherment fails at the level of the outer message." The thesis is an application of Hofstadter's discussion of the location of meaning in *Gödel, Escher, Bach* — the distinction between a message's frame message (that the object is a message at all, and that it is addressed to someone who can be put into the right receptive state), its outer message (the information that tells you how to decode it: what kind of mechanism, what code, what procedure), and its inner message (what it is about) — to the problem of undeciphered scripts. Roughly: I claim that in every well-documented case of a script that resisted decipherment, the missing element was never the inner message but the outer one, and that this is not a coincidence but a consequence of the fact that an inner message can be present and readable-looking while its outer message is unavailable. Etruscan is my stalking-horse: we have the outer message at the level of the alphabet, thanks to its Greek ancestry, so we can transliterate fluently; what we lack is the rest of the outer message, the grammar, and so the inner message stays out of reach even though the signs are perfectly "legible." Conversely, I argue that the frame message of an undeciphered corpus is recoverable from entirely non-semantic evidence — sign inventories that close off, directionality, positional regularities, formulaic sign-groups at fixed slots — and that once the frame is fixed the outer message can be constrained from the inside, which is what Ventris effectively did for Linear B without any bilingual. My normative payoff is what I call the outer-first criterion: a proposed decipherment should be tested by whether it predicts structural and positional features of texts withheld from the analysis, and not, as is standard practice, by whether the translations it produces sound like plausible administrative records or plausible names of gods.

The room will be about twelve people, and I know roughly what I'm walking into. Half are philosophers: a Quinean who thinks indeterminacy of translation is the deep truth here and that my three levels are a ladder I will have kicked away by the end, a Davidsonian who will want to know why the frame message is not just radical interpretation plus the principle of charity under a new name, and one Brandomian who will press me on whether "outer message" is doing normative work I haven't declared. The other half are working epigraphers and archaeologists — one Aegean specialist, one person on the Indus corpus, and a historian of decipherment who has written on Champollion — and their worry in advance is that philosophers comes in and redescribes in fancy vocabulary what is actually a grind of sign-frequency counts, conditional entropy, and contextual disambiguation. A friend of mine who works on the Indus material has already bet me that the frame/outer/inner apparatus adds exactly nothing to what her field already does, and I expect to hear something close to that from the floor. I also expect at least one objection from the observation that some of these corpora may not be writing at all, or may be forgeries, which threatens my claim that the frame message is the recoverable layer.

What I'd like from you is my prep sheet. Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get, ordered by how much damage they'd do rather than by how clever they are, each phrased the way the person who raises it would actually phrase it — a statistician's objection about what positional evidence can and cannot support should not sound like a Davidsonian's. For each: what I should concede outright, what I must hold or the thesis collapses, a reply I could deliver in about two minutes without notes, and an honest note on where that reply is soft. I want them separated into the ones with real force that I need to answer and the ones that are dialectical noise I can dispatch in a sentence, and I'd like to know which of my own examples is the weakest link — I suspect the Etruscan case, since a critic could say I've just relabeled "we don't know the language" as "we don't have the outer message." Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, with a sketch of what an answer would even have to look like if it does get asked.
