I am writing a novel whose narrator resolves every morning to spend the day on her book, never once revises that judgement — she would still say, at four in the afternoon, that finishing it matters more than anything else she could do with the hours — and yet, knowing in advance precisely how the day will go, spends it on something else. She also refuses the obvious commitment devices: not through forgetfulness or laziness, but because she thinks that a writer who must lock herself in a room without a telephone has already admitted something disgraceful about herself.

I had been treating this as weakness of will. Then I read that this sort of thing is merely hyperbolic discounting, and that since what she prefers at the moment of action is the nearer reward, she is not weak-willed at all — only time-inconsistent, with a scheduling problem that a commitment device would dissolve. That conclusion worries me: it seems to make the phenomenon vanish altogether, and to entail that no one who yields to temptation ever really acts against her own better judgement.

Am I right to resist it? Does hyperbolic discounting actually show that there is no such thing as akrasia properly so called — and if it does not, what would a novelist have to add to make a character's failure genuinely akratic rather than merely imprudent, or merely a case of changing her mind? I should be grateful for an answer in British English.
