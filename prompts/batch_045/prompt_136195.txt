I'm applying for a six-month independent research grant — part time, call it 15 hrs a week, from one of those foundations that funds work on reasoning and judgement and doesn't make you get IRB approval — and I need to turn a half-formed idea I've been chewing on for a couple years into a two or three page proposal. The topic is the inside view / outside view distinction and epistemic modesty. I've been reading this stuff since the old blog days and I keep feeling the standard advice bottoms out one step too early: everyone agrees you should look at the base rate for cases like yours and then adjust a bit toward the details of your own case; everyone agrees that picking the reference class is where you can quietly launder whatever conclusion you already wanted (I've started calling this reference-class laundering); and then the discussion basically stops. Also, the whole apparatus seems to get used almost entirely in one direction — to deflate people who are too optimistic — and I can't tell whether that's just a fact about human overconfidence or whether there's some principled reason a base rate should only ever pull an estimate down.

My half-formed idea, which I honestly can't tell is deep or is just a relabeling, is this: the outside view gets its authority from model error rather than parameter error. When an inside-view estimate is wrong, it usually isn't that you misjudged the magnitude of factors you'd correctly identified; it's that you have the wrong set of factors, i.e. you're in the wrong model. On that reading the base rate isn't direct evidence about your outcome, it's evidence about which model you're probably in, and the usual anchor-on-the-base-rate-and-adjust-slightly is a crude approximation to averaging over models — fine as a heuristic when the models can't be enumerated, but something you should replace with something more explicit when they can. If that's right, two upshots I want the proposal to exploit: the outside view's force should be symmetric (evidence about which model you're in can push an estimate up, not just down), and it should be strongest exactly where your causal model of the situation is thinnest, which is a different thing from where you're most overconfident or most emotionally invested. The other thread I want woven in is the self-undermining worry, though I don't want it to become a regress chapter: if the real content of the outside view is that your first-personal evidence about this case is less diagnostic than it feels to you, why doesn't that apply to my judgment that this is a case where I should defer, to my choice of reference class, and to my judgment about how much to defer? Where does it bottom out, and is the answer "nowhere, but the regress converges" or "at some fixed point you're allowed to trust yourself"?

There are four cases I want the eventual account to handle, and here are my current gut verdicts so you can see what you're fitting: (1) a contractor quoting his forty-first kitchen remodel, with his own spreadsheet of the previous forty — I think the inside view should mostly win here, and I'm not sure the standard advice says that; (2) a third-year PhD student who thinks she's eighteen months from finishing — I think the outside view should mostly win; (3) a founder who has signed letters of intent covering a year of revenue, up against a terrible base rate for startups — I think her private information is real, but I have no idea how much it should move her, and "every founder thinks they have private information" feels like both a great point and a way of never updating on anything; (4) a five-person team where each person takes the outside view on their own piece and then the estimates get multiplied into something absurdly pessimistic — I'm confident the multiplication is wrong, but not at all confident what the right aggregation is.
