I work on a four-person team that maintains a deployed reasoning system which is retrained every few weeks. The change we are weighing — and the reason this has stopped being an idle worry for me — is to let the currently deployed system perform the pre-deployment sign-off on the next version of itself, instead of one of us signing off by hand. A colleague argued that Löb's theorem rules this out in principle. His argument generalises so aggressively that I now suspect it breaks somewhere, but I cannot say where, and I would rather find the break before I take it to the team.

Let me fix notation. Let T be a theory standing in for a system's reasoning: strong enough to encode its own syntax, to verify individual proofs, and to prove the diagonal lemma. Write □_S A for "there is an S-proof of A". To trust a source S, in the sense that matters to us, is to be willing to accept A on the strength of □_S A. Löb's theorem, as I am using it: for any sentence A, if T proves □_T A → A, then T proves A. Consequently no consistent T proves every instance of its own reflection schema □_T A → A.

The argument, as he gave it:

(1) A consistent T cannot accept the reflection schema for its own provability predicate; that is just Löb's theorem plus the observation that if every instance were provable, every sentence would be.

(2) The obstacle transfers upward. Suppose T accepts □_S A → A for every A, and suppose T can prove that S extends T — that every T-proof is an S-proof, which is a finite syntactic check when you hold the successor's source code. Then for every A, T proves □_T A → □_S A → A, so T accepts its own reflection schema, and by (1) T is inconsistent. So no consistent agent grants blanket trust to any source it can prove is at least as strong as itself.

(3) Successors are at least as strong, and provably so. Any revision that preserves the current inference machinery and adds to it — more search, longer training, a wider context, an extra derived rule — is an extension; and if the agent writes or inspects the revision, it can prove the extension. A second pass by the very same system is exactly as strong. So blanket self-trust, blanket successor-trust, and blanket trust in a duplicate are all unavailable.

(4) Trust is only ever load-bearing where derivation fails. The entire point of relying on a source is to rely on it for the A you cannot yourself derive; if T could derive A, the source would be redundant. So the case in which trust matters is exactly the case in which (2) forbids it. No agent can rationally rely on any source of its own strength or greater for anything it cannot already establish on its own.

(5) Hence: a system gains nothing epistemically from running itself twice, or from a vote among identical copies, since each copy is barred from trusting the others by (2) and (3); no agent can rely on its own future self, so any plan whose execution depends on a conclusion not yet derived is irrational, and deliberation across time collapses; and, applied to us, I cannot take my having proved p as any reason at all to believe p, which would make mathematical practice, refereeing, and the use of proof assistants irrational.

(6) (5) is unlivable, and its middle clause is contradicted by the fact that long-horizon systems do in fact work. So the argument establishes far more than that we must sign off by hand, and something between (1) and (5) is wrong.

I should also tell you what we had planned to do instead, because my real question is whether the argument reaches it. Call it capped ratification. Rather than blanket trust, the sign-off rule accepts from S only those A admitting an S-proof shorter than a bound n fixed at design time, and only those A of quantifier complexity below a fixed rank k. We reasoned that the sentence the diagonal lemma produces — the one that says of itself that its provability implies A — need not fall inside a class capped in either of those ways, so the diagonal step behind (1) and (2) would have nothing to bite on, and the collapse would not go through. We also took it as given that for a fixed n a system can confirm by a finite check that there is no proof of absurdity shorter than n, which is what made us think the cap yields something genuine rather than merely stipulative.

Where I need help: does the argument as stated actually reach capped ratification, and if not, which step fails to reach it? More generally, name the step you would reject, explain why it looks unobjectionable, and price the alternatives — in particular, what I would be giving up if I rejected (4) rather than (3), and whether rejecting (4) merely concedes that trust is for cheapness and error-correction rather than for new logical strength. I would like to keep (1) and (2) if I can, since they seem to be the part with a theorem behind them, and I would sooner abandon (4) than (3). If the real cost lands somewhere I have not listed — in the definition of trust, or in the move from the schema to individual instances — say so, and say what the neighbouring positions each have to pay.
