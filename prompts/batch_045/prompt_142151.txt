We run a joint reading group -- half my lab (molecular evolution, mostly chemostat work) and half philosophy of biology grad students -- and we've spent something like a year on what a biological function is, and it has completly stalled. Every meeting is the same exchange: the causal-role side (Cummins style: a part's function is its contribution to some complex capacity of the containing system that we've singled out) and the etiological side (Wright's it's-there-because-it-does-that formulation, Millikan's and Neander's selected-effects version, Godfrey-Smith's modern-history variant) each say their piece, trade the three standard objections across the table -- causal-role is too permissive and interest-relative, so the nose has the function of holding up glasses and the heart has the function of making thumping noises; etiological can't assign a function to a brand-new trait and makes function hostage to historical facts nobody can measure -- and then we go home. Nobody's mind has changed, and I genuinely can't tell what would change anybody's mind.

Two cases from our own bench keep coming up and they're the ones I want diagnosed.

First: a plasmid-borne toxin-antitoxin addiction system. The toxin is stable, the antitoxin gets degraded fast, so a daughter cell that fails to inherit a plasmid copy is killed by the toxin still hanging around in it. Everyone in the lab says the antitoxin's function is to neutralize the toxin. But the toxin is only there because the plasmid brought it -- the module manufactures the problem it then solves, mafia-protection style. And the unit whose reproduction is served is arguably the plasmid: the locus is there because plasmid-bearing lineages out-reproduce plasmid-free ones, which is selection at a level below the cell, while at the cell level the whole module is neutral-to-slightly-costly. So depending on what you take as the containing system and what level you take selection to act at, you get different functions out of the same three genes.

Second: a sweep we're watching right now. A mutation that up-regulates a promiscuous efflux pump arose ~400 generations back, it's at 60% and climbing, and we know the effect driving the sweep is pumping out a toxic metabolite. My benchmates say its function is obviously efflux and it's had it since the day it arose; the selected-effects people say not yet, or only derivatively -- wait for the differential reproduction to accumulate. And then there's our engineered toggle switch, where a repressor has a function purely in virtue of what we designed it to do, with no selection history at all.

My hypothesis is that this isn't one disagreement: the two camps want the word to do two different jobs, one earning its keep inside a mechanistic account of how a system currently works, the other inside a historical explanation that can also underwrite malfunction and teleology, and every textbook case -- hearts, eyes, hemoglobin -- is a case where history and current contribution line up, so the ordinary data can't separate them. A couple of people in the group think it's a substantive fight about which notion biology actually needs, and one postdoc thinks it's just an empirical question about what the selection history in fact was.

I don't want a survey of the accounts. I want a diagnosis of the stalemate: verbal, substantive, two questions under one label, a hidden empirical question (levels of selection?), or bedrock intuitions nobody can argue for. Run the accounts on my two cases, and tell me if either case is rigged -- only looks hard because of an ambiguity rather than a real fault line. Then, given whatever you think this is, what would count as progress here: a distinction, a formal result, some empirical finding, a change of question? Or is there a reason to expect none?
