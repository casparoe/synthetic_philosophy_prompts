Analyze the Watergate scandal through the lens of Augustine's theory of lying. I am not looking for a general essay on Watergate or a summary of Augustine; I want a disciplined case analysis that runs in both directions. First, state the theory and use it to map the episode: what the theory says the actors faced, what it says they should have done, and which features of the cover-up it explains or predicts. Then let the episode test the theory: say which parts of Watergate the theory illuminates, which it misdescribes or cannot see, and whether the case is a confirmation, an anomaly, or a kind of case Augustine's individualistic account never anticipated.

The episode, with the facts I want you to assume and use: In June 1972, burglars with links to President Richard Nixon's reelection organization were arrested inside the Democratic National Committee headquarters in the Watergate office complex in Washington, D.C. Over the next two years, Nixon and senior administration officials acted to conceal the connection and the broader campaign of political sabotage: they destroyed and concealed evidence, raised and paid money to the burglars to secure their silence, made false statements to the FBI, to congressional investigators, to reporters, and to the public, and urged the CIA to restrain the FBI's investigation. A Senate investigating committee and investigative reporters uncovered the cover-up; it then emerged that Nixon had secretly recorded many conversations in the Oval Office. After the Supreme Court ordered the tapes released, a recording made days after the break-in showed Nixon discussing a plan to have the CIA limit the FBI's inquiry. With impeachment by the House and removal by the Senate near certain, Nixon resigned in August 1974. For the analysis, I also want you to assume a number of sociological claims that are common ground rather than contested: some participants later insisted they had not lied, on the ground that they had merely withheld information, that their denials were literally true, or that they could not remember; others justified false statements as necessary to protect the presidency, national security, or the country from scandal; and still others claimed to have been following orders or to have remained deliberately uninformed about the details.

Augustine's theory, as I want it applied, has four parts. First, a lie in the strict sense is a statement of something the speaker believes to be false, made with the intention to deceive another person; a false statement made by mistake is not a lie, and neither is a true statement that misleads, if the speaker does not intend to deceive. Second, every lie is a sin; no lie is permissible, not even a lie told to save an innocent life or to avert a great public harm. Third, lies are not equally grave: Augustine ranks them, so that lies told from malice or to injure are worse than lies told to help someone, and a charitable act done alongside a lie may be rewarded for its good motive even while the lie itself remains blameworthy. Fourth, because the wrong of lying lies in the will to deceive, silence, concealment of the truth, and the use of ambiguous but literally true language are not lies in the strict sense, though they may be sinful for other reasons and are not thereby licensed as deception.

Your analysis should move through the following questions. Classify the central acts of the cover-up under this theory: the public denials of involvement, the secret payments to the burglars, the destruction of evidence, the instructions to the CIA, the false testimony to investigators, and the later claims of failed memory or technical truth. Which of these are lies in the Augustinian sense, and which are merely concealment, evasion, or reckless speech? How does Augustine rank the lies of Nixon and his aides? Are they closer to officious lies, told to protect a person or institution the speaker regards as a good, or to harmful lies that injure the polity and pervert justice? Does the political end they served—winning reelection, protecting the office of the presidency—mitigate their gravity at all on Augustine's scheme, given his claim that no good end can make a lie permissible?

Next, test the intention condition against the pathology of self-deception and willful ignorance. I want you to consider a strategy I will call motivated nescience: a participant in the conspiracy deliberately avoids learning the incriminating details, or arranges to be insulated from them, so that he can later say that he had not been involved, that he knew of no cover-up, or that he could not remember the details, without at the moment of utterance believing those statements to be false. Under Augustine's definition, is motivated nescience a lie, given the speaker's lack of belief in the falsity of what he says? If it is not a lie because the will to deceive is absent or divided, is it nevertheless sinful, and how severely? Does Augustine's framework treat the prior choice to remain ignorant as itself the morally significant act, and if so, how?

Then turn to institutional agency. The Watergate cover-up was a distributed practice: statements were drafted by aides, voiced by press spokesmen, and ratified by a president who often dealt in hints and deniability; plausible deniability was a designed feature, not a bug. Augustine's account is individualistic and centers on a single speaker's belief and intention to deceive. Does it have the resources to diagnose a manifestly deceptive institutional practice in which no single person makes a false statement with the full intention to deceive, or is this an anomaly the theory cannot parse without extension? If you extend the theory to cover institutional mendacity, does the extension preserve the core claim that all lying is sinful, or does it force a distinction between individual lying and corporate or official deception that Augustine would not have accepted?

Finally, assess the case in the other direction. Is Watergate a confirmation of Augustine's theory, in that the cover-up was a cascade of lies whose destructive consequences vindicate his severity? Is it an anomaly, in that motivated nescience and distributed institutional lying strain an account built on discrete individual speech acts? Or is it a case the theory never anticipated, because Augustine wrote for a world of face-to-face speakers and had no developed account of bureaucratic presidency, mass media, or party-political justification? Be explicit about where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak; where you speculate, say that you are speculating. Avoid anachronism: Augustine was not a democrat and did not frame political authority in terms of popular sovereignty, so do not attribute to him a modern ideal of democratic accountability unless you flag it as your own. What you should give me is a mapping of actors, beliefs, intentions, and statements onto the theory, followed by an honest verdict on what Watergate does to Augustinian thinking about lying.
