I write a column on science and ideas, and the same quarrel keeps resurfacing in my reporting in different costumes: the evidence desk of a newsroom deciding whether to run a leaked dataset of uncertain provenance; a clinicians' group arguing about whether to trust a case series before the assay exists; founders and organizers who say that without a commitment made in advance the evidence never gets produced at all. Underneath all of them sits the old dispute between the Cliffordian position (a belief has to be earned; the shipowner "had no right to believe on such evidence as was before him," and the wrong attaches to its provenance — "the question of right or wrong has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it; not what it was, but how he got it") and the Jamesian position (where an option is "a genuine option when it is of the forced, living and momentous kind" and the intellect cannot settle it, the passional nature may decide, and abstaining is itself a decision carrying the same risk of losing the truth).

I would like to publish the document an honest adversarial collaboration between those two camps would produce, rather than another he-said-she-said piece. Please draft it.

Constraints. Do not adjudicate between the two positions and do not dissolve the disagreement into "each is partly right." The test of every line is whether a competent, stubborn member of the camp in question could sign it without feeling misrepresented. No citations, footnotes, bibliographies, or suggestions for further reading: work from the concepts and from the cases I supply below rather than from the literature. Define every technical term in-line so a general reader can follow, and keep the whole document under about 1,200 words.

Structure.

1. A two- or three-sentence preamble stating what the document is, who is signing it, and what it is and is not claiming to settle.

2. A numbered section of agreed claims — eight to ten, I would think. I want claims with real content: not "evidence matters," but claims such that one party could coherently have denied them. For each one where it is not obvious, add a half-sentence on what each side gives up by signing it. Among the things I would expect to see treated here: the shipowner case (both condemn him; what exactly is the Jamesian's reason for saying his case is not theirs?); whether the dispute is about the permissibility of belief, the psychology of belief-formation, or the governance of inquiry over time; whether both sides permit acting on a proposition one does not believe; whether there really are cases in which the evidence available later depends on the attitude adopted now (I take James's chemist — "A chemist finds an hypothesis live enough to spend a year in its verification: he believes in it to that extent" — to be one such case, and I take Clifford's duty of inquiry to concede that there are); and what work is being done by the words belief, evidence, sufficient, and option in the two accounts.

3. A disagreements table: one row per residual disagreement, with the Cliffordian's own formulation in one column and the Jamesian's in another, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording, plus a column saying whether the disagreement is genuine or merely verbal (and if verbal, what reformulation dissolves it).

4. A cruxes table — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each crux: what would settle it; the concrete case or piece of evidence both sides agree would bear on it; and each side's prediction written down in advance. Here are the cruxes I suspect matter, which you should revise, merge, or replace as you see fit (and if you think one of them is not a crux at all because settling it would move neither side, say so and say why):

(a) The availability-of-more-inquiry crux. The Cliffordian wants to say that in almost every real case there is further inquiry available, so the case is always a shipowner case: the belief was acquired "not by honestly earning it in patient investigation, but by stifling his doubts." The Jamesian wants to say that under deadlines and non-repeatable opportunities, waiting is not epistemically free. Case: the ICU team in the first fortnight of a novel respiratory illness, with no validated assay, where believing the hypothesis now changes cohorting, sampling and staff behaviour and thereby changes whether the hypothesis gets confirmed at all. What do both accept as bearing on this, and what does each predict?

(b) The substitute-attitude crux. Is there an attitude other than belief — a working hypothesis, a research protocol, acceptance-for-the-purposes-of-inquiry — that does everything the Jamesian needs? Both should agree the answer decides a lot: if yes, the Jamesian thesis either collapses into evidentialism-with-acceptance or turns out to have been verbal. Case: the founder who must commit to a therapy platform before the pivotal experiment, where the commitment is what buys the money and the money is what buys the evidence. Predictions: does belief add anything over protocol-plus-money, and if so does it show up as better evidence later or as worse reading of bad news later?

(c) The self-referential crux. Take propositions whose truth depends on whether enough people believe them (a mutual-aid fund's solvency, a movement's viability, an institution's survival). The Cliffordian will say evidentialism absorbs these: the belief-sensitivity is already in the evidence, so there is no exception, only a practical limitation. I suspect there is a residue the Cliffordian cannot absorb, in the first person: an agent cannot treat her own adopting of the belief as evidence that the belief is true, so the bookkeeping the Cliffordian needs is unavailable from the inside. Is this a real residue or a confusion? Case: contribution rates in a collective-action setting where free-riding is possible — do agents who merely signal commitment sustain the same participation as agents who believe, over a long enough horizon?

(d) The closure-of-inquiry crux. Clifford's side inherits a duty of inquiry that looks open-ended; institutions (regulators, editors, clinicians) must close inquiries on fixed dates and issue findings stronger than the evidence strictly supports. Is a norm of closure part of the ethics of belief or external to it, and does either side's view have anything to say about when it is permissible?

(e) The stakes crux. Does the momentousness of an option permissibly lower the evidentiary bar for belief, or does it only lower the bar for action while the bar for belief stays fixed? Case: a one-time opportunity in the style of Nansen's expedition, where declining is irreversible.

5. A closing paragraph in which each side states, in its own words, what would count against its own view — the finding that would make a Cliffordian concede that evidentialism is too strict, and the finding that would make a Jamesian concede that the permission is empty or that it licenses what Clifford's island agitators did. Then one sentence, signed by both, on what is left over.
