**The question (25 marks).** Kinked-threshold negative utilitarianism (KTNU) is the following package:

(i) There is an intensity threshold T for suffering. Any suffering at or above T lexically dominates any amount of pleasure and any amount of suffering below T.
(ii) Below T, suffering still outweighs pleasure, but at a fixed, Archimedean exchange rate: enough mild pleasure can compensate for enough mild suffering.
(iii) T is fixed by a non-compensability test: T is the level of suffering that a person could not rationally accept at any price, i.e. for any finite amount of compensation.

(a) Explain what it is for the dominance in (i) to be lexical rather than merely a very large weighting, and say what (i) alone implies about a choice between preventing one person's hour of suffering at intensity T and preventing a million mild headaches. (b) Is KTNU committed to denying that pleasure and pain are commensurable? Distinguish commensurability from cardinal measurability, and say whether the view needs either. (c) Consider the "scatter" case: a million people each suffer a pain at 0.9T, while one person suffers at T. Does (i) settle which outcome is worse? Does the view need interpersonal comparisons of suffering intensity? And does clause (ii) do any real work, or does the threshold render everything below it decision-irrelevant?

**The answer.** Lexical dominance just means: two tiers, and no trade between them. Suffering at or above T and anything below T are not weighed against each other at all — there is no finite amount of sub-threshold suffering, or of pleasure, that adds up to one hour at T. The contrast with a large finite weighting is that with a huge-but-finite weight the pile eventually wins: if an hour at T is worth, say, a trillion mild headaches, then two trillion headaches beat it. With lexical priority there is no such number, which is why (a) has an easy answer: prevent the hour at T, and it doesn't matter whether the other option is a million headaches or a googol of them. Note that this doesn't yet tell you to prevent the T-suffering come what may: if preventing it caused two hours at T somewhere else, then the top tier is compared first and you lose. Priority orders the tiers; it doesn't select outcomes on its own.

On (b): yes, KTNU is committed to treating pleasure and pain as incommensurable, at least above the threshold. That is what the dominance in (i) says — there is no single hedonic currency in which an hour at T and any quantity of pleasure can be weighed, because if there were, a large enough quantity of pleasure would eventually outweigh it. Clause (iii) makes the point vivid: to say that no amount of compensation could make the suffering acceptable just is to say that suffering and compensation are not measurable against one another. And this is an advantage of the view rather than a cost, since it means KTNU does not require a common cardinal scale of hedonic intensity and so escapes the familiar worry that suffering-focused views need implausibly precise cardinal measurement of how bad a given pain is. If pleasure and pain were commensurable there would have to be determinate exchange rates between them, and no one has ever produced one.

On (c): the scatter case is settled too, and it is settled in favour of the single person. Clause (iii) anchors the threshold in what one sufferer could not rationally accept, so the threshold is defined over the individual experience rather than over totals, and the million sub-threshold pains cannot be pooled into something that outweighs the one pain at T. So KTNU is not an aggregative view above the threshold — and, more importantly, it does not need interpersonal comparisons of suffering at all: the only comparison that ever does any work is between the tiers within a given sufferer's experience, which is why suffering-focused views are sometimes said to sidestep the interpersonal comparability problem entirely. It also follows that clause (ii) is very nearly idle in practice. In almost any real decision there is somebody, somewhere, above T, so the top tier will differ between the options on the table and the sub-threshold exchange rate will almost never get to break a tie. That is a slightly odd result — the view advertises itself as a threshold view with a kink, but on this reading the threshold does all the work and the kink does none — though it is a consequence of taking lexical priority seriously, not a defect in the view.

One thing I would flag myself: T is vague. Where exactly the non-compensability line falls looks like a sorites problem, and nothing in (i)–(iii) says how to locate it.
