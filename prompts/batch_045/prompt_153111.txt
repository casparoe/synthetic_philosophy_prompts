I'm teaching an undergraduate seminar that pairs W. E. B. Du Bois's 1926 essay on art as propaganda with Ralph Ellison's novel Invisible Man, and I want a close reading that takes both the novel and Du Bois's argument seriously enough to let each complicate the other, rather than settling for the standard classroom cliché that Ellison simply rejected the propagandistic protest fiction Du Bois demanded. As I understand Du Bois, his thesis is not just the empty sociological claim that every artwork has some effect on someone; it is the normative claim that artists, and especially Black artists working under white supremacy, must accept that their work is always in the business of shaping belief, desire, and perception, and must therefore take responsibility for the propaganda they make, while also insisting that propaganda in the service of what he regarded as truth and human flourishing is categorically different from propaganda in the service of lies and dehumanization.

To get my students past the exhausted art-for-art's-sake-versus-protest-literature binary, I've been developing a distinction in my own notes that I want you to test. I use the term directive propaganda for any artwork whose primary aim is to get its audience to accept a specific proposition, party line, or call to action; I use the term propaganda of the sensorium for any artwork that, without necessarily issuing directives, reshapes what its audience can perceive, enjoy, imagine, or love, and thereby changes the conditions under which later beliefs and actions become possible. I suspect Du Bois's real interest is in the second category: when he insists that Black folk must gain the freedom to experience love and joy as part of full humanity, he is asking for more than novels that denounce Jim Crow, and he is also asking for something different from art that is merely beautiful in a raceless way. Read Invisible Man through this distinction and through Du Bois's essay.

In particular, I want you to examine at least three moments: the prologue, in which the invisible narrator lights his basement with dozens of purloined light bulbs and insists he is not a spoiler or a revolutionary; the paint factory episode, in which the narrator's technical precision accidentally produces an explosion after he has been told to make the paint whiter than white; and the narrator's final seclusion, where he remains in his underground hole writing the very book we are reading rather than returning to the Brotherhood or to Harlem's streets. Does the novel vindicate Du Bois, showing that even a work that mocks directives is still reshaping the sensorium of its readers? Does it complicate Du Bois by suggesting that the most politically indispensable propaganda may be propaganda the artist refuses to own as propaganda, or that a work can be duplicitously directive while pretending only to cultivate perception? Where does Ellison's novel resist the Du Boisian reading altogether, say, in the narrator's refusal to be a representative Black voice, in the novel's treatment of the Brotherhood's tracts, or in its suspicion that any claim to speak for the race is already a kind of capture? Please don't quote the novel verbatim; paraphrase its major scenes and analyze them instead. End by saying what the novel adds to the philosophical debate about art and propaganda that neither Du Bois's essay nor a purely theoretical distinction like mine can supply on its own.
