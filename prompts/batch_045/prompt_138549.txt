You are a decision theorist and philosopher of action who has published on rational choice under cognitive and temporal constraints. Think carefully before answering: open with a short brainstorming pass in which you list candidate requirements and then strike the ones that are really only preferences of one research program, and only afterwards give me the polished list.

I want a specification, not a survey. I am drafting a paper whose claim is that bounded rationality is still a slogan rather than a theory, and before I propose anything I need the standard any successor would have to meet. So: write the desiderata that a normative theory of choice for a bounded agent must satisfy, where the purpose is fixed as first-personal — the theory is to be good enough to tell the agent herself what to do, in deliberation, and not merely to grade her from the outside after the fact.

Give the case or consideration behind each requirement. Among those I expect you to address: the substantive/procedural distinction, and whether the theory may collapse it; the regress that arises because deciding how long to think is itself a decision made under the same constraints, so that any rule of the form keep computing until marginal cost equals marginal expected gain must explain how the agent estimates the value of further computing without having already done it; the currency problem, i.e., in what unit computational cost is denominated and whose budget it is charged to; assessability without knowing the answer, so that a procedure that luckily succeeds in one environment is not thereby vindicated; the permissibility of rationally adopting rules or commitments that make the agent worse at local optimisation; and budget plasticity, the fact that attention, memory, and skill are things the agent can invest in, so the constraints cannot be treated as a fixed exogenous parameter.

I also want you to add a requirement I have been toying with and tell me whether it is genuine or spurious: call it grain-invariance, the demand that the theory's verdict about a choice not flip when the very same problem is redrawn with options or deliberation steps individuated more finely, since the cost of computing scales with how finely the problem is described.

Then: flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most work in excluding otherwise plausible candidates. Keep any scoring of the familiar families of views — expected utility with a computation-cost term, Simonian satisficing, fast-and-frugal or resource-rational approaches — to a few lines; the list is the deliverable. And tell me whether satisficing is a candidate theory at all, or only a description of what any adequate theory will have to predict.
