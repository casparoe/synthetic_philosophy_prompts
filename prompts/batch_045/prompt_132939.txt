DRAFT: Grain, Fusion, and Creep

§1. Let OE be the familiar thesis that, necessarily, for any person x and any gamete-pair G, if x actually originates from G then x could not have originated from any gamete-pair distinct from G. I want to hold OE to two jobs it is standardly asked to do. First (call this the intuitive job): it is supposed to explain the pull of the thought that one could not have come from an altogether different sperm and egg, the gametes of two unrelated people. Second (call this the Permutation job): it is supposed to rule out, in a case in which one zygote gives rise to two people A and B, that the person who is in fact A might have been the person who is in fact B. I will argue that no single reading of origin does both jobs, and that the two most promising repairs fail for related reasons.

§2. The problem of grain. In an ordinary monozygotic twinning case, both twins originate, in the coarse sense, from the very same gamete-pair: the pair that formed the zygote which then divided. So OE, read coarsely, is perfectly compatible with Permutation. Had A been B, A would still have originated from that pair. If the essentialist wants the Permutation job done, origins must be individuated finely, by the particular daughter cell or cell mass from which the organism's development proceeded: A from the left daughter cell, B from the right. Call this OE-fine. But OE-fine faces what I will call the discrimination dilemma. Stipulate that the division is symmetric and that the two daughter cells are qualitatively indiscernible and symmetrically situated at the moment of the split. Then the claim that A's origin is the left cell and B's is the right cell is either brute or circular. It is brute if it rests on a haecceitistic fact about which cell is which, in which case the gametes have dropped out of the story and OE-fine is no longer an origin thesis at all but the thesis that transworld identity is primitive. It is circular if it rests on facts about the later development, since the later development in question just is A's development or B's. Worse, OE-fine seems to generalize: if the identity of the originating cell is essential to A, why not the timing of the division, or the local chemical environment in the hours after it? If it does generalize, OE-fine collapses into full causal-history essentialism, on which no ordinary counterfactual about my own life is strictly about me at all.

I should note the obvious escape route, since I do not think I have closed it: the essentialist may simply embrace haecceitism as a commitment rather than a cost, and may combine the collapse into causal-history essentialism with the familiar move of prising apart identity from what matters in survival, so that ordinary counterfactuals come out true in the sense that matters even though no possible world strictly contains me with a different causal history. See question (iv) below.

§3. Interim conclusion: there is no stable grain at which to state OE. Coarse is too weak for the twinning case. Fine is either haecceitistic in a way that renders the gamete talk idle, or strong enough to make all ordinary self-directed modality false. The remainder of the paper tries to relocate the content of the dispute, and argues that no relocation works.

§4. Fusion. Human tetragametic chimerism is actual, if rare: two ova are fertilized by two sperm, the resulting zygotes aggregate at the zygote or blastocyst stage, and a single organism develops with intermingled cell lines. (If I have the biology wrong, say so, but please treat the case as a stipulation in any event.) Let C be a person who develops this way, from gamete-pairs G1 and G2, and stipulate that the case is symmetric or near enough. What is C's origin? Taking G1 alone, or G2 alone, is arbitrary. Relocating the origin to the fusion event generalises OE so that origins need not be gamete-pairs, but it reimports the grain problem one level up: how finely is the fusion individuated, and would C fail to exist if it had occurred thirty seconds later or with the two masses in a different orientation? The remaining option is to let origins be plural. I claim this is the only stable option, and that it forces the essentialist to give up uniqueness in favour of what I call the disjunctive thesis:

(DOE) Necessarily, x originates from at least one member of a non-empty set S of gamete-pairs, where S is the set of pairs that actually initiated x's lineage, and x originates from no pair outside S.

DOE retains the intuitive job: it still rules out my having originated from the gametes of two unrelated people. But DOE raises a cardinality question. If two initiating pairs, why not three, or forty? If the cardinality of S is itself essential, then a person's modal profile is fixed by a cytological accident of the most gratuitous sort. If it is not essential, DOE owes us a principle saying which variations in S are permitted, and I cannot see what that principle could be other than a disguised return to the arbitrariness of the first option or to full causal-history essentialism.

§5. Creep. Here is a case designed to make grain individuation look hopeless. Let embryo E develop from gamete-pair G. By a series of precise interventions, replace the cells of the G lineage with cells descended from G*, one cell at a time, in an order and at a rate that leaves the developmental trajectory unchanged; let G* be a pair whose genome differs from G's at a handful of selectively neutral loci, so that each individual replacement is developmentally inert. Continue until no somatic cell of the organism descends from G, and extend the replacement to the germline so that the resulting adult's gametes also derive from G*. Call the endpoint organism's person P*, and call the person who would otherwise have developed from E simply P. There is pressure to say that each step preserves the person, since each step is by stipulation inert. There is also pressure to say that the endpoint is not P, since every version of OE entails that a person all of whose cells descend from G* did not originate from G. Three replies are available: deny that the person survives the series at all, and locate destruction at some threshold step; accept survival throughout and reject OE; or treat the series as a sorites on identity, so that there is no fact of the matter where in the series P ceases to be present. I want to defend the third reply, and to draw from it a moral: OE may be true as a necessary claim while our intuitions about origins have no determinate object, precisely because we have no determinate handle on the grain at which origins are individuated. The difficulty is that the third reply requires vague identity, and there is a well-known short argument that vague identity is incoherent, turning on the distinction between its being indeterminate whether a is identical with b and a's being indeterminately identical with b. I want to know whether that argument bites here: it may be that what is indeterminate is not whether P* is P, but which person the name in the case picks out, and these may come apart.

§6. The questions I most want answered:

(i) Can fine-grained origins be individuated non-haecceitistically and non-circularly, by a primitive this-lineage relation or by the organism's spatiotemporal path? And if they can, does the resulting view still deserve the name of an origin thesis, or has it become the view that what is essential to me is my organism's life?

(ii) Is the fusion case a genuine threat to uniqueness, or is relocation to the fusion event cheap? If it is cheap, what does that relocation cost when applied to organisms that never fused?

(iii) Is the creep case independent of the cardinality worry in §4, or the same problem in temporal dress? If it is the same problem, I should merge the sections, and I would like your view on which framing is more perspicuous.

(iv) Does prising identity apart from what matters in survival let the fine-grained essentialist have everything she wants, including the truth of ordinary counterfactuals about herself? And if it does, is the resulting dispute with the anti-essentialist merely verbal? More generally, how would you test whether a dispute is merely verbal when the two sides disagree over haecceitistic facts rather than over qualitative ones?
