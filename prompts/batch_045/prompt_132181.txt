I write a monthly column for a patient-facing health newsletter, and much of what I do is evolutionary framing for ordinary complaints: why a fever is usually doing something useful rather than merely making you miserable, why nausea in early pregnancy may be protective rather than a malfunction, why a body shaped by scarcity handles an all-you-can-eat buffet so badly. A reader — a retired biology teacher, writing at length and with great confidence, and not at all impolitely — sent a reply saying, in effect, that this whole style of explanation was demolished decades ago, and I'd like his summary of the demolition audited before I either change how I write or write back. His version goes roughly like this: Gould and Lewontin's 1979 paper showed that adaptationist explanations are unfalsifiable, because for any trait at all you can invent a story about why it was useful to our ancestors, and there is no way to test the story — that is why they called it the Panglossian paradigm and why the field treats "just-so story" as a term of abuse. Their positive claim, he says, was that most traits are not adaptations at all but spandrels: architectural byproducts of how an organism is built, like the curved spaces left over when you set a dome on arches, which means the real explanation of biological form lies in developmental constraints rather than in selection. Their methodological demand, on his account, was that adaptive hypotheses be tested against explicit null models and neutral baselines the way neutral theory is tested in molecular evolution, and since then serious evolutionary biologists have largely stopped treating natural selection as the default explanation for anything. A column that says "this is why we have X" is, he concludes, doing pre-Gouldian biology, and the only honest thing is to stop writing it.

Some of this matches what I half-remember from reading Gould as an undergraduate; some of it sounds stronger than anything I remember him claiming. I am suspicious in particular of "most traits are spandrels," and I have a vague memory that Gould later insisted on separating a trait's historical origin from its current usefulness — which his summary seems to run together, since half my examples involve things that may have started as byproducts and then gotten recruited to a job. I also mentioned the spandrel image to a friend who restores old churches, and she says the spaces at San Marco aren't really spandrels in the architectural sense and that builders chose that arrangement partly because it gives you a good surface for decoration; if that's right, I'm not sure the metaphor carries the weight my correspondent puts on it. And his practical conclusion bothers me independently: even granting the methodological complaint, does it follow that I should stop offering adaptive explanations, or only that I should offer them differently?

So please go through his summary claim by claim — what a working evolutionary biologist today would accept, what they would call a standard simplification, and what they would say is just a distortion — and tell me whether his conclusion about my column survives once the distortions are corrected. To make it concrete, I've been sorting my examples into a private four-way scheme: (a) traits shaped by selection to do what they now do; (b) traits shaped for one thing and later recruited for another; (c) traits that are incidental consequences of how the organism is built, with no function of their own; and (d) traits that were shaped by selection to do what they now do, but in an environment that no longer exists, so that they misfire here — the salt appetite, the response to calorie abundance. My correspondent says (d) is just (a) and that I've invented a fourth box to smuggle adaptationism back in through the back door. Is he right, or is there a genuine distinction there worth keeping, and how should each of the four be hedged, if at all, in a sentence written for a lay reader?
