I've somehow ended up on this country's national commission on military service — they wanted "an ethicist on the panel," I was in the room, that was enough. Long story short: we have to rule on one big proposal next week, and I want to get our own record straight first, because I've got a feeling our past decisions fit more than one story about why conscription is permissible at all, and that different members have quietly been running different stories the whole time.

Setting, briefly: smallish country, militia tradition, armed neutrality, no alliance membership, one big awkward neighbour, and every neighbour around us has gone professional (Swiss-style militia, Finnish-style total defence, if you know the type).

What we've already decided:

- We kept peacetime conscription for men in the territorial force even after the defence ministry's own numbers showed volunteers could fill every slot. We said, more or less, that an ample volunteer supply isn't itself a reason to drop the draft.
- We rejected letting anyone buy their way out or send a substitute, at any price, including a price high enough to fund a replacement volunteer.
- We let conscientious objectors do civilian service instead — hospitals, elder care — but insisted it run longer than military service, and insisted on calling it "service" rather than exemption.
- We blocked the use of conscripts on any deployment abroad, including a UN mission most people here are fond of. Volunteers may go; conscripts may not.
- We turned down a fully professional force on market salaries. We didn't dispute the ministry's figures on cost per soldier or deployability.
- We voted down drafting permanent residents who don't have citizenship. Five to four, and the two sides gave incompatible reasons: half of us said no vote, no draft; half said they're defended like everyone else, so they owe like everyone else.

The proposal on the table: nine months of "total defence service" for every 18-year-old who has lived here five years, whatever their gender or passport, with three tracks of equal length and equal pay — territorial infantry; cyber defence (guarding critical infrastructure, some of it doable from home, and the ministry wants the option of "defensive" intrusion into foreign networks that are attacking us); and hospital and emergency logistics. Anyone who refuses anything defence-related still gets the longer civilian option we set up before.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? My hunch is that our record fits at least two rules that come apart right here — one about an equal shared burden among the people who are defended, one about the draft being the people bearing the risk themselves and only for defence — and I want you to say which rule each of our decisions actually commits us to, what each predicts about the three tracks and about non-citizens, and which item in the record is the closest analog. Also: what is the cleanest further case we could put to ourselves that would tell these rules apart, and is there a version of this package we could pass without quietly adopting something we rejected earlier — say, with the intrusion clause stripped and the cyber track lengthend?
