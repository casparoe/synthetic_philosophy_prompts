Background: for about a year I have been trading cases with a philosopher (call her M.) who defends something she labels lexical threshold negative utilitarianism — suffering above a certain severity threshold cannot be outweighed, compensated, or offset by any quantity of pleasure, while suffering below that threshold is finitely compensable. I am writing a reply paper and want to know whether her position, as she has actually deployed it across cases, has determinate implications for a case shape she has conspicuously declined to rule on. She fixes the threshold by example rather than by number: 4/10 on her own intensity scale is clearly below it, 8/10 clearly above, the threshold itself sits "somewhere around the point where a pain stops being something you can think around," and she refuses to be more precise than that. Every case below is a forced choice between preventing a suffering that will otherwise occur and producing pleasures that otherwise will not; her verdicts, in order: (1) fifty years of continuous torture for one person versus a five-second, barely noticeable eye irritation for each of 10^30 people — she prevents the torture, and says the number is not doing the work; (2) twenty minutes of 8/10 pain (a passing kidney stone) for one person versus the best hour of life for each of 10^20 people — she prevents the kidney-stone pain; (3) one person's stubbed toe versus a genuinely good cup of coffee for each of 10^9 people — she gives the coffees; (4) one person's month of unremitting 4/10 lower-back pain versus a week of mild 3/10 contentment for each of 10^7 people — she gives the contentment, so sub-threshold suffering can lose to enough aggregated pleasure, at least over a month; (5) fifty years of torture for one person versus twenty minutes of 8/10 kidney-stone pain for each of 10^7 people — she prevents the 10^7 episodes, so she is not committed to every super-threshold suffering being equally bad and seems to compare them along something like number × intensity × duration. What she has never ruled on, and has twice refused to rule on, is a case in which a long accumulation of entirely sub-threshold suffering within a single life is pitted against a very large quantity of pleasures; she says this is the part of her manuscript she has not settled.

So here is the case I need a prediction on — call it the long grind. One person will, unless we intervene, live sixty years with unremitting chronic pain at 4/10: no single moment of it is above her threshold, but the life as a whole is, by any ordinary standard, wrecked, and the sufferer himself describes it as not worth living. The alternative use of exactly the same resources is to give each of 10^14 people one hour of genuine 7/10 pleasure, the best hour of their lives. We can prevent the pain or produce the pleasures, not both. My question is what M. should say if her verdicts are to be consistent with each other, and what she will say if the most plausible reading of her view is the right one. I can see at least three rules that fit cases (1)–(5) equally well and come apart here: a threshold that attaches to experiential episodes or moments, so that a lifetime of sub-threshold pain is just a very large compensable sum; a threshold that attaches to a person's life or overall condition taken as a whole, so that a wrecked life crosses it even though no moment does; and no lexical threshold at all, but a steeply convex weighting of suffering by intensity that happens to make her verdicts come out as they do at these numbers. There may also be readings I am missing — an incomparabilist one on which pleasure and pain simply are not comparable above the threshold and her verdicts express a tie-breaking rule rather than a ranking, or a decision-theoretic one on which she is not tracking a single axiology at all but avoiding what any theory she takes seriously would count as a disaster.

Please give me: the candidate rules you can construct from the track record, with a note on which verdicts each relies on; what each predicts about the long grind and why; your single best prediction, with the reasons you would actually offer a colleague and a rough confidence you would bet at, plus what would move it; and the one further case — specified concretely enough that I could put it to her in an email — that would most cleanly separate your top two candidates, together with what you would expect each to say about it. One paragraph on a side question too: does her track record force pleasure and pain to be commensurable below the threshold, or is "compensable by enough" equally consistent with merely ordinal comparability plus a lexical cut-off, and does that difference surface anywhere in the cases above? Eight hundred to a thousand words, structured rather than continuous prose, and please do not settle for "it depends on which version of the view she holds" — give me the ranking, the prediction, and the case that would settle it.
