I have been invited to give a paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on societal transformation next month — twenty-five minutes, then twenty-five for questions — and what I need from you is preparation for the question period, not help with the paper itself.

The room will be about thirty people. Roughly a dozen are archaeologists: half of them work on the Classic Maya lowlands, half on the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, and two of them are settlement-survey specialists whose instinct is to count things rather than to read them. Four or five are historians of environmental narrative, the people who study how stories about environmental self-destruction get built and circulated. Three are philosophers of science who work on natural kinds and on social ontology. And there is one emeritus with a public reputation who has spent a career arguing that societies really do wreck themselves, and who thinks the current fashion for saying transformation rather than collapse is a politically motivated refusal to say so.

The thesis is this. Collapse, as the term actually functions, is not something a society does or undergoes; it is a judgement we make when the medium through which that society was legible to us falls silent. Call it the legibility-cessation account. In the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, what stops is the palatial administrative record: a society stops producing the documents by which we count it as one continuing unit, while settlement form, craft technique and agricultural practice carry on under different management. In the Classic Maya lowlands, what stops is the dated monumental inscription and the institution of divine kingship it served, while a great deal else continues under the same name. On Rapa Nui, where there is no administrative archive of the relevant sort, what stops is the production of the monumental statuary that made the island narratable for us at all — and it is that silence, rather than any measured demographic catastrophe, that carries the collapse story. The general claim underneath this is that the question of whether a society failed or merely changed is badly posed, because whether we see failure or change is settled not by the evidence but by which continuities we treat as identity-constituting, and that choice is normative while presenting itself as descriptive. I end with a methodological proposal, the trait-ledger: before anyone calls anything a collapse, say which traits you are counting as the persistence conditions of the society in question, on what grounds you are weighting them, and what your attribution would look like if you weighted them differently.

What I want is the prep sheet for the twenty-five minutes of questions. Give me the objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage each would do if I handled it badly, and tell me where that ordering diverges from the order in which they will probably arrive. Phrase each one the way that person would phrase it, in their own vocabulary: the survey archaeologists will want to talk about settlement counts, radiocarbon curves and the gap between a change in elite ideology and a change in the material base; the Maya specialists will want to talk about the difference between divine kingship and Maya communities; the historians of environmental narrative will want to know whether my account is just a slower, better-mannered version of the determinism they study; the philosophers will want to know what is doing the work in legible to us, whether I am offering observer-relativity or eliminativism, and what sort of thing a society has to be for the trait-ledger to have an answer; and the emeritus will want to know why I am letting past societies off the hook.

For each objection, give me what I should concede outright, what I should hold, a reply I could actually deliver in about ninety seconds of speaking time, and — this matters most — where that reply is weakest. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks, and say what it is. Then tell me which single reply has to land for the session to go well, and what I should say in the first thirty seconds after the chair opens the floor, before anyone has put a question.

Write it as continuous prose: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold text, no tables, in the manner of a long email sent to me the night before. Around twelve hundred words, in British English throughout. Do not cite, name or allude to any published work, and do not suggest readings; I will supply my own references, and I have no way of looking anything up before I speak.
