I live in a four-person house in Leeds that has a written constitution. We adopted it two winters ago after a dispute about the thermostat escalated to a whiteboard, and because I had just finished an undergraduate option on social choice, I drafted it. Two of its clauses are now causing trouble. Clause 3 (Unanimity) says that if every member prefers option X to option Y, then the House prefers X to Y. Clause 4 (Minimum Liberty) assigns each member one protected pair of options – their own business, in principle – over which that member alone is decisive: if she prefers X to Y within her pair, the House prefers X to Y, and if she prefers Y to X, the House prefers Y to X, whatever the other three think. Clause 5 (Tidiness) requires the House's preferences to be acyclic, which at the time struck all of us as boilerplate.

Last month we found in the loft a trunk of my late grandmother's letters, and the House has to decide among four options: read them aloud at Sunday dinner; hand them to the county archive, where any member of the public may request them; burn them unread; or leave the trunk exactly where it is. The trunk is mine, so I am not a neutral party here, which is part of why I want someone else to write this up. Our preferences are meddlesome in exactly the way that makes the machinery jam: one housemate wants them read aloud largely because of what hearing them would do to another housemate, another would rather see them burned than read, and the rest of us rank the four options in ways that ought to settle something and don't. The result, as far as I can tell, is that the House has no will at all – not indifference, but a genuine cycle – and I would like that failure rendered as comedy.

What I want is the internal memorandum this situation would produce: a report of 900 to 1,000 words, annexes included, from the Office of the Clerk of the House to the four members, in deadpan civil-service register, recommending a course of action on the trunk. It should contain Annex A, a Register of Protected Pairs giving each member's assigned pair, her full strict ranking of all four options, and a one-clause gloss of why she ranks them that way – with at least one gloss openly nosy about someone else's protected sphere and at least one protected pair that has never once been exercised in any division and probably never will be. It should contain Annex B, a derivation of the House's preference, listing three or more edges that form a cycle of length at least three, each edge labelled with the clause that generates it; the Pareto edge must be checked in the annex against all four rankings and must actually be unanimous, and at least one protected-pair edge must override the other three members. Nothing may be hand-waved: a friend of mine who works on formal social choice will read this, and she must be able to verify the cycle and find it sound. The Clerk must himself be impeccable – his procedures exact, his register unbroken, his citations only to internal instruments and invented precedents such as a ruling about the dishwasher from a previous tenancy, never to anything a reader would need to look up – and he must never step outside the bureaucracy to explain the joke or concede that anything is absurd. The comedy should come from the mechanics: that a right consists in sovereignty over precisely one comparison and no standing whatsoever over the neighbouring one; that the House can be certified as preferring the loft to the dinner table while three of its four members want the opposite; that the word prefers is being applied to a relation nobody has ever voted on; that the one clause which fails is the tidy one nobody thought about. It should be legible to my housemate, who thinks acyclic is a kind of bicycle.

The second half of the memo should consider and reject, in turn, at least four remedies the Clerk has costed, each of which should be a real way out of the difficulty and each of which should fail for a real reason he can see: restricting the agenda so that certain comparisons never appear on the same motion; a Non-Interference Amendment ruling out of order any preference that concerns what happens to another member, which he should notice protects members only in the spheres nobody cares about; weakening Clause 3 to something less than unanimity, which he should notice lets the House adopt an option that every single member ranks below an available alternative; and – the one that should visibly alarm him, though he must not say why – a proposal to stop representing rights as preferences to be aggregated into a House ranking at all, and instead simply to assign control: to say who decides, and leave the House with no preference to certify and his office with nothing to do. He may also mention, and dismiss, giving each member two protected pairs. The piece should end without winking: my guess is that he provisionally adopts the agenda restriction, notes in a footnote that this is tantamount to declaring the trunk not a House matter at all, which is what everyone had assumed Clause 4 was for, and refers the rest to a subcommittee that will report in due course.
