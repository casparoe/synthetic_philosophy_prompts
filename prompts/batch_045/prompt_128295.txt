I left school at sixteen and have been reading philosophy on my own in the evenings for years, so I know the rough shape of some arguments without always knowing what they are called. I've been asked to introduce Arthur Miller's *The Crucible* to an adult reading group next month, and I want to use the play to get at one disagreement in particular.

Here is the disagreement as I understand it, in my own words. On one side: when a person tells me something, my reason for believing it has to be assembled out of other materials — what I have seen for myself, what I remember, and whatever general track record I have observed in speakers of that kind. Being told is not a route to belief in its own right; it is a way of being pointed at evidence that I would still have to gather and weigh. On the other side: being told is as basic as seeing. A speaker is normally a source I can simply take, and demanding that I first check her independently misdescribes how human knowledge actually works — nearly everything any of us knows we take on someone's word, and there is neither the time nor the capacity to do otherwise. If either side has a standard label, tell me briefly and move on; I don't want a lecture on terminology, I want to use the disagreement on the play.

Nearly everything that happens in Salem happens because of what somebody says. I don't need exact wording from the play — describe scenes as you remember them — and I assume you know it as a well-known text. I would like three things, and on each of them I want you to commit to a definite position and give your reasons, rather than setting out the available readings and leaving me to choose.

First: which side of the disagreement does the play vindicate? My own sense is that it hands something to each. The accusation against Elizabeth that rests on the doll made in court and the needle found inside it, and the way the girls' fit in the courtroom is used to discredit Mary Warren when she tries to recant, look like the case for the first view: words outrun the evidence, and nobody checks. But the hanging of Rebecca Nurse looks like the opposite, because the community's independent knowledge of her character — the signed attestation to her good name by dozens of neighbours — is exactly the support the first view demands, and it counts for nothing. So my question is whether the play ends up on a side, and if so which, or whether it is instead showing that both pictures share an assumption the play refuses.

Second, and this is where I most want your judgement. I have noticed something in the play that the disagreement I described doesn't seem to have room for, and I want to know whether it is a real thing or whether I have invented a problem. The philosophers I've read argue about a single speaker and a single listener at a single moment. In Salem every telling is public, is repeated, and is taken up by a court that then acts on it irreversibly; and once the court has acted, the cost of anyone's admitting that the telling was false has changed. Danforth, as I read him, is not a poor judge of evidence — he is a man in a position where conceding that one accuser lied would retroactively make the executions murders, so he cannot hear the evidence that would make him concede it. I have started calling this testimonial lock-in: a community that has done something irreversible on the strength of a story acquires a stake in the story's being true, and the story's credibility stops tracking the evidence and starts tracking what the community has already paid for it. It is also asymmetric in a way I find striking — it takes one sentence to accuse and the whole machinery of the court to retract. Is lock-in a genuine phenomenon that the debate misdescribes, or is it only an instance of something the debate already handles, such as biased assimilation of later evidence or the social function of trust? If it is genuine, what would a theory of testimony have to add in order to describe it, and does it sit more comfortably with one side of the disagreement than the other?

Third: what does the play add that the arguments don't? My tentative thought is that both sides treat a speaker's credibility as something a listener assesses, whereas the play treats it as something a community installs and then defends, and that this is why the honest critic has to admit that some scenes resist the reading. I want you to say whether that thought survives scrutiny, and, if it does not, what the play does add instead.

Please answer as a professional philosopher who works on testimony and social epistemology, and who also reads drama seriously. Before you write the reading, brainstorm freely for a while: list the three or four scenes or exchanges in the play that carry the most philosophical weight, say in a sentence or two why each one loaded, then drop the ones you can't make anything of and write the reading that remains. Take a definite line, tell me what would have to be true for you to change it, and end by flagging the point where your reading is most likely to be wrong — including the strongest thing someone who took the other side would say about the scene you lean hardest on.
