I'm writing a chapter on whether 'left' and 'right' pick out anything real, and my supervisor has me defending a line I'm not sure I even beleive: that the two words don't name two doctrines, they name two seating arrangements. Call it the adjacency thesis — a group ends up on a side because of who it sits next to and who it's against, and it would stay on that side even if you replaced its flagship commitments, because the side is held together by its neighbours rather than by its principles. (My working test for this is a swap test: take some coalition partner's signature policy, flip it a hundred and eighty degrees, and ask whether anyone else on that side notices or cares. Nobody noticing is evidence the coalition was positional all along.) The obvious rival is the doctrinal thesis: that there is one real dimension underneath — something about equality, or hierarchy, or who gets to give orders to whom — and that the coalitions only look accidental because that dimension keeps getting repackaged for new issues.

I'm opening a ten-minute talk at our postgrad conference in front of maybe sixty political theory and comparative politics people, about half from outside the UK and US, and I want two jokes for the first minute.

Constraints, because I've been burned by this at these things before:
- Each punchline has to turn on the actual content of the dispute. If the joke would still work with 'left' and 'right' swapped out for 'Team A' and 'Team B', or if the whole thing is just a pun on left and right as directions, it's a generic nerd joke and I don't want it.
- One joke turns on the adjacency thesis, one on the doctrinal thesis. Ideally they're aimed at each other's partisans: the adjacency joke should be one a defender of the doctrinal thesis groans at precisely because they get it, and vice versa.
- No named politicians, no current events, nothing that needs a specific country's party system to land. Assume nobody in the room reads the news and the joke has to survive to 2035. The old story about where people sat in the French assembly is fair game as material, but a joke that just restates it isn't.
- One of the two has to be usable deadpan in the Q&A afterwards as an actual claim someone could argue with, not just a gag.

Then, after the jokes, a short structured write-up: a JSON array of exactly two objects, one per joke, with these fields: setup (string), punchline (string), thesis ('adjacency' or 'doctrinal'), mechanism (one sentence naming the specific feature of the left/right dispute the punchline depends on), hostile_to (the kind of person who'd groan rather than laugh, i.e. who the joke is aimed at), decay ('ages well' | 'decays' | 'unsure', plus a short clause saying why), qa_safe (boolean), laugh_rating (integer 1-10).
