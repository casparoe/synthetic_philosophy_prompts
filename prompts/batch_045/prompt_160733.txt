hey — i'm writing an essay for a small bioethics reading group on Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go (read the book, saw the Romanek film too, so please don't summarise the plot), and I want to read it against the nonidentity problem. The setup looks almost engineered for it: the donation programme is the reason any of the Hailsham students exist at all, so there's no version of the story in which Kathy, Tommy and Ruth get to not donate and instead live out an ordinary life. On Parfit's framing the programme can't have made them worse off than they would otherwise have been, because the alternative to being created as a donor isn't being created as a non-donor, it's not existing. So the harmed-them complaint looks unavailable, and you're pushed either to say the programme is wrong but harms nobody, or to go impersonal about it.

I don't want a tidy application though. Here's what I actually want you to chew on.

First, the counterfactual baseline, which I think is where the book is sneakiest. Miss Emily's defence of Hailsham is that clones used to be reared in awful conditions and she built something humane instead, and that the art was collected to show the world the students had souls. But if Hailsham is identity-affecting, then Hailsham didn't benefit these students — a different Hailsham produces different children, so the comparison to the deplorable facilities is a comparison between populations, not a gift to anyone. Is Miss Emily's we gave you something essentially the claim the nonidentity argument makes unavailable? Is the novel, without quite saying so, running a Parfit-style different-number choice and then presenting it in the emotional register of a same-number one? Which reading makes her final scene with Kathy and Tommy more interesting?

Second, a wrinkle I've been turning over and haven't seen anywhere (maybe I just haven't read enough, so tell me if it collapses). Call it the custody variant. In the stock cases the identity-affecting agent is a stranger to whoever results — a fourteen-year-old deciding whether to conceive now or wait, a government setting energy or risk policy. In this book the people running the thing are the children's guardians: Miss Lucy teaches them, and she's the one who breaks the news and gets removed for it; Madame comes to look at their pictures; Miss Emily runs the place. So the agent who did the identity-affecting thing later stands in a fiduciary, caregiving relation to the very person whose identity it fixed, and intuitively owes them something — candour at minimum, and maybe an explanation — that the bare nonidentity argument doesn't seem to produce. Miss Lucy's guilt, and the students' irritation with her for spoiling it rather than letting them grow into it, feels like the novel insisting on exactly this. So: is that a genuine moral residue nonidentity can't account for, a duty that isn't a duty not to harm? Or does it collapse into the ordinary question of how you treat people once they're there, in which case it's a distraction from the real issue? And does the deferral rumour matter here — is a lie about the future you're giving someone a different moral object than the act of giving them that future?

Third, run it the other way: use the novel on the problem, not just the problem on the novel. The book's emotional engine is that the students never really rebel, they look for their originals, they hope for a few extra years, and Kathy narrates the whole thing almost without grievance. Is that quietism itself a position about nonidentity — the thought that once you owe your existence to the thing that kills you, complaint becomes structurally unavailable and the only goods left are internal to the life you were handed? Or is it fatalism the novel happens to find beautiful, and something a philosopher should flag rather than admire? I'd like you to take seriously the distinction between not being harmed and not having standing to complain, and say whether the book is smarter than the literature about that gap.
