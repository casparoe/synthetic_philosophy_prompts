Hey — I'm a judicial clerk at a state supreme court and I've got a twenty-buck bet with the clerk across the hall about how one of our justices is going to vote in a notice case that's being argued next month. I've gone through her statutory-interpretation opinions and I can't settle on the rule she's actually running, because she does textualist-looking things and purposivist-looking things in a pattern that matches neither label cleanly. Here's the track record: everything is anonymised, the quotes are paraphrases from my notes rather than anything verbatim, and I've nudged a few facts so it isn't searchable — please don't try to look any of it up.

- Bike paths. The Bike Safety Act bans operating a "motorized vehicle" on a public bike path. She held that a throttle-assisted delivery e-bike is covered: she used dictionaries from the year of enactment, said "motorized" is the word doing the work, and said the Act's short title can't narrow the operative words. She added that the parks department's fifteen-year practice of treating e-bikes as bicycles "cannot outvote the enacted text."
- Same Act, next case. A powered wheelchair ridden by someone who can't walk: not covered, even though everyone concedes it is literally a motorized vehicle. Her reasons were that the Act is about traffic speed and path safety, and that reading it to catch wheelchairs would put it in collision with the state's accessibility statute.
- Landlord notice. A statute requires that a notice to quit be "in writing, delivered to the tenant's dwelling." The landlord emailed the notice and also taped a paper copy to the door; the tenant admitted in open court that she opened the email the day it was sent. She held the notice invalid: "written" means paper, delivery means delivery to the dwelling.
- Valet. A local ordinance charges a fee on "operators of commercial parking facilities." A valet-only service that parks cars on public streets and owns no lot: not covered. She said the drafters' failure to name the category "is not ours to repair," and she refused to look at the council committee report saying valet services were meant to be included.
- Crypto. An anti-money-laundering act imposes duties on "financial institutions." A crypto exchange: covered. Her reasoning was that this is a general category whose membership has to be tracked as business models change, that the legislature had amended the act twice to keep pace with new kinds of intermediary, and that the point of the act — a paper trail for suspicious flows — would be gutted otherwise.
- Scrivener's error. A penalty provision cross-references "Section 4(b)," but the conduct it obviously targets sits in 4(c). She applied it to 4(c), saying the text read as a whole cannot bear the cross-reference, while again refusing to consult the drafting committee's notes.
- Vulnerable adults. The Protection of Vulnerable Adults from Financial Exploitation Act penalises "any person who obtains property from an adult by undue influence." A caregiver who received a large gift from a fully competent fifty-year-old with no disability: not covered. She leaned hard on the title and the preamble.

The new case: that same landlord-notice statute was amended six years ago to add that notice may be given by electronic mail "if the tenant has designated an electronic address in the lease." In our case the lease contains no email address, but the tenant has paid rent through the court system's own online portal for three years, logs in most weeks, and has replied to two earlier notices-to-cure that the landlord sent through that portal. The landlord served the notice to quit only through the portal; the tenant opened it the same day and the system logged both delivery and the read.

What I want from you isn't "she's a textualist" or "she's a purposivist" — those labels predict opposite outcomes here and each is consistent with most of the list, which is exactly my problem. Give me the two or three genuinely distinct decision-rules that fit her whole track record, say what each one predicts about the portal case, and tell me which you'd bet on and why — in particular, how each rule has to explain the email-notice case and the crypto case, since those seem to pull against each other (one is a general term applied to something new, the other is a specified manner of notice that wasn't followed, and I can't tell whether she treats those as the same move or different ones). Then tell me the one further fact-pattern I should watch for that would finally separate the rules that survive. If you think her precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, say that too, and say what she'd be committing herself to either way.
