Context: I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy. I got into this after an argument at work about whether it's fine to ship a change that makes the product modestly worse for a small set of users if it makes it much better for a vastly larger set. I tried to write down what I actually think and ended up with six commitments, and someone who reads more philosophy than I do says at least two of them can't both be true. I can't see it.

1. Wrongness is being disallowed by principles that no one could reasonably reject, assessed from the standpoint of each person who has to live under the principle — the Scanlon-style contractualist picture. I like it because it makes each person count separately instead of treating people as containers of value.
2. No interpersonal aggregation: you cannot justify a serious burden on one person by adding up many small benefits to many other people. I bite the bullet even at absurd numbers — one person's significant harm against trivial gains for a billion people.
3. Complaints get compared pairwise; the strongest single complaint wins, and the number of people on the other side does no work at all.
4. I'm skeptical about interpersonal comparisons of well-being. I don't think there's a common scale, I only really trust comparisons within a single person's life, and I even doubt the handful of coarse cross-person judgments (death is worse than a stubbed toe) that contractualists lean on.
5. But coercive policies that impose tiny costs on everyone and produce large diffuse benefits — everyone pays a little, some unidentified handful of statistical lives gets saved — are not merely permissible but required, and here I do think the size of the population is doing part of the work: per-person stakes too small to matter in a two-person case become decisive at a million.
6. My proposed repair when (3) runs out: if no individual complaint is stronger than any other, or if the comparisons come out undefined, break the tie by counting the number of people who could reasonably reject and letting the larger count win. Call it count-the-objections, not count-the-benefits. I think this rescues (5) without giving up (2). Relatedly, I use expected-value reasoning freely whenever the risk and the benefit land on the same person, which a friend says is aggregation sneaking back in.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture. Which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it, ranked by severity rather than flagged as one undifferentiated list; for each real tension, what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere; and whether (6) is a stable repair or just aggregation in a better costume — in particular, what happens to it if you change how finely you slice one person's burden into complaints. Please also say where your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong and what argument or case would change your verdict.
