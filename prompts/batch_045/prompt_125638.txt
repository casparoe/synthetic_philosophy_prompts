I'm putting together a 30-year history of the little methodological-reform corner of my field for a festschrift, and I keep catching myself doing the thing Butterfield complained about, so I've been trying to get clear on when exactly a narrative is objectionably Whiggish by just going case by case on my own gut reactions. Here they are.

1. Plate tectonics told as: Wegener basically had continental drift right in 1912, the geologists of the 1920s refused to look at his evidence out of sheer dogmatism, and it took until the 1960s for the data to finally force them to concede. (I know the actual historians say this version is a cartoon.) My verdict: Whiggish, bad — and note the theory *did* win in the end, so winning isn't what makes it bad.

2. A history of the 19th-century sanitary reformers — miasma people vs contagion people — that closes with something like: we now think the miasma side was wrong about the mechanism, but given the data they had their position was perfectly reasonable, and the ventilation schemes they pushed probably saved more lives than their theory deserved. Verdict: not Whiggish at all, in fact good history.

3. The "how the modern university lost its way" genre: every decade a further fall, a lost golden age somewhere behind us, the people who made each change depicted as either cynical or asleep. Verdict: Whiggish, bad, even though nothing at all is presented as progress. This one bugs me, because every definition I manage to write down makes progress or improvement the essential ingredient, and here the present is being treated as the *worst* point.

4. A museum label of the "he didn't invent it, look at these twenty people who had working versions first" type. Verdict: I want to say this is the opposite of Whig history, and it's clearly much less bad than 1, but there's still something slightly off about it — it feels like it's judging the past by a standard that only matters to us. And I can't say how it differs from 3.

5. A history of the origins of the modern wellness industry (or of modern finance, same shape) that reads a bunch of 18th-century figures as "proto-X", as though they were groping toward something they'd never heard of. Verdict: bad, anachronistic.

6. The thing I'm actually writing: I trace our lineage back through four or five people who mostly loathed each other and would have rejected the label completely. Verdict: valuable, *not* Whiggish. Which I can't defend, because it is exactly the same move as 5.

My tentative principle is that the sin is letting the outcome do explanatory work: the narrative goes wrong when "O happened" is smuggled in as part of the reason earlier people did what they did, or as the explanation for why their opponents were irrational. That handles 1 and 2 and maybe 4. It doesn't touch 3 — there, nothing is explained by the outcome, the outcome is just the yardstick everything gets measured against — and it doesn't separate 5 from 6, where nothing is really being explained at all.

So what's the actual principle? My suspicion is that there are several different jobs the present can do in a story — pick the topic, explain the sequence, hand out verdicts on the dead, decide who "we" are — and that Whig history is some kind of collapse of these into each other, but I can't get it sharp and I'm starting to think my verdicts aren't all saveable.

What I'd like: two or three candidate principles stated precisely enough to be applied, each clause tied to the case that motivates it, plus your verdicts on these I haven't settled — (a) a history written from a standpoint that *rejects* the field's current consensus, the "here's the wrong turn we took in the nineties and how we ended up stuck" shape; (b) the counterfactual, paths-not-taken kind of history, which looks like Whig history's opposite number but might really be its purest form; (c) subject matters that genuinely are cumulative, where the later concept really does subsume the earlier ones — a history of the concept of function in mathematics, say; (d) histories of movements that themselves believed in progress, where the telos is in the material and not just in the historian's head; (e) my own thing, given that the endpoint is still up in the air and I say so in the first paragraph. And please be straight about which of my six verdicts you'd throw out — I'm least confident about 6, and 3 vs 4 is the pair I keep going back and forth on.
