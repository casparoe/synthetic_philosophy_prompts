I'm the fact-checker on a trade book about language, and I've been handed a manuscript chapter on meaning whose central section I can't quite trust but can't fix either. I have no library access this week and I don't want a reading list or citations — I want to know which sentences are true, which are subtly off, and what I'd have to write instead. The passage below is the section's opening, reproduced as it stands (it names no work of Quine's; it just calls it "Quine's argument about the field linguist"):

"Quine asks us to imagine a linguist in the field, listening to speakers of a language no one has studied. A rabbit scurries past; someone says 'gavagai'. The linguist records the circumstances in which the natives assent to that sentence, and gradually assembles a manual for rendering their talk into ours. But the evidence can never settle the manual. Nothing the natives can do or say tells the linguist whether 'gavagai' picks out rabbits, or undetached rabbit parts, or the temporal stages of rabbits, or the single recurring property of rabbithood: these hypotheses fit everything the natives will ever do equally well, and so, in endless variation, do others. Since the evidence fixes no single translation, there is no fact of the matter about what anyone means by any sentence. Meaning is not something a sentence really has; it is a feature of a manual we settle on. And the linguist's predicament is ours: run the argument inside English and it shows that we cannot know what our neighbours mean either. Indeterminacy of translation, as Quine presents it, is thus a form of scepticism about other minds."

A page later the chapter draws its pay-off: "So meaning is a myth, and translation is a practical art with nothing to get right: any manual that works is as good as any other." That last sentence is where my alarm went off, and I'd like to know whether it follows from anything in the corrected version of the passage.

My own instinct is that the passage is right about the set-up and goes wrong somewhere in the last three sentences, and that it runs together at least two things that should be kept apart — a thesis about what our words refer to and a thesis about whether whole manuals can be empirically equivalent yet incompatible — but I can't say which is which, or what follows from separating them. I also tried checking the argument myself at three points and stalled each time: (i) if a speaker of the language later learns English and tells us "we meant rabbit," what in the argument prevents that from settling the question, and does the answer depend on a restriction about what is allowed to count as evidence? (ii) if the argument applies within English too, isn't the claim "there is no fact of the matter" itself stated from inside some manual, and is that a fatal problem or something the view is supposed to live with? (iii) scientists say theories are underdetermined by evidence without concluding that there is no fact of the matter about electrons, so why does this argument yield indeterminacy rather than ordinary underdetermination?

Two cases I invented while trying to get clear, which I'd like diagnosed rather than waved at. Case A: the linguist reaches perfect agreement with the natives on every observation sentence, including a term they apply to canoes that are regularly taken apart and whose planks are redistributed among households. One manual translates the term as a persisting object with loose identity conditions, another as the current plank-arrangement, a third as a mass term for canoe-stuff; all three agree on every native assent and dissent. Case B: same state of evidence, but the disputed sentences are ones about ancestors, and the manuals disagree about whether the natives are quantifying over spirits or saying something else about the dead. Is either case a case of the thing the passage describes, are they cases of the same thing, and is there any further evidence — facts about their brains, facts about what they would say in English, anything — that would break the tie in either?

Please write the audit in the following sections, in this order: "Verdict in brief" (about 120 words) — what I can keep and what I must cut; "Line by line" (about 500 words) — take the passage's claims in the order they appear and mark each as keep, keep with a fix, or drop, giving the corrected version in one sentence each; "The conflation" (about 300 words) — lay out, and keep apart, the several distinct theses the passage blurs (the one about reference to rabbits and rabbit-stages, the one about whole manuals, the one about evidence and theory, and whatever else belongs in the list), and say what changes in the argument once they are separated; "My three checks" (about 450 words) — one paragraph per objection, telling me whether it bites against the real thesis or only against the popularised one, and, where it bites, what a defender would answer; "My two cases" (about 300 words) — which thesis each case instantiates, whether they are the same phenomenon, and whether either tie is breakable by evidence and of what kind; "What survives of the pay-off" (about 200 words) — how much of "meaning is a myth / any manual that works is as good as any other" is left once the passage is corrected, and whether the corrected view is a substantive thesis about meaning or a deflationary one about how such claims must be stated; "A replacement paragraph" (about 150 words) — a passage of the same length and reading level I could drop into the manuscript in place of the opening, accurate and without hedging mush; "Judgment calls" (about 80 words) — the places where your verdict depends on a contested reading of Quine, and which reading you assumed. Keep the whole thing keyed to the passage rather than to a general exposition of Quine: I have the passage, I need the verdict on it.
