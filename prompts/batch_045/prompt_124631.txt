I'm writing up my own position on radical interpretation for a seminar paper, and before I commit to it in print I want someone to tell me whether the pieces actually fit together. I have a sense that two or three of them are pulling against each other, but every time I try to locate the strain it moves. Here is the set.

(1) Semantic theory first. A semantic theory for a natural language should take the form of a Tarskian truth theory: finitely axiomatized, compositional, recursively assigning truth conditions to the sentences of the language from the semantic properties of their parts. Knowing the theory, plus knowing it is the right one, is knowing what the sentences mean. I hold this because it is what explains productivity and systematicity — that understanding a language is a competence with infinitely many sentences — and I don't see a use-theoretic or inferential-role alternative doing that work.

(2) Charity is constitutive, not optional. The radical interpreter starts with no prior knowledge of the speaker's beliefs or meanings and arrives at an interpretation by making the speaker come out mostly right by the interpreter's own lights. I don't treat this as an empirical discovery about how agreeable people happen to be; I treat it as a condition on there being anything to interpret at all.

(3) Holism about the attitudes. You cannot fix the content of a single belief in isolation; beliefs, desires and meanings get identified together, against the background of the whole system. This is what I think makes radical interpretation a genuine achievement rather than a list of word-to-object pairings.

(4) Inscrutability of reference, but not of meaning. The standard proxy-function construction shows that any empirically adequate truth theory has rivals, differing in what the subsentential expressions refer to, that no possible evidence could choose between. I hold that this indeterminacy stops at the level of reference: it does not follow that the sentences lack determinate meanings, and I want to say the interpreter's theory delivers the speaker's meanings even though it cannot single out the reference of the parts.

(5) Content is not in the head, and triangulation is what makes it objective. What fixes the content of a creature's attitudes is its causal commerce with a shared world and with other speakers responding to the same things; the triangle of two creatures and a common cause is where the distance between a thought and its object first gets fixed.

(6) First-person authority is real but defeasible. A speaker's sincere, considered avowals of what she believes and means have default status for the interpreter, and overriding them requires an explanation.

Then two things I've added on my own, either or both of which may be where the trouble is.

(7) A verdict on a case. An informant is fluent in my language, understands the interpreter's candidate translation and the truth conditions it assigns, has no incentive to mislead, and says on reflection that no, that is not what she means and she does not believe that. I want to say the interpreter is entitled to keep her theory anyway, on the ground that the denial is itself more evidence to be interpreted charitably and cannot be allowed to veto the rest of the theory on pain of circularity. I hold this even when the interpreter has no independent story about why the informant would deny it.

(8) A constraint I've been toying with and can't evaluate. Call two theories mutually adequate when each makes the other interpreter's attributions of belief come out mostly true by its own lights. My proposed constraint: a method of radical interpretation is admissible only if, applied simultaneously by two interpreters to one another, it yields mutually adequate theories. I like this because it gives content an intersubjective grip without presupposing a world already carved into the objects the theory quantifies over. But I can't tell whether it is trivially satisfied by anything charity delivers, or whether it actually rules out the proxy-function rivals in (4) — and if it does the latter, I can't tell whether that is a discovery or a reductio of (4).

What I want is an audit. Go through (1) through (8) and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like they are; I'd rather you discriminate than flag everything. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad. For each one, state precisely what has to be given up or weakened, what that revision costs me elsewhere in the set, and whether there is a weaker version of the claim that survives intact. If some of (1) through (8) are doing no work in generating the problems, say which. If (8) is confused independently of the rest, say that too rather than trying to accommodate it.

Two constraints on the answer. First, I don't want to be pointed at the literature and I don't need citations or reading suggestions — I want the connections between these claims traced, not a map of who has argued for which thesis; where you rely on a reading of Davidson that is itself contested, flag that inline. Second, attach a short epistemic note: for each of your main verdicts say how confident you are in it, and single out the one claim in your answer you are least sure of and say what would settle it.
