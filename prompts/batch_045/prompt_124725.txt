I'm organizing the final session of a graduate reading group that sits between philosophy of mind and machine-learning interpretability, and last week's meeting collapsed into people asserting past one another, so I'd like to hand out a written dialogue instead. Write a debate between two researchers who are both genuinely expert and who actually respond to what the other says. Dr. Ana Ruiz is a realist about representation in neural networks: she holds that at least some internal states of trained networks literally represent features of the world, that these states have determinate contents, and that interpretability methods can in principle discover which states have which contents. Dr. Peter Halvorsen denies this. He does not deny that interpretability work is valuable, or that networks have rich causal structure, or that describing that structure in representational vocabulary is often the only tractable way for us to talk about it; he denies that any internal state of a network is *about* anything in the way that a belief, a map, or a sentence is, and he treats talk of features, detectors, and directions as a highly useful façon de parler whose literal truth we have no reason to accept. Aim for roughly 1,000–1,400 words, and have them work through one concrete case study rather than trading programmatic slogans.

The case study is a hypothetical one our group invented, so please treat every detail as stipulated rather than as something to verify: a 700M-parameter image-to-text captioning model, which we'll call Marlowe, is analyzed using a sparse autoencoder trained on the residual stream at layer 19 — that is, the layer's activation vector is approximated as a sparse combination of learned directions drawn from a large dictionary. At a dictionary size of 65,536, one learned direction, call it f_2043, fires strongly on photographs of school buses and yellow taxis, weakly on rubber ducks and on the printed string "school bus"; adding a multiple of f_2043 to the activations makes the caption mention a bus, and blocking it selectively degrades bus captioning while leaving taxi and duck captioning intact. Then three complications. (i) Retraining with a dictionary eight times larger splits f_2043 into three directions — one tracking elongated-vehicle shape, one tracking the hue, one firing on contexts involving children and schools — and steering any one of them alone no longer reproduces the full bus behavior, only fragments of it. (ii) A different decomposition of the very same activations, using a basis rotated within the same subspace and reconstructing them just as well, contains no bus-ish direction at all; the behavioral effect of steering f_2043 is reproduced only by a particular combination of three of its features. (iii) In a variant of Marlowe fine-tuned predominantly on British data, the analogous direction fires on double-decker buses and red telephone boxes, and steering it yields the word "coach": its firing conditions have shifted while its causal role in producing a public-vehicle noun has stayed put.

I'd like the exchange to be genuinely dialectical, with neither side a strawman. Ruiz should be made to say what she thinks fixes the content of a direction — covariation with a worldly condition, causal role in the network's own processing, downstream consumption by later circuits, something like a selectional or training history, or some combination — and she should identify which of the three complications she finds most threatening and why; she should also be allowed to concede something real, for instance that representational claims come in degrees of determinacy, or that some discovered directions are artifacts of the analysis method rather than discoveries. Halvorsen should have to do more than gesture at underdetermination: he should say what would have to obtain for the realist's claim to be literally true and argue that it does not obtain, and he should face the obvious retorts that his position collapses into behaviorism about the network, or into the claim that brains don't represent either, or into the view that any system whatsoever can be described as representing anything. Neither should win through rhetorical fatigue; by the end I'd like each of them to have moved a little, and I'd like the remaining disagreement to be localized to a single explicitly stated crux rather than dissolved into politeness. Please keep the discussion focused on the metaphysics and epistemology of representation in artificial networks: no detours into consciousness, sentience, AI welfare, or whether the model "understands" in some ordinary-language sense, and no appeals to what any particular published paper or author has argued.
