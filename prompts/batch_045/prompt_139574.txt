I've somehow ended up on the works council at our parcel depot — about 900 of us, I'm one of five reps, I get two days a month of company time for it and I have to explain whatever we decide to a room of members in about ten minutes. My mate Dave's back is finished at 52 and I did a weekend course the regional union runs last month, and I've been chewing on it ever since.

Here's the practical problem. We get five or six automation proposals a year. They always arrive labelled as productivity, safety, or improving the job — never as anything else. We can't fight all of them: we'd lose the room, and honestly management would just roll the same thing out at the new site up the road instead. So we need to pick. What I have is a gut, and the gut is unreliable in a specific way — it says no to anything with a motor in it and yes to anything with a screen in it, and I don't think that's actually tracking what matters.

What I think matters, from the course, is something like: what happens to the time that gets freed up; what happens to what a worker's labour is worth, both to the company and to the worker; and what happens to the knowledge that currently lives in people's heads rather than in a manual. And the bit I found genuinely strange and can't stop thinking about — the same technology that could cut the working day is, in our situation, the thing that makes it longer and faster, and that isn't a fact about the technology. That's the bit I want operationalised.

So: write me a procedure. Questions in the order I should ask them, what counts as an actual answer to each (as opposed to a number management made up), where the procedure stops and says oppose, where it says let it through with conditions and what those conditions are, where it stops and says this is a values question and has to go to a membership vote, and where it just honestly can't tell. Constraints: one page, runnable in the time I actually have, we never see the financial model, some of the inputs are going to be guesses and the procedure has to say what to do with a guess.

Please keep these distinctions if they're real, and tell me if I've garbled them: cutting the hours needed to make something vs. cheapening the stuff workers buy (I think those pull in different directions and both matter for whether we're better off); who sets the pace once the machine is in; machinery only paying for itself while it's running, so pressure toward nights and weekends; and the people who would have been hired, not just the ones on the payroll now.

Run it on these:

1. A sort retrofit. About 40 of 300 sorter jobs go over three years by attrition, no compulsory redundancies, retraining into exception handling, pay bands unchanged — but the line sets the pace and logs everybody's individual rate.
2. Lift-assist arms on the heavy line. The injury logs say they genuinely work, I've seen them. Management also wants tonnage-per-hour up and wants to keep staffing as is.
3. A routing app. Drivers finish earlier; the saved time goes into more stops; pay per stop is unchanged; and it makes it easy to top up with on-call agency drivers at peak.

And one I suspect your procedure can't handle, which is the one I most want you to be honest about: they want to record our experienced coders doing customs classification — headsets, cameras, the lot — to train a system that might someday do the coding. Nothing is cut now. The data leaves now, the machine turns up in three years, or never, or at a different depot.

At least one of these has to come out as don't fight this when my instinct says fight it. I'd much rather find that out here than standing up in front of the members.

Also: don't just tell me the name of a book to go read, I've got a shift on Monday. And tell me which step of your procedure is really a judgement call wearing a clipboard, plus what would change your mind about the whole shape of it — if who gets the time is the wrong master question, say what should replace it and which of the four cases would show that.
