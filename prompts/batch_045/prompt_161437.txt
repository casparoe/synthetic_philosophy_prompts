I'm a junior in high school, and most of my free time outside class goes into a procedural survival game that two friends and I are building: seeded world generation from a PRNG, terrain chunks that only get instantiated when a player is close enough to see them, level-of-detail swapping on meshes, physics that stops ticking for anything off-screen, deterministic replay from input logs so we can reproduce a bug we saw once, headless builds that run the simulation with no rendering at all. That's the vocabulary I think in most comfortably — seeds, ticks, culling, lazy evaluation, snapshot and restore, compute budget. In my philosophy elective we spent one class on the simulation argument in the form it usually circulates: the trilemma where either civilizations like ours almost always destroy themselves or lose interest before they can run enormous numbers of detailed simulations of their ancestors, or else minds like ours are vastly more likely to be simulated than original, given the assumption that a mind doesn't care what hardware or substrate it's implemented on. The discussion went nowhere; everyone just repeated the same three sentences at each other. I suspect that happened because we were using the words 'simulation' and 'many' and 'probably' without any concrete model of what it costs to run one.

So here's what I want. Translate the argument as faithfully as you can into the vocabulary I just described, and then tell me what didn't survive. Concretely: restate the three-way fork as a claim about how much compute a civilization has and how it chooses to spend it, and say what the engine analogue of an ancestor-simulation is — is it a headless batch job run once for research, a persistent multiplayer server, or something else? Then take the step that actually carries the weight, the inference from there being many more simulated minds than original ones to *you* being probably simulated, and tell me what that step looks like in engine terms: it seems to me it requires somebody to sample a mind from some distribution over all instantiated minds, and nothing in an engine fixes that distribution, since it depends on choices made by whoever writes the spawner. My friend, who is better at math than I am, also pointed out two things that I think break the counting badly and that I'd like you to deal with using the same vocabulary. First, if a simulation is deterministic and seeded, one seed can be replayed a thousand times, or forked mid-run into two branches that both contain a copy of the same person mid-sentence: how many minds is that, and does the answer depend on facts about the base reality's hardware? Second, a simulator who only instantiates what an observer is currently looking at, the way we only generate the chunks near a player, gets an enormous saving in compute per mind — but then the number of minds a civilization can afford depends on a rendering policy rather than on physics, and the fact that our world looks fine-grained all the way down stops being evidence about anyone's compute budget in the way the original argument needs it to be. Please also say what the argument looks like from inside a simulation that is itself running simulations — in engine terms, at recursion depth three, with each level's budget shrinking — and whether the argument needs the recursion to bottom out somewhere.

I don't want a verdict on whether we're in a simulation; I want the translation, and especially the residue. My own hypothesis is that once you do the translation honestly, almost everything in the argument turns out to be an empirical claim about the economics of computation and the habits of whatever civilization sits at the bottom of the stack, and that the only part with no counterpart in my vocabulary is the assumption that being simulated is something that can happen to a mind *from the inside* — my engine has no notion of what anything feels like, and no way to distinguish a simulated physics tick from a real one, since from inside the process a tick is just a tick. Is that the real residue, or is there more? And please be explicit about how you read it: does what's left over show that my vocabulary is missing something important, the way it's missing indexicality and consciousness, or does it show that the original argument was less clear than it looks — that it was trading on 'simulated' meaning both 'implemented in someone else's computer' and 'therefore unreal or doubtful' at different moments? I'd rather have an honest answer about which of those it is than a diplomatic one.
