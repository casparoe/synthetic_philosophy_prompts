I am one of nine members of the Long-Range Programs Committee of the Caledon Health Research Council, and in five weeks we have to send the Council board a written recommendation on whether to renew the Thornbank Cohort Study for a third decade. The study enrolled 8,412 infants in 2003–04 in a river valley whose main employer, a metal smelter, closed in 2009; the original hypothesis was that early-childhood growth and nutrition predict cardiometabolic disease by midlife, which means the primary outcome only becomes measurable now, as participants turn forty. Twenty-two years of follow-up have cost roughly 74 million; retention is at 63 per cent and falling; the external review rated the science good but not excellent; several instruments are now obsolete. Renewal would be ten years at about 3.6 million a year. A modern replacement cohort could be launched for roughly a third of that, but it would yield comparable midlife outcomes only around 2050, and it could not recreate the exposure gradient, because the smelter is gone and the families have dispersed. The study's director, who sits on our committee and is not a neutral party, argues that stopping now would waste twenty-two years of work; our health economist has circulated a one-page note saying the 74 million is spent and gone, that the only question is whether the next 3.6 million buys more usable knowledge than the alternatives, and that how much we have already spent is simply not a reason.

I have gone back and forth on this. My instinct was to renew. Then the economist's note made the case for renewal look like the sunk cost fallacy in its purest form, and now I cannot tell which of the arguments I find persuasive are genuine reasons and which are rationalisations. Three of them in particular: that the money was committed by earlier, better-informed committees whose judgement we should partly defer to; that an institution known to abandon long projects whenever the news is bad loses the ability to commit to long-horizon science at all, and that this loss is real even if it is hard to quantify; and that some of what we call sunk spending is really a half-finished asset — an unrepeatable cohort, twenty-two years of stored biospecimens, a field team with two decades of rapport with participants — whose value is realised only if the project continues. There is also a promise-keeping dimension nobody on the committee has quantified: participants were told at enrolment that the study would follow them for life, and the Council's own recruitment material for a planned 2031 cohort leans on that history.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before our 20 November meeting. It should open with a clear recommendation — renew, terminate, or renew under stated conditions, with a time frame and a scheduled re-review — and then give the reasoning in language a mixed committee of scientists, an economist and two lay members can actually act on. I need it to do two things at once: separate the arguments above into those that are genuinely load-bearing and those that are the fallacy wearing a better suit, and give the strongest case for whichever recommendation you reject a treatment honest enough that its advocate would recognise it. If you recommend renewal, say what would have to be true, or what measurement at the next gate would have to show, for termination to become correct; if you recommend termination, say what would have to be true for renewal to have been right. Note that half the committee will read any invocation of past expenditure as fallacious by definition, so the memo has to meet that objection rather than go around it; and please do not lean on the point that the money is already spent's being irrelevant as a conversation-stopper, since the interesting question for us is precisely which of our reasons survive that point.
