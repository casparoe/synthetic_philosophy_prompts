I am trying to put a number on a question that is almost always answered with "it depends what you mean by *xing*," and I would like the number decomposed so that the evasive answer gets replaced by an argument with its weak points marked.

The proposition I want a credence about is this: Mencius and Xunzi are making genuinely incompatible claims about human nature, in the sense that there is some concrete question about what a human being raised without instruction would reliably become, or about where the norms of ritual come from, on which the two of them would have to give different answers — and not merely two different descriptions of the same answer. Compatibility by redefinition does not count as agreement; I want to know whether, after both have been read as strongly and as fairly as they can be, there is still something one of them must deny.

Please give me the following.

1. Your credence in that proposition, as a single number, together with a one-sentence statement of what would count as its being settled.

2. A decomposition into the sub-questions the credence actually rides on, each with a rough probability and one line of justification. At minimum these:
   (a) How Mencius's claim should be read: is it the substantive claim that ordinary human beings reliably develop incipient moral tendencies — the "sprouts," the spontaneous alarm at another's suffering, the sense of satisfaction in getting things right — under conditions that are merely non-obstructive, or is it the thinner claim that we have a distinctive *capacity* that is as much in need of being worked on as Xunzi's raw material is, leaving the pair of them agreeing about the psychology and differing only in emphasis?
   (b) How Xunzi's "bad" should be read: moral corruption, or something weaker — unformed, crooked, liable to disorder when the innate desires for profit, sensory gratification and status are left to run without measure or external standard?
   (c) Whether Xunzi's account of ritual as the deliberate construction of the sages leaves the raw material wholly inert, or whether the sages' own first act of construction must itself draw on something in human nature — in which case Xunzi is committed to something functionally sprout-like even though he denies it.
   (d) Whether the real disagreement, if there is one, is empirical psychology at all, or rather a disagreement about the source and authority of the norm: about whether the way of the sages is discovered in us or imposed on us, and about what that difference licenses politically and pedagogically.
   (e) Whether Xunzi's own claim that anyone at all could become a sage is best explained as a concession that the material is universally malleable, or as a rhetorical use of "could" that does no psychological work.
   Add any further term you think carries real weight, and say if you think I have weighted one of these too heavily.

3. How the terms combine into your headline number, and which single term the final figure is most sensitive to — that is, which one, if it flipped, would move you furthest and in which direction.

4. A discriminating case, since I would rather test the disagreement than define it. Consider a child raised from birth with no ritual instruction, no teacher, no deliberate training of any kind, but with adequate food, safety and ordinary human company. Ask two things. First: do the two accounts actually predict different observables here, or does each predict the same disorder and the same need for subsequent cultivation, so that the case fails to discriminate? Second, and more sharply, run what I will call the pupil-without-alarm test: suppose a being that feels no distress at another's suffering and no particular satisfaction at getting things right, but is otherwise responsive to conditioning, praise, and punishment. Could the training still produce a morally adequate adult? I take it Mencius must say no, because on his account the sprouts are the material the work is done on. My question is whether Xunzi is genuinely committed to saying yes, and what your credence is that he would, if pressed, accept that commitment rather than quietly help himself to a Mencian ingredient. Tell me what that credence does to your answer in (1).

5. Two scholars I have been reading with land, respectively, at about 0.85 and about 0.15 on the proposition. Given your decomposition, tell me which sub-question their disagreement most likely lives in, and what each of them would have to be assuming to be so far apart.

Finally: what single piece of textual or argumentative evidence would move your number most, and is that evidence the kind of thing that could in principle settle it, or does the disagreement sit somewhere no evidence will reach?
