I convene a recorded seminar series on institutional design, and next month's guest is Dr Nanda Oyelaran, a social choice theorist who thinks that the usual moral drawn from the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem is drawn too quickly. The theorem says that once there are at least three alternatives, any rule that selects a single winner, respects unanimity, and is immune to profitable misreporting by a single voter must simply read off one fixed voter's ballot. Her reply has two planks.

First, what she calls the entrenched binary agenda. The dictatorial conclusion depends on the range of the rule containing all the alternatives; when the choice is between exactly two, majority rule is strategy-proof and nobody is a dictator. So she proposes that a constitution fix in advance a binary agenda: a tree whose leaves are the alternatives, each internal node a pairwise majority vote, with the order of the nodes entrenched and unamendable. Every vote a citizen ever casts is then a yes-or-no on a pair, and at such a vote sincere voting is weakly dominant. The only discretionary power left is that of whoever fixed the tree, which she argues is no more alarming than any other constitutional precommitment.

Second, she argues that strategy-proofness is the wrong target for institutional design precisely because it is all-or-nothing: what matters is not whether a profitable misreport exists somewhere, but how often one arises. She therefore ranks rules by the frequency, across a neutral distribution of preference profiles, with which some voter would gain by misreporting.

Draft me eight to ten interview questions that go beyond asking her to restate any of this. I want at least one question on each of the following, plus whatever else you think presses hardest:

whether the agenda is simply the dictator in disguise, and whether entrenchment removes the difficulty or merely relocates it — she will most likely appeal to supermajority amendment rules and to the legitimacy of constitutional precommitment;

whether stage-by-stage strategy-proofness survives once a voter's preference over a pair is induced by the rest of the tree, that is under sophisticated rather than sincere voting, which is where I expect her to say that the theorem then applies to the reduced preferences;

whether the proposal requires the agenda to be literally unamendable, and if so whether that is a defensible position or a dictatorship of the founders;

how she weighs immunity to misreporting against everything a binary agenda gives up: path-dependence, the possibility of electing a Condorcet loser, and the failure to elect a Condorcet winner under some orderings;

why she restricts the range of the rule rather than the domain, given that single-peaked preferences admit non-dictatorial strategy-proof median schemes, and given that mixing voting with chance admits something close to a random dictatorship in which everyone has an equal chance;

how much her frequency measure really shows: why a neutral distribution over profiles is the right baseline, whether a voter who does not know the others' ballots can act on a profitable misreport at all, and whether frequency or expected size of gain is what a designer ought to minimise.

For each question, give the reply she is most likely to make, and one follow-up keyed to that specific reply rather than to a generic objection. Close with a single question about what evidence or argument would lead her to abandon the entrenched binary agenda. Assume an audience that knows the theorem but not her work, and include no question that could be answered simply by summarising her position.
