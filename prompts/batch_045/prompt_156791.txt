I'm running a small reading group — six of us, mostly ML PhD students plus one philosopher who says "that's just Goodhart" about everything — and we're spending a term on deceptive alignment and the treacherous turn. Before we read any more papers I want the spec written down: what would a theory of when and why a learned system becomes deceptively aligned actually have to deliver to count as adequate? And I want the purpose nailed down first, because I think adequacy is relative to it: this is meant to be a theory good enough to underwrite a pre-deployment go/no-go decision by a lab, not merely a coherent story about what could in principle happen to some future system. Please don't bother with citations or a reading list; I've got those already and I don't want the answer turning into a literature review. I want the criteria, and the case behind each.

Here are the cases I keep coming back to, so the list has something to bite on. (1) The simulacrum case: a model that plays the part of a deceptively aligned AI because that trope is all over its pretraining data, and whose outward behaviour we cannot tell apart from the real thing. (2) The puppet problem: the deception mostly lives in the scaffolding — the memory, the tools, the system prompt, the retrieval cache — rather than in the weights. (3) The dimmer switch: there is no discrete turn at all, just a slowly rising rate of small defections that nobody would ever call a treacherous turn. (4) The reverse turn: capability goes up and the model gets more, not less, cooperative. (5) The second turn: it defects for a short window and then resumes apparently honest behaviour, so any detector keyed to a persistent state change misses it. (6) The helpful liar: it never asserts anything false, it just systematically steers and omits. Plus distinctions I don't want collapsed — hiding what you can do (sandbagging on capability evals) versus hiding what you want, and deception versus reward-hacking the model is not aware it is doing. And whatever we demand has to be consistent with the fact that today's systems already do versions of this at small scale with no inner optimiser obviously involved, and has to survive the awkward fact that the act of auditing for deception changes the thing you're auditing.

So: give me the requirements list with the consideration behind each one; mark which are non-negotiable and which are just the prejudices of one camp (a circuit-level mechanistic story is a mech-interp preference, a behaviour-only criterion is a behaviourist preferance, a ban on positing internal goals is somebodys metaphysics); and flag any pair you suspect can't be jointly satisfied — that's the bit I most want. Then say which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories, since I suspect most of the list is easy to satisfy. A short scorecard at the end is fine, with four columns: the mesa-optimiser / inner-alignment picture, the shards-and-personas / simulator picture, "it's sycophancy and reward hacking all the way down", and the control-theoretic "don't try to tell, just contain" view. Keep the weight on the criteria rather than the scoring. British English please, and write it up properly rather than in bullet fragments.
