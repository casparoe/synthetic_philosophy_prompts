I'm in this reading group — two physics grad students, one philosophy guy, and me (I'm an undergrad who took one thermo class and barely passed) — and we got stuck on something that feels more like a philosophy question than a physics one, so I'm bringing it here.

Here's the thing I can't get straight. The story we were taught about Maxwell's demon is that it sorts fast molecules from slow ones, seems to break the second law, and the fix is that the demon has to *write down* what it sees, its memory fills up, and eventually it has to clear the record — and erasing a bit carries a floor of roughly 3×10⁻²¹ joules at room temperature, which is more than the demon earned by cheating. Fine. But then look at what people who actually build computers do and say, and it's three attitudes stapled together: (a) acquiring information is treated as, in principle, free — measurement can be done reversibly, no floor — while *erasing* it is treated as the operation with a mandatory bill, so the cost gets located in one specific kind of thing you can do to a bit; (b) the hardware everyone actually builds is made of logically irreversible gates (an AND gate throws away information about which input it saw), nobody builds reversible or adiabatic logic at scale, the industry meters and sells computation as energy-per-operation rather than per-bit-erased, and real dissipation per operation is something like a billion times the Landauer floor, so whatever is making my laptop hot, it isn't the price of erasing bits; (c) yet the same community says "information is physical," puts the Landauer limit on slides about the far future of computing, and treats it as the reason reversible computing matters someday — and I've heard people have even shown you can pay the erasure bill in some conserved quantity other than energy, angular momentum or something, so "erasure costs heat" isn't even clean.

And then there's the bit my friend in the group keeps pushing, which I can't tell is deep or a muddle: she says the real cost isn't per bit erased but per bit of *correlation destroyed* — that if the demon's record is correlated with something else it also holds (a backup copy, the gas itself, whatever), then resetting that register destroys less information than resetting an uncorrelated one, so the bill depends on what else is lying around and on what the demon still has access to. If that's right, the thermodynamic bookkeeping is indexed to an agent's total holdings, i.e. to what it in some sense knows, and not to a property of the bit itself. Our philosophy guy muttered something about "epistemic" versus "ontic" entropy and then waved his hands a lot. So: what does this whole package actually presuppose about what information *is*? Does locating the cost in writing-and-erasing rather than in seeing-and-knowing only make sense if information is a physical quantity a system can literally carry around, or could a view on which information is just what an agent can predict and entropy is ignorance license the exact same accounting? Where is the practice strictly committed and where is it merely convenient — e.g., is nobody building reversible computers evidence that engineers don't really believe the story, or just that the floor sits a billion times below anything they care about, making the theory idle for them? Is there a genuine inconsistency in saying acquisition is free and erasure is costly, given that a demon with a full memory has to clear space before it can record anything new (doesn't the bill just move back one step)? And concretely, if my friend's correlation view were the right one, what would change in how we'd assess a real, built information engine: would two demons with identical physical memories but different back-ups owe different amounts of heat, would "never erase anything, just keep buying hard drives" become a legitimate efficiency strategy, and what would you even print on the spec sheet instead of joules-per-bit-erased?
