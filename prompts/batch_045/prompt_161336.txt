I'm a novelist, on what I hope is the last-but-one draft of my third book — near-future thing, mostly set in and around a fertility clinic, with a big flashback section set in 1962. My editor has twice now come back with the same note: my named characters are roughly half and half, but the people in the background default to male and the people who get *described* are almost always female. The embryologist just does things; the nurse has a grey bun and small hands. A senior consultant is a man until I say otherwise; when a woman has authority I apparently feel obliged to add a sentence about her shoes or her kid. I want to catch this myself, before copyedit, instead of paying someone to tell me again.

Here's the half-formed thing I keep circling back to. There's that 1985 Hofstadter satire written under a pen name, where he takes the structure of an earnest essay arguing against racist language and runs it backwards — arguing that the traditional, generic-sounding usage is obviously right and the reformers are cranks, using exactly the arguments people actually use to defend the status quo, with the markers swapped. The clever bit isn't that he invents new arguments; it's that the reader's discomfort at the mirror is the whole evidence. That move — don't argue, just flip the marker and notice what feels wrong and *why* — feels like it should be something I can actually run on a chapter, and not just on individual words like generic he or the -man compounds.

So: write me a procedure. Concretely what I need:

- Something I run alone, on a chapter, in twenty minutes or so, with no linguistics background and no software. I have about forty chapters and six weeks.
- The questions in order, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each — not vibes. Say where the procedure stops with a no (fix this sentence before moving on) and where it hands the decision back to me because the question is about what I'm trying to do rather than what the text does.
- It has to go past pronouns to the defaults underneath: whose competence is assumed, whose body gets described and in what register, who gets interiority versus who gets explained, who acts versus who is acted on, which characters need a credential supplied and which are just granted one. And it should generalise the flip to other axes where it works — race, class, nationality, age, disability, religion — and tell me honestly where the flip stops being a good test.
- Be explicit about which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure. I can tell when I'm being handed a rubber stamp.

Run it on three things from my draft. One: the clinic opening I described above, where the unnamed embryologist acts and the unnamed nurse is a body. Two: chapter 9, where I already "fixed" a flagged scene by changing the lab technician from a man to a woman and leaving everything else about him/her exactly as written — I suspect this fails the flip, and I'd rather be told plainly than have it explained gently. Three, and this is the one I'll argue with: chapter 12, where my narrator (a burnt-out middle-aged male fixer, very much a character and not me) describes a government minister's legs in the middle of a scene where she is refusing him something. I think that's characterization and voice and load-bearing; if your procedure says cut it, I want to see the argument, including the version where it says keep it but do something else.

Also: chapter 21 is set in 1962 and the whole point of several scenes is that a woman couldn't do the thing she's doing. The flip is anachronistic there, and I don't want a procedure that either lets me off the hook or tells me to write a false world. Tell me what running it on that chapter is supposed to produce.

And tell me what the procedure can't do. My worries: that the jolt I feel on a flip tells me about me or about the reader rather than about the sentence; that a flip can launder a bias rather than remove it; that sometimes the asymmetry is real-world base rates and not my prose; that some of this is style and voice and has no correct answer.

Last thing. As you go, separate what's actually settled among people who work on this from what's contested. I mean: is it established that generics like he and -man compounds are not read as neutral, or is that a live fight? Does changing the words do anything to how people think, or only track what they already think? Does the satire actually show anything, or is it just a good joke? I'd rather you flag a claim as contested than hand me a confident line I'll repeat at a reading and get corrected on. Don't put in any citations or reading suggestions — I'll chase those myself if I ever want them, and I definately don't want a bibliography in this.
