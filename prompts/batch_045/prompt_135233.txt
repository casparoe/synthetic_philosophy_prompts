I'm finishing a PhD in philosophy of biology and have been drafted onto the advisory panel of a small foundation that wants to spend something like two million dollars over four years on conceptual and theoretical work about what heritability estimates do and do not license. They are explicitly not funding new data collection; the remit is conceptual clarification, formal modeling, and re-analysis of existing summary statistics, and the practical motive is that heritability numbers keep surfacing in places where they do real work — education policy debates, arguments about whether social outcomes are worth trying to change, polygenic screening of embryos, mitigation arguments in criminal sentencing, insurance and employment underwriting. We are not interested in funding a tenth explainer essay on how heritability is not the same as immutability. We want to know which unresolved conceptual questions, if actually settled and the settlement actually absorbed by the relevant audiences, would most reduce the amount of damage done by the way these numbers get used.

My own shortlist, which I suspect is partly confused, runs something like this. First, the non-portability problem: heritability is a variance ratio within a specified population under a specified distribution of environments, so it is indexed to a population and a time in a way that users systematically forget, and I would like a clean account of what the portability conditions are — under what circumstances, if any, an estimate from one cohort licenses an inference about another. Second, the intervention gap, by which I mean the gap between the fraction of variance not attributed to genotype and the fraction of variance that any actually feasible intervention could remove: those are different quantities, the first puts no upper or lower bound on the second, and I don't think we have a good formal vocabulary for stating the relationship. Third, what I have been calling reactive phenotypes: for traits like educational attainment or income, the measured variable is partly a record of how other people responded to you, so its heritability is in part the heritability of other people's responses, and it seems to me this changes what the number means without making it meaningless. Fourth, the thing I can't quite state properly: heritability goes up as environmental variance goes down, so a highly successful equalization program would be expected to raise heritability, and there is something unstable about a quantity that rewards you with a larger number for fixing the thing the number is used to argue you can't fix. Fifth, the well-known point that within-group heritability places no constraint on the explanation of between-group differences — I include it only because I want to know whether the failure of this point to stick is a conceptual problem or a psychology-of-communication problem, since the two would call for very different funded projects.

What I would like from you is a defended agenda rather than a survey. Rank the questions you would fund, including ones not on my list, and make the ranking explicit about its criteria: how much downstream misuse a settled answer would actually prevent, tractability for people doing conceptual and formal work without new data, whether the audiences who get this wrong could absorb the result, and how neglected the question is relative to its importance. I especially want the trade-offs and the dependency structure stated — which questions are upstream of others, which you would fund specifically because an answer would change what we should say about a different question, and which you would decline to fund even though they are genuinely open, and why. Tell me also what would move things: what result or development over the next four years would push a question up or down your list, and what would count as a funded project having failed. Please say directly if you think my fourth item is just my second item in different clothes, or if the reactive-phenotype point is less novel than I think. I will fill in the literature and citations myself, so please don't spend words on references; I'd rather have the argument about what deserves four years of someone's career and why.
