I'm preparing a two-page handout for a first-year seminar on ethics and public life, and I want its centerpiece to be a decision tree that sorts students among the main positions on the problem of dirty hands in politics — the case of an official who does something morally forbidden in order to avert something worse, or permits something bad in order to secure something good.

Build the tree out of the logical space rather than out of a list of named views: six or seven branch questions, each phrased as a concrete claim or case-verdict that a student with no political philosophy can answer, and only then locate the actual positions among the answer-combinations. Each leaf should carry a name if the position has one and "unnamed" if it does not, one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and one sentence on what it implies about how the public should treat the official afterward — praise, tolerate, demand public confession, remove from office, or punish.

The distinctions I want doing the branching work are: whether the dirt lies in what the official does or in what she merely permits; whether the dilemma was imposed on her or is partly the product of her own earlier choices or of an institution she upholds; whether the mark of an uncorrupted agent is a willingness to be punished or removed afterward; and whether the dirt attaches to the person or to the office and its successors.

Cases that must appear as nodes: a legislator voting for a badly flawed bill in order to block a worse one; a mayor who authorizes a raid she knows will likely get someone killed; a minister who does nothing and thereby allows preventable deaths; a campaign strategist who quietly benefits from a supporter's smear; a civil servant who leaks to derail a lawful policy she believes is unjust; and the case where the official refuses and a worse successor takes the job.

Required leaves: there is no distinct problem here, only ordinary moral conflict; the act is justified but the agent is guilty; the office changes what may be done and no guilt is required; refusal is required whatever the cost; the dilemma should be dissolved by institutional design rather than resolved; and the wrong lies in doing it covertly, so publicity is the real requirement.

Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, and note one or two unoccupied answer-combinations with a brief diagnosis of why nobody is there — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
