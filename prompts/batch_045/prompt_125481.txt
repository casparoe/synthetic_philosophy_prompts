Draft the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between two camps on moral compromise between value systems would produce, if it went well.

Camp A — the moral-division-of-labor camp — holds that when two communities order the same practical question differently, there is very often an arrangement under which each side does, funds, tolerates, or refrains from obstructing what its own values treat as cheap, and receives from the other what its own values treat as dear, so that both end up better off by their own lights than at the no-agreement point. It treats this as an analogue of comparative advantage and insists the gain is real even with no shared scale of value, because each side's improvement is measured from inside its own ordering.

Camp B — the integrity camp — denies that this framing survives contact with what is actually at stake. It holds that what is given up in such arrangements is typically not a quantity forgone on a scale but a change in what the parties stand for; that an improvement in rank-order terms inside one's own value system can nonetheless be a worsened relation to one's own reasons; and that describing the exchange as trade already presupposes that whatever is at issue is the sort of thing one may concede in return for something, which is precisely what is in dispute.

What I want is the document, not a verdict on the dispute.

1. An agreed-claims section, numbered, that both camps could actually sign. Make the claims load-bearing rather than platitudinous: about what the no-agreement baseline is and why it is itself morally loaded rather than neutral; about what would have to be true for the economic analogy to be more than a metaphor; about what neither side is claiming (Camp A is not claiming the arrangement is best from nowhere or required by a standard both accept; Camp B is not claiming that every cross-value accommodation is corrupting or that isolation is costless).

2. A disagreements table with columns: the issue; the formulation as Camp A would sign it; the formulation as Camp B would sign it; and whether the residual dispute is substantive or merely verbal, with, in the verbal cases, a statement of what each side means by the contested word.

3. A cruxes table. For each crux give: the sub-question; why settling it would move at least one side; the case, test, or argument both camps accept as genuinely bearing on it; and each camp's prediction written down in advance, in terms an observer with no stake could in principle check after the fact, so that a later reader could say which side the evidence disfavored.

The cruxes I expect to matter, and which you should treat as candidates rather than as a checklist — replace or add where the drafting shows a sharper one:

(a) The numéraire problem. Gains from trade are normally stated in a common unit. Can a gain be identified when the only admissible measure is each side's own ranking, or does any reported gain secretly import a shared metric that one side rejects?

(b) Lumpiness. Standard gains-from-trade arguments assume smooth substitutability rather than threshold or lexicographic commitments. Does the analogy hold when what is traded is not marginally substitutable at all?

(c) Legitimacy from the inside. Each side must be able to justify entering the arrangement by its own values. But for a side whose values forbid trading in this domain at all, justifying the trade requires a second-order change in those values. Is the value system that comes out the other side the same one whose gain is being measured?

(d) Endogeneity of the baseline. Repeated accommodation plausibly reshapes what each side values, so the baseline against which gains are measured moves. Camp A and Camp B should state opposed predictions about the direction and the size of that drift.

(e) Third-party standing. Arrangements of this kind impose costs on parties who are not at the table and who may not accept either value system. Say whether their losses enter the accounting at all, and what follows if they do.

(f) Redescription. Parties frequently make an exchange acceptable by re-describing the action — a permit rather than a sale, a stewardship payment rather than a price, an abstention rather than an endorsement. Is this a genuine expression of the value in a new form, or a change of label only? This is where I suspect part of the dispute is verbal, but say exactly which part and why, and what substantive question remains once the verbal part is cleared away.

4. Build the tables around one shared case rather than scattered examples, and sharpen the case until neither camp could call it a straw target. Use this one: an upstream community whose doctrine treats a river as inalienable kin, not as a resource, and a downstream farming community that treats water as an alienable input; the proposed arrangement is a long, revocable, ritually framed use-permit plus a downstream-funded restoration and monitoring trust, with no transfer of title. Then add one matched stress case that flips a single feature — for instance, a version in which the upstream doctrine admits no internal re-description under which any permit may be granted — and record what each camp predicts there.

Close with two short sections: for each camp, the one observation its own adherents would count as a reason to change their minds; and a sentence or two on whether anything both camps agree would actually settle the matter, or whether the honest joint conclusion is that it does not settle.

Format the whole thing as the numbered agreed-claims section, then the disagreements table, then the cruxes table, then the two closing sections. Flag in brackets any sentence you could not get both camps to sign, naming the objecting camp and its objection. Roughly a thousand to twelve hundred words of actual document; no citations, no appeals to authority, and no hedging boilerplate.
