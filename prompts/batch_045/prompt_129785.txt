Write a short story of 1,200 to 1,800 words that tests the Buddhist doctrine of no-self — the claim that a person is not a single persisting substance but a shifting bundle of physical and mental processes, and that the sense of being an abiding "I" is something constructed, habitual, and mistaken — against a case in which that claim is put under real practical pressure. I want the story to be a genuine working-through, not an illustration of a view already settled in advance.

The setting is near-future and otherwise ordinary: a city, a hospital, a family. No monasteries, no robes, no wise Eastern figure who explains the doctrine; if anyone holds the no-self view, let it be because one of the characters has practised with it for years or reasoned her way into it, and let the idea enter the story through what that character can and cannot say, do, or bear. The one piece of speculative apparatus is a therapy. A fatal degenerative neurological illness can be treated by staged transfer: over roughly eighteen months, and with no moment at which anyone could point to the change as having happened, the patient's episodic memories and trained dispositions are written into a healthy recipient whose own are correspondingly attenuated. The transfer need not be total. Clinicians set a mix ratio, and in this case — because clinical time is rationed and because the couple in question asked for it — the ratio is set at sixty-forty.

The characters are a married couple. One is ill and will be transferred; the other is healthy and will be the recipient, and will be attenuated to roughly forty percent of what she was. They have, among other things, a custodial and financial obligation to a dependent sibling of the ill partner, a long unfinished collaborative project the ill partner cares about more than anything, and a substantial debt incurred by the healthy partner before the marriage. After the procedure there will be one body, one mind, and a set of questions that the metaphysics does not obviously answer: who is bound by the consent form the ill partner signed; to whom the survivor's obligations run and in what proportions; whether the continuation is a survival, a replacement, or a new person with inherited paperwork; and what the attenuated partner is entitled to fear in advance.

Some constraints on how you handle this. Tell the story in alternating first-person sections from the two partners before and during the transfer, and then in a single unattributed first-person voice after it — a voice the reader cannot confidently assign, because on the terms of the case there may be nothing to assign. Do not resolve that ambiguity, and do not build toward a twist ending or a final line that reveals who survived. Let the partner who is about to be attenuated make the strongest available case against the doctrine: the anticipatory dread, the first-person certainty that it is *she* who will be diminished, the way prudential concern seems to require a continuant. Do not let her be stupid or merely frightened; let her win the exchanges she is in. Let the partner who accepts no-self pay a real price for accepting it — something she can no longer say, some grief she can no longer formulate, some injustice she can no longer name in the only vocabulary that would move a court or a relative.

What the story should finally make visible is this: that even if there is no fact of the matter about who really survived, the allocative question does not dissolve with the metaphysical one. Something has to decide who gets the dependent sibling, the project, the debt, and the right to speak for the dead — a default rule, a legal convention, a family's habit — and the no-self view, precisely because it denies that the question has a factual answer, leaves that convention doing all the work, and therefore leaves it open for the convention to be unjust. Show a moment, without editorialising, in which the survivor uses the word "I" fluently and unself-consciously, and make us feel how far the grammar outruns anything the character could defend.

No character should state the correct view, and none should be a mouthpiece. No invented scriptures, no attributed quotations, no closing paraphrase of the doctrine. Story only, with no preface or afterword.
