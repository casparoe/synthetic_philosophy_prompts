I'm on the small committee that runs a volunteer-run music festival, and last year we had to decide what to do about one of our volunteers after an accusation of harassment came up. We suspended him, the story turned out muddier than it first looked, we quietly reversed it, and he never came back anyway. I've been chewing on it ever since, and I've started using the Danish film The Hunt (Jagten, Vinterberg, 2012) as my mental model for what happened — which I'm suspect is partly because it flatters me. So I want someone to read the film against the philosophy properly and tell me whether the model is any good.

The distinction I keep coming back to is this. When a community turns on someone, three different things get run together: (1) expression — I want it on record that I don't stand with this; (2) protection — I want this person away from the people or the role where they could do harm; (3) credibility damage — from now on the accusation is part of how the person gets read, so anything they say in their own defence counts for less. My claim is that a formal process that clears someone can undo (2) but not (3), and that nobody is in a position to undo (3), because it wasn't anyone's decision: it's the aggregate of hundreds of small acts, none of which its author can take back, and none of which leaves anyone with an obligation, or really the standing, to restore anything. Call that the restoration gap.

So read the film through that. Where does it bear the reading out — the Christmas Eve supermarket scene, the church service, the reconciliation a year later, that last shot in the woods — and where does it resist it? Two things I want you to be honest about. First, the film stacks the deck: the accusation is flatly false and Lucas is close to a saint, whereas my case was muddy and the guy was never going to be anyone's saint. Does that make The Hunt a bad model for the cases that actually occupy committees like mine, and if so is it bad in a way that matters or only superficially? Second, is the film really about the epistemics rather than the sanction — about a bad information environment, leading interviews, adults who assume children don't lie, accusations that travel faster than retractions — in which case my restoration gap is beside the point, because get the information right and the sanction sorts itself out? Or does the film show that even epistemically faultless belief doesn't justify what the town does? And please don't just tell me that shunning is protected freedom of association: I've heard that move and it feels like it answers a different question than the one I'm stuck on.

Don't give me references or lists of further reading, and don't quote the film at me, I've seen it enough times. Just tell me what you think it shows, and what it adds here that the argument alone doesn't. Then tell me which part of your reading you're least confident about, and what in the film or what argument would change your mind about it.
