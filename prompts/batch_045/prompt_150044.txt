I serve on the Naming and Memorials Committee of the Rivermont Unified School District, a seven-member body appointed by the elected board (six adults and one voting student member) that recommends names for district buildings and can recommend removal of an existing name. Our recommendation goes to the board, which may override it by a simple majority, and our memos are posted publicly, so they are read by parents, alumni, and reporters as well as by trustees.

The case before us is Whitfield Middle School, named in 1959 for Josiah Whitfield (1832–1904), a newspaper editor and later state legislator. The 1959 naming honored two things: his campaign for the city's sewer and clean-water system after the typhoid epidemic of the 1870s, and his founding of the public library. Since then, research by local historians has established that, as editor of the Rivermont Herald, Whitfield ran a sustained campaign from 1885 to 1887 that ended in the violent expulsion of the city's Chinese American residents and the burning of their neighborhood; several hundred people were driven out, and property was never restored. He never retracted the campaign and later supported exclusion legislation in the state legislature. The school is now 38 percent Asian American. Its mascot is the "Whitfield Minutemen," a granite monogram "W" is set into the front lawn, and a bronze relief portrait of Josiah Whitfield hangs in the lobby.

A petition signed by 1,400 residents asks for the name to be gone before the next school year begins; a counter-petition from the alumni association, with 900 signatures, asks that it stay. Three positions have emerged in our own deliberations, and I need the memo to say which one the committee should adopt and why, rather than splitting the difference:

- One member argues that every use of the name — morning announcements, report cards, diplomas, letterhead, the scoreboard — is a fresh act of honoring by the district today, so present standards apply directly and the 1959 decision settles nothing.
- Another argues that the name has become the ordinary identifier of a place and the container of tens of thousands of people's memories, that its connection to Josiah Whitfield has largely evaporated, and that a name of that kind is not ours to reassign by vote; on this view the district holds the name in trust for a community rather than speaking through it.
- A third argues that the point of removal is not to withdraw honor but to stop a specific ongoing injury to a specific group, that injury should be measured by what the name actually does to the people who must live with it daily, and that if it can be stopped without removing the name, removal is optional — possibly a cheap substitute for repair.

Our governing policy permits removal when the honoree's "principal legacy" is "fundamentally at odds with the district's mission." That phrase is doing a great deal of work, and the memo must say how the committee should apply it here, given that Whitfield's record contains both a public-health legacy that plausibly saved many lives and an expulsion campaign that destroyed a community.

Three further complications must be resolved, not noted and deferred:

1. The superintendent has proposed keeping the name but officially rededicating it to Elias Whitfield (1880–1963), a Black teacher and school founder in the district whose grandparents were enslaved by the Whitfield family — that is, the surname in question is the enslaving family's name. She argues this changes whom the name honors without the cost of rebranding.
2. A district-wide poll found that 62 percent of respondents, shown the school's name and asked whom it honors, could not name anyone. The memo should say what weight, if any, that finding should carry, since it seems to cut both ways.
3. Rebranding is estimated at $190,000 (signage, uniforms, athletic equipment, archival reissue of transcripts). The Rivermont Chinese American Heritage Coalition has instead proposed a permanent exhibit on the 1886 expulsion plus a curriculum module, at roughly $150,000 from the same capital fund. The current budget allows one or the other this fiscal year, not both. Separately, a family trust that donated land for the school in 1959 has a reversionary clause triggered if the school "ceases to be known as Whitfield"; district counsel thinks it probably unenforceable but warns of litigation costing years and perhaps $200,000.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the committee before our next meeting. Requirements:

- Written in American English, no more than 1,200 words, addressed to committee members and intelligible to readers with no background in philosophy, history, or law; define any term of art you use.
- Bottom line up front: a single concrete recommendation the committee can vote on, with a timeline, including what happens to the mascot, the monogram, and the lobby relief, and a clear disposition of the rededication proposal and of the budget choice.
- An explicit adoption or rejection of each of the three positions above, with the reasoning that carries the most weight.
- One paragraph on whether the threat of litigation should change the recommendation, and what the committee should do if suit is filed.
- An honest statement of the strongest consideration on the losing side, in its best form — not a restatement of the winning view's premises. If your recommendation is to keep the name, that means the best case for removal; if it is to remove it, the best case for keeping it.
- Do not cite scholarly literature, court decisions, or other outside sources, and do not include a bibliography or suggested reading; the memo must stand on its own reasoning, since it will be read and acted on without any lookup.
