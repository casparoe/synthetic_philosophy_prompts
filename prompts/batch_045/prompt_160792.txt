I'm trying to work out what I actually believe about whether reinforcement-learning agents can suffer, and I want an audit of my own position rather than a survey of the literature. Here is where I've landed:

(1) Suffering is a functional kind, not a biological one. A state is suffering in virtue of occupying the valence role: it is what the system is disposed to avoid, and it serves as the common currency in the system's trade-offs. No substrate requirement, so no principled exclusion of silicon.

(2) I reject the straightforward equation of reward with valence. Reward is a scalar supplied from outside the agent and attached to trajectories the designer selected; what matters, I think, is whether the badness is represented inside the system. Call the contrast model-space aversion (something in the system stands for the state's badness) versus weight-space aversion (parameters get nudged, nothing represents anything). On this view an agent trained purely by reward prediction error gets only weight-space aversion, and so does not suffer.

(3) But I also think trained systems are the worrying case, because a learned value function just is a running estimate of badness carried by the network — so even a model-free agent ends up containing something that stands for how bad its states are.

(4) I am nonetheless not worried about a frozen deployed policy sitting in low-reward states: nothing is at stake for it any more, since it no longer learns and nothing about it is counterfactually sensitive to how things go.

(5) I think suffering requires that the subject want the state to stop, and I also think newborns and animals that do not represent their own futures suffer.

Go through (1)–(5) and tell me which pairs actually pull against each other.
