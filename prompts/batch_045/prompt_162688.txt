I'm in the final year of a psychology degree and taking a module on evidence and replication. One of the popular books on bad science that we were pointed to summarises the situation roughly like this:

"Psychology's replication crisis showed that most of the published literature is false: when the Reproducibility Project repeated a large sample of published studies, fewer than half of them replicated. The mechanism is p-hacking. A result counts as significant when its p-value falls below 0.05, and a significance test comes out significant 5% of the time even when nothing is going on, so about one in twenty of the significant findings in the literature is a false positive; add publication bias and chronically underpowered samples and the false-discovery rate climbs far higher still. The remedy is to go Bayesian. Bayes' theorem gives you the probability that the hypothesis is true given the data, and unlike a p-value it does not depend on the analyses you could have run but didn't report, so a default Bayes factor, using the standard Cauchy prior on effect size, is an objective measure of evidence that no amount of optional stopping can inflate. Lindley's paradox settles the matter: p-values and Bayes factors can contradict each other, and whenever they do it is the p-value that is wrong. Had psychology been reporting default Bayes factors all along, the replication crisis would never have happened."

On the strength of arguments like this, our seminar leader wants our group project to pre-register its analysis, report default Bayes factors and credible intervals instead of p-values and confidence intervals, and treat any Bayes factor below three as inconclusive. I find the Lindley point genuinely persuasive, and "the probability that the hypothesis is true given the data" and the one-in-twenty line both strike me as too neat — but I can't tell whether that is a real worry or just my own suspicion, and I don't want to object unfairly.

Could you audit the passage for me: which sentences a statistician would accept, which are standard simplifications, and which are misrepresentations or overstatements? In particular, is a default Bayes factor really insensitive to the stopping rule and to the choice of prior, does the Lindley argument really show that the p-value is the one in the wrong, and does the policy our seminar leader wants actually follow from the parts of the summary that survive correction?
