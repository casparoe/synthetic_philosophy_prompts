In most common-law jurisdictions the following pattern holds. A clinician negligently fails to detect a severe fetal impairment, or botches a sterilization, and a child is born who would not have been born had due care been taken. Three actions are conceivable, and the law treats them very differently. The child's own action — the claim that she was wronged by being brought into existence, or at least into an existence marked by a serious disability — is almost everywhere refused. Courts say that even an impaired life cannot be an injury; that there is no state of nonexistence with which her actual condition can be compared; that a person has no right not to be born; and sometimes that recognizing such a claim would express the view that lives like hers are not worth living. The parents' action is almost everywhere allowed: they recover the additional costs attributable to the disability — medical care, equipment, special education, lost earnings — but not the ordinary costs of raising a child, which are held to be offset by the benefits the child brings. And where the negligence was a failed sterilization and the child is healthy, courts split: some allow the costs of rearing an unplanned child, others confine recovery to the pregnancy and birth itself, as though a healthy child were pure gain and no injury at all.

I would like the implicit theory underneath that pattern reconstructed, and tested for coherence.

Two complications make it harder to explain than it first looks, and I want both addressed. First, the counterfactual that the parents' damages are measured against is unstable. If the parents, properly warned, would have terminated and later conceived again, then the careful world contains a healthy child — numerically a different child, since a conception weeks later would not have produced the same person — and it contains the same ordinary costs of child-rearing. On a cost-based measure their loss largely disappears, yet courts award the extraordinary costs anyway and generally do not allow the defendant to argue the point. If instead the careful world contains no child at all, then the parents' recovery rests on a comparison between a world containing this disabled child and a world containing nobody in her place — which looks like the very comparison the child's own action was refused for requiring. Second, consider a statutory reform, which I will stipulate rather than cite: a narrow action available only when the child's life is and foreseeably was not worth living, with damages measured only by the shortfall below the threshold of a life worth living and paid into a fund for the child's care rather than to the child.

So: what view of harm, benefit, and counterfactual comparison does the existing practice presuppose? Distinguish, please, what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and be explicit about where several rival theories would all license the pattern. Is it committed to the claim that there is no subject of a harm when a person never exists, to the claim that any life worth living is a net benefit to the person living it, to a threshold view, to a doctrine about the incomparability of existence and nonexistence, or to nothing philosophical at all — a mixture of administrability, the dignity of disabled people, and judicial reluctance to price a life? Is the parents' action really a welfare-based claim about avoided costs, or is it a reproductive-autonomy claim in cost-shaped disguise, and does the benefits-offset rule make sense on each reading? Does the pattern presuppose an answer to the asymmetry between the absence of pain and the absence of pleasure, or could that asymmetry be true while the practice is still correct — because the asymmetry is a claim about what there is reason to prevent rather than about what counts as a compensable legal injury? And if the asymmetry were accepted and built into the law, what exactly would have to change: the child's action, the offset rule, the treatment of the healthy unplanned child, the judicial rhetoric about life as a benefit, or something in the way damages are measured that I have not thought of?
