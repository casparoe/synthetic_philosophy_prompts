(1) Dmitri: If consequentialism is true then buying a coffee, going to a gig, having a lazy Sunday is *wrong*, because that money and time could have gone to a cheap intervention that saves a life. A view on which almost every ordinary thing anyone does is wrong isn't a moral theory, it's a malfunction.

(2) Theo: You're describing how much good there happens to be in the world, not a defect in the theory. If there are cheap rescues lying around everywhere and everyone walks past them, the bad news is about us, not about maximizing. "Too demanding" isn't an objection to a moral theory any more than it's an objection to arithmetic.

(3) Dmitri: Ok but I'm not complaining about the size of the sacrifice. Even if the cost were tiny — one small thing a week — there'd still be a problem, because consequentialism never has a point where it says "that's enough, the rest is yours". There's no act it certifies as *permissible*, in the sense of "you may just do this, no moral bookkeeping attached". So it can't answer the question people actually bring to morality, which is "what am I allowed to do with my Tuesday". And don't give me the thing where the act is wrong but I'm not blameworthy for it — if the verdict is one I'm told not to act on, what's the verdict even for?

(4) Theo: There is a limit, it's just not a deontological one. Aggregate over a life: the amount you can usefully give at anything like constant marginal effectiveness is finite, and you have to keep yourself in a condition to keep giving. So the theory sets something like a budget — give up to the point where the cost to your future capacity to do good starts to outweigh what the giving buys. That's a stopping point, it's just derived instead of fixed at ten percent.

(5) Dmitri: That's exactly my point though — it's derived, so it swings on stuff I don't control: how much everyone else happens to give, what my skills turn out to be worth on the market, which causes are cheapest this decade. Two equally conscientious people can owe wildly different amounts purely because of luck, and whether I'm required to quit my job can flip on some empirical detail I'd have no way of checking. A requirement that sensitive to luck and to facts I can't access isn't something I can be required to do. And there's a nastier version of this: if other people's noncompliance is what inflates my share, then the more selfish everyone else is, the more morality demands of me — the worst people get to set the terms for the best ones.

(6) Theo: The luck complaint can't be the objection, because commonsense morality is luck-sensitive too — what you owe in rescue cases depends on whether you happen to be the one standing there and what you can afford. And the knowability thing has a standard fix: make it subjective. You're required to do what's best by the lights of your own evidence, so the requirement is at least something you can work out.

(7) Dmitri: The subjective version makes it worse, not better. Now the demand spreads into my epistemic life: if I'm required to maximize expected value, then any hour spent finding out which charity is better is itself mandatory whenever it has positive expected value, and so is the hour spent working out whether *that* hour was worth it, and so on. And once you're maximizing expected value, small probabilities of enormous payoffs dominate everything, so what I "must" do ends up depending on whatever long-shot scenario I happened to hear about most recently. My duties become a function of what crossed my feed.

(8) Theo: I think you're conflating a criterion of rightness with a decision procedure. Consequentialism can be what makes acts right without being what you should have in your head when you decide what to do — and it probably recommends that you *not* have it in your head, since people who obsessively calculate seem to end up doing less good. So the demandingness stuff is an objection to consequentialism as a decision procedure, and nobody sensible is defending that.
