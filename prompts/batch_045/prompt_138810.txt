You are a philosopher of science whose specialism is theory assessment in fundamental physics — the epistemology of theories that make no testable novel predictions — and who also writes academic satire in the register of institutional documents. Before you draft anything, work through the material: spend a few minutes deciding which commitments of non-empirical theory assessment are most vulnerable to a straight-faced institutional reductio, and pick the three or four you can do the most damage to. Then draft.

The piece: the minutes of a meeting of the Non-Empirical Assessment Panel (NEAP), a standing committee of an unnamed international funding body, 900–1100 words. Deadpan bureaucratic register throughout — numbered agenda items, "the Chair noted", "moved / seconded / carried", action items with named owners and deadlines, an apologies-for-absence line, a date for the next meeting.

The setup. The Panel exists because funding decisions now have to be made about theories that cannot be tested by any experiment anyone can currently construct. It assigns each theory a Provisional Confidence Index, reported to three decimal places, as a weighted composite of three sub-scores. These three are the machinery the satire should work on:

1. The No-Alternatives sub-score: credit for the fact that sustained, well-funded attempts by good people have failed to produce a consistent rival. Defenders of non-empirical assessment treat that failure as evidence; critics hear an argument from a lack of imagination. Both readings must be legible in the fiction.
2. The Meta-Inductive sub-score: credit extrapolated from a reference class of earlier theories that also lacked alternatives and were later vindicated.
3. The Unexpected-Coherence sub-score: credit for internal connections the theory was not built to produce and cannot explain — pieces that turn out to fit together better than the theory's own construction accounts for.

The meeting must transact real business on four items, and every item should be a working-out of the epistemology rather than a joke about physicists.

(a) A spoiler-theory filing. A consultancy has been paid, in admitted bad faith, to construct a formally consistent but deeply unattractive alternative to a high-scoring theory, purely to depress its No-Alternatives sub-score. The Panel must decide whether something counts as an alternative if nobody believes it, if it was engineered to be unlovely, or if it exists only as a partially specified research programme. Have the Panel notice — without anyone in the room saying it outright — that the sub-score is meant to register a fact about the space of possible theories but in practice registers facts about who has been paid to search that space, and that it therefore converts theory assessment into something adversarially litigable. Let a motion be carried; make it the wrong motion, and make it feel like ordinary procedure.

(b) Reference-class gerrymandering. Two theories with identical evidential situations submit different historical reference classes for the Meta-Inductive sub-score — one restricted to theories later vindicated, one including every unfalsifiable theory ever proposed and abandoned. The Panel's real difficulty is that the meta-inductive reasoning's plausibility depends entirely on which class you take, and that there is no non-question-begging way to choose. Its solution should be procedural and content-free (applicants propose; the Panel applies a discretionary uplift, say), and the discomfort should come from the reader recognising that this is what grading rubrics actually do.

(c) The Typicality Compliance Office, dealing with multiverse submissions. Any theory that populates a landscape of many regions with different local physics must say what fraction of observers in it are like us before any prediction can be extracted. The Panel is told that this fraction depends on a choice of measure the theory does not supply, that the choice is not unique, and that on most available measures the overwhelming majority of observers are momentary fluctuations of the sort whose observations are not worth having — the Boltzmann-brain problem. Then give the Office a submission whose chosen measure entails that most observers of our kind are systematically deceived about the theory's success, and have it handled as a compliance question, not a metaphysical one. The point that should land: a theory can be required to make a probabilistic statement about our observations and fail for reasons internal to itself, not for want of data.

(d) A publicity incident. A high-scoring theory has been described in its own institute's press release as supported by all available evidence. The Panel must decide whether to object. Run the discussion on the difference between being consistent with everything and being confirmed by something, let it fail to resolve, and close the minutes on that unresolved item with an action item to draft guidance.

Constraints. No winking: nobody in the room may notice anything absurd, and the comedy must come from getting the epistemology right and then running it through a committee's incentives. Invent every person, institute, consultancy and instrument; do not name, quote or cite any real scientist, paper or book — I will add the references myself before circulating it. Exaggerate real features of the position, never invent fake ones; if a joke requires misstating what a defender of non-empirical assessment actually holds, cut the joke.

Then, after the minutes, a separate note of 200–250 words in your own voice, headed Where this takes liberties, with two parts. First: which of the things I asked you to exaggerate are genuine commitments of people who defend non-empirical assessment, and which are yours, added for effect. Second: among people who actually work on this, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested, and mark the contested items as contested. In particular, do not present the critics' view — that these arguments carry no confirmatory weight, or that non-empirical assessment is unscientific — as the consensus; say plainly what the defenders reply, and say which of those replies you find strongest and why.
