I write a recurring column for a science-and-ideas magazine — working title "What Kind of Money Is This?", which my editor hates but hasn't replaced — and every three weeks or so I have to take some cross-border payment from a rich country, or a rich-country foundation, to a poorer one and explain in about 900 words whether it's discharging a duty, doing charity, or buying something. The house rule is that I can't type the words "should" or "owe" unless I can say which kind of owing it is, and I've noticed I'm inconsistent: last year I called a maternal-health programme "reparations-adjacent" and a cash transfer programme "charity", and a reader wrote in to point out that if anything it should be the other way around. So I want an actual procedure, not a vibe.

Here's the material I think it should be built out of, but you should tell me which bits survive the translation into something usable. First, the split I keep coming back to between a remedial duty (we broke it, we pay) and a general duty to assist (they need it, we can help at low cost to ourselves) — the drowning-child style argument for the second, and the view associated with Pogge, I think, that the real duty is not to impose an unjust global institutional order, for the first. Second, a test I've been using informally and have no name for: whose description of the good the money is denominated in. A transfer that arrives as "here is money, spend it on what you think matters" and one that arrives as "here is money for a thing called maternal health, delivered by our contractors, measured against our indicators" seem to me to be doing different moral work even when the dollar figure is identical — the second one has the donor's name on the good. Third, something I've started calling obligation laundering, where a payment that is straightforwardly a purchase — of border cooperation, of a vote, of access — gets described in the language of duty, and the language does real work by shutting down the question of whether the price is fair.

What I want from you is this: a decision procedure, in the order I'd actually run the questions, that takes a press release plus maybe one phone call and gets me to one of a small number of labels, or to a stop. For each question say what counts as a sufficient answer given that I'm working from a press release and a phone call in twenty minutes and can't see internal budget documents; where the procedure just stops and says "no label — describe the mechanics literally and don't reach for duty language"; and where it has to hand the question to a person because it's a disagreement about values rather than facts (whether a collective can owe anything at all, whether responsibility transmits down generations, whether duties of assistance are enforceable — I assume some of these are undecidable for my purposes and I want them flagged as such, not quietly settled by the procedure). Then run it on three cases: (1) a former colonial power announcing a ten-year, few-hundred-million-dollar maternal health program in a country it ruled until the 1960s, delivered through its own contractors, timed a few months after a court ruling about abuses in the colonial period; (2) a rich government paying a poorer government to host asylum-processing centres and accept deportation flights; (3) unconditional cash transfers to very poor households, funded by a private foundation with no government involved. One of the three needs to be a case where the honest output makes me look soft on something I've been hammering for years, and if none of them is that case, tell me which one I'm misreading and why. Finally, be explicit about what the procedure can't do: I'd rather have a checklist that admits it misfires on, say, multi-donor trust funds or concessional lending than one that pretends to cover everything, and I need to know which of my invented bits — the name-on-the-good test, obligation laundering, and whatever you'd call the remedial/assistance split — are load-bearing and which are just my hobbyhorses that a cleaner version would drop.
