I teach an upper-level seminar in the philosophy department on ethics and narrative, and for the past few years I have opened it with a genre rather than an argument: pairs of lives written to be imitated. The canonical examples are Plutarch's Parallel Lives and the Christian saint's life, but I also assign one modern descendant of the same impulse — the admiring life-of-a-great-leader of the kind still handed out in leadership programs — partly because analytic moral philosophy has lately revived exemplar-based accounts of virtue, and I want to know what the older practice was actually committed to before I decide what to make of the revival. What interests me is not whether the genre is respectable but what it must assume in order to work, and it seems to me to run on conventions that are not the conventions of history writing. First, selection obeys what I have been calling the revelatory-priority rule: the biographer prefers the small unguarded moment — a jest, a remark at dinner, how someone received bad news — to the battle or the statute, on the grounds that the latter may be the work of fortune or of subordinates while the former shows the agent; Plutarch more or less says as much in distinguishing the writing of lives from the writing of histories. Second, there is a closure rule: judgment on a life is suspended until the life is complete, the thought Herodotus attributes to Solon about calling no one happy before the end, and it is why a death is treated as evidence rather than as an appendix. Third, there is what I call the typology rule, clearest in hagiography and in confessional autobiography: the childhood is narrated as a figure of the whole, and the subject's faults are retained but redescribed as the material of a turning, so that nothing in the life is permitted to remain merely unassimilated. Fourth, there is a license that would be flagrant anywhere else: where the record is silent, the writer supplies what such a person in such circumstances would have said or done — a convention ancient historiography avowed openly, that hagiography inherited, and that survives in the composite scenes and invented dialogue of the contemporary biopic. And fifth, the pairs invite ranking: the two lives are followed by an explicit comparison and a verdict about which was better.

Two features of this puzzle me. The revelatory-priority rule and the closure rule seem to pull in opposite directions: if a single jest discloses character, why should the end of the life be evidentially required? My own tentative answer is that they answer different questions — the jest shows who someone is, the ending settles whether that character held — but I cannot tell whether that reconciliation costs me a substantive commitment to the stability of character across a life, or whether it is equally available to someone who thinks conduct is highly situation-dependent. The license bothers me more. The stated point is to learn from a real person, and yet the genre treats the documented record as something to be completed rather than merely reported. So I have been contrasting what I call the documentary constraint (the narrated incidents must have occurred) with the legibility constraint (they must be the sort of thing this agent could have done), and I suspect the educational aim is satisfied by the second and not by the first — which would mean that the modern exhaustively footnoted definitive biography is not a more honest version of the same enterprise but a different enterprise that has quietly abandoned the aim. A colleague in the history department, who regards the whole genre as a confidence trick, says the license simply proves the aim was never truth-tracking; that strikes me as too fast, but I do not have a better formulation. I also give my students a test case: a woman whose life the genre could render beautifully and who then dies at thirty-two in an accident that reveals nothing whatever about her. Under the closure rule her life seems to lose something. Should it? If it does, the genre is committed to a view of lives as possessing narrative shape that I take to be far from obvious.

So I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory: what view of character, of moral knowledge, and of truth in narrative does this practice require, and which rival views does it quietly rule out? I want the strict presuppositions separated from the merely congenial ones, and I want to know whether the five conventions genuinely hang together or only look as though they do because we inherited them as a package; where several theories would license the same convention, say so rather than settling it, and where you think the genre is cheating, say that as well. Please commit to a verdict on the two questions I cannot answer myself — whether the editorial license is internal to the educational aim or a corruption of it, and whether the genre requires lives to have a narrative closure they may not in fact have — and give your reasons. Mark which of your claims you hold with real confidence and which are readings a reasonable person could reject; I am less interested in a survey of positions than in a reconstruction I can argue with. I am not asking for a bibliography, and I would rather you reasoned from the practice as I have described it than reached for sources.
