You are a professional philosopher working in metaphysics, specifically on causation and agency. Please think carefully and slowly about the task below. Before you give your verdict, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming: lay out the main available positions on whether omissions (failures to act) can be causes; note which distinctions you expect to carry the weight in this particular dispute; and flag any point where you think the two speakers below are talking past each other or trading on an ambiguity. I was at a dinner after a metaphysics colloquium last week where two of my colleagues got into an argument I have not been able to settle. They have both asked me to referee it and I honestly cannot. They agreed about the starting case and disagreed about everything downstream of it.

The case they agreed on: a warehouse night watchman, Vernon, is required by his job description to deadbolt the loading-dock door at 10 p.m. He does not. A thief walks through the door at 2 a.m. and wheels out a pallet of server components. Both of them accepted, at least for the sake of argument, that it is fine to say that Vernon's failure to lock the door caused the loss. Their dispute was about what that claim amounts to and how far it extends. Nadia spoke first.

1. Nadia: The truth in the neighbourhood of that sentence is not a causal claim at all. When someone acts, there is a process linking her to the outcome -- muscle contractions, a bolt sliding, further physical events -- and causation is that sort of link. When someone omits, nothing links her to the outcome: there is no event of her omitting located anywhere, no time at which it does its work, and no process running from her to the warehouse. The real contents of the claim are that she allowed the loss, or failed to prevent it, or is answerable for it. "Caused" is a façon de parler we tolerate because the counterfactual happens to be true. Notice how thin it is: you cannot say her omission caused the thief to walk in, or caused the door to be unlocked, since the door was unlocked all along. Omissions never make anything happen. They only ever fail to stop something happening.

2. Oscar: That is too quick. There is a process -- the thief's walk, the pallet going out the door -- and Vernon's role is that he did not interrupt it. Causing a failure of prevention is still causing. Had he acted, that process would have been stopped before it reached the outcome. That also handles the location and timing worries: the omission is located where and when he was, doing nothing, and the process it failed to interrupt is located in the warehouse. The asymmetry you point to is real, but it is a difference in how the two kinds of cause do their work, not a reason to deny that omissions are causes. Positive causes act; omissions let a process through. The sense of "cause" is derivative, second-grade, but it is the same relation: counterfactual dependence on what the agent did or did not do, along a route that actually reaches the outcome.

3. Nadia: Then your test overgenerates, and you cannot fix it without smuggling norms back in. Had the city council voted for a streetlight on that corner, the thief would have been deterred. Had the owner installed a camera, likewise. Had the thief's parents raised him differently, likewise. Each is a true counterfactual about something that did not happen, and each would have prevented the loss along a route that reaches the outcome. You want to exclude them by saying Vernon was supposed to lock the door and the council was not supposed to install a streetlight. But "supposed to" is a normative fact. So either causation by omission depends on what the agent ought to have done -- which collapses the distinction between causation and responsibility -- or you keep causation norm-free and you are committed to saying that the council's not installing a streetlight caused the theft.

4. Oscar: The overgeneration complaint proves too much, because it has an exact analogue for acts. Every claim of actual causation is made against a backdrop: we do not say the firefighter's mother caused the fire, even though had she not given birth to him it would have burned unsuppressed. Selectivity is what actual causation is; it is not a special problem for omissions. And you can do the selecting without appeal to duty. Hold fixed what actually happened, and ask whether the agent's alternative action would still have made a difference given that actual history. The streetlight case fails, because installing one would have required a different prior history -- a vote, a budget, a contractor -- and nothing in the actual sequence was sitting there waiting for a streetlight to stop it. Vernon's locking the door is different: the door was unlocked, the thief was coming, and the bolt was the one thing in the actual arrangement positioned to stop him. No norms needed.

5. Nadia: Then try this one. At a remote Arctic research station, the night technician is required to run an automated diagnostic on the backup generator's fuel line at 02:00. If it detects a fault it sends a text message to the duty engineer, who would then drive out and fix the line. One night the technician sleeps through it. There is a crack in the line, the generator fails at 04:00, the heating cuts out, the station's water recirculation loop freezes and bursts, and a rack of calibrated instruments is destroyed. Had she run the diagnostic, the instruments would have survived. But look at the route. Nothing at the station would have changed except current in a phone speaker forty kilometres away. All of the difference-making sits in what the engineer would have come to believe and then chosen to do. So: is her omission a cause of the destruction? If yes, your account now permits causation whose entire route runs through another agent's counterfactual deliberation, and that deliberation is not itself an effect of anything -- causation with no physical connection anywhere in it. If no, then the thing doing the work in the Vernon case was never the route but the bare counterfactual, and you owe me an account of why one bare counterfactual is causal and the other is not.

6. Oscar: Yes, her omission caused the destruction. A signal, a belief, a decision, a truck, a wrench, a fuel line: chains that run through agents are still chains. What makes this one fragile is that it depends on more contingent facts, not that it is a different kind of relation. And the case actually helps me against your move 3, because what makes her omission count is not that she was under a duty but that running the diagnostic was, in the actual arrangement, positioned to set off a route to prevention. Duty is a useful heuristic for that and nothing more.

7. Nadia: Then take the same station with one change. No technician was ever assigned to run the diagnostic; the station manager simply never created the role. The fuel line fails anyway, same crack, same instruments destroyed. Now consider the counterfactual: had someone been assigned, and had she run the diagnostic, the engineer would have driven out and the instruments would have survived. On your own test -- a route in the actual arrangement positioned to prevent the outcome, no norms needed -- the non-existence of an assigned technician caused the destruction. But there is no omission here at all, since there is nobody who omitted to do anything. So either you say that the absence of a person causes things, which I take to be a reductio, or you readmit the duty filter at precisely the point where you said you did not need it. And note that the two stations differ only in whether the role exists, which is an administrative and hence normative fact. The causal claim rides on that fact.

8. Oscar: The two cases are not the same shape. "Had someone been assigned" is not a counterfactual about what a particular person would have done; it rewrites the prior history of the station. My test asked you to hold actual history fixed and vary only the agent's own action. With no technician there, there is no action to vary, so the test never gets going. No omission, no cause. That is not me readmitting norms; it is just the requirement that there be something whose alternative is being assessed.

9. Nadia: That answer concedes the interesting point. Your test delivers a verdict only once a person occupies the right role, and whether she occupies the right role is exactly the normative fact. Vernon is on the roster; at the second station nobody is. So the difference between a station where an omission causes the destruction and one where nothing causes it is made by an administrative decision rather than by anything in the causal structure of the world. If that is where we end up, then "caused by an omission" is a claim that essentially embeds a normative judgement about roles, and my original point survives in moderated form: whatever we are doing with that word, it is not the same relation we have between events, even if we are happy to use the same word for both.
