I have no philosophy background myself, so please don't assume I know the jargon, and explain any term you do use.

The thing I'm trying to show: the standard picture of a rational agent as a Bayesian with a prior can't model the value of *computing* something. My worry is that when the open question is a mathematical fact that's already settled in principle (the refactored module either preserves behavior on every input or it doesn't; a proof of the equivalence either exists or it doesn't), a strict Bayesian has nowhere to put "I'm about 70% on this, let me spend three days of compute to find out," because the kind of agent that picture describes assigns 0 or 1 to every such claim already.

So: build me a decision problem where

(a) the framework I'll call "strict Bayesian" (prior over possible worlds, update by conditioning, mathematical facts don't vary across worlds so credences in them are 0 or 1) says: don't run the computation, act now, the run has no epistemic value, only cost;

(b) a "logical uncertainty" framework — a bounded agent with intermediate credences in decidable mathematical claims, the sort of thing people gesture at with that "logical inductor" stuff I only half understand — says: run it, and pays a real cost to do so;

(c) judged from outside by someone who knows the actual answer and the actual costs, the (b) agent really does better in expectation. Not just different, better.

Constraints, so the case isn't cheap:

- Ordinary setting: a team, a codebase, a deadline, and a check you could actually run (a solver, a fuzz campaign, a proof search, whatever fits). No oracles, no uncomputable magic, no perfect reasoners, no sci-fi.
- The uncertainty has to be *purely* mathematical. I know the usual escape: "ah, but you're not really uncertain about the math, you're uncertain whether your checker is correct, or whether the machine bitflipped" — and then the run becomes a normal empirical experiment and the Bayesian is happy again. Please close that door in the setup (verified checker, deterministic run, hardware fine, nothing empirical left to learn). If there's another escape of that shape, close it too, and tell me which one you closed.
- The bounded agent must not look stupid or incoherent. Specifically: I want the case to involve TWO logically related claims (a strong one and a weaker one it entails, or a claim and something it rules out), and I want you to show that the bounded agent's two numbers respect the logical relation — so the "you're just making up numbers, I can Dutch-book you" objection doesn't get off the ground from the start. Then say something about the version of that objection I actually care about: could a bookie who is limited to roughly the same computation the agent has still exploit it?
- The verdicts have to fall out of the frameworks, not be written in. If your case only works because you stipulated "and then the agent reasonably has credence 0.7," that's cheating — I want to know what feature of the situation generates the numbers.
- Scenario itself in one paragraph-ish; then as much analysis as you need.

Deliverable: the scenario; then what each framework says and why, in terms my team will follow; then the single feature doing the work and what happens to the case if you remove it; then the strongest pushback from someone who thinks the strict Bayesian framing is the only coherent game in town, and whether you think that pushback lands.
