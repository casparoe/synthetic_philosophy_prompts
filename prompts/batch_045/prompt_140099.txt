I teach a final-year seminar on political ethics and I'm putting together a small reader of satirical pieces to set next to the straight readings, because my students keep reacting to the dirty-hands literature in one of two useless ways: half of them decide it's just an excuse politicians reach for, and the other half decide it's a profound tragic insight and repeat it back to me in essays. What I want is something that gets at the second failure by taking the literature absolutely seriously and trying to administer it. So: write me a leaked government consultation paper — a green paper or draft statutory instrument, 700–900 words, in the voice of an exhausted but genuinely competent civil servant — proposing to institutionalise dirty hands. The tone is deadpan and the voice never breaks: no winking, no sentences that tell the reader this is appalling, nothing like "of course, one might worry…" The recoil has to be the reader's job.

The proposal, which the paper should treat as obviously sensible, is this: some acts are genuinely morally wrong and yet are what the official must do, and the official who does them and sleeps fine is not a good leader but a monster — so a department must be built to guarantee both halves at once. Things I want in it: (1) a Guilt Preservation regime — because guilt erodes with repetition, Hands serve a single term, are never re-used, and have their remorse audited against a target, with an acknowledged problem that the target is being gamed and a proposed fix that makes the gaming worse; (2) something I've been calling the Non-Justification Clause (use it or rename it) — since the act can't be justified to the agent without destroying the moral remainder, the Hand is contractually barred from being told the operative reasons, which the paper notes with satisfaction keeps the residue intact and somehow never notices also leaves her unable to tell whether she isn't simply a criminal; (3) punishment as a credential — the Hand must be publicly disgraced, since willingness to pay the price is supposedly the mark of the good official, and then quietly pensioned, with the contradiction handled in a footnote that resolves nothing; (4) a rejected annex on recruiting officials with clean hands, dismissed as useless, and (5) a short note on the recruitment crisis: candidates who are eager are automatically disqualified, because enjoying it is disqualifying, and the Directorate is short-staffed. End with the audit problem — to certify that guilt is sincere you need auditors unmoved by guilt, i.e. the Department must go out and recruit the monsters it exists to prevent — and let the paper register the circularity in exactly one flat sentence and move on. Add a short "Questions for Consultees" section, and one quote from a moral philosopher the department has retained as a consultant, quoted approvingly, who finds the scheme theoretically elegant; invent everyone, don't name any real person, don't cite anything, and try to work in at least one line from a serving or former Hand.

The constraint that matters most: the jokes have to come from getting the ethics right. Every clause should be an honest consequence of a real claim in that literature — that the wrongness survives the justification, that the residue is guilt rather than relief, that routinisation turns dirty hands into mere immorality, that we need such people and should not trust anyone who wants the job — rather than an invented absurdity bolted on. Someone who knows the material should be able to point at each paragraph and say yes, that really does follow. And I want the satire to land as fair: the takeaway shouldn't be "politicians are hypocrites," it should be "here is what this view commits you to the moment you try to run it as policy." Spelling and formatting should be blandly bureaucratic throughout (I've imagined it as British-ish, with numbered clauses and explanatory notes between them, but don't feel tied to that), and please keep it to under 900 words — it has to fit on one side of a handout alongside the real thing.
