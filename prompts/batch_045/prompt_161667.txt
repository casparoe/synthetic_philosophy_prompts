I write about science and ideas for a national magazine, and several times a week my desk hits what is recognisably the same decision in a different costume: whether to publish an identifying detail about a named or nameable individual. Last month it was a photograph of a graduate student taken outside the lab where a misconduct investigation was underway; this week it is whether to name the author of an intemperate email that surfaced in a public-records release about a university's handling of a biosafety incident, and whether to include the street, though not the house number, of a patient whose rare-disease case is the spine of a feature. Our working rule has always been roughly that if the material is already public and relevant to the story, we run it. That rule has started to feel as though it answers a different question from the one we are actually facing — a friend who works in data protection got quite exercised about this over dinner and I could not say why she was right or wrong — and my standards editor has asked me to bring her something she can actually run in twenty minutes, with a reporter who has no philosophy training, a lawyer whose entire vocabulary is defamation, and her own sign-off required before the story closes.

What I want from you is a decision procedure, not an essay on privacy. Please build it as an ordered set of questions, each with a statement of what would count as a sufficient answer, each with an explicit point at which the procedure stops with a no, and each with an explicit point at which it stops and hands the decision to my editor because the unresolved question is one of values rather than of fact. It should be grounded in whatever is genuinely defensible about what privacy is and why it matters, and I would like it to have a step — or a stated refusal to have a step — for each of the difficulties I keep tripping over. First, whether privacy is a quantity, a matter of how many people know something, or a matter of who comes to know it and in what capacity, given that the same fact seems to change its character depending on whether the second audience is a clinician, an employer, a neighbour, or a searchable archive. Second, the aggregation problem: each item on its own discloses little, but the set assembled discloses something the subject never put forward, and our item may be the missing link rather than the revealer. Third, the already-public defence, including its awkward variant in which something was legitimately public in one setting years ago and is now being re-presented to a far larger audience with different purposes and a longer memory. Fourth, the complaints that are not about harm at all, where nothing bad is likely to happen to the person and the objection is still that something of theirs has been taken; the procedure needs to say what to do with those if privacy has a value that is not just a stand-in for downstream injury. Fifth, whether the thing that can have this interest is only a living individual, or whether a group, a company, or a dead person can have it too.

Then run the procedure on three of our cases: naming the author of the leaked email; publishing the photograph of the graduate student, who was never charged with anything and whom the piece mentions in a single clause; and printing the street name in the rare-disease feature, where the patient consented to being written about but the consent form did not contemplate an address. Tell me what the procedure says and where it stops. I suspect it will rule against me on at least one of these in a way that will make me look squeamish to an editor who wants the colour — do not soften that one, and give me the sentence I would have to say to defend it. Finally, be straight about the procedure's failure modes: the kinds of case it will systematically get wrong, the situations in which a twenty-minute version is a sham, and what no such checklist can do for us at all.
