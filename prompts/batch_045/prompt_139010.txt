So, slightly weird situation: last year my partner and I moved to Budapest because her mum got sick, and since I needed something to do I ended up doing the numbers for a tiny rule-of-law watchdog here (my degree is in statistics, not politics, so I am defintely making a lot of this up as I go). We publish an annual early-warning score for a handful of countries in the region, and now that it is actually getting quoted by journalists and one ratings agency I have started worrying that I don't know what the thing I built is even claiming.

Roughly how it works, so you don't have to guess. We score governments on things like: the share of legislation passed under fast-track or emergency procedures that skip committee review; how often the head of an independent agency or court is replaced before their term expires, especially when the replacement then gets a fresh full term instead of just the remainder; new rules that move something out of ordinary statute into the constitution or otherwise require a supermajority to undo; the split of public-media airtime between government and opposition; and any change to electoral law in the twelve months before a vote. Two design choices are mine and I'm not sure I can defend them. First, a ratchet: the score can basically only get worse. If a government repeals something we flagged, we stop adding points but we don't hand back the ones already on the board. Second, a change passed with opposition support is discounted heavily, almost to zero, while the same change forced through on party lines counts at full weight.

The puzzle for me is that my father-in-law, who votes Fidesz and is otherwise a perfectly reasonable person, says: we won, twice with a two-thirds majority, we get to govern, and you've just built a spreadsheet that says sore losers are right. And he has a point I can't shake: every government in Europe used emergency fast-track during covid, a new government with a big mandate is supposed to pass a lot quickly, and most of what we flag was approved by voters and was legal when it passed. So I have three connected questions.

One: what view of democracy does this practice actually presuppose? My best guess is that it isn't measuring how democratic a decision was, it's measuring whether incumbents are reducing the chance they can lose the next one — that democracy is a repeated contest, not a single mandate. But is that the only theory that fits, or could someone with a purely majoritarian view endorse the same indicators for instrumental reasons? I want to know which bits of the score strictly require the anti-majoritarian commitment and which bits are underdetermined by it, i.e. which parts several rival theories would happily license.

Two: the ratchet and the aggregation. Does refusing to give points back presuppose something substantive — that institutional trust or a habit of restraint is easier to destroy than to rebuild — or is it just institutional caution dressed up as measurement? And is there an argument that it makes the score unfalsifiable, since no country can ever get better? Separately, we boil down cases that seem to fail in completely different ways — legal entrenchment here, court capture in Poland, emergency rule plus jailing rivals in Turkey, mostly subnational election administration and just norms in the US — into one number. Does producing a single score commit us to those failures being commensurable, or is that just convenient and we should be publishing a dashboard instead?

Three, and this is the one that keeps me up: who decides? Most of our indicators need a judgment call — was this fast-track urgency or abuse? — and my colleagues are pretty open that they already know which governments are the bad ones. So is backsliding more than a partisan label here? What non-circular test could distinguish our number from a vibe: something the concept predicts that isn't just a restatement of what we already counted? And does it matter that in the US case almost nothing we flag is a change in formal law at all — does the practice presuppose that democracy is a property of a whole system including unwritten norms and subnational units, rather than of statutes?

Also worth saying: individually, nearly every step we flag is legal and would be unobjectionable on its own, and it's only the sequence we call backsliding. Does that mean the concept applies to sequences in a way it doesn't apply to the individual acts, like a heap, or is there a way to locate the wrongness in the steps after all?

I'd like: the implicit theory reconstructed, then the strict-presupposition vs merely-goes-well-with distinction, then where we're philosophically underdetermined, then what would concretely have to change in the score if we swapped in each rival view you name. Plain language please, no citations needed, and end with a one-sentence bottom line followed by a last line in fixed form: \answer{...} containing a two- or three-word label — something like strictly anti-majoritarian, compatible with several views, or just politics with numbers — as your verdict on what our score commits us to.
