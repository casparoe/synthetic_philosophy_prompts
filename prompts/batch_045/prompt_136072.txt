You are a philosopher of mind who is also fluent in how software is actually built, ported, emulated and lost — someone equally at home with the literature on functionalism and multiple realizability and with virtual machines, clean-room reimplementations and trained surrogates. Please think about this carefully and in stages. Before producing the deliverable, do some unstructured setup: list the distinctions from the functionalism literature that you think will actually carry weight for my problem, say which ones you are deliberately setting aside and why, and flag in advance any point where you expect the analogy between mental states and software artifacts to break. Then write the deliverable.

Here is my situation. I direct the software and moving-image preservation unit of a national archive. Several times a week — roughly two hundred cases a year — my team has to decide whether a later artifact is the same work as an earlier one, or merely a version, a derivative, or a new work inspired by it. The stakes are not academic: the answer determines legal-deposit status, whether a re-issue may carry the original title and catalogue number, whether we can grant a loan or an exhibition copy, and in three live cases it determines whether a rights holder's permission is needed at all. Our decisions are published and can be appealed.

Our current in-house rule was written by a committee years ago and reads roughly as follows: two artifacts are the same work just in case they have the same functional organisation — the same internal states, related to one another and to inputs and outputs in the same way — and the physical stuff they are built out of is irrelevant. I am told this is close to a position called functionalism, and that the irrelevance of the stuff is something called multiple realizability. The rule has started to fail us, and I can list how:

One, it does not separate two things we keep confusing: an artifact that reproduces the documented input-output behaviour of the original, and an artifact that reproduces the original's internal organisation — the same intermediate states doing the same causal work on one another. Our cases now include a learned surrogate: a neural network trained on tens of thousands of hours of recorded play sessions which reproduces the original's behaviour on our entire test corpus, but in which nobody can point to anything that plays the role of the original's program states. Two, a colleague has pointed out that if you are allowed to choose the mapping from physical states of a system to states of the program freely enough, then almost any physical system "implements" almost any program, which would make our rule say yes to everything. Three, in practice our answers flip depending on how finely we describe things: at a coarse grain two artifacts are the same, at a fine grain they are not, and nothing in the rule tells us which grain is the official one. Four, we have chains: a 1993 original, a 2001 port, a 2011 rewrite, a 2020 rebuild for a new runtime. Each link in the chain was accepted by my predecessors as preserving identity, but the 1993 and 2020 artifacts share almost no code, no hardware and no documented states. Five, we are beginning to receive realizations — analog, neuromorphic, quantum — in which it is unclear whether there are discrete internal states to be organised at all.

What I need from you is a decision procedure my team can actually run, not a position paper. Concretely:

A numbered sequence of stages, with the question to ask at each stage, what counts as a sufficient answer to that question, the point at which the procedure stops and returns a no, and the point at which it stops and hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than fact. Mark the judgment calls that cannot be turned into a test as judgment calls rather than dressing them up as tests.

An operational fix for each of my five failures above. In particular: how to choose and state the grain of description in advance; how to choose the set of counterfactual cases a candidate must be tested against when the original is no longer runnable and its behaviour on untested inputs is unknowable; and how to rule out the gerrymandered-mapping problem in a way an archivist can check — my own attempt is to require that the state mapping be specified in advance of testing and be the mapping a competent engineer would reconstruct from the documentation alone, and I would like you to take that attempt apart and replace it with something better.

Honesty about the translation. Tell me which distinctions from the theory must survive being made usable by non-philosophers, which can be dropped without loss, and where the simplified version will give answers the unsimplified theory would not give.

Then run the finished procedure on four cases and show the work: (a) a virtual-machine emulation of the original binary, running the original instruction stream on new hardware; (b) a clean-room reimplementation from a written functional specification, where the source is lost and the reimplementation is known to diverge from the original on a small set of undocumented behaviours that no surviving documentation describes; (c) the learned surrogate described above; (d) the 1993-to-2020 chain, where the question is whether preservation of identity is transitive across accepted links. I expect at least one of these to come out as a no that we would very much rather not hear — the surrogate is, for several works, the only way anyone will ever be able to experience them again, and the rights holders are untraceable. Give me that unwelcome answer directly and defend it against the objection I have just handed you.

Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do: the questions about sameness that it silently converts into something else, the cases where our use of the word work is doing legal or institutional work rather than metaphysical work, and the point at which my team should stop asking what the artifacts really are and start deciding what we owe the public.

Constraints on the answer: the person running this has an undergraduate computer science degree and no philosophy; a decision must be reachable within two weeks using evidence we can realistically assemble; a three-person committee of a curator, an engineer and a rights officer has to sign off on the output; and the procedure must still return something usable when the source code is lost, the original hardware is gone, or the documentation is a single manual page. I am not asking for citations, references or further reading — I want the argument and the procedure themselves.
