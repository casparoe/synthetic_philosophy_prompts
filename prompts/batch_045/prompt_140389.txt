I want the specification before I go back to any particular account: what does a theory of the badness of human extinction, over and above the badness of the deaths it involves, have to deliver in order to be adequate, where adequate means good enough to tell a legislature or a large funder how much weight to give to reducing extinction risk as against other uses of the same money? Write the desiderata, each with the case or consideration behind it, covering at least these: whether the badness has to be bad for someone, and what a theory is permitted to say if it need not be; the replacement case, in which every human being dies painlessly at once but is succeeded by a species whose lives go about as well as ours would have; the remnant case, in which a thousand people survive in isolation with no realistic prospect of growth; the consensual case, in which extinction is wanted by everyone then alive and nobody is killed against their will; the non-identity wrinkle, that the people who would exist if extinction were averted are almost none of the people who would otherwise have existed; and the question of whether the theory owes a cardinal weight at all or may refuse to give one. Then separate the non-negotiable requirements from those that are only the preference of a camp — my own suspicion is that the demand that the badness be someone's is a prejudice in a desideratum's clothing, while the demand for a number is not — and if some subset of the desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied, say which and why, since a list with a contradiction in it is itself the useful result. Close with a single paragraph on which one requirement excludes the most accounts currently on offer, and on whether Scheffler's afterlife conjecture, the claim that our confidence in a collective human future is a condition of our valuing anything at all, gets past that requirement. Please write in continuous prose: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
