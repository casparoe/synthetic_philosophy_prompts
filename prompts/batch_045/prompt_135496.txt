I cover science and ideas for a newspaper, and I'm stuck on a piece I'm filing Friday about a hospital system's sepsis alert — one of those machine-learning flags in the electronic health record that pings a nurse when it thinks a patient is going downhill.

Here's what I've got. Across the whole system, patients who got flagged died in the hospital at roughly twice the rate of patients who never got flagged. But inside every severity band at admission — mild, moderate, severe, critical — the flagged patients died less often than the unflagged patients in that same band. The hospital's data team says the raw comparison is garbage because the flag only fires on sick people, so of course flagged patients die more; they want me to lead with the within-band numbers and an adjusted odds ratio of 0.71. A patient-safety guy I interviewed says that's a shell game: the flag is being used on real patients in a real building, the case mix is what it is, and if the raw numbers look bad then that tells you something about how the thing is actually being used. My editor wants one sentence saying whether it works.

Then it gets worse. The same system ran a randomized rollout last year — alert switched on for randomly chosen shifts, so nobody can say sicker patients were being targeted — and I got those numbers too. There, the effect inside each individual hospital site was protective, but the pooled number across all sites landed slightly on the wrong side of 1, because the sites that enrolled the most patients were also the sites where the sickest patients turn up. Nobody was confounded there as far as I can tell. Someone's note from a call has "odds ratios don't collapse" scribbled next to this and I've no idea whether that's the same problem or a seperate one.

What I actually need, in plain english, reasoning rather than a verdict:

1. Is there a fact of the matter about whether the alert works, or am I asking one question when there are really two? If it's two, which one is the hospital answering and which one is the patient-safety guy answering?
2. When is slicing the data legitimate and when is it just a way of burying the answer? The hospital sliced by severity at admission. Their critics point out the system also logs whether a patient reached the ICU within four hours, and if you slice by that, the alert looks terrible — but that happens after the alert fires. How should I think about a slice like that?
3. If the alert helps in every severity band, shouldn't a doctor want it for her patient even before she knows which band he's in? That feels like it ought to settle the whole thing, and I can't tell whether it does.
4. What do I do with the randomized numbers, where the reversal shows up without anybody being sicker? Same phenomenon or a bookkeeping artifact, and does it change what I should print?
5. Concretely: what's my one sentence? And what should I tell readers to look for next time a press release pulls this move?

Don't give me citations or a reading list — I can't check them and there's no room in the column. Just tell me how to think about it, and flag the places where you think I'm the one who's confused instead of just picking a side.
