Two people at the Sunday dinner I host for the local rationalist crowd got into it about tipping, queueing and jaywalking, went about eight rounds, and neither would budge. I said I'd referee and then realised I actually can't, so I'm outsourcing.

The question: are everyday conventions like these moral obligations or just etiquette-with-sharp-edges, and does a visitor have to follow the local version.

1. Rosa: These are pure coordination, like driving on the left vs the right. There's no moral content in the rule itself; the only duty is to conform, because conforming is nearly free and deviating breaks a Schelling point everyone else is using. And it cuts both ways: in the city I grew up in, everyone crosses whenever there's a gap in traffic, and the tourist standing obediently at the crossing is the unpredictable one who makes drivers brake. So even the jaywalking case is just coordination. A visitor should follow local conventions for exactly the reason they'd drive on the right, and nothing more is going on.

2. Nate: That model is wrong for queues. The queue rule doesn't merely coordinate, it allocates: it decides who waits and how long. Cut to the front of forty people and you haven't "broken a convention," you've taken roughly forty person-minutes of other people's lives and handed them to yourself. Driving on the wrong side doesn't do that. So at least some of these are moral after all, and a visitor who skips them isn't being rude, they're imposing costs.

3. Rosa: Do the arithmetic tho. What the jumper saves is approximately what he imposes on everyone behind him, so it's near enough a pure transfer rather than a net loss. So the wrongness has to live somewhere else, and I only see two candidates: (i) the convention is what holds the queue together, and enough defection tips it into shoving, which is worse for literally everyone; (ii) the anger and humiliation people actually feel when someone cuts. And (i) is just my original point restated — comply because it's the local equilibrium you're a guest in.

4. Nate: (i) can't carry the weight. In a city of millions, any single person's contribution to tipping the equilibrium is negligible, so it can't be what makes *his* act wrong, and the people in the line do have a complaint against *him*. So (ii) is doing the work — and notice what that concedes: the wrong is causing real distress by violating something people have invested their sense of fairness in, which is moral, not coordination. There's also a third thing you skipped: everyone in that line is restraining themselves conditionally on everyone else doing the same, and the jumper takes the benefit of their restraint without paying it. That's free riding.

5. Rosa: Free riding requires that you've accepted the benefit, and the visitor arrived this morning; the standard objection to fair-play obligations is that benefits you didn't request don't generate duties. But my real problem is that your account has no filter. Local conventions include slipping the customs officer a twenty. "It's what's done here" is not a reason there, and "don't impose costs on others" doesn't sort it either, because sometimes the convention is itself the thing generating the cost — if everyone here expects 20% and I'd rather pay menu price, whose expectation gets honored and why? And it fails in the other direction too: an American tipping 20% in a country with no tipping culture isn't conforming, he's unilaterally renegotiating a term for every local who comes after him. So "follow the local convention" isn't even reliably right, let alone obligatory.

6. Nate: Right, and that's because the convention isn't the duty, it's the local carrier of the duty — like the local currency of an obligation to pay. The real duties are ordinary ones: don't take people's time, labour or money without their consent, don't free ride on restraint you're benefiting from, don't dump risk on people who can't price it. Locally variable conventions are just how those get discharged here. Which gives the answer to your question: a visitor must follow the convention when the convention *is* the mechanism (the queue), must not when it's extraction (the officer), and otherwise only has to treat it as evidence of what the local duty looks like. Evidence, not obligation. Sometimes that even means deviating: in a city where everyone jaywalks, the visitor should still wait, because he hasn't got the local calibration to judge the gaps.

7. Rosa: If the convention is only ever evidence, explain the phenomenology: people feel actual guilt, and bystanders feel actual indignation, about violating conventions they personally think are arbitrary, and visitors feel it too, in places whose rules they think are silly. That looks like the rule itself being moralised, not a duty being locally priced. Also, if your underlying duties run through "what people here expect," the bribe comes straight back in, because there the expectation is locally real as well.

8. Nate: The guilt is about what your deviation signals to people about whether you're the sort of person who takes from them, and signalling works off whatever local code exists. The bribe I'll bite: the officer's expectation doesn't count because it's an expectation he created and can withdraw at will, whereas the queuer's expectation is one he can't unilaterally reset. But I admit I don't have a clean test.

One thing neither of them touched, which I kept my mouth about: a lot of queues now have a paid skip option sold by the operator — the line's "price" is your wait, and you can buy your way out of it. That seems like it should change the analysis of what a queue-jumper takes and from whom, but I couldn't quite say how.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the eight moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification each. Then say who's more right overall and, most importantly, what both of them are missing — the thing that would have unstuck the argument. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, no preamble and no restating any of this back at me.
