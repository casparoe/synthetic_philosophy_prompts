Hi — could you take the role of a philosopher of economics who's spent years on decision theory and the foundations of choice? Please think it through properly, and do a bit of unstructured brainstorming before you commit to an answer; I'd rather see how you get there than just the conclusion.

Old-style revealed preference theory treats my preferences as whatever my choices are consistent with, and utility as just the number that represents them. That now seems to me either empty or false — but I still talk about what's good for people every day, so I want to know what I'm actually committed to. Here are my gut verdicts:

- Mango or apple, same price, both in front of me, I know both well, I take the mango. Obviously reveals a preference.
- Grabbing the chocolate bar at the till at 1am after a fourteen-hour day because it's at eye level. I don't want to say that reveals a preference for chocolate over the apple I'd have taken at lunchtime — and yet I keep doing it.
- I take the red packet believing it's salt; it's sugar. Reveals a preference for salt, not sugar. How the thing actually tastes doesn't seem to count.
- I book the opera seat expecting two hours of boredom, because I want to become the sort of person who likes opera. Reveals something — a preference about my future preferences? — but not a preference for opera.
- I pay extra for the gym contract with a punitive cancellation fee, precisely because I know I'll skip sessions otherwise. This looks like the reverse of revealing a preference: me refusing to let tomorrow's choice count.
- A pill that makes future consequences feel vivid; on it I pick the gym, off it I pick the pub. I want to say the pill choice reveals more of my real preference, not less.
- The fox who can't reach the grapes and announces he never wanted them. No preference revealed, or the wrong one.
- An app trained on years of my viewing data predicts what I'd watch and autoplays it; I let it run, night after night. I go back and forth — it's my behaviour, it's accurate, and it feels like nothing I chose.

The two I can't square are the chocolate bar and the pill: one influence on my attention makes the choice reveal less, the other makes it reveal more, and I can't say what separates them.

My tentative principle is: a choice reveals a preference only if I'd still make it with full information, with my attention where I'd want it, and with nothing riding on being seen. Test that against the cases and repair it where it breaks. Is there one account that gets all these verdicts, or nearly, and which of mine does it force me to give up? And what does it say about two cases I haven't put a verdict on — my sister, who has never really had the option of leaving our town and says she's content to stay; and me choosing a treatment for my dad, who can't decide for himself? Whose preference is revealed there, if anyone's? Finally, if half my choices turn out not to reveal anything, what is utility still doing in the story — a tidy summary of my behaviour, or the name of something my behaviour can get wrong?
