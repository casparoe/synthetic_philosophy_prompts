I work in behavioural ecology, in a lab that builds optimality and game-theoretic models of foraging and signalling, so I have some skin in this game; but I read outside my subfield, and I have just finished a recent popular book on evolution whose chapter on adaptationism I cannot tell how much to trust. The chapter is well written and I think mostly right, but it reaches conclusions that would embarrass people in my department if they were fair, and I cannot locate where it goes wrong.

It says, in substance, this: Gould and Lewontin's 1979 paper attacked what they called the adaptationist programme, the near-universal habit among evolutionary biologists of breaking an organism into separate traits and then telling a story in which each trait was individually built by natural selection for the job it now does. Their central metaphor comes from the Basilica of San Marco, where the tapering triangular panels between the arches under the dome are not there for the sake of the mosaics that fill them; the panels are forced by the geometry of arches and vaults, and the glorious decoration came later and had to fit the space it was given. In biology, a spandrel is the same thing: a feature that exists as a by-product of how an organism has to be built — the standard textbook examples are male nipples and the human chin — rather than as a product of selection. Crucially, the book says, this means that a trait whose present usefulness was not the reason it evolved counts as a spandrel rather than as an adaptation; bird feathers, which arose before flight and were later recruited for it, are on this view spandrels with respect to flight. The book calls this the double-lock rule: a trait is an adaptation only if selection both built it for its current role and currently maintains it for that role. The methodological moral the book draws is that the null hypothesis for any trait should be non-adaptation, with adaptationists bearing the burden of proof, because a theory that can cook up a plausible advantage for any feature whatsoever explains nothing — which is why the paper likened adaptationist stories to Kipling's Just So Stories and to Voltaire's Dr Pangloss, with his insistence that noses were made to carry spectacles. The upshot, the book concludes, is that the adaptationist programme was refuted: form is driven mainly by architectural and developmental constraint rather than by selection, most traits are not adaptations at all, and the discovery that much of the genome is non-functional is the molecular vindication of the whole critique.

My own hypothesis is that the chapter is running together distinctions that the careful literature keeps apart — being a structural by-product, being co-opted for a new use, having no current function, and having no selectionist explanation — and that most of its force comes from sliding between them. But I am not confident, and I would rather be corrected than confirmed.

So please do an audit rather than a survey: take the claims one by one and tell me which a working evolutionary biologist would accept, which are defensible simplifications, which are distortions, and which Gould and Lewontin would themselves have rejected, given that they insisted they were not denying the centrality of natural selection. Then: does the double-lock rule follow from anything in their argument, and how does it handle a trait that was co-opted and then extensively reshaped by selection for the new role? Do the chapter's conclusions about my lab's models and about how common spandrels are survive the corrections? Is the frequency question even the one the paper raises? Finally, a philosopher friend said the architectural example is itself shaky — that the panels are not really forced by the geometry, and that someone proposed pendentives as the better term — and I want to know whether the argument actually depends on the example being clean, or whether that is a distraction. No bibliographies, please; I want the reasoning.
