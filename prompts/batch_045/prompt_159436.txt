Hey — I need a teaching plan, not an explanation of Smith himself (I've read the chapters, I don't need them summarized). I'm running a fifty-minute discussion section next week for first-years in an intro ethics course. They've just come out of Mill and then Kant's formula of universal law, and the reading is a couple of chapters from Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments on sympathy and the impartial spectator. I ran this section last year and it went badly: everyone walked out convinced Smith was basically a vaguer, kinder rule-utilitarian with a bit of interior monologue bolted on, one student said "so the impartial spectator is just your conscience" and I flubbed the answer, and by the end the only thing that had landed was that Smith is the capitalism guy. So: what has to come first and what depends on what — I'm guessing that sympathy as imaginative projection of someone's *situation* (rather than catching their feeling) has to be in place before propriety, and propriety before the spectator, and that the bit about general rules has to come *after* the spectator rather than before, but I'm not confident about the order or about what to leave for last.

Two things I want built in, and I want you to tell me if either is a mistake. First, I want to teach the spectator as the destination of a loop rather than as a little person in your head: you notice you're partial, you imagine a well-informed bystander, you notice that you've rigged *that* bystander in your favour too, you imagine someone watching him, and the impartial spectator is what the sequence settles toward — the thing the recursion converges on. I like it because it explains why the spectator isn't just "what my actual peers think" without me having to dodge the relativism objection with a hand-wave, but I don't know where the framing breaks or whether it smuggles in something Smith doesn't want. Second, the hard bit as I see it is that Smith's question is about whether a passion is *proportionate* to what provoked it — the volume of the sentiment, not the rightness of the act — and I need one metaphor to carry that. I've been toying with photographic exposure versus a volume knob versus tuning an instrument; tell me which one to build the section around, what that metaphor has to have and what it must not have (I worry exposure implies a single correct setting and the knob implies more is just more), and give me the one concrete case to hang it on. I was thinking of something where the action isn't clearly wrong — someone getting visibly, disproportionately angry at a friend who turned up forty minutes late, at a table where everyone else is laughing it off — but say if a case about *resentment at a slight* is the wrong choice, and why.

Then the misconception map, with a place in the sequence for each: the spectator as conscience; the spectator as Kant re-labelled or as Mill's impartial benevolent spectator, given that they've just had both and that all three words start with "impartial"; sympathy read as empathy or as pity/benevolence; the collapse into "morality is just what the people around you would think"; and whatever they'll form from the metaphor I use. Also: should I raise at all, at this level, whether the spectator is merely a well-informed ordinary person or something more idealized — and if not, what should I openly refuse to explain and say out loud that I'm refusing? Finally, a check: one question or task I can put to them in the last five minutes that would show they've got the structure rather than just being agreeable or just uncomfortable — ideally something that separates the spectator from the average opinion of their friends and from a rule, and something that would let me tell the difference between a student who understands it and one who has only learned to say "it depends on propriety". I'm not asking for extra readings, just the arc.
