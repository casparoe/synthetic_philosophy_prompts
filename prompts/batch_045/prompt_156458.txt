I convene the research group behind an annotated edition of the Jesus tradition — roughly three hundred discrete units (sayings, parables, brief reports of actions), graded one at a time for an adult-education curriculum that will be printed with the grades in the margin and used by teachers with no Greek and no formal training. We meet twice a month, work through about a dozen units per session with a hard ten-minute cap on each, and any contested grade goes to a board that includes two non-specialists and a bishop, who have to be able to see from two sentences why the grade is what it is. So I need an instrument, not an essay: an ordered set of questions my group can actually run, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, a point at which we stop and grade low rather than press on, and a point at which we hand a unit to the board because the issue is doctrinal rather than historical. A reading list would be no use to me; we have one. I need the procedure it has not given us.

Three constraints make this harder than the version in the textbooks.

First, our group is not agreed on the literary relationships. Most of us work with Markan priority and a sayings source behind Matthew and Luke; one member holds the Two-Gospel ordering, with Matthew first, Luke second, and Mark as a later conflation of the two. I want a procedure whose grades do not quietly depend on which of those is right — or which says so explicitly, in the margin, when they do.

Second, the unit-by-unit grade has to survive a second, global pass. The set of units we end up grading high should be compossible as the profile of a single agent, and at present we do this informally by testing each new unit against the ones already graded high — which means the order in which we happen to take units can change the outcome. Tell us how to run that pass properly, and what to do when the profile refuses to close.

Third, two devices of our own that I want stress-tested rather than simply written up:

- The dependence ledger. We try to count causal chains, not witnesses: two Gospels that both copy Mark count once, and two performances of what may be a single oral form count once unless we can show they diverged upstream. I want the ledger's rules stated precisely, and I want to be told where it systematically over-counts and where it under-counts.
- The criterion of unused capital, which we invented last year. The idea is that a unit which would have been highly serviceable to the known concerns of the transmitting community, but which that community in fact never quotes, develops, or alludes to, is more likely inherited than composed — on the reasoning that inventors spend their capital. I want to know whether this does work that the dissimilarity and embarrassment criteria cannot do, whether it is secretly one of them in costume, and what its failure modes are.

One member also maintains that the whole unit-by-unit framing is mistaken: that what we have is a community's remembered Jesus rather than a chain of separable items, and that atomizing it into graded units is already the wrong question. Tell me whether the procedure can make room for that objection anywhere, or whether it lies outside what an instrument of this kind can do.

What I would like from you, in order:

1. The procedure itself, as numbered questions with a sufficiency condition for each — what kind of consideration would discharge it, and what kind would not.
2. The kill screen: the step at which we stop and grade the unit low, and the separate condition under which we should record no verdict at all rather than a low one.
3. The hand-off: which questions are not factual and must go to a person or to the board, stated so that a non-specialist can see why.
4. The ledger rules, with their bias in each direction.
5. The global profile pass: how to run it, how to resolve a profile that will not close, and how to tell the difference between a profile that is genuinely incoherent and a first-century Galilean who is simply not consistent in the way our categories want him to be.
6. A keep / revise / drop verdict on each of our two devices.
7. Three worked applications: the baptism of Jesus by John (Mark 1); the saying about destroying the temple and raising it in three days (Mark 14; John 2); and the empty-tomb narrative (Mark 16). I should say that the baptism anchors the opening session of the curriculum and our group has always treated it as the strongest embarrassment case we have, so tell me plainly if the procedure demotes it.
8. A frank statement of which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and of what the procedure cannot deliver even when run perfectly.
9. Finally, what the printed grade may be read as meaning. I am worried about two equal and opposite misreadings by teachers: that "uncertain" will be heard as "did not happen," and that "well attested" will be heard as "these are his words." Say what a marginal grade is and is not licensed to assert, in a sentence that could be printed in the front matter.
