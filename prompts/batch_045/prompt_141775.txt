I am a graduate student in a multi-agent systems group, and one of the postdocs uses an argument to shut down discussions of cooperation that I cannot find the hole in, mainly because every step looks like something I already believe. I would like your diagnosis.

The setup, which he calls the transparent game: two players meet once; each submits a program; each program receives as input the source code of the other and, by the usual quining trick, its own source; each must halt and output C or D. Payoffs are the ordinary one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma: T = 5, R = 3, P = 1, S = 0. No shared randomness is available beyond what can be derived from the two sources themselves. A pair of programs is a program equilibrium if neither player can improve her payoff by unilaterally submitting a different program.

His argument, as I have reconstructed it:

(1) The only argument for defection in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma is the independence rationale: your move is already fixed when I make mine, so nothing I do can affect what you do, and C is therefore strictly dominated.

(2) Transparency defeats the independence rationale. Let MirrorBot be the program that outputs C if and only if the opponent's source is byte-identical to its own, and D otherwise. Two copies of MirrorBot both cooperate, and this is a strict program equilibrium: if I submit anything else, my opponent reads a different source, defects, and the best I can then do is 1, which is worse than 3. Note what makes it work: had I submitted a defecting program instead, she would have defected. My choice makes a difference to what her program outputs.

(3) So cooperation in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma is rational for perfectly self-interested agents with mutual source access, with no iteration, no reputation, no third-party enforcer, and no change of preferences. Call this the transparency result.

(4) The construction is completely general, and this is the part I find hardest to attack. Nothing in it uses the payoffs of the Prisoner's Dilemma. What does the work is only that each player can condition her action on a decidable predicate of the other player's program, can refer to her own source, and can simulate the other's program on her own source (with a step bound and a fallback) to see what it will do against her. So for any two-player game and any feasible outcome o in which each player does at least as well as her minmax value, there is a program equilibrium yielding o: each player i submits the program that plays her part of o when the opponent's source is the prescribed Q_j — correlating on a hash of the two sources, if o is a mixed or correlated outcome — and otherwise simulates the opponent on her own source and plays the action that minimises the opponent's payoff given what the simulation returns. Any unilateral change of source is detected by string comparison and punished down to minmax, which is why no deviation is profitable.

(5) It follows that the transparent game selects nothing. In the transparent Prisoner's Dilemma with the payoffs above, mutual cooperation (3,3) is a program equilibrium; mutual defection (1,1) is one; and so is what he calls the Ratchet: a correlated outcome in which a hash of the two sources decides who is exploited this time, yielding expected payoffs of (4, 1.5), with the loser held to 1.5 because any change to her source triggers the punishment branch and caps her at 1. One player receives more than two and a half times the other, on average, and cannot escape. A mechanism that produces (3,3), (1,1) and (4, 1.5) with equal ease has not explained cooperation. It has only shown that a certain kind of conditional behaviour is possible.

(6) Nor, he says, can the result be repaired by a selection story. There are exactly three kinds of reason one could add for agents landing on the cooperative program rather than the Ratchet, and all three are fatal: (a) an other-regarding preference or norm, which concedes that self-interest plus transparency was never sufficient and quietly withdraws the clause that made the result interesting; (b) a general equilibrium-selection principle such as symmetry, fairness or salience, which either selects (1,1) as readily as (3,3) or does all the explanatory work itself and leaves transparency idle; (c) an appeal to the counterfactual about what she would have done had I submitted otherwise, which is precisely the counterfactual (2) relies on and which the Ratchet profile possesses just as fully, since deviation there also triggers her defection.

(7) Therefore the transparency result is either false or empty. And the argument generalises: the same reasoning applies to iteration, reputation and enforcement, each of which also supports a broad range of individually rational outcomes, so none of them explains cooperation either. Nothing in the neighbourhood distinguishes a mechanism that explains cooperation from a mechanism that is simply weak.

I am sure (7) is too strong. Whatever is wrong here, it is not that the repeated-game explanation of cooperation is empty; that is the one success story we have, and it does not collapse merely because the equilibrium set is large. But I cannot locate the damage, and I am not even sure whether the chain is valid with a premise I should give up, or invalid at some transition I am reading too charitably.

Two things I keep returning to, and cannot evaluate. First: the equilibria catalogued in (5) are equilibria of different program pairs, not options spread out before one pair of agents facing a single decision problem, and the Ratchet in particular requires the two players to submit different programs, or to agree on a hash, and I do not know how two symmetrically placed agents settle on which of them gets the 4. I cannot tell whether that is a real objection or just another coordination problem with the same solution as all the others. Second: I suspect (1) and (2) may not be about the same thing, since the independence in (1) concerns my action while the difference-making in (2) concerns my choice of program, but I also cannot tell whether that is a genuine equivocation or two descriptions of one fact.

If I have to choose, I would rather keep (2), (3) and (4) than (5) and (7): the first three look to me like mathematics, and the last two like philosophical gloss on it. Please tell me which step or transition you reject, why it looked sound, and what rejecting it costs — in particular what each available rejection commits you to about the iterated case, about pre-play commitment, and about the Hobbesian story the result is usually taken to undermine. Price the alternatives you do not take as well.

Finally, the thing I most want: for each verdict you give, say what would change your mind. If you reject (5), say whether a version of the argument restricted to symmetric profiles — both players submitting the same program, or submitting to the same public selection procedure — would alter your answer, and tell me where you think your own diagnosis is most likely to be wrong.
