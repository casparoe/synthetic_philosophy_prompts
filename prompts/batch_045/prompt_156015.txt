im doing a history project (like a national history day kind of thing) on a student walkout at my school in 1971, and the archive is super lopsided. We've got the principal's daily log, the local newspaper, and the yearbook — all basically the admin's point of view — and almost nothing from the students: no flyers, no meeting notes, nothing from the Black Student Union. The public library actually has a folder of student stuff, but only because one teacher dumped her personal box of papers there in like 2004. So i started reading about this and found a long article in some magazine my mom gets, and it's defintely made me see the whole project differently. The part i wrote down goes like this:

"Archives aren't neutral storage, they're the residue of power. Every record has to get through what we might call the survivorship funnel: somebody had to be in a position to make the record at all, somebody had to decide it was worth keeping, an archivist had to box it and list it in a finding aid, and now somebody has to choose it for digitization — and at every one of those stages the people doing the filtering were the people with power. So the gaps aren't accidents; they're the shape of the power. And that means a gap is itself real evidence. If a group doesn't appear in the record, that absence is a positive fact you can cite, not just a blank space where nothing is known. Which is why histories built on institutional archives are basically the winners writing their own story, and a historian who says 'we simply don't know what happened' is really saying 'I'm comfortable leaving the silences the way they are.' There's never any record of what got weeded out either, so the filtering is invisible. The good news is that digitization is fixing all of this — once everything is scanned and searchable, the material that got shoved into a back room becomes visible to everyone."

I really want to use this, because it turns my project from "i found some old papers" into something more interesting, and i was planning to write that the archive's silence is itself evidence that the school suppressed the students' side. But my history teacher mentioned something about reading against the grain and the article never talks about that, which makes me think it might be leaving something out, and my mom says the magazine is the kind that turns everything into a conspiracy.

So: which parts of that paragraph would someone who actually studies archives and historical sources accept, which parts would make them wince as a simplification, and what's just wrong? I care most about the sentence saying a gap is a citable fact — can i actually claim that in my project, and if not, what's the honest version of what i can claim? And does the last bit about digitization follow from the stuff earlier in the paragraph, or is it a separate mistake?
