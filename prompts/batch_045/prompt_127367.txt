I have no formal training in philosophy or physics — I work in logistics planning, and I came to all of this through pop-science books and a long argument on a physics forum, where a graduate student has backed me into a corner I can't climb out of. The argument is the one from the relativity of simultaneity that I have seen referred to as the Rietdijk–Putnam argument, and as I have reconstructed it, it runs like this.

(1) In special relativity, whether a distant event is happening now depends on which inertial frame you are in: there is no frame-independent fact about which distant events are simultaneous with me here-now.
(2) There is no such thing as existing relative to a frame. If an event is real for one observer, it is real, full stop.
(3) Any two events anywhere can be linked by a chain of steps, each step connecting two events that are simultaneous in some frame. My friend rubbed this in: my birth and my death are timelike-separated, so no single frame makes them simultaneous — but some event far off in space is spacelike-separated from both, so it is simultaneous with my birth in one frame and with my death in another, and you can keep hopping like that across all of spacetime.
(4) So every event in the history of the universe is real: a static four-dimensional block, in which nothing ever comes into being and now is just an indexical, like here.
(5) If my future events are as real as my past ones, the future is not open in any sense in which the past is not: what I will do tonight is no more up to me than what I did last night.
(6) So my deliberating about what to do tonight cannot make a difference to what I do. The outcome is already sitting in the block whether or not I deliberate, so the deliberation is decoration.
(7) And worse: whether I end up convinced by (1)–(4) is itself an already-existing feature of the block. If the block is real, then no reasoning of mine brings about my conviction; the conviction is simply there, and would have been there however the reasoning had gone.
(8) But a conclusion that reasoning cannot bring me to is not one I can rationally accept on the strength of reasoning. So if I accept (4), I have to admit that I do not accept it for the reasons I think I do — which seems to make my acceptance of it, and of the physics behind it, arbitrary.

I think something is cooking in the second half, because steps (6) through (8) don't seem to use relativity at all. Run them in a Newtonian universe with absolute time, where the future is not real yet and no block exists, but where some Laplacian calculator has already written down everything I will ever believe. My conviction is already there in the ledger, so my reasoning did not bring it about, so by (8) I cannot rationally accept it. That conclusion is one nobody would want, and nothing in the route to it depended on the block — so the rot has to be in (6), (7), or (8), and I can't put my finger on which move is doing the damage.

What makes me doubt the first half is that the argument seems to ride on an empirical number. In a universe with the same structure but a much smaller invariant speed — say the speed of a bicycle — an observer a hundred kilometres away can find a frame in which events of mine more than five hours from now are simultaneous with my present, so the argument's grip on my future becomes enormous and vivid. Take an enormous invariant speed instead and the grip at any fixed distance shrinks toward nothing; take an infinite one and simultaneity is absolute again, and the argument never gets off the ground. Yet the conclusion does not seem to fade away as the speed grows: for any finite value, however huge, the hopping in (3) still takes you from my birth to my death. So the reach of the argument at any one distance depends on the value of a constant while its conclusion depends only on that constant being finite, and I cannot tell whether that shows the argument is unsound or shows that it was never really about simultaneity in the first place.

I have tried three exits and each one costs something I would rather not pay. If I deny (2) and let reality be relative to a frame, I seem to be saying that the same event both is and is not real, and I can't tell whether that is a thesis about the world or only about what different people can say. If I keep the block and deny (6), I don't know what making a difference is supposed to mean once the outcome is already there. If I swallow (8), I have pulled the ladder up after myself. Someone on the forum also said the argument only rules out a single global present, not becoming understood locally along a worldline, but I couldn't tell whether that was an answer or a retreat. If I have to choose, I would rather keep (1), (3), and the claim that my reasoning and deliberation actually do something than keep (2) — but I can't see what giving up (2) would even look like, or how it would help me with (6) and (7).

So: which step do you reject, why does that step look unobjectionable on the surface, and what exactly does rejecting it cost me? And if the block does survive your diagnosis, I would like to know what the future is open could still mean inside it, and whether adding genuinely chancy events — the kind quantum mechanics is supposed to give us — puts anything back.
