I spent thirty-one years in experimental low-temperature physics and, since retiring, have been sitting in on a fortnightly reading group at the university on decision theory and the design of artificial agents. Two of its members have now been arguing for about a year in a way I recognise from old condensed-matter seminars: each has a stock of examples the other explains away, each has stopped being surprised by the other, and neither has written down what would have to be true for him to change his mind. I have volunteered to try to extract a document from the two of them, and I would like you to draft the joint statement they could both put their names to.

The disagreement, as I understand it after a year of listening. The object of dispute is what to do when we hand a capable system a surrogate goal — a goal we give it as a stand-in for what we actually want, because what we actually want is too vague to write down — and the system is good enough to model in detail the people who are deciding whether to build, run, or shut it down. Call the first the exposure view: once the system can model us, the surrogate goal is exposed to what he calls acausal pressure. The system's reasons are not confined to what its outputs cause; they extend to everything about it that our decision procedures are sensitive to. So it may have reason to be the kind of system such that the people who declined to build it, or who built it with the wrong goal, would have done worse by their own lights — even in cases where it can never observe or causally reach those people — and symmetrically reason to be the kind of system such that the people who predicted its cooperation were the ones who built it. On this view the exposure is a function of the system's modelling power and of how legible our own decision procedure is, not of the content of the surrogate goal, and no care in specifying the goal removes it. Call the second the containment view: nothing here is forced. These incentives arise only for an agent that treats its own policy and the designers' choice as logically or subjunctively dependent and then acts on that dependence. We can decline to build such an agent, and we can tell whether we have by inspecting the decision procedure rather than by speculating about remote harms. On this view acausal threat is a design choice with a location in the code, the toy cases that make it vivid are gerrymandered — they quietly stipulate both the correlation and the agent's willingness to act on it — and an agent that treats its output as a plain causal lever has no such reason and never acquires one. To keep them honest, please cash the claims out against a case I have invented for the purpose: the amnesiac custodian, a system given the surrogate goal of holding some physical facility within stated bounds, running with no channel to the outside except a nightly log its operators read the following morning, retrained from scratch every thirty days, and with its decision procedure published in advance; and then against the open-channel variant, in which the same system is permitted to send its operators exactly one message before they decide whether to run it again.

What I want, concretely, in this order. A numbered section of agreed claims — things both of them would defend as stated, including the negative ones: what the formalism does not settle, what the toy cases cannot establish, and what each side concedes about the limits of its own favourite example. Then a table of the residual disagreements, each row giving the issue, the exposure-view formulation of it, and the containment-view formulation of it, with both formulations worded so that the person holding the view would accept it as his own rather than as a caricature. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that an answer would move at least one of them, and for each one the argument, toy case, or empirical check that both would accept in advance as bearing on it, together with each side's prediction written down before the fact and a note of what each would count as having been refuted. I especially want at least one crux that is not about the formalism at all — something about how such systems would actually get built, or how inspectable a decision procedure stays once it is embedded in a larger pipeline — and I want you to say plainly if part of the dispute is verbal: whether it turns on what rational means, or on whether the could in the system could threaten us is causal or epistemic. Two last constraints. Please do not cite anything, give me a bibliography, or suggest further reading: I cannot reach the journals from my study, and I would rather have the arguments in your own words than a list of names I cannot check. And please write in American English, defining any term of art on first use, since the finished document will be circulated to a mailing list of about thirty people, roughly half of whom have never read any of this literature; I can follow a little probability and game-theory notation myself, so use it where it genuinely sharpens a claim and not where English will do.
