Hey — I organise the end-of-term revue for our logic reading group (I'm a philosophy PhD student, mostly phil-math with a bit of formal epistemology, and I TA the intro logic course a lot), and this year I want the centrepiece to be about Gödel-misuse. Can you write it?

What I want: a client-facing brochure, about 650 words, from a management consultancy — something like Incompleteness Solutions or Gödel & Partners — selling "Gödel audits" to institutions: a university administration, a legal code, an AI safety team, whatever fits. Headings, a bulleted "what you get", a pricing table that is quietly a subscription, and a short FAQ at the end. Deadpan throughout. No winking, no "as any logician knows", no character who says the quiet part out loud — the reductio should be the business model itself.

The jokes have to come from the actual theorem, and I want the details right: about half the room will have genuinely worked through a Gödel proof and will wince at anything sloppy. Things I want in there:

- The Gödel sentence is bespoke: it's relative to a particular recursively axiomatised theory. There is no generic "gap" to go find, you have to redo the construction for each client — and once they add it as an axiom they've got a new theory with its own sentence. The firm should be selling an infinite regress as a retainer and pitching it as a feature ("guaranteed recurring findings", "our engagement never terminates, which we consider a strength").
- The theorem only bites for theories that can encode enough arithmetic — Robinson arithmetic or thereabouts. So for a client whose rulebook is too weak (a lunch rota, a fire-drill sign-up sheet, anything interpretable in Presburger arithmetic) there's literally nothing to find: give them an upsell for installing an arithmetic module into their HR policy so that the theorem applies. There should also be something about how the firm first has to make the client's rules computably enumerable, so "unwritten rules" and "we sort of decide case by case" void the warranty.
- When you decode the sentence, it's a claim about natural numbers with no content about the client's organisation. I want that in: the deliverable is a true, unprovable and completely uninteresting sentence about their business.
- The "we can see that it's true" step. Please be careful here. The firm needs to establish that the client's theory is consistent, which the client can't do from the inside (second incompleteness) and which nobody can do for something as messy as a university's statutes; and I think there's a further wrinkle that seeing it as true needs the intended/standard interpretation, which the theory doesn't pin down from within (non-standard models of PA and all that). My own understanding is that plain consistency is enough for the truth of the Gödel sentence itself, because it's Π1 — so it's the disquoting step and the consistency claim that are load-bearing and shaky, not the truth of G. If I've got that wrong, fix it in the piece and flag it below.
- Don't do the Lucas/Penrose consciousness-and-minds thing. Our group is sick of it. If antimechanism appears at all, make it a bolt-on product nobody buys.
- No fabricated quotes, please: nothing in quotation marks attributed to Gödel or anyone real, and no glowing testimonial from a real person. Invented clients and an invented in-house expert are fine.

After the brochure, give me a short note, 150 words-ish: where the piece takes liberties or leans on a contested reading, how confident you are in the technical claims overall, and the single point you're least sure about. Say something about the Π1 / consistency-versus-soundness business either way, since that's where I'm least confident myself.
