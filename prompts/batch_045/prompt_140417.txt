hey — I need a satirical piece for a zine im putting together, and I want it to land with people who've actually followed the "do language models understand anything" fight, not just be vibes about chatbots.

form: a page from an official government website — a public-information leaflet with a beurocratic title along the lines of "Understanding: Guidance for Households", plus a little "how to talk to your household assistant" box and a three-question FAQ at the bottom. deadpan civil-service voice, numbered sections with headings, 550-650 words.

the setup: in this country a Ministry of Language has made the anti-understanding argument official doctrine. you know the one — a system trained only on linguistic form, with no access to a shared world and no access to what anyone was trying to do in saying something, can shuffle words fluently without any of them meaning anything. don't name real researchers, papers, or famous thought experiments anywhere in the piece; invent your own authorities instead (an Office of Provenance, a Standing Revision Clause, whatever you like). but the jokes have to come from getting the actual argument right:

- the core inference — form-only training, no communicative intent, therefore no meaning — should be stated by the leaflet in admiring terms and applied with total seriousness to everyday cases
- the Ministry's comprehension tests keep getting withdrawn: every time a system passes one, it's recalled on the grounds that the test only ever measured form. no goalpost ever stays put
- about halfway through, run the Ministry's own criteria on a human. invent the case — someone who picked up a whole language or dialect from letters, texts, or recordings with nobody there to point at things for them, or who uses fluent phrases they can't explain. apply the criteria straight, let the reader feel the squeeze, and never say out loud that the argument might be proving too much
- take a swipe at the other side too: a rival office hands out "fluency certificates" to anything that clears a benchmark, and the two offices have to share a letterhead

don't wink, don't tack on a closing paragraph explaining the joke, and don't let any official break character — the reader should be able to tell what's being made fun of. the test is that someone who's basically on the parrot side of this debate should still think the piece is fair to them.
