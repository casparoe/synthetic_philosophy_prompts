I've got a 45-minute talk in a workshop series on collective decision-making under catastrophic risk in three weeks, and I want Q&A prep more than I want another round of feedback on the slides.

The talk defends three connected claims, and I'll sketch the argument so you know what to aim at.

(1) The standard presentation says the unilateralist's curse is a reason for the less confident to defer to the more confident or more expert. I think that's mis-specified. The selection effect that generates the curse — conditional on *you* being the one who finds the action worth taking, your estimate is drawn from the upper tail of a spread of estimates — is driven entirely by the *idiosyncratic* component of your error, the part you don't share with the other agents. If everyone's error were perfectly shared, the group would effectively be a single agent and there'd be no curse at all. So the variable that should govern deference isn't confidence or track record, it's how much of your error is already priced into everyone else's.

(2) Which means the usual remedy is partly self-defeating. A principle of conformity shrinks the idiosyncratic component by raising correlation. But the correlated component is exactly the one the group cannot see from inside, since no procedure that only pools members' opinions can detect it. So conformity converts a bias that's at least in principle measurable into one that's invisible by construction, and it makes the group more homogeneous and more confidently wrong. The prescription I want instead: radically open *voice* (idiosyncratic error is information when it's pooled) paired with near-total closure on unilateral *triggering* (the same idiosyncratic error is pure noise when it acts alone). Same person can be cheap as an actor and invaluable as an informant.

(3) The part I'm shakiest on: the argument screens out the people it most needs to reach. Publicizing it discourages exactly the modest, reason-responsive agents, and leaves the population of unilateral actors increasingly selected for the trait it was supposed to neutralize.

The room will be about 25 people: roughly half formal epistemologists and social epistemologists — Bayesian, into judgment aggregation, Condorcet-style jury results, forecast aggregation, the diversity-beats-individual-accuracy stuff. They're going to go straight for my independence assumptions and for whether "idiosyncratic error share" is even identifiable from the inside. Then four or five existential-risk / global-priorities researchers who already *use* the curse in governance arguments and will defend it, plus two ML-ish people who think in ensembles and will want to know why this isn't just variance reduction with extra steps. And one political theorist who works on expertise, legitimacy and epistocracy, who I expect will go after the voice/trigger split as rule by an unaccountable committee that listens to everyone and does what it likes. There are no decision theorists in the room, so flag it if one of your objections secretly needs that machinery.

Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that group would phrase it (I want to hear the formal epistemologist's version and the political theorist's version separately when they'd differ), with a roughly two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes to buy goodwill, and what the weakest point in my own position is that I should volunteer rather than hope they miss. No citations or reading lists, I've read the standard material.

Also — and this matters to me — for each reply give me one line on what would change my mind about it, i.e. what the reply is assuming that could turn out false. At the end, tell me which of my three claims you'd put the least money on and what observation would flip you on it.
