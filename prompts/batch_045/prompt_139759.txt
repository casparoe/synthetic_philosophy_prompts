The setup as I understand it: Berlin's two-concepts distinction — negative liberty is roughly "nobody interferes with you", positive liberty is roughly "you're self-directed, you're actually acting on your own real goals" — and then a third thing I keep running into from Pettit and the neo-republicans, where liberty is non-domination, and the point is supposed to be that a benevolent master who never actually interferes still leaves you unfree because they could at any time.

My model, which my coworker says is broken: all three are rival metrics for one underlying scalar, "how much freedom does this person have", and they only disagree about what counts as a constraint and how to weight it. So I've been trying to implement them as numbers:

- negative liberty = count of actions open to you that nobody blocks. So someone who's broke, or too junior to understand our codebase, or uses a wheelchair in a building with no ramp, has less negative liberty, because there are fewer things they can actually do.
- positive liberty = fraction of your open actions that you actually endorse and that move you toward your own goals.
- republican liberty = expected interference: sum over everyone who could mess with you of P(they interfere) times how bad that would be. On this reading a master who interferes with probability 0.02 is barely a constraint at all.

Two things seem to follow and I want to know whether they're right. First, the republican metric looks like the negative metric with a probability weight bolted on, so once the probabilities get small it should collapse into negative liberty and I don't see what the third concept is adding. Second, if the mere possibility of interference counts against your freedom, and the state is the biggest possible interferer, republicans are committed to wanting the smallest state possible — which seems incompatible with the fact that the republican people I've read also want welfare provision and labor regulation.
