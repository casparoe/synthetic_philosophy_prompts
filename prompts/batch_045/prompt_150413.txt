I sit on the bankroll committee of my university's forecasting club — forty members, a \$200,000 endowment from an alumni donor, and a charter that lets us stake the money more or less however we like provided we can state a rule we would defend to the donor. The endowment is meant to roll on after we graduate, and members' shares have so far been pure accounting entries: we bet the pot as one pot. I am the member without a finance background, which is why I am writing this out. I have been reading a little about geometric rationality — the idea that when something compounds you should maximize the geometric mean rather than the arithmetic one — and I cannot tell which of our own rulings even follow that idea.

Our precedents: (1) A market paying even odds on a binary we estimate at 60/40, offered every week, stake freely scalable, winnings reinvested: we stake 20% of the bankroll each week. We explicitly rejected staking everything, after someone argued that since every week is positive-expectation, more is always better, and we also rejected 10% as timid; someone identified 20% as the Kelly fraction and the meeting moved on. (2) A fixed-dollar offer — up to twenty \$500 tickets on independent events, each returning \$1,000 with probability 0.6 and nothing otherwise, small next to our bankroll: we bought every ticket and said we would take every ticket they would sell us. Nobody mentioned Kelly; we simply treated it as positive expectation at negligible risk. (3) A structured note where a stake s returns +1.0s with probability 0.6 and −0.1s with probability 0.4, monthly, with borrowing available: the growth-optimal stake comes out at more than five times the bankroll (one member got 5.6×), and we authorized only 1.5×, on the stated grounds that we do not trust either the 60% estimate or the contractual loss cap well enough to borrow four times our endowment, and that the charter does not require us to be maximally aggressive.

The new proposal, for a decision in September: each of the forty members directs an equal one-fortieth share into the same kind of 60/40 even-odds bet as in (1), but on independent events, and at the end of every week all proceeds are pooled and redistributed equally before the next week's bets. One member has circulated numbers claiming that with weekly redistribution the typical member's wealth multiplies by about 1.2 per week — a growth rate he puts near 18% a week, against roughly 2% a week under our usual 20% rule — and that we should therefore stake everything and borrow up to the 1.5× cap we allowed ourselves in (3), since with forty independent bets shared, the chance of a catastrophic week is negligible. Others say redistribution is only an internal transfer and cannot change how fast the fund compounds, so the answer should stay at 20%.

What should we stake, if we want to be consistent with (1)–(3), which of those is the closest analog, and what rule are we implicitly running? As far as I can see, at least three rules fit our track record and disagree here: that the fund is the persisting agent and its wealth is what compounds, so internal transfers among members are irrelevant; that what matters is the growth rate experienced by the things that actually persist, i.e. the individual members, whose wealth behaves quite differently once risk is shared, so a multiplicative problem becomes nearly an additive one; and that we haircut the theoretical optimum whenever the inputs are estimates we do not trust, as we did in (3), which here would mean discounting the claimed diversification because our forty bets all live in the same market. Tell me where exactly these diverge on this case, which one our precedents actually support, and what further case would separate them — in particular, would the answer change if the club had four members instead of forty, or four thousand, or if the redistribution happened only once at the end of the year rather than weekly? And is the 18%-a-week figure even right, or is there an error in it I should point out at the meeting?
