I've got a 45-minute slot at the innovation/IO workshop in the econ department in two weeks — its a joint thing with the CS department, so there'll be a couple of ML people in the room too — and I'm defending a claim about the competitiveness constraint and the alignment tax that I genuinely can't tell anymore whether it's deep or whether I've just been staring at it to long.

Quick setup so you know what I mean by the terms. By "alignment tax" I mean whatever it costs — dollars, compute, raw capability, calendar time — to make a system safe enough to deploy rather than just deploying it. By "the competitiveness constraint" I mean the familiar worry that the actors actually building frontier systems can't afford to pay that tax: whoever ships first captures enough of the returns that unilateral restraint is irrational, so the tax doesn't get paid even by people who'd rather everyone paid it.

My thesis is that the familiar framing mislocates the problem, in three steps. (1) What binds isn't the size of the tax; it's that the returns to going first are *believed* to be lumpy — there's supposed to be some capability threshold past which the payoff jumps — so even a small tax is worth skipping, and conversely an enormous tax would be worth paying if the prize were smooth. (2) More importantly, nobody knows the size of the tax, the distance to the threshold, or where their rivals are; and under that kind of uncertainty the expected cost of waiting is close to unbounded (you might never get another turn) while the cost of rushing is bounded and looks small. So racing is rational even for people who don't believe the risk story — which means the "everyone would prefer a coordinated slowdown" premise of the standard collective-action picture may be straightforwardly false, not merely unenforceable. (3) Hence the remedies that fall out of the standard picture — binding commitments, penalties, externally enforced deployment thresholds — aim at the wrong variable. What moves this race is what people know, and my punchline is that shared, credible evaluation infrastructure does disproportionate work here, not because it regulates anybody but because it works on the belief channel and breaks something that is partly an information cascade.

The room: maybe eight innovation and IO economists, mostly empirical, one theorist who will want an actual model, the two ML people, and one person who works on financial regulation and believes that every "race to the bottom" story is secretly an argument for whichever regulator the speaker happens to like. They think in patent races, standards races, tournaments. They want an equilibrium story. They get impatient with anything that turns on an unobservable counterfactual, and at least two of them think "existential risk" is a rhetorical move rather than a magnitude. I'm a philosopher by training, which they'll already have guessed from the abstract, so I need to not sound like I'm moralizing at them.
