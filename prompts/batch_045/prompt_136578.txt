I direct the research team at a small institute that publishes a quarterly monitor of democratic integrity across roughly thirty countries. Each quarter, for each country, one analyst — a country specialist with no formal training in political theory, working only from public sources, with about half a day per country — has to return two items: a binary flag for whether a backsliding event occurred in the quarter, and a severity rating on a four-point scale. That is on the order of a hundred and twenty decisions a year. Journalists cite the flags; two funders use them in disbursement decisions. Our credibility rests almost entirely on not reading as a partisan scoreboard: a false positive hands a government that voters elected a story about foreign meddling, and a false negative is a warning we should have issued and did not.

What I need from you is the coding protocol itself, written as an ordered procedure an analyst can run in half a day: the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient affirmative answer to each, the gates at which the procedure halts with no event, the gates at which it escalates to me rather than being coded by the analyst alone, and the points at which it hands the decision back to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. For each step, give the case or consideration that makes it a step.

Four constraints on the design. First, the analysts disagree with each other politically and must still converge: build in whatever symmetry checks you think actually discipline this, and say how we would test for convergence on the same file. Second, the protocol must be defensible in public, so it has to code the institutional change before it names it, and it has to have a rule about whose complaints count as evidence — the domestic opposition's, the government's own self-description, outside observers', voters' own reported assessments. Third, analysts cannot adjudicate contested questions of motive in half a day; wherever intent seems indispensable, say how it is to be inferred from the public record, and say what the default coding is when it cannot be. Fourth, the protocol must handle the fact that some countries in our sample are already below the democratic threshold, and that some are federal.

I have four candidate tests and I want you to judge them rather than simply implement them. The mirror test: would the same move by the other major party, described in the neutral vocabulary of the protocol, trigger the same flag? The turnover test: does the change raise the cost to the incumbent of losing the next election, or lower the probability that a party that loses actually leaves office? The irreversibility test: would the change survive a change of government, or is it a ratchet? The latent-capacity test: does the change grant powers that are not exercised this quarter, and if unused powers count, how is the analyst to code them without speculating about the future? Tell me which of these you would keep, which you would drop as a partisan prejudice wearing procedural clothing, whether the ordering I have given is the right one, and what you would add. Also tell me which distinctions the procedure cannot afford to collapse: policy change versus change in the rules of the contest, legality versus democratic valence, capacity versus exercise, national versus subnational, erosion from a democratic baseline versus tightening within an already-authoritarian one, and whatever else belongs on that list.

Then apply it. Treat the following as stipulated descriptions, not as invitations to reach for facts about real countries; if an answer would depend on an empirical claim our analysts could not verify in half a day, say so rather than stipulating it.

Case A. A governing coalition wins 52 percent of the vote and, thanks to the electoral formula, 66 percent of the seats — the largest share any party has won in twenty years. In its first year it increases the share of seats allocated in single-member districts, replaces the fixed terms of the media regulator's board with indefinite tenure and fills the vacancies with its own nominees, lowers the mandatory retirement age for judges by six years, allowing it to appoint a third of the supreme court, and converts fixed-term contracts for ministry directors into at-will appointments. Every change passes by ordinary legislative majority, no constitutional amendment is required, and for each one the government cites a different established democracy that has a similar arrangement.

Case B. In a federal country, six of twenty states redraw districts mid-decade, tighten identification requirements for voters, and move control of the state election board from an independent commission to the governor's office. All six have slim incumbent majorities. The national government does none of these things and has no legal power to stop them; the national presidency is held by the opposition, which has publicly condemned the changes.

Case C. A country our index has rated below the democratic threshold for six years closes the last independent television channel this quarter, and in the same quarter lifts a state of emergency that had been renewed continuously for four years. Separately, in a second country, a new government that campaigned on reversing eight years of institutional change restores the previous judicial appointment rules and reopens the public broadcaster, doing so partly by reconstructing appointment powers of the kind it had criticized.

Case D. A government elected after a long period of institutional deadlock abolishes the upper chamber, replaces a judicial appointments body whose members were named by the legal profession with one named by a legislative supermajority, and removes the president's power to dissolve parliament. Each of these has at some point been recommended by a domestic reform commission; each also removes a check on the government.

Run A, B, and D through the protocol gate by gate, code C's two halves separately, and tell me what the protocol returns when a period of restoration follows a period of erosion. I want at least one application where the honest output is something I would rather not publish.

Finally, tell me where my own priors fail the protocol. I hold that a change that is legal and that some established democracy also has cannot count as backsliding; that nothing should be flagged unless the government meant to entrench itself; that Case D is genuine reform and should not be flagged; and that a country already below the threshold cannot backslide further. Say which of these survive contact with the procedure you have written, and close by stating plainly what a protocol of this kind cannot do — what questions it must leave to someone else.
