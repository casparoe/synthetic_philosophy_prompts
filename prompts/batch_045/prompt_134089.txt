I'm an undergraduate in a metaphysics seminar this term, and I've been asked to put together a one-page classification key for our reading group on actual infinities: the Hilbert's Hotel material, Cantor's argument that no pairing of the natural numbers with the real numbers can be exhaustive, and the finitist reactions to both. What I want is a decision tree that takes a person from a starting commitment that everyone in the room already accepts and leads them to a named position. About half of us have taken a set theory course and the others have only had intro logic, so the key has to be self-contained.

The starting commitment is this: ordinary finite arithmetic is unproblematic, and given any finite collection you can always add one more member. Nobody in the group disputes either claim. Everything downstream of that should be a branch.

Constraints on the tree:

1. Seven or eight branch questions, twelve leaves at most. Every node must be phrased as a question a student could answer without knowing which position it leads to, and without using technical vocabulary (bijection, cardinality, power set, omega, aleph) unless the question itself glosses the term in a sentence.

2. Each question should allow three kinds of answer: a yes-ish one, a no-ish one, and one on the order of the setup is not coherent or there is no fact of the matter here. Where the third is chosen, route it explicitly rather than letting it dead-end, and say what it commits the answerer to later in the tree.

3. Three of the questions must be built on the following cases, which I made up for the handout.

   - Two hotels. Hotel N has rooms numbered 1, 2, 3, and so on, every room occupied. A single new guest arrives, and the manager's instruction is that every current guest moves to the room whose number is one higher; this frees room 1. Hotel Z has rooms numbered by all the integers, negative and positive and zero, again every room occupied. The same uniform instruction frees nothing, because there is no lowest-numbered room. Now ask first whether the single new guest can be housed in Hotel Z at all, and second what happens when an infinitely large bus pulls up to Hotel Z and the uniform instruction is that every guest moves to the room whose number is double theirs: that frees infinitely many rooms. So the harder-sounding job has a uniform solution and the easier-sounding one does not. The options I want available are: Hotel Z can house the bus but not the single guest; the availability of a uniform rule is irrelevant and both can be housed; the dependence on whether a uniform rule exists shows that can be housed is only a formal fact about pairings and not a fact about what could really be done; or the whole setup is incoherent.

   - The hotel that was always there. Rooms numbered 1, 2, 3, and so on, no first day of operation, and on each day exactly one new guest arrived and took the lowest-numbered empty room. No guest ever left, no room was ever empty or demolished, and today every room is occupied. Ask whether the story is coherent, and give as options: yes, because a series with no beginning raises no problem that a series with a beginning would not; no, because a collection built up one member at a time can never be finished; and no, for the same reason that a fully occupied Hotel N is impossible.

   - The hotel that was never built, plus the doorman. Same hotel, same full occupancy today, but no first day and no arrivals ever: every room has simply always been occupied. Ask whether the answer changes from the previous case, and say explicitly that a change of answer locates the objection in how the collection was produced while a stable answer locates it in the collection itself. In the same node, ask about the doorman who has been keeping a tally, adding one to his count each day forever, where his count was a finite number on every earlier day: is his count today finite, infinite, or is the question malformed?

4. At least one further question must separate the abstract from the concrete, along these lines: even if no concrete collection is infinite, is there a single collection containing all the natural numbers, and is the claim that there are more real numbers than natural numbers a determinate one with a determinate answer, a determinate one whose answer we cannot settle, or not determinate at all?

5. At least one question must locate whatever impossibility the answerer has been invoking: is it a matter of what is possible at all, of what the laws of nature in fact permit, or of what we could ever have evidence for or ever be causally affected by?

6. Leaves must include at minimum: a full Cantorian realist who accepts actual infinities in the concrete world as well as in mathematics and accepts the unlimited hierarchy; someone who accepts actual infinities among abstract objects but denies that anything concrete is infinite; a strict finitist for whom no completed infinite totality exists anywhere; an ultrafinitist who is suspicious even of indefinite extendability once the numbers get large enough that exponentiation stops being total; a view on which the natural numbers are indefinitely extensible so that all of them never forms a single definite totality even though each of them is available; a countabilist who allows infinite totalities but holds that every one of them is countable; a predicativist who accepts the natural numbers as a totality but not arbitrary power sets or impredicatively specified ones; a causal finitist who finds the infinite unproblematic in the abstract but holds that nothing concrete can stand in causal relations to infinitely many things; a nomological-only finitist who thinks infinite collections are possible but not in a world like ours; and a deflationist who thinks the whole dispute is a choice between two equally serviceable notions rather than a disagreement about how things are. Use a standard label where you are confident one exists; where you are not, write no standard label rather than inventing one. Describe each leaf by its answer path, in the form 1b-2a-3c and so on, and by one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

7. Build the tree from the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, and only then place the positions in that space. Identify at least three combinations of answers that no one occupies and give each a one-line diagnosis: is nobody there because the combination is incoherent, because it is merely unattractive, or because it is unexplored?

8. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot actually separate, rather than merging them silently, and tell me what additional question would pull them apart; at most two such extra questions. I suspect the real weakness is that the setup is incoherent and there is no fact of the matter answers converge even when they are reached for quite different reasons, and I would like to see whether anything separates them.

Please do not give me a bibliography, citations, attributed quotations, or any as Professor So-and-so argues material: I already have the readings, and what I need is the structure. Mention a person's name only in parentheses attached to a label if it is genuinely standard, and never attribute a specific text or passage. The two places I most want the tree to earn its keep are the two-hotels case, where our group split in a way I could not predict, and the traversal-versus-existence distinction in the hotel that was always there.
