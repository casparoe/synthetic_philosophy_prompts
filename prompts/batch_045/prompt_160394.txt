I'm in a metaphysics reading group — four of us, one of whom works on the foundations of physics and keeps steering every discussion back to identical particles — and we have spent most of this term going round in circles about the identity of indiscernibles and Max Black's two-sphere universe. The case itself is familiar enough: two spheres of exactly the same size, composition, colour and temperature, in an otherwise empty universe, placed symmetrically, so that the whole universe admits a reflection that swaps them and there is no qualitative difference between them at all. One half of the group says the case refutes Leibniz's principle outright, and that if two things can be exactly alike and yet two, then what makes them two cannot be any qualitative fact about them — it has to be primitive thisness, or nothing. The other half says the spheres are not indiscernible in the relevant sense at all, because there is an irreflexive relation — two-miles-from — that holds between them; on their view the principle only forbids absolute indiscernibility, and weak discernibility is enough to vindicate it. Every move on one side has a stock reply on the other: the weak-discernibility defence is said to be trivial, since non-identity is itself an irreflexive relation and so any two distinct things are weakly discerned, which collapses the principle into the claim that distinct things are distinct; and the schema itself is said to be trivially true if its second-order quantifier is allowed to range over haecceitistic properties like being-identical-with-this-very-sphere, and false only if it is restricted to qualitative ones. Nobody has moved in three months, and the arguments are by now ritual.

To break the deadlock we tried two variations of our own, and both seemed to make things worse rather than better. The first was a world of two spatiotemporally and causally disconnected island universes, each containing a single sphere and nothing else, so that no spatial, causal or dynamical relation links the two; there the defender said the spheres are still weakly discerned by lies-in-a-different-connected-component, and the sceptic said that is non-identity wearing a hat. The second was a generalisation to three mutually equidistant spheres, where every pair bears exactly the same relations, so that nothing is absolutely discerned from anything and yet weak discernibility holds throughout; that made it look as though the number of objects was never the issue and the symmetry was, but we could not agree on what follows from that. What complicates it further is that the principle seems to be doing at least three different jobs in our discussions: sometimes it is offered as a criterion for counting and re-identifying things, sometimes as a claim about what grounds numerical difference, and sometimes as a premise in a relationalist programme about space or about whether quantum mechanics needs individuals at all. My physics friend says the permutation invariance of the formalism is where the real data lies; the rest of us suspect that no physical formalism settles which relations count as qualitative.

My suspicion, offered as a hypothesis and not as a conclusion, is that we are not in one dispute at all but three or four sharing a name: a formal question about the range of the second-order quantifier, which encodes a substantive commitment to or against haecceities; a question about whether the principle is epistemic or metaphysical in ambition; and a question about whether the physics case is even a data point for the others. So I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a verbal disagreement, a substantive one, or several different questions being asked under one heading? Do the sides differ about which relations exist, about standards for what counts as an admissible or qualitative relation, about which cases count as evidence, or about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? Is a hidden formal question — about the logic of weak and relative discernibility, or about quantification — doing all the work while everyone thinks they are arguing about spheres? Please engage the positions as I have described them rather than surveying the literature, and I do not want references or reading lists. Given your diagnosis, tell me what would actually count as progress: a distinction to be drawn, a formal result about discernibility, a change of question, an input from physics — or, if you think no progress should be expected here, why the question is one we should stop asking.
