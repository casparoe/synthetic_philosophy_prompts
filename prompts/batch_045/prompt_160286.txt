I am a professor of philosophy, and every time I teach a graduate seminar on nineteenth-century liberalism we reach the same impasse about J. S. Mill. The topic is his views on India and colonial rule, and how they relate to the liberal principles for which he is canonical. I want a diagnosis of why the debate has stalled, not a verdict on who is right.

Let me lay out the shared background. Mill spent most of his adult working life at the India House, ending his official career when the government of India was transferred to the Crown in 1858. In On Liberty, published in 1859, he states the harm principle in a way that applies to mature members of communities that are already civilized; he also holds that children and peoples he regards as not yet capable of self-government or as standing in need of improvement may be governed paternalistically or even despotically, and he presents the whole principle as grounded in utility, in the permanent interests of human beings as progressive beings. That much is common ground in the seminar. The dispute begins when we ask what follows from it.

The seminar reliably divides into three camps, and the same three moves get repeated. The first camp says this is a flat inconsistency or an ideological limit: the colonial exception shows that Mill's liberalism was never meant for everyone, and that the harm principle functions as a provincial doctrine with a built-in civilizational bar. The second camp says there is no inconsistency once we reconstruct Mill's indirect utilitarianism together with his developmental or stage theory of civilization: if the final appeal is to long-term utility, and if the empirical claim about what improves a less advanced society is granted, then paternalistic or despotic rule over such a society follows from his own premises. The third camp says the first two are not really disagreeing about Mill: they are using the concept of liberalism differently, one as a set of timeless normative principles and the other as a historical tradition that has to be judged by its institutional effects, so the quarrel is verbal. My own suspicion, which I want you to test rather than assume, is that the deadlock persists because each camp is implicitly evaluating a different grade of consistency, but I am not certain.

Please proceed as follows. Adopt the persona of a professional philosopher who is equally fluent in normative political theory, the history of political thought, and conceptual analysis. Think carefully and do not rush to judgment. Start by brainstorming at least four distinct candidate diagnoses for the stall. Candidates you should at least consider include: a purely verbal disagreement about the extension of the concept of liberalism; a substantive normative disagreement about whether utility can license paternalism; a hidden empirical or sociological disagreement about stages of civilization and the probable consequences of colonial rule; a disagreement about the proper object of interpretation, namely whether we are assessing Mill's explicit principles, his institutional role in the East India Company and the India House, or the later ideological uses of his texts; and a bedrock clash of starting intuitions about whether a canonical liberal author can be evaluated apart from the imperial context in which he wrote and worked. After brainstorming, eliminate or downweight the diagnoses that fit the evidence poorly, and then present the single most defensible diagnosis with explicit reasons.

In your final diagnosis I want you to make central use of a distinction I have been developing in my own work, which I would like you to sharpen and test. Call it the difference between principle-consistency, application-consistency, and expressive-consistency. Principle-consistency asks whether the colonial exception can be derived from Mill's harm principle plus the utilitarian standard without relying on any contested empirical claim about non-European societies. Application-consistency asks whether the exception follows once we grant Mill's empirical and sociological assumptions about civilization, improvement, and the likely consequences of colonial rule. Expressive-consistency asks whether the exception is compatible with the critical and emancipatory function that the harm principle is supposed to perform against majority opinion and against paternalism, irrespective of whether it is formally derivable. Tell me which of these grades each camp is really arguing about, whether the debate has stalled because the participants are answering different questions under a single heading, and whether your diagnosis supports my hypothesis about different grades of consistency or points instead to one of the other candidates.

Finally, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress. Be specific. Would progress consist in a clearer taxonomy of these grades of consistency; in a decision about which grade should be salient when we evaluate a liberal theorist; in an empirical or historical argument about Mill's sociology of civilization; in a reframing of the question from whether Mill was inconsistent to which kind of inconsistency matters; or in the honest conclusion that no further progress should be expected, together with an explanation of why the question is ill-posed or why the disagreement is ultimate? The deliverable is the diagnosis itself, argued for, not a survey of scholarship.
