I have no academic background in philosophy or economics; I came across the St. Petersburg game in a popular book and cannot stop thinking about it. A fair coin is tossed until it lands heads, and the prize doubles for every tail before that — two dollars if heads comes on the first toss, four on the second, eight on the third, and so on without limit — so the expected payoff is larger than any finite number, and yet nobody I ask will pay more than the price of a coffee to play, and I do not think they are being irrational.

I am less interested in being told which answer is right than in what a right answer would have to look like. So please write me the specification: what must a resolution of this puzzle deliver in order to be adequate to the only purpose I care about, telling an actual person what to do at the booth? Give me the requirements list — the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the results it must not contradict — with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and say which are non-negotiable and which are only preferences of one camp. The distinctions I have in mind are between a fair price and a price worth paying, between one play and a bundle of a thousand plays bought together, and between what the arithmetic says about the average and what it says about what usually happens; the result it must not contradict is that the same expected-value arithmetic is the arithmetic I rely on when I judge whether a raffle ticket or a phone-insurance policy is worth buying.

Three cases I keep turning over, and I want every candidate resolution tested against them: a version with a posted maximum payout of about a billion dollars, so that the expected payoff drops to roughly thirty dollars; a version that pays nothing at all unless the first heads arrives after the twentieth toss, so that the expected payoff is still infinite while you almost certainly receive nothing; and a version in which you may buy any fraction of a ticket, or sell one you already hold.

Then the part I most want: is some subset of your own requirements jointly unsatisfiable, so that the list itself turns out to be the result? And which single requirement knocks out the largest share of the answers people actually give — diminishing value of money, a finite casino bankroll, or averaging over many plays instead of one? Keep the weight on the criteria; a one-line scorecard at the end for those three familiar answers is enough.
