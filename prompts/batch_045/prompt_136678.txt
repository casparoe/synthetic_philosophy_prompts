I spent my career in molecular spectroscopy and have come to philosophy only in retirement. I am preparing a short paper for a local discussion group on Parfit's teletransporter and on what he calls what matters in survival, and I have run into a calculation that I cannot make come out right. I would be grateful for help in locating the error.

As I understand the argument, the teletransporter is supposed to show that personal identity is not what matters, because what matters is a purely psychological relation — call it R — whose holding depends only on the preservation of an informational pattern: memories, character, intentions, and the like, rather than on the persistence of any particular matter. If that is right, then it seems to me that what matters ought to be treatable as a measurable quantity, a degree of fidelity between the psychological profiles of two person-stages, something rather like a correlation coefficient that one could in principle read off a scan. I have been assuming further that this quantity behaves like the quantities I am used to: that it is conserved, or at any rate divided among branches, so that in the fission case, where two replicas are produced, each receives half of what matters, much as a beam splitter divides a beam's energy between two output ports.

That cannot be Parfit's view, since he treats fission as involving no loss at all; and it leads somewhere I take to be absurd. Consider a variant I have been turning over. The scanner has finite resolution: it records the connectome down to some scale, and the reconstruction must fill in the sub-resolution detail stochastically, so that each replica matches the original with, say, ninety-seven per cent fidelity, and the replicas differ slightly both from the original and from one another. If what matters is additive across branches, then three reconstructions at ninety-seven per cent yield nearly three times as much of it as a single perfect reconstruction; and by lowering the fidelity while raising the copy number one could manufacture an arbitrary amount — a fission pump, a source of what matters out of nothing. I take this as a reductio of the whole approach, but I would like to know precisely which step is the mistaken one.

Two further things puzzle me. First, a former colleague objects that the case is void, since the no-cloning theorem forbids copying an unknown quantum state, and that where such transfer is physically possible at all, as in quantum teleportation, the original is necessarily destroyed, so that the branching case never arises: is the thought experiment hostage to physics in this way? Second, I have been assuming that the difference between teletransportation and the gradual replacement of my neurons one by one is a difference in kind, because the latter involves no break in the continuous process realizing my consciousness, and that this is why the second case preserves what matters and the first does not.

If the paper is to work I need to know where I have gone wrong, and I would much rather be corrected on the physics-flavoured assumptions I am smuggling in than be given a survey of what others have said on the topic.
