I have become fairly convinced by Peter Turchin's structural–demographic theory of secular cycles: the claim that large agrarian societies generate political instability largely endogenously, on a timescale of roughly two to three centuries, through the coupled dynamics of population pressure and falling real wages, elite overproduction with its resulting intra-elite competition, and the fiscal distress of the state — the cycle closing only when population decline, state breakdown, or a restored fiscal bargain recreates the conditions for renewed growth. I use it less as a forecasting engine than as a lens on long-run history, and as a working hypothesis about why periods of elite expansion and wage stagnation are dangerous in combination. I want you to tell me what would count against it.

Please structure your answer as a map of the theory's exposure, and be specific rather than programmatic. In particular I would like:

1. A ranking of defeaters, from fatal through serious to merely awkward, with the ranking justified rather than asserted. Distinguish clearly between what would falsify the theory's distinctive causal mechanism, what would merely show it is incomplete, and what would only embarrass a particular quantification of it.

2. An honest statement of what defenders of the theory would themselves concede as counting against it — not merely what its critics allege. If the field has already absorbed certain objections by weakening a component (say, by making elite overproduction do most of the work once the fiscal link looks doubtful, or by allowing exogenous shocks to trigger the endogenous dynamics), say so, and say whether the resulting version still asserts anything.

3. Engagement with the null hypothesis that worries me most: a damped or excitable system, repeatedly kicked by exogenous shocks such as plague, war, and climate excursions, with slow recovery times, can produce apparently periodic instability over a few centuries without any endogenous oscillator at all. What observable pattern would discriminate genuine endogeneity from shock-plus-recovery? I care about the details here — the expected phase relations between real wages, elite numbers, state fiscal capacity and unrest; whether the amplitude should depend on the size of the triggering shock; whether instability should still recur in periods with no major exogenous shock; and what the cross-variable lag structure should look like if the coupling is real. Tell me which observations would settle this and which would be compatible with both stories no matter how they came out.

4. A treatment of the measurement problem. The theory's central variables are not directly observed: elite overproduction gets proxied by things like degree counts, office prices, or numbers of aspirants to office; popular immiseration by real wages or stature; state breakdown by fiscal or administrative indicators. Does the freedom to choose among such proxies give the theory enough slack that it will always fit, and is there a principled, non-ad hoc way to constrain proxy choice in advance?

5. A direct confrontation with the extension to modern states. Modern polities have administrative and fiscal capacities that earlier ones lacked — withholding and broad-based taxation, central banks, borrowing in their own currency, high-resolution and near-real-time economic data. The recent round of instability in the United States occurred without anything resembling state fiscal collapse or loss of administrative control, and indeed with an enormous expansion of state borrowing. Is this a genuine defeater for the modern extension, or does the theory have the resources to relocate the mechanism — to intra-elite competition and status competition alone, for instance — without thereby becoming a different and much weaker theory?

6. A test of a rival I have been toying with, which I want you to take seriously rather than humour. Call it the legibility-lag model: the variable that matters is not demographic pressure or elite numbers as such, but the gap between a polity's fiscal and regulatory claims and its capacity to see, measure and extract from the society it governs. On this view, instability breaks out when the state's demands outrun its informational reach, and therefore states with high-resolution administrative data and automated extraction should be able to run large elite surpluses and stagnant wages indefinitely without the fiscal phase of the cycle — i.e. the secular cycle is a regime-specific phenomenon of low-capacity states, and its apparent recurrence is an artefact of applying a pre-modern regularity to a changed environment. What would discriminate this from structural–demographic theory? Where do the two make genuinely conflicting predictions rather than redescribing the same events? And which of the two is, on your assessment, the more exposed?

7. A verdict on insulation. After all the qualifications have been granted, is there anything left that this theory could not accommodate? If it has been qualified to the point where no historical sequence could refute it, say so plainly, and then tell me whether that is fatal for a theory whose whole appeal was that it made history quantitative and predictive — or whether a good heuristic is allowed to be unfalsifiable in that way.

Two statements about what I personally find persuasive, so that you can weight your argument. I would be most moved by a demonstration that a discriminating prediction — especially one about phase relations or about the absence of a cycle in a specified kind of polity — was fixed in advance on variables measured independently of the outcome, and then simply failed or succeeded. I would be least moved by general methodological complaints to the effect that history is not a science, that human agents are not particles, or that quantitative macrohistory is overreaching; I do not find those defeating, and I would rather you spent your words elsewhere.

Practical constraints on your answer. Write in British English. Do not give me a bibliography, citations, or a reading list: I have no way to check them here, and a list of names would be worth nothing to me, so argue from the structure of the theory and from historical cases you can describe substantively. Where you rely on a contested empirical claim, flag it in a clause as an assumption rather than hedging your whole argument around it. Assume, for the sake of argument, that the much-discussed pre-2020 anticipation of rising instability in the United States was genuinely made in advance and was not a post hoc calibration; do not spend your space on that question. Commit to conclusions throughout, including about which defeaters are live rather than merely logically possible. Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words.
