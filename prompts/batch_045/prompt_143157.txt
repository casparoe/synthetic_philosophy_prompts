I am writing a paper on whether a moderately affluent democracy may permissibly direct its social spending to its own citizens rather than to comparably or much worse off foreigners, and before I commit to a line I want the specification that any account of compatriot partiality would have to meet. I would like you to write that specification rather than to survey the field. Assume I have read the literature; make the criteria do the work, and name no authors and cite nothing.

Fix the purpose of the theory as follows: it must be adequate for guiding institutional design and official action, not merely for delivering verdicts about what one private person owes another. Everything below should be assessed against that purpose, and you should say if any requirement on my list is a demand that only makes sense for a theory with a different purpose.

Give me the requirements list, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and with each marked non-negotiable, negotiable, or a prejudice of one camp. Here is my own tentative list; correct it, reorder it, and add at least two requirements I have missed.

1. Contrastive sensitivity. The account must say what happens when the candidate grounds are varied one at a time: shared institutions without shared culture, shared culture without shared institutions, co-residence without citizenship, citizenship without residence, coercion without reciprocity, reciprocity without coercion. Motivate with matched pairs, e.g. the long-term resident who has been denied naturalization against the citizen who has lived abroad for forty years.

2. Degrees and nested jurisdictions. Membership is layered: municipality, province, federation, supranational union. The account must say whether the claims come in degrees, how the degrees are ordered, and whether that ordering is transitive. Motivate it with a case in which a flood-relief budget must be divided between a richer province of your own federation and a poorer region of a neighbouring state.

3. Conflict among partiality-generating groups. The account must handle a dual national whose two states are at war with each other, and a person whose religious or linguistic community straddles borders while most of her compatriots do not belong to it. Does either group retain a claim, and is the conflict decidable?

4. Exit, entry, dissent, and duration. Motivating cases: the emigrant who has formally renounced; the citizen naturalized last year; the lifelong resident who has never voted and dissents from every act of the state; the state that is about to dissolve or the province that is about to secede. The account must say whether the relevant relation is acquired and lost, and over what time scale.

5. The deontic structure. Is compatriot partiality a permission to favour, a duty to favour, a constraint on how a general duty may be discharged, or an intensification of a duty that would exist anyway? Motivate with a tax increase whose proceeds would do more good abroad: is spending them at home permissible, required, or merely not an injustice?

6. Two dimensions kept apart. An account must not treat the permission to benefit compatriots and the permission to exclude or coerce non-compatriots as the same thing, and must say something about each. Motivate with the pair: topping up a domestic pension versus closing a labour market to foreigners.

7. The discriminating ground. The account must state what does the work, and must not silently substitute a surrogate such as coercion, reciprocity, associative history, or shared identity for shared citizenship itself. Say what would count as evidence that the stated ground rather than a surrogate is doing the work.

8. Distinctions that may not be collapsed: having a claim versus how strong a claim is; the justification of a state's existing at all versus the justification of partiality within one; distributive claims versus non-distributive ones; and what people in fact feel about compatriots versus what they owe them. Give a case for each.

9. Bound against abuse. The account must not entail that any group with a shared identity generates comparable claims, and it must survive the case of a nation partly constituted by the exclusion of some of the people who live under its institutions.

10. Uptake under conditions of disagreement. The theory must say what officials may do when the electorate's partiality is stronger than the theory permits, and how much moral cost attaches to non-compliance by institutions rather than by individuals.

Then, and this is the part I most want: identify any pair or subset of the requirements that you think cannot be jointly satisfied, and argue for the incompatibility rather than asserting it. I suspect something in the neighbourhood of requirements 1, 2, and 10 pulls apart. Also identify which single requirement excludes the largest number of otherwise attractive accounts, and say why.

Finally, give me a compressed scorecard and no more than two sentences each for four broad families of account: coercion- or institution-based, associative or relationship-based, identity- or culture-based, and thoroughgoing egalitarian-cosmopolitan. Score them against the non-negotiable requirements only.

Two further constraints. Keep every motivating case inside ordinary political life: no science fiction, no disconnected thought experiments, one or two sentences per case. And if a requirement on my list can only be motivated from inside one camp's framing rather than from the cases, say so and demote it rather than defending it. A table for the requirements is fine; prose for the incompatibilities.
