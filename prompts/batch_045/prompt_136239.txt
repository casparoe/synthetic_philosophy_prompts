Retired chemist, been drifting into philosophy the last couple of years, and I've got stuck on how to define honesty and deception for AI systems. Before I read any more proposals I want a spec: what would a definition of "this system was deceptive" / "this output was a lie" have to deliver to count as adequate? The purpose I care about is a regulator's or an auditor's — someone who has to rule on the behaviour of a deployed system from the outside, with no priveleged access to the internals. So give me the requirement list: the cases it has to sort correctly (making up a fact it has no grounds for; telling the user what they clearly want to hear; an answer that is literally true but built to leave a false impression; bluffing inside a game or a negotiation; relaying a falsehood on behalf of the user who asked for it; asserting something false as part of a fiction or a roleplay); the distinctions it must not collapse (error vs. deception, deception vs. causing harm, the system deceiving vs. a person using the system as an instrument to deceive, what is stated vs. what is merely implied, output that is false vs. output that has no clear truth conditions at all); and the constraints it can't violate (it has to be checkable from behaviour, and it can't depend on a state like belief or intent that we could never in principle confirm). With the case or consideration behind each. Then tell me which of these are real desiderata and which are just one camp's prejudice — I suspect "lying requires believing the thing is false" is something moral philosophers are attached to but an auditor can't use, but I may be misreading that literature. And if some subset of the requirements turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable, say exactly which and why: that's the part I actualy most want to see. A brief scorecard at the end for the obvious families of definition (belief-plus-intent, purely functional or information-theoretic, something built on calibration or on what the system "knows") is fine, but keep the weight on the criteria.
