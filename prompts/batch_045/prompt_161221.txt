I've been reading a popular book on paradoxes in my own time — I have no philosophy training, I work in data engineering and got pulled in by a podcast episode — and the chapter on the Liar paradox left me both convinced and suspicious in a way I can't sort out by myself. Rendering it as faithfully as I can, the chapter says something like: Dialetheism is the view that some contradictions are true. Its main motivation is the Liar: the sentence that says of itself that it is not true seems to be true if and only if it is not, and the simplest way out is to conclude that it is both true and false. Classical logic validates explosion, the rule that lets you derive any conclusion at all once you have a contradiction, so dialetheists have to move to a paraconsistent logic in which explosion fails, usually by giving up disjunctive syllogism. The standard version, which the chapter associates with Graham Priest, is called LP: it is classical logic with a third truth value, both, added on, so everything classical logic counts as valid stays valid and the only difference is that certain sentences get the extra value. Dialetheism is therefore a form of logical pluralism: classical logic is the right tool for mathematics, paraconsistent logic is the right tool for talk about truth. The obvious objection is trivialism — once you let one contradiction in, the distinction between truth and falsity collapses and you can no longer deny anything — and Priest's reply is that only some contradictions are true, not all, which is meant to stop the collapse. Critics call that reply ad hoc, and add that dialetheism cannot explain why nobody in ordinary life ever goes around asserting contradictions.

What I want is an accuracy audit I can act on: which sentences a real dialetheist would sign off on, which are the sort of simplification a popular writer can get away with, and which are outright misrepresentations — and for the misrepresentations, whether correcting them helps or hurts the view. Two things in particular. First, a mathematically-minded friend insists the pluralism line is a three-way conflation: that dialetheism is a thesis about truth, paraconsistency is a separate thesis about what follows from what, and that most dialetheists think classical logic is simply wrong rather than fine for its own subject matter, so calling them pluralists gets them backwards. Is he right, or being pedantic about a distinction without a difference? Second, the claim that everything classically valid survives in LP: is that a harmless slip, or does it distort the picture the chapter goes on to build? I also want to know whether the trivialism objection as stated actually lands, or whether it is a strawman, and whether the accurate version of the view has a better answer than the chapter reports.

The other thing I can't shake is an idea I had before I read any of this, and I'd like it tested rather than flattered. I thought you could treat a true contradiction as quarantined: contradictions are allowed to be true, but they sit in a sealed compartment, no inference is permitted to cross the boundary, so the rest of your reasoning stays classical and the paraconsistent machinery only ever runs inside the quarantine. I've since seen the phrase classical recapture float past in connection with this area and I don't know whether that is the same thought, a better version of it, or something else entirely. So: is my quarantine coherent as stated, does it quietly collapse into something the view already contains, and what would a dialetheist say against it? My own worry is that it makes the position unfalsifiable — you can always reclassify an inconvenient contradiction as quarantined — and that Liar-style paradoxes just reappear at the boundary of the compartment, but I don't trust my judgment here. Please don't send me to a reading list; I'd rather have the reasoning, so that I can decide whether to trust the chapter the next time it sounds this confident.
