I work on the reliability team for a fleet of autonomous yard tractors operating inside container terminals, and I need help with a problem that has turned out to be conceptual rather than statistical, and that my team keeps talking past each other about.

We maintain what we call a mechanism register. When an incident is confirmed, we assign it to a mechanism; when a new anomaly arrives, we decide whether it is a recurrence of an already registered mechanism or a genuinely new one. The classification has teeth. A recurrence reopens a closed remediation, re-triggers fleet-wide re-validation of a fix that was previously signed off, and is reported to the port authority under a different heading than a new mechanism is. So for us, is this the same mechanism is not an academic question, and we have to answer it within days, usually from partial telemetry.

Three terms as I use them: the site of a failure is the stage of the pipeline at which the failure is generated (detector, fusion, planner, actuator); the signature is what we can observe afterwards (log footprint plus operational outcome); the path is the chain from initiating condition through site to outcome. A compensator is a downstream process that masks a failure that is still occurring.

Below are verdicts I have actually recorded over the past two years. I have stated them as I recorded them, including the ones I am no longer confident about.

1. Trigger swap. A known mechanism, M1, is a detector failure at low sun: partially occluded container edges are assigned high obstacle confidence, producing a phantom obstacle and a hard brake. It was fixed by retraining plus sun-angle gating. Later, at a coastal terminal, the same site failed with the same path and the same signature, but the initiating condition was salt-fogged lens fouling, not sun angle. Verdict: recurrence of M1. I do not require the trigger to match.

2. Outcome swap. The same M1 path fires, but by then a planner change means the response is a slow drift toward the wrong lane rather than a hard brake. Different signature, different severity, same site and path. Verdict: recurrence of M1. I treat outcome severity as irrelevant to mechanism identity.

3. Identical signature, different proximal cause. A hard brake with a log footprint indistinguishable from M1 turns out to have been caused by a real but stale mapped obstacle: a container had been moved, the map had not been updated, and the vehicle braked correctly on an object that is no longer there. Verdict: new mechanism, not a recurrence, even though the signature and the coarse description (the planner acted on a spurious obstacle hypothesis) are the same as M1's.

4. Compensated latency. After M1 was remediated at Terminal A, a geofenced override continued to mask the detector failure there, so nothing was observable for seven months. The same misclassification then produced an incident at Terminal B, where the override is not active. Verdict: recurrence of M1. I treat the mechanism as having been continuously present through the masked period, even though it had no observable manifestation.

5. Substrate swap. The camera module was replaced with a second-source unit. The M1 pathology recurs, but retraining no longer suppresses it; a change to the calibration routine does. Verdict: recurrence, on the grounds that the mechanism survived its substrate. A colleague filed it as new, on the grounds that the part number changed and the fix changed, and I could not dislodge him.

6. Same path, fix no longer works. A mechanism consisting of a stale-timestamp path through fusion recurs after a timing window shifts; the original patch no longer prevents it, and a different patch is needed. Verdict: recurrence. So I do not treat sharing a fix as necessary for identity.

7. Different paths, one fix. A dropped-message failure and a buffer race in the same module are both suppressed by the same watchdog timeout. Verdict: two mechanisms, not one. So I do not treat sharing a fix as sufficient for identity either.

8. Grain. A dropped message and a clock skew are unrelated at the fine grain, but both are describable as the planner acting on a stale world model, and both are prevented by a staleness check. I filed them as a single mechanism, M4. I am aware this sits badly with case 3, where two incidents sharing a coarse description and an identical signature were filed as different mechanisms; I have never been able to say in general what fixes the grain, only that it felt right in each case.

9. Drift. Over eighteen months and four firmware releases, a mechanism changed its trigger, its site, its substrate, and finally its signature. I recorded each adjacent pair as the same mechanism, but I recorded the current version and the original as different mechanisms. That is, I accepted A same-as B, B same-as C, C same-as D, and A not same-as D. If identity is transitive, one of those four verdicts is wrong; I have not been able to give up any of them.

10. Artefact. A spike in a signature turned out to be produced by a fleet-wide change in telemetry granularity rather than by anything happening in the vehicles. Verdict: not a mechanism at all; struck from the register.

11. The case I keep going back and forth on. A structurally isomorphic failure appears in a different vehicle line: separate codebase, different sensors, same functional role in the architecture, same pathology. Half the time I think functional role is exactly what mechanism identity consists in and this is a recurrence; half the time I think a mechanism has to be made of something and this is an analogy, not a recurrence.

The rough rule I have been working with is an intervention-profile rule: two incidents involve the same mechanism when they share the set of interventions that would prevent the failure, at the grain at which we can actually intervene. It gets cases 1, 2, 3, 4, 8 and 10 right as I record them, but it fails on 5, 6 and 7 in three different ways, and it says nothing about 9 or 11. I suspect the rule is wrong in an interesting way rather than just incomplete, because all three failures are about the relation between fixability and identity.

What I would like from you:

A principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to next month's incidents, with each clause attached to the case that motivates it. In particular I want an answer about what fixes the grain at which failure roles are described, since that is what cases 3 and 8 disagree about and what my rule silently helps itself to.

Verdicts on four cases I have not yet had to decide. (a) After the M1 remediation, operators at one terminal had learned to hover over the emergency stop at dusk; the habit outlived the bug and now produces manual stops with M1's old signature and M1's old operational cost. Same mechanism, at the level of the socio-technical system, or a different one? (b) An incident occurs only when M1 and a rare network delay co-occur; neither alone suffices. Is the conjunction a third mechanism, or a recurrence of M1 against a background condition, and does your answer depend on how often the delay occurs? (c) We deliberately re-enact a registered mechanism in simulation as a regression test. Does a simulated re-enactment count as a recurrence for the register? (d) A registered mechanism fires, but a newly added redundancy absorbs it and no harm results. Is it still the same mechanism if the system now tolerates it?

An honest statement of which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and which single verdict you would overturn if you had to pick one.

Finally, a recommendation about the register itself. Cases 9 and 11 make me suspect that sameness of mechanism comes in degrees, or is relative to a grain we have to choose, while the register forces a binary that then drives remediation and regulatory reporting. If you think identity is graded, say what the dimensions of the grading are and how we should choose a threshold; if you think it is binary and I am misdescribing my own cases, say which case is misleading me.

Please do not spend the answer surveying literature or supplying references. If a position you rely on has a standard name, naming it in passing is enough; I can find the sources myself.
