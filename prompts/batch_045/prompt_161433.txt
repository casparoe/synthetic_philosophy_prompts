I write about science and ideas for a national newspaper, and this spring I am teaching a graduate seminar for science-communication students on how to handle claims that arrive wrapped in a metaphor — the brain is a computer, the genome is a code, the market thinks. For the final I want one ninety-minute essay question on the first of those, and I would like you to draft it for me, together with what a strong answer to it looks like.

The question has to do more than invite a survey of the standard positions. Every student arriving will have encountered the Chinese Room argument, the bare definition of a Turing machine, and the observation that a simulation of a storm is not wet. An answer that lays those out, finds them unsatisfying, and then plumps for one side should earn a B at best. I want the question to turn on a crux that has to be located before any of the stock material becomes usable, and I want the marking scheme to reward locating it.

To that end, I have been playing with a scenario from a piece I am currently reporting, and I would like you to build the question around something like it. A large consortium announces a complete, causally verified simulation of a human brain, assembled from a reconstruction of one person's connectome and run at the resolution of individual ion channels. Interfaced with a synthetic body, the system is behaviourally indistinguishable from the person it models: it passes every clinical interview, reports the same memories, and makes the same characteristic slips. Two further details, both emphasised by the team in their own write-up. First, the system can be formally described as implementing indefinitely many distinct functions depending on the grain at which its states are described, and the team found no non-arbitrary way to single out one of those descriptions as the one it really computes. Second, for every candidate mapping of its internal states onto representational contents, they were able to construct a rival mapping equally well supported by the causal and behavioural evidence.

Students are asked whether this result settles the question of whether the brain is a computer — and, the part I care about more, what kind of question they take that to be.

Please give me three things.

One: the question itself, in a form I could hand out. Self-contained, scenario stated cleanly, phrased so that no student can tell which way the examiner is inclined, and short enough to read in a minute.

Two: a model answer of roughly seven hundred words that argues rather than surveys. It should be able to stand without references of any kind; I do not want a reading list inside an essay exam, and I do not want the answer to depend on who said what first.

Three: a marking scheme out of twenty marks, built around four or five must-hit points, each with the marks it carries, the specific move an A answer must make to earn them, and a one-line note on how strong students typically get that point wrong.

For calibration, an A answer should at minimum have to do three things: say what would have to hold for an ascription of computation to a physical system to amount to more than a trivial redescription; explain why perfect behavioural and causal fidelity does not by itself deliver the conclusion in either direction; and take a position on whether the indeterminacy of content ascription refutes the computational claim or merely relocates it. An answer that names the right considerations without adjudicating between them is a B. An answer that treats the matter as settled by pointing out that a simulation is not the thing simulated should lose marks, even if it is otherwise competent.
