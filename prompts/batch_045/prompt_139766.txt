I have to submit an 800-word research proposal for a final-year philosophy of action module. The paper itself, of about 5,000 words, is written over a twelve-week semester, and the proposal is marked on how precisely the question is stated and on whether the planned work is realistic, so I would rather it be sharp than ambitious.

Here is my half-formed idea. In Kavka's toxin puzzle, a billionaire offers you a large sum for being in a certain state at a certain time: you must be intending, at midnight, to drink a vial of toxin that will make you miserably ill for a day. A perfectly reliable device reads the intention off, the money is paid on the spot, and you need never drink. The standard explanation of why you cannot collect is that the reward destroys the point of the intention: once the money is in hand you have no reason to drink, and since you can foresee this, you cannot now intend to drink. A rival explanation says the puzzle has little to do with reasons: intentions are simply not the sort of state one can acquire directly, at will, in the way one can raise one's arm, and the case only dramatises that fact.

My suspicion is that both camps treat the toxin structure as if it belonged uniquely to intention, and that this can be tested by running the very same structure on attitudes for which the no-reason-to-act story is unavailable. Suppose the device reads instead whether you believe that it will rain tomorrow, the reward being for the belief rather than for its truth and no action of yours being in question; or whether you hope that a rival will fail; or whether you fear something you know to be harmless. If the same kind of impossibility reappears for belief, hope and fear, the puzzle is not about intention or about practical reasons at all, but about a single constraint on acquiring attitudes for rewards. If it appears for some of these and not others, the interesting question is what property separates them, and the obvious candidates — having a correctness condition, being under direct voluntary control, being essentially tied to action, being something one can be held responsible for — can each be isolated by further cases in which only that property varies. I also want to know whether the impossibility iterates: can one, for a reward, intend to intend to drink, or hope to hope?

Turn this into a proposal. I need: the central question stated so that it has a determinate answer and does not collapse into a survey of what people say they could do; why the two explanations I sketch above, together with a third, control-based explanation, leave that question open; the method — what I would actually do across the twelve weeks, and why that method rather than the obvious alternatives such as canvassing intuitions or trying to derive the answer from a general theory of intention; the range of results the project could reach, and what would count as each of them; and an honest statement of what would make it fail, including the possibility that my battery of cases merely restates the original puzzle in a new vocabulary. Say plainly if the project is too large for a semester, and what you would cut.

Do not supply a bibliography or any citations: I will insert the references myself, and I do not want invented ones.

Format: open with the central question as a single bolded sentence of no more than forty words, then four short headed sections — Why the question is open; Method and timetable; Possible results; What would count as failure — and nothing besides. Use British English throughout, and keep the whole response to a maximum of 800 words.
