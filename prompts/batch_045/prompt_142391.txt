I should say up front that I have no academic background in philosophy. Everything I know about it I picked up on my own, reading here and there, mostly because of an argument in an online game community I help moderate. I am writing a long open letter to the studio that runs the game, and before I send it I want the argument put into its strongest form — the version that would still be standing after a clever person disagreed with it in the comments.

The situation, with the identifying details changed. The game is a long-running persistent sandbox; player-built structures survive across years and the servers have never been wiped. Several years ago a guild of roughly forty people finished a building project that took, by their own logs, something in the order of eleven thousand player-hours. Inside the finished fortress they hung a single unique object: a banner that the game's code generates only once per server, awarded automatically to the first group to complete a particular chain of world events. The studio has said it will never create another, and there is no other one anywhere.

Recently a player from a rival guild found an exploit that let him destroy structures inside a zone flagged as safe, and he destroyed the banner. The destruction was permanent. The studio banned his account and then issued a statement saying that they had restored an identical banner — same item ID, same statistics, same appearance, placed in the same spot — and that nothing real had been lost, at most some playtime. The guild says the restored banner is a replica and the original is gone. I think the guild is right, and I think the studio's reasoning is not merely callous but false. But I cannot get the argument to come out right, and every version I write ends up sounding like special pleading.

Here is the shape of the case I want made, and the routes I deliberately do not want it to take.

First, do not rest it on the labour, the time, or the money spent. I have three reasons, which you should feel free to sharpen or to tell me are bad: it invites the reply that the loss is merely wasted time and is fully compensated by handing the time back; it makes the wrongness depend on how much the victims invested, so that the very same act would be no wrong at all if the banner had been found lying on the ground; and the labour-mixing line of argument is, as far as I can tell, regarded as a poor argument even for ordinary physical property, and I do not want the letter standing on it.

Second, do not rest it on the distress. The studio can concede that people are upset while changing nothing, and arguing from distress seems to grant the very point in dispute, namely that the upset is directed at nothing real. So argue on the object side: the banner itself was a particular thing, and that thing no longer exists.

Third, the core of it, as I currently have it. Some things are such that what they are depends partly on where they came from, so that a duplicate sharing every intrinsic property is not a substitute but a copy or a forgery — the way a structurally identical new building is not the old building that was demolished. Call this provenance-sensitivity, and define it properly if my term is clumsy. The claim is then that the banner was provenance-sensitive and that its provenance — made by these people, at that time, through that sequence of events, in a world that has since moved on and cannot be re-run — is not reproducible even by the studio, even in principle. Hence the loss is non-fungible: there is no possible later state of affairs in which the guild has what it had before, and restoring an intrinsic duplicate is a category error rather than a repair.

What I most need is for the four objections below to be answered rather than sidestepped, since these are the ones the studio's defenders will actually post.

The first is that the banner just is a database record, and its identity over time is nothing but the continuity of that record or of the causal process sustaining it. The studio could, after all, have rolled the server back to a backup taken an hour before the exploit, and a rollback would have restored the very same item rather than a replica. If that is right, then what does the work is not history but continuity of an underlying process — a process the studio owns and controls — and the guild's grievance is really only a complaint about what the operator owes them by its own conventions.

The second is that provenance inside a virtual world is itself just recorded data: a timestamp, a creator field, a log line. The studio could have spawned a banner with a fabricated creation record, and no one could tell the difference from inside the game. If provenance is duplicable in that way, my distinction between original and replica collapses into something disappointingly thin, namely whichever record the operator's database happens to endorse.

The third is the dependence objection. If the banner exists only because a company keeps a server running, then calling it real looks like overreach; what is genuinely real is the players' experience and the social facts among them. Real objects do not wink out of existence when a company goes bankrupt, and an argument that ignores this will be laughed at.

The fourth is the one I am most afraid of, and I would rather you demolish my position than let it be demolished later. If I win, then every balance patch, every nerf, every server merge, every expansion that makes an old achievement trivial is the destruction of a provenance-sensitive particular and therefore a wrong. That is absurd, or at least it proves far too much, and I need to know exactly where the line falls and why my case does not cross it.

I want the piece to end with a short statement of what the argument does and does not establish about the general question of whether virtual objects are real and whether virtual harms are real harms. In particular, I want to be able to say that a virtual object can be a genuine particular without claiming that it is on a par with a physical object, or that destroying one is anywhere near as bad as destroying something physical. And I want help with a hunch I cannot formulate: that the fact these objects exist only as long as a platform sustains them is a reason to treat them as more precious, not less — the way something fragile or unrepeatable commands more care rather than less. I suspect that is where the letter is weakest, and I would like it either made rigorous or cut.

Two constraints, please. Do not lean on named theories, authors, or citations: I have no wish to drop names I could not defend if challenged, and I want the argument to work in plain and precise language, coining and defining any distinction it needs. And please flag explicitly any step where you think the reasoning is thinner than it sounds, because I would much rather remove a weak step myself than have it removed for me in public.
