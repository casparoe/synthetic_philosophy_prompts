I teach a second-year game theory course for philosophy students: 18 students, one 80-minute seminar, no laptops or calculators, payoffs in poker chips. They have already done iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies where the dominator is a pure strategy, and they can solve a 2×2 game for its mixed-strategy equilibrium. Next week is the step I have never managed to make land: allowing the dominator to be a mixed strategy — that a strategy can be strictly dominated while no single alternative beats it against every opponent profile, and that switching this on can restart an elimination process that had already stopped.

Design the session as a runnable game or short sequence of games: rules, payoff matrices small enough to verify by hand, roles, a timeline, and a debrief.

Constraints:
- The idea must sit in the machinery, not in my voice. Nobody should be told that mixtures are the point: arrange the rules, the roles, or the scoring so that students hit a strategy they cannot eliminate, and then produce the mixture themselves before I ever name it.
- I want one wrinkle built in. I will hand out randomizers of different granularity: half the room gets a coin, so the only mixture they can implement is 50/50 over two of their own actions; the other half gets a die, so they can implement any mixture with weights in sixths over up to six actions. Arrange the game so that at least one domination is visible to the die-holders and not to the coin-holders, and so the two halves can compare afterwards what each was able to eliminate.
- That raises the question I actually care about, and I want the activity to make it arguable in the room. Say a strategy is 2-dominated when some mixture over at most two pure strategies strictly beats it against every opponent profile. Does iterated elimination of 2-dominated strategies stop short of unrestricted iterated elimination, and does it — unlike unrestricted strict elimination — become order-dependent? My guess is yes to both, because eliminating a strategy inside the dominator's support can force the replacement dominator to need a larger support; if that guess is wrong, say so and re-aim the activity at whatever the real difference turns out to be.
- Debrief: map specific moments in the play onto the formal claims (domination by a mixture versus by a pure strategy; the coincidence of strict domination with never-being-a-best-response in two-player games, and my understanding that this comes apart with three or more players — correct me if not), say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure, and be frank about which part of the idea the game cannot produce and I will have to state in words. Give me one thing to listen or look for that distinguishes understanding from mere procedure.

After the design, return a single JSON object with this schema:

- setup: object with grouping (string), materials (array of strings), assumed_prior_knowledge (string)
- games: array of objects, each with purpose (string), roles (array of strings), payoff_matrix (string), randomizer ("coin" | "die" | "none"), intended_discovery (string)
- timeline: array of objects, each with minutes (string), stage (string), facilitator_does (string), facilitator_may_not_say (string)
- debrief: array of objects, each with moment_in_play (string), formal_point (string), prompt_question (string)
- failure_modes: array of objects, each with symptom (string), cause (string), fix (string)
- restricted_elimination: object with notion (string), stops_short_of_unrestricted (boolean), order_dependent (boolean), example_or_argument (string)
- game_cannot_deliver: array of strings
- understanding_check: string
