I write about science and ideas, and I've come to hold a strong version of accuracy-first epistemology: the only fundamental epistemic value is closeness to the truth; a credence function is to be graded by a strictly proper, additive measure of inaccuracy; and the familiar epistemic norms — probabilism, conditionalization, the Principal Principle, and even conciliationism about peer disagreement — are all theorems about minimizing expected inaccuracy rather than independent demands of reason.

I also hold a load-bearing second thesis, which I'll call robustness: once you restrict yourself to strictly proper and additive scores — Brier, logarithmic, or anything else in that class — the framework's verdicts no longer depend on which score you pick. Robustness is what makes the program falsifiable at all. If verdicts floated freely with the choice of score, the framework could be tuned to deliver whatever norm you antecedently liked, and would predict nothing.

I want a map of that package's exposure: what would have to be true for it to be false, and how decisive each thing would be.

1. Where does robustness actually break? I want the structural conditions under which admissible scoring rules come apart — self-locating or centered content, infinite or non-measurable possibility spaces, imprecise or indeterminate credence, unbounded inaccuracy, comparisons across different partitions or different questions — and an assessment of whether those cases are peripheral curiosities or central to what the framework is used for.

2. Which derivations are measurement-sensitive in a way that matters? Do the arguments for conditionalization, for the Principal Principle, or for conciliationism in peer disagreement quietly presuppose a particular scoring rule, a choice of which alternatives are live, or a convention for scoring a hypothesis about a peer's reliability?

3. What does the view risk that its rivals don't? It seems committed to there being no irreducible value in understanding, explanation, or reliability as such, and to the value of evidence being purely instrumental in the expected accuracy of a posterior policy. Are those fatal consequences, or bearable costs?

4. What would a committed accuracy-firster herself concede as counting against the view, as opposed to what its critics allege? And has the package been qualified into untestability, given that anomalies are typically answered by narrowing the class of admissible scores or comparisons — and if so, is that a problem for a view of this kind?

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. I would personally be most moved by failures showing that verdicts depend on how the space of possibilities is carved up, or that a normative conclusion was smuggled in through the choice of score. No citations or reading lists, please — I want your own analysis. Lead with your bottom line, and keep the whole response under 700 words.
