A philosophy podcast I half-listened to on a long drive last week summedup Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history like this:

"Nietzsche says there are three kinds of history. Monumental history goes to the past for heroes and great deeds that can inspire us to do something great ourselves. Antiquarian history is the reverent, conservationist kind — it wants to preserve everything it can dig up about where we came from. Critical history is just doing history properly: checking sources, exposing pious legends, refusing to take the past's own account of itself at face value. He thinks each has its place, but critical is obviously the one he actually wants, because his whole complaint is that his own age is suffocating under undigested facts and reverence for tradition. So the essay is really an argument for forgetting most of what we know and using whatever history is left strictly as a tool for whatever we happen to be trying to do."

The critical-history bit set off an alarm for me — I remember it being described as something harsher than careful scholarship — but the rest feels about right, or at least like the version I half-remember from undergrad, so I don't trust my own reaction. Is this fair? I'd like a sentence-by-sentence-ish audit: what would someone who actually works on Nietzsche let stand, what would they wince at or quietly rewrite, and is there a real error here or just simplification? Also: does that last line about history being a tool for the present actually follow from the three-way distinction, or is the podcaster sliding Nietzsche into a position he wouldn't own?
