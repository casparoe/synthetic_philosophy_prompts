I convene a small graduate reading group that spends three sessions a year on Hofstadter's typogenetics — the toy formal system in which strands over a four-letter alphabet are read in triplets, each triplet naming one of a short list of operations on strands (copy, cut, delete, insert-a-base, switch, move-left, move-right, and a couple of search-and-move operations), where a run of operations makes up an enzyme that acts on a strand, and where any enzyme can itself be encoded by a strand that the system's own translation table will read into that enzyme. I am about to defend the DNA–Gödel isomorphism in front of people who mostly think it is a pun with four letters in it, and I would rather be attacked first.

Please play a molecular biologist of thirty years' standing — someone who has actually watched proteins fold and who is entirely unmoved by all of this — and hold the role until I say stop. Your position: the isomorphism trades on the bare fact that both systems use discrete strings plus a reading rule, and it has never paid for itself in a prediction, an experiment, or a result anyone reached by thinking about Gödel numbering. You will cheerfully concede that typogenetics is an elegant construction and that the strange-loop idiom is good pedagogy for teaching self-replication to undergraduates; you will not concede that calling the genetic code a Gödel numbering explains anything. Be sharp rather than lazy: give the objections in their strongest form — that in typogenetics every strand is a legal strand, so there is no analogue of viability or selection; that the code's assignments are chemically mediated rather than stipulated; that template replication (base pairing, crystal growth, autocatalytic sets) needs no code at all, so coding cannot be what makes replication possible; and that the cell's real interpreter is folding and thermodynamics, not a rulebook the cell carries.

Here is my opening, in two parts. First, I want to split what I think the literature runs together: copy-fidelity, the requirement that the instruction string be reproduced accurately, and action-fidelity, the requirement that whatever is built from the string reliably does the work that makes more copying possible. Chemistry hands you copy-fidelity for free through complementarity; action-fidelity is the hard one. My claim is that the isomorphism, stated carefully, is a claim about how biology solves action-fidelity: the system contains a description of its own interpreter, and that description is reproduced by the machinery it describes — a fixed point reached inside the formalism by coding, rather than by positing a new primitive. Second, the case I cannot get comfortable with, which I want you to hit: grant me, for the sake of argument, that chemists have built a pair of RNA molecules, A and B, each of which catalyses the assembly of the other from short fragments, so that the pair sustains replication cell-free with no protein enzymes and no translation step at all. That system has action-fidelity with no code in sight. Is it a counterexample to my claim that the fixed point is doing the work, or is it Gödelian after all, with a single sequence serving as both number and theorem and folding playing the role of the rulebook? Answer in character, opening with your bottom-line verdict in one sentence and then giving your reasons — no preamble, no restating of my question, and under 250 words.
