I need your help preparing for a two-hour session with our Allocation Methods Committee next Thursday, at which I am asking them to replace the rule we use to rank candidate funding allocations.

The setting: I work at a public research-funding agency with roughly 400 million dollars of discretionary money per cycle, paid out in about two hundred awards. Realised value is extremely skewed — a small handful of awards account for most of what the agency ever accomplishes — and our outcome estimates are noisy, model-based, and in many cases little better than order-of-magnitude guesses.

The proposal. We currently rank allocations by expected value over a common numeraire (say QALYs, or dollars of social value), with an informal overlay: program officers are told not to let any one theme absorb too much of the cycle and to keep a few long-shot awards in every round. I want to replace both the expected-value rule and the overlay with one explicit rule: value each candidate allocation by risk-weighted expected utility, defined as follows. Rank an allocation's possible outcomes from worst to best; walk up the ranking and weight each utility increment by r applied to the probability of doing at least that well, where r is an increasing function from [0,1] onto [0,1]. Because r need not be the identity, this keeps statewise dominance and transitivity while giving up the independence axiom, so Allais-style choices become permissible rather than axiomatic errors.

Three further specifications matter, and I expect them to be where the fighting happens:

One, I take utility in the numeraire to be linear and put all risk attitude into r, parametrised as r(q) = q^k with k above 1, so that increments in the upper tail count for less than their probability. k is to be calibrated by putting a dozen or so binary choices between a sure cohort-level outcome and a gamble to the committee itself; pilot runs land between about 1.3 and 1.6, and I intend to report every recommendation for the whole range k between 1 and 2.

Two, r is applied once, to the distribution of total cohort value, and not award by award. The risk function does not decompose additively across independent awards, and the two orderings differ sharply: applied award by award the variance penalty grows roughly with the number of awards, applied to the cohort distribution it grows roughly with its square root, which at our scale is a difference of more than an order of magnitude.

Three, a carve-out. Ten to fifteen awards per cycle are genuinely one-shot — an observation window that closes, an outbreak that will have run its course, a field season that cannot be rescheduled. For these the diversification that dilutes risk aversion at cohort level is not available, so I want them weighted on their own rather than pooled.

The room. Two microeconometricians who run field experiments and whose default is to maximise expected social value; our chief statistician, a Bayesian who will care about what is identifiable and what is not; one former reinsurance pricing actuary, who is sympathetic to risk aversion in principle but will insist that institutions manage risk with capital requirements and risk measures rather than with preferences; three program directors who are attached to the balance overlay and who will read any risk-averse rule as an attack on high-variance, high-upside work; and one board member with a decision-theory background who will know the framework and press the technical objections. Assume I know the literature, so I need no citations, references, or suggested reading.

Please give me a preparation sheet with these five sections, in this order and at roughly these lengths:

1. "Where the room is" — about 150 words. Who speaks first, which blocs align with which, what each group is actually protecting, and which objection is likely to be raised by someone other than the person I expect it from.

2. "The objections, in the order they will come" — about 900 words. Eight objections, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are rather than by whose they are. For each: who raises it; the objection in that person's own vocabulary and idiom, not in mine; a rough damage ranking; the reply I should give in about two minutes; and one line on where that reply is soft or question-begging. I would rather have the two or three genuinely dangerous objections treated at length than all eight treated evenly.

3. "What I should concede before anyone asks" — about 200 words. The points I should give away in the first five minutes so that I am not forced into them later, and how to concede them without conceding the proposal.

4. "The reply that has to land" — about 250 words. The single response on which the proposal stands or falls, what it has to establish, and how it fails if it is delivered badly.

5. "The question I am hoping nobody asks" — about 200 words. My own guess is that it concerns whether the agency's soft ruin state — the fact that a cycle with no visible successes invites a budget cut that forecloses later cycles — is a feature of the world, which belongs in the model, or a feature of our attitudes, which is then just being relocated into r. Tell me if that is the right thing to fear and, if it is, what I should say if it comes anyway. Override my guess if you think something else is worse for me.
