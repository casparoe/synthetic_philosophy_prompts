Two people in an epistemology reading group I sit in on got stuck on the Meno problem — why, if at all, knowledge is better than mere true belief — and asked me to referee. The exchange went roughly as follows.

1. Ana opens with a swamping argument: hold fixed that the belief is true, and compare someone who formed it by a reliable method with someone who guessed and happened to be right. The truth is the same and the practical payoff is the same, so there is nothing left for reliability to contribute. Whatever value reliability has is value it has in advance of the outcome; once the true belief is in hand, that value is swamped. So knowledge is not more valuable than mere true belief.

2. Ben replies that the argument presupposes a single scale of value, on which truth contributes a fixed quantity and reliability has to be added to it. Knowledge might instead be valuable in a different way: as an achievement, a case of getting things right through one's own competence rather than by luck. Achievements are not valued merely as producers of good outcomes, so a ledger of outcome-value cannot register what is at issue.

3. Ana says that if the surplus value is credit for competence, it should appear wherever competence is exercised — a detective who reasons impeccably but is defeated by an improbable coincidence exercises exactly the same competence and deserves the same credit, yet does not know. So the credit account attaches its value to something other than knowledge, and therefore fails to answer the question actually asked, which is what knowledge has that true belief lacks.

4. Ben concedes the point about credit but relocates the difference: the comparison should not be confined to a single proposition. Knowledge is fertile — someone who knows that p can typically also say why p, what follows from p, and what p rules out, whereas the lucky guesser's true belief is a dead end. That is a real difference between knowledge and mere true belief that survives holding the truth of p fixed.

5. Ana answers that fertility is forward-looking, so what it values is the method or the agent's dispositions, not the state of knowing; Ben has switched to the easier question why it is good to be reliable. Worse, fertility can be simulated: imagine someone who, by sheer luck, holds true beliefs about p and about every question in its neighbourhood, and who will never be asked another question on the topic. If Ben says she is missing something, he has smuggled back the process-based value he just gave up; if he says she is not, then fertility does no work at all.

6. Ben responds that the case is rigged: it stipulates away every respect in which the difference could ever show up, and then objects that no difference shows up. The Meno question, he says, is not an autopsy of two states in a single frozen case but a question about what inquiry should aim at — and inquiry can only aim at what an inquirer can try to produce, whereas nobody can try to guess correctly by luck. So knowledge is the right target even if its advantage never appears as an additive bonus.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the six, say whether it is clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken, and briefly why. Then say who has the better of the argument overall, and what, if anything, both parties are missing — in particular whether they are even asking the same question.
