I'm in the last stretch of a theory PhD — complexity, plus a bit of crypto — and I've somehow been handed a small no-strings fellowship for next year, call it nine months of real time once teaching is subtracted, where the only constraint is that I have to be able to call whatever I do "foundational" with a straight face. I've decided I want to spend it on places where computational complexity genuinely changes a philosophical question instead of just decorating one. What pushed me here: last summer I worked on parameter selection, and every argument we made had the same shape — no known algorithm beats X on the structured instances an adversary would actually have to solve, and here's why you should believe that — and I got this creeping feeling that the jump from an asympototic worst-case classification of an infinite problem family to a claim about one particular finite instance was doing a huge amount of work that nobody could state precisely. Meanwhile a friend of mine does staff scheduling for hospitals, provably hard in general, and her solver eats the real instances for breakfast. So my half-baked organizing worry is that complexity results are denominated in worst-case classes and asymptotics while every actual instance any agent or institution ever faces is a single finite input, and those can come arbitrarily far apart.

Here's roughly what's on my board, so you can tell me what I'm missing rather than restate it back to me. One: what should a normative theory be sensitive to, if not worst-case asymptotic class — average case under some distribution, smoothed complexity, parameterized structure, or the single actual instance? And is there a principled answer, or does it split depending on whether the norm is there to guide action, to assess agents, or to design institutions? Two: ought-implies-can when the bottleneck is search rather than verification — if checking that a choice was optimal is feasible but finding it isn't, does the relevant "can" change, and does that do anything to blame and praise? Relatedly, shouldn't the bounded unit often be the institution rather than the individual, since courts, markets and labs spread computation across many people and long stretches of time? Three: equilibrium concepts — the standard solution concept in game theory is known to be computationally intractable in general, so what happens to its use as a prediction or as a normative benchmark: is that a deep problem for the theory or just evidence that it's the wrong solution concept? Four: whether the resource-bounded critique bites harder on hypothesis *search* than on deduction, i.e. the hard part of inquiry is which hypotheses to even entertain, which looks closer to abduction and to learning-theoretic limits than to NP-hardness. Five: the metaphysics-flavored end of things — whether the physical world "solves" hard problems, simulation arguments, that whole neighborhood — which I suspect is mostly confusion but can't yet say why.

Give me a ranked shortlist of three or four of these (including ones I haven't listed) that you'd actually spend the nine months on, and defend the ordering — I want the criteria and the tradeoffs, not "this is interesting." Concretely: which questions are upstream of which; which are genuinely tractable for one person who has reductions and crypto but only undergrad-level philosophy; which are neglected versus crowded; and, the part I care about most, which questions are still worth answering either way versus ones where the honest answer is "it depends" and nothing downstream actually moves. For each, also say what evidence, or what specific kind of result appearing in the next year or two, would push it up or down your list. Commit to one ordering and argue for it — don't hand me a menu of pros and cons and leave the choosing to me — and flag where you're going out on a limb as opposed to where you take there to be something like a consensus. I'm not asking for papers or a reading list, I can find those myself; I need the arguments and the prioritization.
