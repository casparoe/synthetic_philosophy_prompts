**Question 4 — The Löwenheim–Skolem theorems and Skolem's paradox (25 marks)**

(a) State the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem, with its hypotheses, and state Skolem's paradox in two sentences. [5]

(b) Explain why the paradox is not a formal contradiction. Your explanation must say what becomes of the bijection between ω and the power set of ω, and must address whether the standard explanation tacitly appeals to an absolute or 'external' standpoint from which the model is countable — and, if it does, whether that appeal sits badly with the use that is standardly made of the paradox. [10]

(c) A handout circulated in this seminar asserts: 'Once you have understood (b), you have understood the relativity of the set-theoretic concepts: the theorem establishes that no set is absolutely uncountable.' Assess this claim. Does the relativity conclusion follow from the theorem alone, or does it require a further premise? If a further premise is required, state it, and say whether the proof of the theorem gives any reason to accept it. Finally, does the upward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem matter to the argument one way or the other? [10]

**Student's answer**

*(a)* The downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem: if T is a first-order theory in a countable language and T has an infinite model, then T has a countable model. (The upward theorem adds that T then has models of every infinite cardinality.) Skolem's paradox is this: ZFC is a theory in the countable language whose only non-logical symbol is ∈; if it is consistent it has an infinite model and hence a countable model M; but ZFC proves that there is no bijection from ω onto P(ω), so M is a countable structure that satisfies the sentence 'P(ω) is uncountable'.

One caveat that matters below: the M delivered by the theorem is not in general transitive or well-founded, its membership relation is not the real ∈, and its ω need not be the real ω. The Mostowski collapse therefore cannot be applied to it, and nothing I say assumes that it can be.

*(b)* There is no formal contradiction, because the sentence that M satisfies is not the sentence that we assert. Satisfaction is defined by restricting the quantifiers to the domain of the structure, so what M satisfies is the relativization to M of 'there is no bijection from ω onto P(ω)', and that is a different sentence from the original: it says that no such bijection is an element of M. What we assert from outside is only that a bijection exists in V. Claims about different sentences cannot contradict one another. Put concretely: since M's domain is countable, the objects that M calls ω and P(ω) are at most countable as seen from V, so a bijection f between them does exist in V — ZFC itself proves that it does — but f lies in V and not in M, and the sentence quantifies only over the bijections that are in M.

As for the appeal to an external standpoint, it is harmless. 'M is countable' is elliptical for 'M is countable in V', and a relativist about the set-theoretic notions can index countability to a background universe exactly as she indexes it everywhere else.

*(c)* The handout is right, and this follows from the theorem alone. Uncountability is not absolute: whatever model of ZFC you start with, the downward theorem yields a countable model of the very same sentences, so the axioms are satisfied both by structures in which a set the axioms call uncountable is counted and by structures in which it is not, and the theory itself supplies no way of choosing between them. Hence 'x is uncountable' can only ever mean 'x is uncountable in M', and since the first-order axioms cannot single out one model as the intended one there is no further fact of the matter. Nothing is absolutely uncountable.

The upward theorem raises no parallel worry, and treating the two directions differently is not ad hoc. Passing to a larger model adds sets and functions but removes none, so it cannot turn an uncountable set into a countable one; the downward theorem, by contrast, drops the bijection, and that is what does the work in the paradox. The downward direction is therefore the significant one.
