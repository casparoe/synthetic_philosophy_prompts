My thesis, roughly: the no-free-lunch theorems get read in two ways, both of which I think are wrong. Reading one is the boring reading: of course you need an inductive bias, everyone has known that since forever, nothing to see. Reading two is the practical reading: it's a caution about overfitting, use cross-validation, done. I want to argue they're actually the sharpest formal version we have of Hume's problem of induction, and specifically that they undercut a move formal epistemologists make constantly — the move of answering Hume with convergence results. You know the move: sure, priors are subjective, but given enough data our posteriors converge and opinions merge, so the subjectivity washes out and induction is vindicated after all. My claim is that all those convergence/merging results need the truth to sit inside the support of the prior with positive probability, and that requirement is just the uniformity of nature assumption relocated into the prior rather than dissolved. And what NFL adds is the bit Hume couldn't say: it tells you the failure mode is not graceful degradation. Averaged over all environments every algorithm does exactly as well as every other one, so you don't get induction approximately works, you get a flat zero. There is no fallback position where induction is 60% reliable. So the problem isn't that we need one extra premise; it's that the premise is carrying the entire epistemic load and there's nothing underneath it.

The move I'm most worried about, and the one I think is actually new (maybe naively): I distinguish uniformity as a modal constraint — crossing possible worlds off the list — from uniformity as a measure over worlds, i.e. a distribution that weights them. I want to say Hume's real problem is about measure, not modality, and that this is why the future resembles the past sounds like something you could satisfy by narrowing the space of candidate worlds when actually you can't; narrowing the space doesn't tell you how to spread credence over what's left, and NFL is what happens when you spread it flat. I've been calling it the support-versus-weight point. I'll probably also use the little toy example where the universe lasts two days and is sunny or cloudy each day, so there are four histories and every prediction rule scores 0.5, to make the flatness vivid early.
