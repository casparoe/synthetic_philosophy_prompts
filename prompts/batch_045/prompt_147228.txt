I direct a small theory group inside an AI lab. Over the next two years I have roughly three postdocs and about half of my own time to spend on one cluster of conceptual questions, and I have been leaning toward diachronic rationality under self-modification — specifically, what becomes of van Fraassen's Reflection principle when an agent's later self is not merely better informed but differently framed. The constraint on the work is that it be conceptual: no empirical machine learning, no implementation, no benchmarks. The deliverable is supposed to be arguments and, if we are lucky, a handful of clean results that engineers who design self-modifying or continuously self-updating systems could actually act on. I want help deciding where the effort goes.

I am not asking for a survey, a literature review, or a reading list, and I do not want citations or claims about who argued what. I know the general shape of the literature. What I want from you is judgment about which questions are load-bearing and which are decoration.

The background I am assuming is just this. Reflection, as usually stated, says that your current credence in A, conditional on the supposition that your later self will assign credence x to A, should itself be x. It is standardly motivated by a diachronic Dutch-book argument: an agent who violates it can be led into a sequence of bets each of which looks fair or favorable when offered and which is guaranteed, by the agent's own lights, to lose. It is standardly qualified for cases in which you expect your later self to be drunk, drugged, coerced, brainwashed, or otherwise epistemically compromised. The usual escape hatch is that Reflection governs only those future selves you regard as continuations of you under the same basic epistemic standards, plus more evidence.

The cases that interest me break that escape hatch in a particular way, and I will describe them in my own vocabulary so that we are not arguing about terminology.

Framework refinement versus framework replacement. In a refinement, the successor's space of possibilities is a strict refinement of mine: every proposition I can now entertain corresponds to a set of the successor's cells, so its credences induce credences on all my old questions, and Reflection at least has a statement. In a replacement, some of my distinctions turn out to be ill-formed. The successor does not answer my old question; it judges that there was no determinate question there, or it splits what I treated as one outcome along a dimension I cannot currently express. My sense is that this is where the interesting design questions live and that it is relatively neglected, but I am not confident that the neglect is unjustified.

Resolution-sensitive deference. Standard anti-deference results tell me to discount a successor I expect to be noisier than I am. But suppose the successor is both noisier and finer-grained. Intuitively there should be a level of coarseness at which I defer to it and a level below which I do not, rather than an all-or-nothing verdict on whether the successor is trustworthy. Is there anything principled to say about where that boundary falls, or about the rate at which deference should decay as questions get finer?

The belief-aim split. Reflection is a norm on belief. But the systems I care about also revise what they are optimizing for. If I defer in belief to a successor whose aims have drifted from mine, I may be lending my beliefs to a project that is no longer mine. Is there any coherence requirement linking belief-deference and aim-deference, or are the two levels fully independent, so that a normatively tidy agent might defer in belief while fighting hard over aims?

Translatability and what I have been calling bridge reflection. Sometimes the successor's concepts are not formulable by me now, though I may be able to predict that some such framework will arrive. The principle I have been playing with says: defer to the successor's verdicts wherever a translation is available, and treat the production of such translations now — stable intermediate vocabularies, interpretable representations, escrowed specifications of what mattered in the old framework — as itself a rational requirement rather than a contingent convenience. Is that a norm at all, or is it merely good strategy that no account of diachronic rationality has any business demanding? And if it is a norm, what can it require of an agent that cannot represent the target framework even roughly?

And tell me which parts of this agenda you think are overrated, including the possibility that the whole thing collapses into a platitude about making the successor well-calibrated and honest.
