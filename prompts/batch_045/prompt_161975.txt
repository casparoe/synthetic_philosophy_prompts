Hey — I'm on the allocations committee for a limited-equity housing co-op, about 90 units, and we're the ones who decide who gets offered the next empty apartment. There's 7 of us, we meet one evening every month or two when a unit frees up (occasionally two at once), and the output has to be a full ranking of the shortlist, not just a winner: if the top pick turns us down the offer cascades to #2, then #3, so ties are no use to us at all.

Right now each of us writes down a ranking of everyone on the shortlist and we add up Borda scores. Last spring this bit us badly: someone added an extra applicant to the shortlist more or less as a joke — a guy with no income documentation who was never going to get the unit — and adding him moved a *different* applicant from 3rd to 1st. That feels like it shouldn't be possible, and it's made two of our members distrust the whole process. I've since read a bit of the lay explanations of Arrow's theorem, so I get the rough shape of the argument, but I need something I can actually run on a Tuesday night.

What I want from you: turn Arrow's result into a decision procedure our committee can use. Concretely:

- The questions to ask, in order, when we're picking or auditing a rule — starting with "what kind of input can we actually collect from seven volunteers" and ending with "which of Arrow's conditions are we willing to give up, and what does giving that one up cost us in practice."
- What counts as an adequate answer to each question, phrased so someone with no social choice theory can follow it; where the procedure just says no to a rule; and where it has to stop and hand the decision back to us because the question is a values call (which unfairness we'd rather live with) rather than a factual one.
- The two failure modes I now care about, stated as tests we can run on a spreadsheet in about ten minutes: (1) our ranking of the people already on a shortlist shouldn't change when someone obviously irrelevant gets added, or when the person we just housed gets taken off the top; (2) a committee member's ballot shouldn't be able to hurt their own top choice, and a member who stays home shouldn't be able to help theirs.
- How to handle partial ballots and recusals, since the theorem assumes everyone hands in a complete ranking and we often can't: people only rank their top five, or they know an applicant and refuse to rank them.
- Where the procedure genuinely can't give us an answer.

A detail that matters to me: the shortlist is drawn up by our membership coordinator, not by the seven of us, so if shortlist composition can swing the outcome then that person has real power none of us voted to give them. Please treat that as part of the problem, not a side note.

Then run the procedure on three cases:

(a) The Borda mess above, with the decoy applicant.
(b) Two units free at the same time in March — do we just take the top two off the ranking, or re-run the ballot on the remaining applicants, and does the difference actually matter?
(c) A proposal one member made: stop ranking applicants at all, have each of us place every household on a single 0–10 "housing need" scale and average the scores. I suspect this dodges the theorem somehow, and I want to know how, what it costs us, and whether it's really dodging anything or just relocating the problem somewhere we won't notice it.

Constraints: one evening, a laptop with a spreadsheet, no special software; two of our members are in their 70s and will not sit still for anything that can't be explained in about five minutes at the annual meeting; and our lawyer wants the rule written down and applied identically every time, which I think rules out "the chair decides when it gets messy."

I have a feeling the honest answer here is going to be something we don't want to hear — maybe that we should let one person decide, or that the ballot is doing less work than we pretend — and if that's where this goes, say it plainly and tell us what we'd be buying. And be honest about confidence throughout: for each part of the procedure say how sure you are that it's a faithful operationalization of what the theorem actually says, and single out the bit you're least sure of.
