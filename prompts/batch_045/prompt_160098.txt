Odd request, but it feels like the sort of thing you'd be good at. I'm a long-time LessWrong/ACX reader — read the Sequences as they went up, once sat through forty minutes at a meetup of two people arguing about whether you can be a consequentialist and still have a favourite colour — and a few of us from a local reading group have been plugging away at Aristotle on money and exchange for the last month or so. Two bits keep coming back and we've definately failed to settle them. One is the business in book V of the Nicomachean Ethics, where he runs the builder-and-shoemaker example with the diagonal proportion, says that what really holds a community together in exchange is need rather than money, and treats coin as a conventional stand-in for need whose job is partly to guarantee that you can get what you want later even if you don't want it now. The other is Politics I, where he splits household management, which has a natural limit set by self-sufficiency, from wealth-getting, which doesn't, calls the retail trading branch unnatural, and then reserves something close to genuine contempt for lending at interest: money was brought into being for exchange, it doesn't breed, so interest is money born of money and is the most unnatural form of acquisition there is. We tried tabooing 'natural' and it got worse, not better.

So: I want sustained satire out of this, and I want it to be funny to someone who actually knows the texts rather than someone who skimmed a summary. Write me a deadpan parody of a UK government consultation — call for evidence, draft ministerial foreword, numbered questions, an impact assessment, whatever the genre needs — put out by some department or other (a Ministry for Natural Acquisition, a joint Treasury/DBT exercise, your call) that takes Aristotle's position as settled policy and works out what implementing it would actually involve. The proposals have to follow his logic rather than a made-up one: if need is the real measure behind coin, then prices shouldn't be left to the market but certified against need, so there's some authority fixing exchange ratios in an official unit — I keep imagining a Shoes-per-House Unit, or whatever the diagonal construction would actually require — with a worked example applying it to something contemporary, a plumber and a software engineer, say, or a landlord and a tenant. And if money is barren and interest is unnatural generation, then interest-bearing debt is the thing to be regulated away: licences, a register of sterility certificates, a punitive rate, follow it through. Nobody in the document should ever say 'this is absurd'; the comedy comes from earnest officials costing out the administrative consequences of a view they treat as obvious, and from exaggerating a real feature of the position rather than inventing one. Where there's an actual tension in the material — money as a conventional token that guarantees future exchange versus money as intrinsically sterile, or wealth-getting being unlimited versus household management having a natural ceiling at self-sufficiency — let the consultation spot it and resolve it with a straight face in a way that makes the tension worse rather than better.

Aim for 700-900 words: short ministerial foreword, a handful of numbered clauses or proposal headings, a 'who is affected' box, and four or five consultation questions with the department's preferred answer quietly indicated. British English throughout, please — 'honours', 'licence', 'programme', single inverted commas for quoted speech, that sort of thing. And don't have anyone in it win an argument against Aristotle; the joke should land without anyone winking at the reader about how silly the whole thing is.
