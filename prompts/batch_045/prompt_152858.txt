Having retired some years ago from a career in experimental physics, I have lately been attending a philosophy reading group at the local university, and last week's session was on Zeno's dichotomy. Two of the members, Malcolm and Ruth, fell into a disagreement that I was asked to settle and could not. I should be most grateful for your help with it.

Malcolm opened by saying that the paradox is simply dissolved by the arithmetic plus the right account of motion. The distances 1/2, 1/4, 1/8 and so on have a finite sum, the corresponding times do too, and there is, on the account he favours (the one that identifies motion with nothing over and above being at various places at various times, sometimes called the "at-at" account), no further fact about "getting through" the sub-journeys that needs explaining. Zeno's mistake, he said, was to conflate an infinite number of parts with an infinite total magnitude. (1)

Ruth replied that this changes the subject. Summing a series tells you where the limit lies, not that the sequence of sub-journeys is ever *completed*; on every reading of the paradox that has real bite, the worry is about whether an unending sequence of acts with no last member can be finished at all, and that worry is untouched by the convergence of a series. The at-at account, she said, does not answer that worry but legislates it out of existence, and it says nothing about the reversed version of the argument, in which there is no *first* sub-journey to begin with. (2)

At that point I mentioned, as a bystander, a variant I had been turning over since the previous session, and it became the centre of the argument. Suppose the runner must not only cover the successive half-distances but also pass a turnstile at each of the points 1/2, 3/4, 7/8 and so on, each turnstile costing a fixed, non-shrinking delay τ — a minimum re-setting time that does not get smaller as the stages get smaller. The turnstiles accumulate only at the far end, so nothing prevents the runner from setting off; but the total time is now the convergent sum of the traversal times plus the divergent sum of the delays, and the runner never arrives. Ruth seized on this: here, she said, is a case with exactly the same task structure — the same ω-sequence of sub-journeys, the same absence of a last stage, the same limit point — which everyone agrees is impossible, and the only thing that distinguishes it from the original is the arithmetic of the durations. So the paradox was never about infinite divisibility, or about completing an infinite sequence, at all; it was always and only about whether the times sum, and the metaphysical apparatus is idle. (3)

Malcolm gave two answers. First, that the difference between the two cases is not bookkeeping but structural: in the original, for every positive ε there is a stage after which the entire remaining business fits within ε, so the infinite tail is negligible, whereas in my variant it is not; that is a fact about order and measure together, and it is precisely what the convergence reply was pointing at. Secondly, that my variant is not the same journey made harder but a different journey altogether: I have stipulated a worldline with infinitely many pauses accumulating at the end, so of course there is no time of arrival, and nothing follows about the ordinary case. (4)

Ruth's response was that negligible tails just is convergence restated, so Malcolm's first answer concedes her point; and that his second answer cuts against him, since the accumulation of stages at the endpoint is a feature of the original as well. She added a further observation: in the turnstile case the runner is not blocked anywhere either — for every distance short of the end there is a time at which he is beyond it, so he passes every point of the interval short of the destination and still never arrives. Hence, she concluded, "traversing" cannot mean what Malcolm needs it to mean. (5)

They then both turned to me, and I had nothing useful to say.

Would you go through the exchange move by move? For each of the five moves I should like your judgement on how far it succeeds, from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification, and then an overall verdict on who has the better of the argument and on what, if anything, both of them are missing. I have deliberately not said where my own sympathies lie, and I would rather you did not simply tell me what the standard secondary literature supposedly says; my own sense is that physicists are apt to wave the convergent series about far too cheerfully, and I want to know whether that suspicion is fair.
