**Question 3 (25 points, aim for about 700 words).** A forensic lab works with two propositions all year:

- (H1) the chain-of-custody log entries correspond to actual transfers of evidence;
- (H2) the DNA kit lot used in the case was contaminated.

In January the lab treats H1 as standing fast: nobody in the lab offers or asks for grounds for it, and it is what disputes about the results are measured against. H2, by contrast, is an ordinary empirical hypothesis, something the audit confirms or disconfirms. By December, after the audit, the roles have swapped: H2 is treated as standing fast and H1 has become something the lab runs tests on and gives reasons for and against. Assume the lab's aims, techniques and accreditation standards are unchanged — it is doing the same thing in December that it was doing in January. Call a proposition *migratory* (my coinage — you don't need to know any literature under that name) when it moves, within a single practice whose aims are unchanged, from standing fast to being an ordinary hypothesis open to confirmation, or back.

(a) 8 points. Explain, without slogans, the difference between Moore's treatment of his hand example and the hinge treatment of the same sort of sentence. Then explain why the hinge treatment isn't just foundationalism with a fancier name.

(b) 9 points. Take the hinge account as a *role* account: what makes a proposition a hinge is the job it plays in a practice, not its content, its subject matter, or how confident anyone feels. Say whether such an account can accommodate migration as I've defined it, and argue for one side. If you say it can, say what follows for the claim that hinge certainty is a species of necessity.

(c) 8 points. Here is an objection: if something that stood fast can end up withdrawn because of recalcitrant experience, then hinge epistemology is just coherentism with extra steps — everything revisable in principle, everything answerable to the system. Give the strongest reply available to a hinge epistemologist, and then the strongest reply to your own reply.

Here's one student's answer, pasted exactly as it came in:

---

**(a)** Moore thinks that when he holds up his hands and says he knows they are hands, he is stating a piece of knowledge, and that this is enough to refute the skeptic, whose argument he takes to rest on the false claim that he doesn't know it. The hinge reading says Moore has misdescribed what is going on. The sentence is not functioning as an empirical report for which grounds can be given and demanded. It is functioning as one of the propositions that have to stand fast if anything is to count as giving grounds at all. That is why the proof strikes us as empty: he is offering as a bit of knowledge something whose job is not to be known. This is also why it isn't foundationalism. A basic belief in foundationalism is still inside the justificatory structure: it is supposed to be true, to be justified without being justified by other beliefs, and to pass support on to the rest. A hinge is not in that structure at all. You don't infer from it, you proceed in accordance with it; it is closer to a rule of the game than to a premise. So the certainty of a hinge is not doxastic certainty, the property a belief has when it is very well supported or held with maximal confidence. It is the standing-fast of something that neither needs nor admits of grounds.

**(b)** Migration is compatible with the role account, and the lab case shows why. What makes H1 a hinge isn't its content — there's no syntactic or subject-matter test for hinge-hood — but the job it does: the log is what identifications are measured against, not something the lab measures. Nothing in the role account says that assignment is permanent, so there is no contradiction in H1 and H2 swapping places. And that is just Wittgenstein's own view about forms of life: which propositions stand fast is fixed by the form of life we share, and adopting a form of life is not a theoretical matter. Carnap made the same point about frameworks — the external question of which framework to adopt is a practical decision rather than a matter of truth, while the internal questions get settled once you're inside. So a migration is nothing more than the community landing on a different practical decision, and once it has, the new hinges simply are the ones that stand fast. As for necessity: there is no absolute necessity here at all. Hinge certainty is necessity relative to a framework, and since the framework is adopted by decision, the necessity is inherited from that decision and is nothing more than that.

**(c)** The objection is that if experience can get us to give a hinge up, we are just doing coherentism: beliefs adjusted against each other, all of them revisable. My reply: no hinge is ever overturned by evidence. Evidence can only bear on a proposition that is already inside the space of reasons, and a hinge isn't. What happens in the migration case is that the proposition stops being a hinge *first*, and only then can evidence get a grip on it. So the direction of explanation is the reverse of the skeptic's: the evidence doesn't defeat the hinge, the demotion of the hinge is what lets the evidence in. What demotes it is simply the community ceasing to be certain of it: hinge status is maximal, socially distributed confidence, and migration is what happens when confidence in a proposition falls below the threshold, or, in H2's case, rises above it. That is what keeps this from collapsing into coherentism. Coherentism has every belief supported by other beliefs, whereas here the standing-fast propositions are held with certainty but supported by nothing, and the revisable ones do all of the adjusting. The best reply to my reply: it is hard to say in non-metaphorical terms what a proposition's stopping being a hinge amounts to, and if the answer is that confidence fell, I haven't really explained anything. And the skeptic will add that if hinges can be demoted, the only difference left between us is that he revises quickly and I revise slowly.
