Hi. Bit of context first so you know who you're talking to. I'm a retired hydrologist — thirty-odd years of measuring things in rivers and arguing about whether the numbers meant anything — and over the last couple of years I've got into philosophy of science properly for the first time, mostly out of stubbornness after a friend told me science obviously "gets at the truth". So I've got the rough shape of the Duhem-Quine sort of point, and the worry that the alternatives we're able to list today may not include the one that turns out to be right, and the difference between a theory being true and it just saving the appearances. But I'm self-taught, I can't look anything up, and I don't want you to either: no reading lists, no "as So-and-so argues", no dates or titles I'd have to check. Give me the argument in plain words, as if to someone competent who happens to have no schooling in it. No web access here, so anything you'd need to verify is something you should leave out.

The reason I need it is practical. I chair the evidence panel of a small catchment trust. Twice a year we recieve fifteen to twenty short proposals from local groups — anglers, parish councils, school groups, a couple of retired types like me — asking for up to about £12,000 for monitoring or restoration work, and they come with whatever data they've already collected. I get roughly twenty-five minutes per proposal. Two of the five panel members are lay trustees who have to be able to follow my reasoning, and we publish a one-paragraph rationale for every decision, which gets appealed, so it has to stand up in writing without leaning on my authority.

The problem I keep hitting is always the same shape. A group turns up with honest, carefully collected data and a story about what's causing the pattern, and the data simply doesn't separate that story from three or four others that would fit it just as well. I want to stop writing "the evidence is a bit thin" and start being able to say what kind of thin, and what would thicken it, and whether it's worth spending trust money to find out.

So: give me a decision procedure my panel can actually run. Ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, a clear stopping point where the answer is no, and a clear hand-off where the remaining question is one of values rather than evidence and the trustees have to decide it. A flowchart in words is fine. Ground it in whatever is actually known about the underdetermination of theory by evidence, but keep only the distinctions a practitioner genuinely can't do without — and tell me which ones you've dropped and what I lose by dropping them. Be honest about which steps are judgement calls wearing a lab coat.

There are specific hard cases it has to handle, because these are the ones that make me stall:

One. Rivals that fit the data by construction. I can always cook up a story that fits any dataset if I'm allowed enough epicycles, and I don't want a procedure that tells me to take those seriously. But I also don't want the rule to be "prefer the story you were already inclined to", which is what my gut does. How do I tell a serious rival from a contrived one without smuggling in my prior?

Two. Two stories that disagree about the mechanism but imply exactly the same thing to do about it. My instinct is that this is underdetermination I should stop caring about, since the money goes to the action either way. Is that right, or am I throwing away something I need?

Three. What I've started calling chameleon claims, for want of a better name: whatever the numbers come out as, the group has a story ready — counts go up, it worked; counts go down, the lag hasn't elapsed yet; flat, we've halted the decline. Is that a species of underdetermination or a different disease entirely, and should the procedure treat it the same way?

Four. Alternatives nobody has thought of yet. The procedure obviously can't list them. What's the best proxy a practitioner can use for the risk that the whole space of options on the table is wrong?

Five. "We can't tell yet" versus "no evidence could ever tell the difference". Does my panel need to care which of those it's in, given we're deciding now and can't wait?

Six, and I think this is the big one for us: the case where the effect itself is well supported but the explanation isn't. I suspect we should often fund the claim that something is happening and decline to fund the claim about why, and I want the procedure to make that a distinct output rather than a fudge.

Then run the thing on three proposals, so I can see it work.

The first: a volunteer group with three years of fortnightly phosphate readings and monthly fish counts at ten sites on one tributary, wanting money for fence-and-buffer work, on the claim that run-off from a particular farm is causing the decline in brown trout. The correlation is real and the data collection is decent, but I can think of four other stories off the top of my head, including one where there's no real decline at all and the volunteers just got better at counting.

The second: a before-after-control-impact study on a re-meandered reach, two years either side, one restored stretch and one unrestored control, invertebrate diversity up about thirty per cent in the restored stretch and flat in the control. Much better design than the first one. Tell me whether it's actually better under the procedure, and what the cheapest single measurement would be that would knock out the remaining rivals. I should declare that I advised this group informally and unpaid, so it's the one I'm most inclined to wave through.

The third: a proposal to plant three kilometres of bankside trees for shade, to protect salmon from high summer temperatures. The physics and the fish physiology are not in doubt, there's decent regional air-temperature data and a model, but there's no water-temperature data from this river at all and I genuinely don't know where the cold-water refuges on it actually are. So the underdetermination here is about which stretch, not about whether shade cools water. If your procedure says no to something I'd have funded on instinct, or yes to something I'd have turned down, say so plainly — I'd rather find out from you than from an appeal.

Finally: the procedure can't do everything, and I want that written down too, especially the places where underdetermination is real and irreducible and we still have to spend the money, because we can't not decide.
