I serve on the four-person grants committee of a foundation whose charter tells us to do the most good per dollar over the long run. We meet monthly, review about twenty-five proposals per cycle, can spend no more than twenty minutes and one page on any single one, and must attach to each recommendation a short written rationale for a board whose members are split on whether the long-run framing is sound at all. The proposals that repeatedly stall us are the ones whose case is astronomically large rather than probable: one asks for $250,000 for technical work on a nascent technology, justified by a one-in-ten-thousand chance of steering a civilisation away from a bad trajectory whose stakes are set by something like 10^30 future lives, many of them digital; one asks for the same sum for work aimed only at the qualitative character of a future that may be endless in time or space, where the case is that any nonzero chance of affecting an unlimited future outweighs anything with merely finite effects, and where the probability resists any number; one asks for the same sum for a program with well-measured, near-certain benefits to a few thousand people alive now, which looks negligible beside either of the others.

Please turn what formal decision theory and population ethics have to say about astronomical and infinite stakes into a procedure my committee can actually run in twenty minutes without any formal training: the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as an answer to each, the point at which we stop and record the proposal as unrankable rather than forcing a number, and the points where what remains is a question of values, or of what our board will ratify, and must be handed to a person instead of settled by arithmetic. Centre it on a device of your own choosing — a cap on how large a stake may be entered into the ranking, a probability threshold below which a proposal is set aside, a split between a ranked budget and an exploratory budget, or something better — and say plainly which failure the device is there to prevent. It must cope with a proposal that is best under one credible model of the far future and worst under another, with an arithmetic that wants to hand nearly the whole cycle to one speculative grant, and with the case where our intuition and the arithmetic conflict and we cannot tell which of the two is the faulty input.

Then run the finished procedure on the three proposals above, including at least one where it returns an answer my committee will not want, and close by saying what the procedure cannot do for us.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence: the single rule that settles most cases, stated so that I could read it aloud to the board. Then give the procedure, the applications, and the limits, with no preamble and no restatement of this brief.
