Two colleagues of mine at a policy institute are drafting a one-page brief on whether the world is becoming more or less violent, and I sat in on their meeting. To keep the argument from collapsing into a fight about data, they agreed to argue over a stylized case instead: suppose that across the period in question the annual rate of violent death per head of population has fallen by roughly half, while the population has roughly quadrupled, so that the absolute number of violent deaths per year has roughly doubled; and suppose also that deaths from disease have fallen faster still, so that violent deaths now make up a larger share of all deaths than they once did. They also agreed to set aside any disputes about what the real numbers actually are. Here is how the exchange went.

1. Ines: The headline has to be about risk to a person, not totals. The question the brief is answering, if it is meant to matter to anyone, is whether a given individual is safer than she used to be, and on that question the answer is yes: her chance of dying violently in a given year has halved. Absolute totals are just the rate multiplied by the number of people, so a rise in totals can be produced by population growth alone, in a world where every single person has become twice as safe. Using totals means registering "more people now exist" as an increase in violence, which is a fact about how many of us there are, not about how violent the world is.

2. Malik: The rate/total distinction is real, but framing it as "risk to a person" quietly assumes that risk is the only thing the brief could be about. The total is the sum of the harms, and if the badness of violence aggregates across victims — if five thousand violent deaths is worse than two thousand, other things equal — then a world with more violent deaths contains more violence even though each person in it is safer. You can think the per-person rate is the right measure of safety and still think the total is the right measure of how much violence there is. Those are different questions, and the brief's title names the second one.

3. Ines: If totals are what we track, then a country that becomes perfectly peaceful and has a baby boom counts as becoming more violent, and an epidemic that kills ten million people counts as reducing violence, because it shrinks the population that later dies violently. Something has clearly gone wrong. The general point: totals are fine when the population is held fixed, but the moment you compare across time with a changing population, the total fuses two separate things — how violent the world is, and how many people are in it — and there is no way to read off which one moved.

4. Malik: Agreed that raw totals are contaminated by population size. But I don't think the repair is the annual per-capita rate either. People don't die of nothing: if disease deaths collapse, the same homicide rate means that a much larger share of everyone who dies dies violently, and a much larger share of life-years lost is lost to violence. If what we care about is violence as a feature of a human life — what a life in this world is exposed to — then the natural denominators are all deaths and all life-years, not all persons per year. On that way of measuring, the world can be getting more violent at the same time as the annual per-capita rate falls by half.

5. Ines: I think we've located the actual problem: the denominator is doing all the work, and there are at least four different quantities in play — annual deaths per person, annual deaths in total, violent deaths as a share of all deaths, and the lifetime risk that a given person will die violently. They can move in opposite directions at once. And the last one is not just the annual rate scaled up: if people live much longer, then half the annual risk spread over twice the lifespan leaves lifetime risk roughly where it started. So "the world is becoming less violent" isn't one claim awaiting a verdict; it is a family of claims, and the annual per-capita rate happens to be the member of the family that is easiest to measure, which is not a reason to think it is the one the brief means.

6. Malik: I'll grant that they can diverge. Where I part company is with the thought that we can then go looking for the right one, because the choice among them is a moral choice rather than a discovery — and the same is true of the numerator, which is worse. Do we count only intentional interpersonal killing, or also deaths that were foreseen but not aimed at, or the killing of animals for food, which by number is far larger than any human category, or non-lethal violence, or coercion and threats that never produce an injury? Each of those inclusions can flip a trend. And there is no theory-neutral way to decide what goes in. So I'd put it this way: there is no fact of the matter about whether the world is becoming less violent; there are only facts of the matter relative to an operationalization, and the brief should say exactly that and stop.

7. Ines: The "no fact of the matter" line is too fast. Whatever else is true, a massacre involves more violence than a shove, so the concept isn't infinitely plastic, and that shows the inclusions aren't all up for grabs. The better version of your point is a test: if every admissible way of making the question precise points the same direction, then there is a fact of the matter and we should assert it flatly; if they diverge, then the honest claim is a conditional one. That converts a metaphysical standoff into an empirical question about a class of measures, which is a question we can actually make progress on.

8. Malik: The robustness test just moves the fight to the word "admissible," and that class is gerrymandered by whoever assembles it. It will contain the kinds of violence we have long-run series for — homicide, battle deaths — and omit the ones we don't: coercive control, psychological harm, violence against animals, the slow violence of neglect. So "robust across admissible measures" will reliably return the answer that the well-measured kinds are declining, which is close to where we started. And robustness across measures still doesn't settle two things. First, severity: fewer incidents that are each far worse is not obviously less violence. Second, distribution: a decline averaged over everyone is compatible with a rise for whoever already bears most of it, and "the world" getting less violent while the worst-off quarter gets more violent is not the headline I want to sign. There is also a problem neither of us has touched, which is not about concepts at all: some of any measured rise in the neglected categories may be better recording rather than more violence, and that is an evidential problem masquerading as a conceptual one.

They have to hand in one page by Friday and they asked me to referee. I couldn't tell who was winning and I still can't.
