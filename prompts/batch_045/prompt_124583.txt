I sit on my county's Hazard Review Board as one of two members without a science background. We advise the county commission on whether to allow things that nobody can yet prove are safe, and over the past two years I have voted to block some and to allow others in a pattern I cannot state as a rule. The nearest I have got to a rule is: hold back when waiting is cheap and the damage cannot be undone afterwards. That gets some of my votes right and, as far as I can tell, cannot distinguish two of them from each other.

Here are the votes, as close as I can give them honestly.

1. A new active ingredient for treating the county's playing fields. The state regulator has approved it. The only worry is one university lab's finding of liver changes in rats dosed at something like a hundred times anything a child on the field would get. The alternative product costs roughly three times as much and the parks budget is already cut to the bone. I voted to hold off one season. I know the rat study is thin and I know the budget cost is real, and I would vote the same way again.

2. A university team wanted to release a couple of kilograms of reflective particles from a tethered balloon over the desert east of us, purely to test whether their instruments can detect the plume at all. The objection was that this is the first step toward dimming the sun. I cannot see a mechanism by which this release hurts anyone, and if we are wrong we find out immediately and the material is gone in an afternoon. I voted to allow, and I think that was plainly right — but I do not know why it is plainly right while case 1 was plainly a hold.

3. A company asked to store a solvent it has only just begun making in a deep aquifer beneath the county's drinking water wells. Our hydrogeologist says that if it migrates it will take twenty to forty years to reach the wells, that by then nothing can be done about it, and that our monitoring would tell us almost nothing until it arrived. I voted to block. This is my clearest case, and I think the reason is that the harm would not announce itself: no early signal, no chance to change course. In cases 1 and 2 we would get one.

4. A hospice asked permission to let patients with months to live take a drug never approved here, imported from a country where it is sold for something else. No efficacy data, unknown side effects. I voted to allow, on the grounds that here the waiting is what does the damage. But this makes me suspicious of my own rule, because it means the rule has to be able to point backwards at what we are already doing, not only forwards at what somebody wants to start.

5. A residents' group petitioned us to suspend fluoridation of the county water, on the basis of observational studies of children in places where fluoride occurs naturally at levels several times ours. I voted to keep fluoridating. What bothers me is that I cannot see that this evidence is worse than the rat study that moved me in case 1; if anything the human data is more relevant. The difference I keep returning to is that people have been drinking this water for seventy years, and if it were doing anything on the scale the petitioners fear I think we would have noticed by now. That is an absence-of-evidence point that cuts in case 5 but is simply unavailable in case 1, because the pesticide has no history. Is that a real epistemological difference, or only a bias toward whatever we happen to have been doing?
6. A ski area wanted to open expert-only terrain with genuine avalanche exposure. The people taking the risk are the people buying the ticket, and the terrain can be closed the moment the snowpack looks wrong. I voted to allow.

7. A battery recycler wanted to build on the far side of the fence from a mobile home park. The county's air model puts that neighborhood's particulate exposure just over the state threshold. The jobs and the tax base accrue to the whole county. The residents I spoke to rent and cannot move. The company told us it will build in the next county if we say no — same airshed. I voted to block, and I said the company should have to show safety rather than us having to show harm. It seems to matter to me who is in the room and whether they can leave. It also seems to me that our blocking may buy nothing at all.

8. The one I suspect breaks any rule I could state. Twelve separate farms applied to spread treated sewage sludge in the same watershed. Each application is within state limits, each farmer has a real problem the sludge solves, and I would have voted yes on any one of them alone. But our lake is near a nutrient threshold, a wet year with all twelve permits live could flip it, and a lake that has flipped does not flip back. I voted to cap the total at six. I do not know how to justify restricting the twelve together when I would have approved each one separately, and if the honest answer is that I was irrational, I would rather be told.

9. A lab at the state university submitted a paper describing how to make a known avian influenza strain pass more easily between mammals. The benefits go to a few hundred researchers; the risk is spread over everyone; the probability is small; and a release could not be recalled. I voted to ask them not to publish in full. I do not know whether my reaction to very small probabilities of very large outcomes is something a principle should honor or something it should explain away.

The one I keep changing my mind about: a company that currently does grocery delivery by van wants to pilot small autonomous robots on the sidewalks of our main street, which includes a school crossing. If a robot hits a child we would know within a week and pull the plug, which seems to count for allowing it. But the pedestrians did not choose it and cannot avoid the sidewalk, and the vans it would partly replace kill people too, and we have never asked the van company a single question. I have gone back and forth four times.

What I would like from you: a principle, or at most two or three candidates, stated tightly enough that I could apply it to a permit I have not seen, with a note on which of my votes motivates each clause. In particular: (a) is the distinction I glimpsed between harms that announce themselves early enough to act on and harms that stay silent until they cannot be fixed doing any real work, or is it just case 3 in other words? (b) Is the who-bears-it-and-can-they-leave condition independent of that one, or does it collapse into it? (c) What does your principle say about the robots? (d) Which of my votes can it not accommodate, and what would it cost me to give them up?
