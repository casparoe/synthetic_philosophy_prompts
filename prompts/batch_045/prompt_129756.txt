I work in a memory and assistive-technology clinic, and several times a month our team faces variants of the same question: whether some external resource a patient depends on should count as part of that patient's own thinking, or merely as a tool they happen to use. The decision is not academic. If the resource is part of the patient's mind, then degrading or removing it looks like impairing a person rather than depriving them of property; if it is a mere tool, it does not. The same question arises in the opposite direction: when a relative supplies the missing half of a decision — the retrieval cue, the unfinished sentence, the standing set of values — should we score that as the patient's own decision-making capacity, or as someone else's influence on it?

Please turn the extended mind thesis into a decision procedure my team can actually run, in about fifteen minutes, on a real case. Take as raw material — and do not feel obliged to keep — these candidate conditions: the resource is reliably available when needed; its contents are easily accessible; it is trusted and taken at face value rather than checked against something else; consulting it requires no special effort; past reliance on it has become automatic; and the store is durable over time. Say which conditions you keep, which you drop, which you tighten, and which you demote to mere evidence rather than requirements; say what the team should do when a condition is only partly met or cannot be assessed in a quarter of an hour; and mark clearly the points where no amount of procedure removes the need for a value judgment, and who on the team has to make it.

One distinction I want handled explicitly, because I think we have been conflating three things that can come apart. First, whether the resource is a component of the cognitive system that actually produces the performance. Second, whether the resulting memory, belief, or action is attributable to the patient as theirs, in the way that matters for credit, responsibility, and consent. Third, whether interfering with the resource wrongs or harms the patient, and whether maintaining it falls under our duty of care. A resource could satisfy the first without settling the third, for instance if it is owned and remotely rewritable by a third party. Tell me how the procedure keeps these separate, and what it does when they disagree.

Then run it on three cases: a man with dense amnesia whose entire prospective and autobiographical memory lives in a cloud-synced voice assistant that his daughter and the manufacturer can both edit; a single jointly maintained notebook that both partners of a couple rely on for their shared life, where one partner now wants it destroyed; and a spouse who, during a capacity assessment, reliably supplies the cues and values the patient cannot retrieve. I expect to dislike the answer on the last one, so give it to me straight. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do — whether it settles legal capacity, personhood, or consent — and where a clinician should refuse to use it as if it did.
