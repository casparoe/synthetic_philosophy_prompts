I'm a software engineer — distributed systems, mostly — with no formal training in philosophy, and I'm writing up my position on the simulation argument for a small reading group of colleagues who work through one paper a month. Before I circulate it I'd like to know whether the set of things I believe actually holds together.

Here is roughly where I've landed:

1. I accept the trilemma form of the argument: if a nontrivial share of technologically mature civilizations end up running many large-scale ancestor simulations, then most observers are simulated, and I should count myself among them.
2. My actual credence that I am in a simulation is somewhere around five to ten percent.
3. I treat it as interesting but not actionable. I do think a simulation that gets shut down early ends the lives of everyone inside it and that this would be very bad, but I cannot name any action of mine whose expected value differs between the simulation hypothesis and base reality, so I don't let it change what I do.
4. I'm a functionalist about consciousness: a simulated mind running the right computation is genuinely conscious, and suffering inside a simulation is real suffering.
5. Relatedly, if we ever get the compute, I would be glad to work on building ancestor simulations ourselves. I don't think we would owe the simulated people anything, and I don't think it would be a moral problem.
6. I think the hypothesis is evidentially inert: any observation that seemed to disconfirm it could itself be part of the simulation, so no possible evidence could move my credence up or down.
7. On sampling, I think reasoning as if I were a random draw from the set of all observer-moments is legitimate, and I rely on it to take the argument seriously — but I also think the doomsday argument is junk.
8. On copies: I think that if an operator checkpoints a running simulation and re-instantiates it ten times, that does not create ten times as much experience, because duplication does not multiply consciousness — yet I still want to count those ten instances when I do the arithmetic about what most observers are. I think of this as the rollback problem, and I have never been sure my two verdicts about it are compatible.

I don't know which of these I would give up, if any; that is what I am asking you to find.

What I want is an audit rather than a lecture: which pairs genuinely pull against each other and how badly, which only look as though they do, and for each real tension what the cheapest repair would be and what it would cost me. Please assume I know the argument itself and don't restate it, and gloss any term of art in plain language.

Reply as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no preamble and no closing summary. Items 1 through 4 should run from the most severe tension to the least severe, each naming the commitments involved, saying how bad the conflict is, and saying what would have to give; item 5 should name the single revision that would clean up the most and what it costs me. If a pair I listed only appears to conflict, dispose of it in a clause inside one of the items rather than promoting it to an item of its own.
