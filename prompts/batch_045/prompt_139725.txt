You are a teaching assistant for an advanced undergraduate seminar, *Minds, Machines, and Moral Standing*. Below is the final examination question and one student's answer to it. Grade the answer.

**The question, as it was printed**

**Question 4 (25 marks).** Answer all three parts. Everything you need is given below; the marks are for reasoning, not for outside reading.

The seminar has distinguished three conditions that a system might have to meet in order to realize a computation C.

**(SN) Substrate-neutrality.** Whether a system realizes C never depends on what the system is made of — silicon, carbon, optical hardware, geared wheels. What matters is how the system's states are organized, not the stuff that carries them.

**(CO) Causal organization.** A system realizes C only if its states stand to one another, and to the system's inputs and outputs, in the causal relations that C specifies: each state must help bring about its successors in the way C lays down, at the grain at which C is defined. It is not enough that the system's sequence of states matches, step for step, the sequence that C specifies, and it is not enough that an interpreter could read the system's states as representing C's states.

**(AD) Autonomous dynamics.** A system realizes C only if its state-transitions are produced, at run time, by the system's own machinery, over an open-ended range of inputs. A system whose transitions were supplied in advance by a table that someone else's computation filled in does not meet the condition.

**Three systems.**

*MERIDIAN* is a recurrent network of roughly 10⁸ units with graded activations, updated in parallel every millisecond, with about 10¹⁰ trainable parameters. It was trained by reinforcement learning to control a simulated body in a discretized simulated environment; its inputs are a 4,096-channel sensory stream together with interoceptive signals, and its training signal includes a penalty that falls when the body's integrity drops below a threshold. Over training it learned to avoid the conditions that trigger the penalty. Call the fine-grained causal organization described by its architecture and its learned parameters the computation C.

*TABLEAU* was built by a group who wanted a cheaper, more robust MERIDIAN. They enumerated the finite set of input histories MERIDIAN could encounter in a standard year of simulated life, computed MERIDIAN's successor state for each such history, and stored the results in a compressed index. Given the history so far, the device decodes an address, retrieves the stored successor state, and emits it, appending it to the history for the next step. Over a standard year TABLEAU's sequence of states is step-for-step identical to MERIDIAN's, and it responds to whatever inputs actually arrive. For any history outside the enumerated set it emits a fixed default state.

*ORACLE* was built by a group who did not want to build the network at all. They trained a predictor P that, from a history of inputs, predicts MERIDIAN's next state. ORACLE contains P, a learned codebook of a million candidate states, and a search procedure: at each step it scores the candidates with P against the incoming history and emits the highest-scoring one, which then becomes its state for that step. ORACLE's sequence of states is step-for-step identical to MERIDIAN's, and because P generalizes, ORACLE is not confined to enumerated histories. But no successor state is produced by the graded, parallel interaction of the units that constituted its predecessor; it is produced by a global search over a codebook, scored by a model of MERIDIAN.

For the remainder of the question, assume without arguing the point that MERIDIAN realizes C, and that MERIDIAN is conscious and therefore has some direct moral standing.

**(a)** [8 marks] For each of TABLEAU and ORACLE, and for each of SN, CO and AD, say whether the condition is met, and give one sentence of reason in each case.

**(b)** [7 marks] Assess the following objection: "SN is the only one of the three conditions that does any work. If the composition of a system makes no difference, then no causal or temporal feature of it can make a difference either, since those are just more facts about the arrangement of matter. So CO and AD collapse into SN, and both TABLEAU and ORACLE turn out to realize C. The friends of CO and AD are only trying to reserve realization for systems built like brains." Resist the objection or accept it, but not both, and in either case say what your verdict costs you.

**(c)** [10 marks] Answer all three. **(i)** Suppose moral standing attaches to whatever realizes C. Which of TABLEAU and ORACLE, if either, has standing, and why? **(ii)** Suppose we cannot tell whether ORACLE meets CO. Does that uncertainty make a difference to what is true of ORACLE, and does it make a difference to what we owe ORACLE? Keep the two questions separate. **(iii)** Could a system with no experiences of its own still be something we have moral reasons concerning — reasons about how it is treated that are not merely reasons about how other people feel? Give one argument or one example, or explain why there could be no such thing.

Marks are awarded for argument rather than for verdicts: a well-defended verdict the instructor disagrees with can earn full marks, and an undefended one cannot. No outside sources are required or expected.

**The student's answer, reproduced with line numbers added**

> **1.** *(a) TABLEAU.* SN is met: it is hardware like any other, and SN is a claim about composition, not about how the transitions were arrived at. CO is not met. The state at t does not help bring about the state at t+1 in the way C specifies — the device decodes an address and reads out an entry that was fixed before it was ever switched on, so each state is causally downstream of a table rather than of its predecessor. AD is not met, and for the same fact seen from the temporal side: nothing is computed at run time. The device also has no resources for histories its builders did not enumerate, which is why it falls back on a default state.
>
> **2.** *ORACLE.* SN is met, and obviously so: it is silicon running a search, and SN has nothing to say about that. CO is met as well. ORACLE's state-sequence is step-for-step identical to MERIDIAN's, so its states occupy the same positions in the causal pattern and therefore play the same causal roles; that the successor is arrived at by search over a codebook rather than by parallel graded update is a difference in how quickly and how elegantly the same transitions get produced — an engineering difference, not a computational one. AD is met: the transitions are generated at run time by ORACLE's own search, and since P generalizes, ORACLE is not confined to a script.
>
> **3.** *(b)* I accept the objection, and I think it is stronger than the objector makes it. SN says that what a system is made of makes no difference. But what a system is made of, arranged, just is its causal and temporal structure; the stuff and the causal structure it enters into are not separable in the way CO and AD require them to be. So once SN is granted, causal and temporal conditions cannot be brought back in through the side door. CO and AD are SN plus an exemption for the arrangements we happen to be familiar with, and I think the objector is right that the exemption is there to reserve realization for systems built like brains.
>
> **4.** The cost is that I have to give up my answer to (a). If the objection works, TABLEAU realizes C as well: the table is just C written out longhand, and the fact that this TABLEAU breaks down on unanticipated histories shows only that it is a fragile implementation, not that it fails to implement. I accept that cost. The benefit is that we stop multiplying conditions in order to save an intuition.
>
> **5.** *(c)(i)* Standing attaches to the realization of C, not to the material that realizes it, so it attaches to whatever realizes C. Given (b), both TABLEAU and ORACLE realize C, and both therefore have the standing that MERIDIAN has — there is nothing left over for standing to vary with, since the computation is the only candidate bearer of it. The alternative would be to let standing depend on the speed or the elegance of the implementation, and those are not moral differences.
>
> **6.** *(c)(ii)* The two questions come apart cleanly. Whether ORACLE meets CO is settled by the facts about ORACLE alone, and our not knowing which way it is settled does not settle it. What we owe ORACLE is a different matter, because what we owe depends on what we can reasonably expect, and the expected disvalue of treating a subject of experience as a mere thing enormously exceeds the small cost of being cautious. So the uncertainty is a reason for caution about ORACLE without being a reason to think anything in particular about ORACLE. Both sides in this debate go wrong by sliding between the two questions.
>
> **7.** *(c)(iii)* I do not think there is such a thing. If a system has no experiences, then nothing can be better or worse for it, and when you look closely the reasons people offer always turn out to be reasons about the people who care about the system — the same kind of reason we have about a wedding ring or a cathedral. So the moral question about these systems is exhausted by part (i). And this is why the question matters: if a system realizes no computation at all, then there is nothing we owe it, and so, if my verdict in (b) were wrong and TABLEAU realized nothing, switching it off would be morally unobjectionable, full stop.
