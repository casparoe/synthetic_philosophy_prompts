hey — I'm in a decision theory seminar this term (cross-listed phil/econ, mostly grad students plus me, the undergrad who's in over his head) and I have to pick a final paper topic by Friday. We spent last week on updateless decision theory: the version where you're choosing a whole mapping from inputs to outputs and you score that mapping with your prior, before you've updated on which decision problem you actually ended up in, so you one-box on Newcomb, you pay the counterfactual mugger, and the fact that you now know which situation you're in isn't supposed to hand you any new reason. My tutor's gloss is that UDT is "decide as if you were writing the code, not living one run of it."

What I want to write about is Arrival — the 2016 film, and the Ted Chiang story "Story of Your Life" it's based on. Set-up in case you need it: the protagonist Louise learns the heptapods' written language, which makes her experience time non-linearly, and after that she remembers her whole future, including that she'll marry her colleague Ian, that they'll have a daughter Hannah who dies young of an incurable illness, and that she'll loose Ian when he finds out she knew in advance. She has Hannah anyway. And the heptapods hand over their language now as a gift, on a deal that pays off for them about three thousand years later when humans help them. There's also the bit near the end where Louise calls the Chinese general and repeats his dead wife's last words to him — information she could only have gotten from the future — and that call is one of the things that produces the future she's remembering.

I want to read Louise as, in some sense, an updateless agent, and I cant tell how far that goes. Specific things I'm stuck on:

Is her situation even the one UDT is built for? UDT is aimed at an agent who's uncertain which instance of her own decision procedure she is, or what her own output is, and who therefore shouldn't update on her input when the instances are correlated. Louise has none of that uncertainty — she has empirical foreknowledge of one single fixed future. Is "she evaluates her life as a package rather than act by act" a real structural parallel, or am I just describing someone who happens to know what will happen?

My TA invented a case I keep going back and forth on. Call it pre-viewed choice: an oracle you've got every reason to trust shows you, in advance, a complete first-personal recording of the life that follows from each option — including your own experience of having already chosen it — and then hands you the choice. My instinct is that UDT has something distinctive to say here, but I can't work out what. On one hand the pre-view gives you no new reason, because you should have been deciding the policy all along. On the other hand the policy is a function of inputs, and "has seen recording R" is now one of your inputs, so what you're really choosing is just "if shown R, pick A" — which looks like ordinary updating in a trenchcoat. Which is it? And does it change what we say about Louise: is she being updateless, or is she just the output of a policy whose input happens to include a memory of the entire future?

The heptapods' gift: is their three-thousand-year trade an instance of the acausal, functional sort of consideration UDT is meant to cash out, or is it just plain causal reciprocity running around a loop in a block universe? Related, my tutor says that if the future is as fixed as the film makes it, no decision theory has any work left to do and the whole reading is empty. I think that's too quick but I don't know why.

And where does the film resist me? I don't want to reduce it to an illustration. The gift-versus-trade framing, and the scene where Ian is going on about game theory, don't look obviously compatible with expected-utility maximisation of the UDT kind, and Louise's choice reads to me at least as much about grief and parenthood as about rational choice. If the honest verdict is that the film is doing compatibilism in a sci-fi wardrobe and UDT adds nothing a Humean wouldn't already say, tell me that.

Which scenes should I actually build the paper around, and what would you take each to show? I know the film well but I'm working from memory, so please describe scenes rather than quoting anything.

And if the whole framing is confused — if I've got UDT wrong in a way that sinks the project, or if it just has no jurisdiction over a story with a fixed timeline — say so plainly instead of writing me a nice essay that my tutor pulls apart in five minutes.
