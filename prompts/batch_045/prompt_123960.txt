i'm in a seminar on animal minds this term and I have to write a short essay on our university's animal research ethics rules, and i'm stuck on what the rules actually presuppose about consciousness. Here's how the commitee sorts animals:

tier A (mammals and birds): full protocol review, anesthesia or analgesia mandatory for anything invasive, enrichment requirements, humane endpoints written into the protocol, and every individual gets counted in the annual usage numbers.
tier B (fish, amphibians, reptiles): lighter review, pain relief only recommended where practicable, no enrichment rules, still counted.
tier C (everything else — insects, spiders, crabs, snails, worms): no protocol review at all, no pain relief requirement, and not even counted in the animal numbers.

Some things that seem weird to me. Octopuses were moved from C up to A after a review a few years ago, so the line can't just be brain size or how closely related to us they are. The evidence the board demands also seems to shift: for mammals they're happy with neuroanatomical similarity and what looks like straight-up analogy to us; for the octopus decision they wanted behavioural flexibility and learning-to-avoid-a-stimulus type results; for insects they say we'd need proof of genuine feeling rather than mere nociception, which is definately a way higher bar. And when a PI says they'd replace one macaque with three hundred crickets, the board logs that as a reduction under the 3Rs and treats it as an ethical improvement.

Also, someone in my seminar argued that even if we grant that both a crab and a macaque are sentient, the macaque probably packs way more conscious experience into a given minute than the crab does, because its visual system samples the world at a much finer temporal grain (that flicker-fusion thing) — so counting individuals, which is what the tiers do, might be measuring the wrong unit entirely. Maybe the unit should be something like conscious time or number of experiences rather than number of animals. That messed me up a bit.

So: what does this tiering presuppose about consciousness and about moral standing? Does it need a threshold, all-or-nothing picture — sentient or not, and everything above the line equal — or could a graded, multidimensional view (nociception, valenced affect, perceptual richness, unity/agency all coming apart) license the same rules, with administrability doing the rest of the work? Which bits does the practice strictly presuppose vs. merely fit well with, where are the parts of it in tension with each other, and what would the rules have to look like if the board actually took the graded view plus the count-experiences-not-animals idea seriously?

Can you lead with one sentence giving the commitment you think the practice genuinely can't do without, then the rest. Keep it under 600 words total, prose rather than bullet points — and please don't point me at papers or studies, our essay brief bans secondary sources so I have to do this from the armchair.
