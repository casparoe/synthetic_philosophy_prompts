I'm retired now — thirty-odd years in a materials testing lab, mostly failure analysis — and a few months back I joined a philosophy reading group that meets at the library. We got onto probability a few weeks ago and two of the regulars, Nadia and Malcolm, had a proper set-to about it that nobody else in the room could settle. I'm hoping you can.

The thing they were arguing about is what I've seen written up as the Principal Principle: roughly, that if you learn the present chance of some outcome is x, then — provided the rest of what you know bears on that outcome only through the chances — your confidence in the outcome ought to be x.

Nadia opened by saying it's either trivial or vacuous. Trivial, because that's just what the word is for: someone who says the chance is 70% and then bets as though it were 40% hasn't got a mistaken belief about the world, they just don't know what they're saying. And if that's all there is to it, there's nothing specially philosophical about chance — you get exactly the same shape with any well-calibrated forecaster. A weather forecaster, a bookie, a model: find out what they say, line your confidence up with it. So "deference to chance" is just deference to a reliable expert with a grand name.

Malcolm said no, on two counts. First, it isn't a one-off instruction, it's a constraint on the shape of your whole prior: for every value x, conditional on the hypothesis that the chance is x, your confidence has to be x. So you can't start out fairly confident that the world is rigged so that high chances systematically go unfulfilled — you can't be a chance-sceptic in advance and still obey it. Second, with the weatherman, deference is *earned*: you check her calibration over a load of cases and defer to the extent she's been reliable. Nothing like that is going on with chance. You're not supposed to keep a track record and then decide how much to trust it.

Nadia's reply was that the whole thing is shielded from refutation by the escape clause. Her example: suppose the lab has a standing rule that the failure-probability certificate only goes out with samples that have already been condemned — you get the slip, number and all, only when the component is going to fail. The slip says 70%, and you know the rule. Your confidence in failure should be 1, not 0.7. But the principle doesn't fail, because you're told this evidence is "inadmissible", it bears on the outcome by some route other than the chances. And what is inadmissible, except whatever would have refuted the principle? You can always say that. So it can't be falsified, and something that can't be falsified isn't saying anything.

Malcolm said the clause isn't ad hoc, and there's a test for it. In her certificate case the number on the slip is doing no work at all: hand her a slip saying 3% and she'd be exactly as certain of failure, because what's moving her is the rule about who gets slips, not the figure. She isn't deferring to the chance in that example, so it can't be a counterexample to deferring to the chance. Admissible evidence is the evidence whose bearing on the outcome goes entirely through the chances — facts about how things are now and how they've been — and that's an independent characterisation, not a rubber stamp.

Then Nadia tried a case where the extra knowledge isn't knowledge of the outcome. A pipeline component; the present chance that it fails within the year is 4%. But she's seen the work order, so she also knows for certain that in a fortnight the maintenance crew are swapping in a batch of dodgy valves, and that after the swap the chance of failure will be 90%. Her information concerns what the chance is *going to be*, not what the outcome is going to be. So: does the principle tell her 4%, which is daft, or 90%, in which case it isn't a principle about the present chance any more? Either way, she said, the clean version she was handed at the start doesn't survive contact with it.

Malcolm said the example is only coherent if the numbers add up in a particular way, and once they do, the problem goes. The present chance of failure has to equal the present-chance-weighted average of what the chance will be later — a present chance defers to later chances in just the way a credence is supposed to defer to chances. With 4% now and 90% on the swap branch, if the no-swap branch sits at about 3% then the swap itself can only carry a shade over 1% present chance. Fine. And the general rule is then: defer to the present chance conditional on your evidence. Conditional on the swap going ahead that's 90%, which is the sensible answer, and the bare "present chance" version is just the special case where your evidence adds nothing. Nothing ad hoc about it — it's the same conditionalisation for chances as for credences.

Nadia said that's changing the subject. She was told to defer to the chance; Malcolm's version tells her to defer to the chance after the chance has been bent to fit whatever else she knows. Once the chance function gets conditionalised on her evidence, two people with different evidence defer to different numbers, and the "objective" part is doing no work that couldn't be done by just telling her to hold sensible, well-calibrated degrees of belief. Which was her original point: the objectivity is decorative.

His last word was that it isn't, because what gets conditionalised is the *chance function*, not her credences: two people with different evidence get different numbers out of one and the same chance function, and that's a real constraint, whereas if each of them just conditionalised their own credences there'd be no constraint at all. Then we had to give the room back.

Could you go through it and say who has the better of each exchange, not simply who wins overall? I'd be grateful if you'd take the moves in order and say for each wether it's basically right, basically wrong, or right but not quite for the reason given, and why. I did try drawing the second example as a tree diagram and got stuck, which is probably just me being out of practice. And please say what, if anything, both of them are missing — it felt like they were talking past each other somewhere in the middle, but I coudn't put my finger on where, and I've no training in this beyond a bit of reading.
