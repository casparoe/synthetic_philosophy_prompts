I'm an undergraduate in an upper-level seminar on practical rationality this term, and I've been asked to take one thirty-minute session of a student-run decision-theory reading group — mostly economics and computer-science majors who have had expected-utility theory and some value-of-information and exploration/exploitation material, and no philosophy. The topic assigned to me is the problem of transformative experience: the distinction between experiences that are epistemically transformative and ones that are personally transformative, and the argument that a choice involving them cannot be settled by standard expected-utility reasoning. Please don't write the session for me; tell me how to sequence it.

Concretely:

1. The dependency structure. I can't tell whether to lead with the "you can't imagine what it will be like" half or the "you won't want what you now want" half. Which has to come first, what has to be in place before either one lands, and why? My own guess is that they first have to accept that it is the agent's *own* utility function that does the work in the expected-utility calculation — otherwise the whole thing looks like ordinary risk and nothing I say afterward registers.

2. The carrying example. I've been toying with a nine-month Antarctic winter-over versus staying in a well-understood job: testimony about it is abundant and yet seems to fail, the alternative has a payoff they can model, and it looks reversible, which I think matters. Say whether that case can carry the whole arc or what I should swap it for, and say what a case like this must have and must lack. My constraints: it must be epistemically and personally transformative at once; it must have a non-transformative alternative whose payoff they can genuinely compute; it must have plenty of available testimony, so that "read the memoirs" is a live suggestion that then fails; it must not be morally or identity-loaded the way becoming a parent or a religious conversion is; and it must not be a one-off taste case, which would only dramatise half the problem.

3. The misconceptions, and the point in the sequence where each gets headed off. The ones I expect: collapsing it into ordinary risk over an unknown quantity; thinking testimony or a survey of participants solves it; concluding that reason gives out and one should just be spontaneous; "pilot it first, then back out" — including the wrinkle I want handled somewhere rather than bolted on, that the decision whether to continue or reverse is made by the transformed self, so reversibility doesn't return control to the ex ante agent; and treating any large life change as transformative. Someone will also say "oh, like affective forecasting and impact bias" — tell me whether that empirical literature is a bridge into the problem or a detour, and where it goes if anywhere.

4. A variant I can't place: a transformation invisible from the inside, where the person sincerely reports that their values are unchanged while their choices have shifted. It seems to cut against the "choose in order to discover who you become" framing, and I don't know whether it belongs in the main arc, as a closing hard case, or nowhere in thirty minutes.

5. The check: one question for the last two minutes whose answer distinguishes having seen that the difficulty is missing *values* from having heard it as missing *information*. My candidate is handing them a nearby case in which expected-utility reasoning is fine and asking what changed; give me a better one if you have it.

6. What to openly refuse to cover at this level — I suspect the metaphysics of personal identity, which someone will raise, and whether the discovery-based alternative can be formalised or consequentialised.

Format it as a numbered sequence of moves with approximate timings, each misconception attached to the move where it should be defused, followed by the check question and the list of refusals. No more than 700 words.
