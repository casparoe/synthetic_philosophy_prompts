Last week at a reading group I sit in on, two people got stuck on whether a shared religious outlook can really be a *cause* of an economic outcome, or whether saying so is just a dressed-up way of restating the outcome. Weber came up at the start — the Protestant ethic thing, and that railway-switchman image of his where ideas don't push the train but decide which track it goes down — but they drifted off Weber pretty fast and I honestly cant tell who was winning. Here's roughly how it went.

(1) Mira: It can be a cause, and the usual objection is backwards. The circularity worry — you read the culture off the behavior and then explain the behavior with the culture — only bites if you have no independent grip on the outlook. But you do: sermons, catechisms, what got enforced in church courts, which villages switched when. Once you can identify the doctrine independently of the outcome, you can compare communities that got it against otherwise similar ones that didn't, and "culture" is a variable like any other.

(2) Dev: Grant all that and culture still isn't doing causal work, because any chain you trace will run through individuals — what they believed, who they trusted, what skills they had, who they married. The shared outlook is a summary of that, and summaries don't cause anything. And here's my deeper worry: the thesis is elastic. Thrift and punctuality show up? That's your evidence. What shows up is drinking and debt? Then the doctrine was only half internalized, or got distorted by the local power structure. A claim that survives every possible finding isn't a causal claim, it's a vocabulary.

(3) Mira: The elasticity thing is a research-practice problem, not a metaphysical one — you fix it by committing in advance to which outcomes, in which occupations, in which generation, and by treating a null result as a genuine loss. And "having a mechanism doesn't cancel a cause": oxygen doesn't stop causing fires because the mechanism is oxidation. As for "it's only a summary" — individual beliefs are abstractions too, so that cuts both ways, and sometimes the group-level fact is the one that matters: if whether I'll lend to a stranger depends on how many other people around me already hold the outlook, the variable doing the work belongs to the group, and no description of my private psychology captures it.

(4) Dev: But "how many others hold it" is just other people's behavior. That's an environment, not a culture. I can redescribe the whole thing as social learning — copy whoever seems to be doing all right — and lose nothing. Your oxygen analogy is a false friend, too: oxygen has a stable, measurable effect on any fire anywhere. Doctrines don't. Whether an outlook produces frugality or fatalism depends on everything else going on around it, which suggests the outlook isn't a cause with an effect at all but a label for a whole institutional package — schooling, discipline, marriage rules, who gets to stay. And once you say "package," you've stopped explaining with values.

(5) Mira: Fine, call it a package. That just moves the question to which parts are load-bearing, and the answer might well be that the doctrine is — not because it shoves people around, but because it settles for them what counts as the same kind of conduct. That isn't force, agreed. But it isn't nothing either, and it isn't a redescription: two towns with the same incentives and different doctrines really do diverge, and you can't say which way without knowing what the doctrine said.

(6) Dev: Can't you, though? Maybe the arrow runs the other way — maybe people of a certain temperament moved to the town whose doctrine suited them, or the doctrine spread afterwards because it flattered what they were already doing. Sorting and post-hoc rationalization both generate the same pattern you're calling causation.

(7) Mira: Both are real alternatives, and that's a reason to design a study that separates conversion from sorting and fixes the timing. It's not a reason to say cultural explanation is empty.

I stayed out of it and they both half-turned to me at the end, so: please adjudicate. I don't just want a winner, I want to know at each step whether the point actually lands, or whether it's a good-sounding move that changes the subject. Assume no internet, no citations, no reading suggestions. Sections, please:

1. "What's actually in dispute" — about 100 words: what the real crux is underneath all this.
2. "Move by move" — the bulk, maybe 700 words: one short paragraph per move (1) through (7), each with a rating (lands / mostly lands / half lands / misses) and a sentence or two on why.
3. "Verdict" — about 120 words: who is more right overall, and by how much.
4. "What both of them missed" — about 250 words: the distinction, option, or case that would have unstuck them.
