I am a retired physical chemist, and for the past two years I have convened a monthly philosophy discussion group at our local lifelong-learning institute. The members are eighteen retired scientists, engineers and physicians, most of them between sixty and eighty, argumentative by temperament and almost entirely without formal training in philosophy. We meet for ninety minutes, with no homework between meetings and no assumed background.

The session I am preparing concerns rationality over time. The argument I have been reading runs roughly as follows: if an agent's preferences between two options reverse as time passes, then an offerer can put that agent through a sequence of trades each of which looks, at the moment it is made, like a clear improvement, and which in total leaves the agent strictly worse off than if they had refused every trade. This is supposed to show something about diachronic rationality that the corresponding static arguments do not: that inconsistency across time is not merely untidy but exploitable. I find the argument persuasive in outline and unclear in force, and my group will find it both.

I do not want to explain this to them. I want them to be pumped: to hand over tokens or points several times over, each time willingly and for what looks like a good reason, and then to discover that they are worse off than someone who simply sat still. Ideally they construct the sequence themselves, or at least consent to it step by step, and only see where they have arrived afterwards. They should not hear the term money pump, or any term for the argument, until the debrief.

Please design such an activity for this group and this time slot. Materials should be limited to what fits in a briefcase: index cards, envelopes, a pencil each, perhaps a bag of something small.

I would be grateful if you would specify the following.

One: a payoff scheme that does not involve cash but is not so weightless that nobody cares. I am open to a small real stake divided at the end, and I would like your view on whether a real stake is necessary for the loss to register, and on what I should do if it plainly does not register.

Two: three rounds over the same sequence of offers, differing only in what participants know and may do. In the first, each trade is offered with no warning of what follows. In the second, the entire schedule of offers is visible from the outset, so that participants can plan ahead. In the third, the schedule is visible and participants may make one binding declaration before the first trade that they are then held to. Using one offer sequence across all three rounds is essential if we are to compare outcomes. Tell me whether the pumping still works in the second and third rounds, what the expected scores are in each, and how to handle a participant who refuses every trade from the beginning and is therefore never pumped — with this group that is a genuine possibility and I would rather have a response prepared than improvise one.

Three: at least one reversal that is not a fault. The object on offer should genuinely change across the sequence — a ticket to a concert that is months away and then imminent, a wine that has had five more years in the cellar — so that preferring it later is not a change of mind at all. And at least one reversal driven purely by nearness: the pattern in which a smaller reward available soon is preferred to a larger one later once both are close, but not while both are distant. I want the group to feel the difference between these two cases, and to find that the second cannot be dissolved by redescribing the options as different goods.

Four: if it is defensible, one cycle in which the same instability leaves the participant better off than a consistent agent would be. My suspicion is that not every preference cycle is a liability, and I would like the group to stumble on this rather than be told it.

Five: a debrief, with the questions in the order I should ask them, mapping particular moments in the activity onto particular steps of the argument — the local attractiveness of each individual trade, the accumulation of small losses, the point at which someone says they should have refused everything.

Six: what will predictably go wrong. I expect participants who refuse to trade on principle, participants who do not care about the points, participants who see the shape of the sequence in the first round and thereby spoil the second, participants who try to bind themselves in a round where binding is not allowed, and participants who dispute whether the points mean anything at all. For each, tell me what to do, and tell me whether the contamination between rounds is a defect to be engineered away by running them on different people or different days, or material to be used.

Seven: an honest account of what this activity cannot show, which I must supply myself in discussion. As I understand it, the open question is not whether the pumping works but what follows from it: whether being exploitable in this way is irrational or merely imprudent, whether seeing the sequence in advance and choosing accordingly is a solution to the problem or a different way of failing it, and whether a person is permitted to change their mind about what they want. I would like to know which of these the game leaves entirely untouched.

Finally, please include a complete session plan: the exact offer sequences with the numbers, a record sheet participants can keep, a minute-by-minute allocation of the ninety minutes, and a short fallback statement of what the argument is, suitable for me to give in plain language if the activity collapses and we end up simply talking.
