Some context: I sit on my hospital's incident-review committee, and the case that set this off was an overnight cross-cover intern who never escalated a critically abnormal potassium result; the patient arrested a few hours later and died. The committee wrote "failure to escalate" into the causal chain, and my friend (a law-and-econ person who likes to ruin things) says that entry is metaphysical nonsense, and then gave me an argument that ends up eating not just our report but causation in general. Here it is, more or less as he wrote it up:

(1) A singular causal claim like "c caused e" is true just in case e counterfactually depends on c in the actual situation — in the closest world or worlds where c doesn't occur, e doesn't occur either. (Set aside preemption and overdetermination for now; take but-for dependence as the core of the thing.)
(2) But which world is "closest" isn't pinned down by the actual facts; it's a similarity ranking. So the truth of a singular causal claim depends on which nearby possibilities we treat as nearer.
(3) For an omission you have to say what replaces it, and the obvious replacement is the matching positive act: we evaluate "her failure to escalate caused the death" by looking at the closest world in which she escalates.
(4) There are endlessly many acts that would count as escalating, and they come apart downstream: she pages the attending and he rolls over and goes back to sleep; she pages and he comes; she walks to the bedside herself and notices something else first. So which world is the closest one where she escalates is not settled by how things actually were.
(5) And we also have to decide how much of her to hold fixed. Hold her reasons, her attention and her beliefs fixed and there's no world worth counting in which she escalates at all, because not noticing the result was exactly why she didn't. Let her notice, and we've rewritten her mental states, at which point all sorts of other differences are in play and the verdict floats free again.
(6) So whether her omission caused the death is settled by the choices we make in (4) and (5) — which possibilities we admit, what we hold fixed — and not by the way the world was. Causal claims about omissions aren't discoveries, they're artifacts of what the person asking was interested in.
(7) But (2) is doing the very same work in ordinary positive cases. "The faulty wiring caused the fire" is true only if the fire doesn't start in the closest world without the fault, and whether that's the nearest such world depends on exactly the same kind of ranking: does someone leave a space heater on in that world too, does the sprinkler still work, does the night shift still prop the door open. So if (2) through (6) show that omission-causation is interest-relative, they show that every singular causal claim is interest-relative.
(8) And that has to be wrong. A fire investigator's finding that the wiring caused the fire is a finding, not a decision; and if it were a decision, there'd be nothing for our committee's report to be right or wrong about, and nothing left for the intern to be responsible for.
