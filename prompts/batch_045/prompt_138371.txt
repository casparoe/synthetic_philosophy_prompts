I sit on a risk committee that has to publish an interval rather than a point for claims like "this levee overtops within ten years," and I've noticed that my own verdicts about when an interval is really called for — and about what I'm allowed to do with one afterwards — don't line up. I'd like the principle behind them, if there is one.

My tentative principle: my credence in H should be an interval [l, u] exactly when my evidence admits more than one admissible sharpening and gives me no reason to prefer any of them to the others; otherwise it should be sharp. Call this the Underdetermination Test. Here are the cases; I'd like the principle tested against them and repaired rather than replaced, if it can be.

1. An Ellsberg-style urn: 30 red balls, 60 that are blue or green in a proportion I have no information about. My verdict: P(red) is sharp at 1/3, P(blue) is the full [0, 2/3], and this asymmetry is correct rather than a quirk. The Test gets this one right.

2. A coin drawn from a bin whose distribution of biases I know exactly (uniform over biases, say). My verdict: sharp 1/2 for heads. Higher-order uncertainty about *this* coin doesn't make my credence imprecise, because the known chance structure pins a unique admissible value. I take this to be the same reason case 1's P(red) is sharp.

3. A coin from a sealed crate of unknown provenance: I know nothing about how the crate was filled, not even whether the mix is uniform. My verdict: [0, 1] for heads, and I will not take either side of an even-odds bet. I regard the refusal as rational, not as a malfunction.

4. Three equally credentialed modellers, who never saw each other's work, give 0.20, 0.45 and 0.70 for the same event; I'm not an expert and can't inspect their data. My verdict: [0.20, 0.70] — not a weighted mean, not the median. Note that the Test is at best silent here: a weighted mean looks like a perfectly good "unique admissible sharpening," so the Test needs an extra clause for peer disagreement that I can't adjudicate.

5. The 0.70 modeller finds an arithmetic error and withdraws. My verdict: the interval narrows to [0.20, 0.45], and that's fine — even though nothing about the world changed.

6. A fourth modeller, just as credentialed, reports a tight [0.30, 0.34]. My verdict: I do *not* narrow to anything like that; I stay roughly at [0.20, 0.70]. This is the pair I keep failing to reconcile with case 5: deleting a panel member narrows me, adding one doesn't, and in both cases the only thing that changed is the panel.

7. The gauge case. I'm 90% sure the instrument is calibrated and 10% sure it isn't (if it isn't, it can read anything). Before I open the envelope telling me which: I accept that if it says "uncalibrated" my interval for frost widens to nearly [0, 1], and I *also* accept that I'd pay something not to open the envelope — but I hold as a separate verdict that declining free evidence is never epistemically rational. (I take this to be the phenomenon people call dilation.)

8. Stakes. My interval for a component failing is [0.30, 0.60] and option A beats option B iff the true value is at or above 0.45. Small, reversible stakes: my verdict is that picking either, or flipping a coin, is fine. Large, irreversible stakes: my verdict is that I must either buy more evidence or take the option that's safe across the whole interval; flipping is not fine. My doxastic state is identical in the two, so a purely doxastic principle can't distinguish them and something has to give.

9. Laundering. Our charter forces a single number for the regulator, so we publish the midpoint. My verdict: that's fine, it's not dishonest and it changes nothing doxastic. But when someone at the next meeting reasons "last quarter we said 0.45, so..." — my verdict is that this is illegitimate, precision smuggled in by an administrative requirement. And yet: after several years of being scored against outcomes, I find I no longer have intervals for this class of event at all, and I'm inclined to call that a genuine improvement rather than laundering, even though I can't point to the first-order evidence that bought it.

What I want: a principle, or a small number of candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it cold, with the case that motivates each clause. Tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which it says I should overturn — I'd rather lose a verdict than inherit a five-clause principle, but say what each option costs. Then give its verdicts on the cases I haven't listed: the panel in case 4 grows to ten modellers all sitting in [0.30, 0.35] while my original three stay put (does the interval shrink, and at what point?); the gauge case with 50/50 instead of 90/10; and the scoring regime in case 9 is switched off — do the intervals come back? Finally, tell me where your reasoning is most likely to be wrong and what would change your mind: which argument, or which further case, would lead you to a different principle.
