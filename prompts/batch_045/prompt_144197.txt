I convene a small interdisciplinary seminar on existential risk and population ethics, and I am designing a ninety-minute handout titled Which Extinction Pessimist Are You? The trigger was an argument after a colleague raised a version of the afterlife conjecture: the idea that much of what we value now depends on a largely unarticulated confidence that humanity will continue after our own deaths, so that the end of the species could be bad in a way that is not just a matter of the deaths, suffering, or frustrated desires of the people involved. I do not want a literature review, reading list, footnotes, or citations; please keep this a self-contained conceptual exercise, and feel free to rename any position rather than trying to tell me who holds it.

What I need is a decision tree that sorts a person into one of exactly eight positions about what, if anything, makes human extinction bad beyond the deaths and lost experiences of the individuals who die. Please build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: sketch the branching questions first, note which combinations of answers are possible, and then place the eight leaves in that space. Use no more than six branching questions, label every internal node with the concrete question that separates the paths rather than with a theorist or tradition, and phrase every question as a case a bright undergraduate could answer without already knowing the labels. Begin with the Sealed Dome, in which the last generation lives full, happy lives and then humanity painlessly ends with no descendants or mourners; let the first branch be simply whether the respondent sees any remaining badness once the deaths themselves are set aside, with a no routing directly to the austere leaf. At least the following eight leaves must appear, each with a one- or two-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment: (1) an austere person-affecting or nothing-over-and-above view on which there is no extra loss at all; (2) an impersonal future-people view on which the extra loss is just the missing good lives and could in principle be compensated by creating different well-off beings; (3) a species-relative cultural-continuity view on which the loss is that human projects, history, or culture would have no successors; (4) a Scheffler-style collective-afterlife view on which extinction retroactively undermines the point or intelligibility of our current valuing, even if no future person is deprived; (5) a stewardship or civilizational-progress view on which humanity is a valuable cumulative project that would be cut short; (6) a plenitude or axiarchic view on which any loss is a reduction in the total amount, variety, or quality of value in the universe, human or not; (7) a suffering-focused or anti-natalist-leaning view on which extinction is not bad beyond the deaths and may even be preferable because it prevents future harm; and (8) a quietist or no-deep-fact view on which the extra question has no further factual answer and our judgments merely express attitudes. If some coherent combination of answers has no occupants, mark it as unoccupied and say briefly whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Also flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot really separate, and tell me what additional case would be needed to pull them apart.

To keep the tree from collapsing into an abstract formula, please build the branch questions around at least these five invented scenarios, adding others only if you need them: the Sealed Dome, already described; the Successor Reserve, in which an equally flourishing but culturally unrelated species arises right after us and creates just as much value; the Frozen Vault, in which stored embryos or backups could, if revived by aliens, produce good lives disconnected from all current human projects; the Museum of the Last Generation, in which the last humans knowingly complete all their personal and collective projects and build an archive that no one will ever see; and the Sterilization Choice, in which a future society must choose between a risky continuation of humanity and a painless sterilization that leads to extinction after the current generation. After the tree, include a brief scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto the eight leaves, and keep the whole thing readable for students from economics, literary studies, and other departments outside philosophy.
