I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and I am finishing a feature on the Ship of Theseus — not as a chestnut of ancient philosophy but because it keeps turning up, unrecognised, in places my reporting actually takes me: arguments over which restoration of a historic aircraft is the original, whether a research group that has turned over its entire staff and moved buildings twice is still the same lab, whether a century-long temperature series is one record when every thermometer, screen, observer and site behind it has been replaced several times over. My draft currently does the standard tour — the ancient version in which the ship is kept seaworthy by replacing rotting timbers one at a time, the variant where the removed timbers are reassembled into a rival ship, the four-dimensional view on which the ship is a temporal worm whose early and late stages differ in matter, the conventionalist shrug that identity is interest-relative — and I dislike it, because every section closes by saying that it depends on what you mean by same, which is both true and useless. My editor has written so what? in the margin at precisely the point where the hedging begins.

What I want from you is the essay I keep failing to write. Roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words, addressed to an educated reader who has encountered the puzzle but never read the literature, with no citations, footnotes or bibliography: argue it out rather than gesturing at authorities, and give the strongest version of the positions you reject rather than the convenient version. State your governing principle in a single sentence early on, and then let that one principle — or at most a tightly interlocked pair of them — generate your verdict in each of four cases, instead of supplying a fresh intuition per case. The cases: (a) the classic, in which one timber is replaced at a time over decades, the old timbers are burned, and the vessel never ceases to be a functioning, continuously maintained, registered ship; (b) the reassembly variant, in which the removed timbers are kept and, in the same week the final replacement is made, built into a complete second ship, so that there are two candidates, one of which has never sailed; (c) a variant a naval architect wrote to me about after a radio segment, which I have not seen discussed anywhere: the overnight swap, in which every timber is stripped out at dusk and a prefabricated duplicate built to the same drawings from new timber stands in its place by dawn, the original timbers stacked in a shed — is the rate of change doing any real work here, or is the only difference between this and case (a) that in (a) we can follow the ship hour by hour?; and (d) my own addition, which is the one I most want a philosopher to chew on: the dispersed case, in which the replaced timbers are never reassembled into a ship at all but are planed down and laid as the floor of a temple, so that at the end there is a working new-timber ship in the harbour and, in a temple, a floor consisting of all and only the original matter. Is the original ship in (d) now a scattered object that has simply moved indoors? Has it gone out of existence? Or is the ship in the harbour it? Whichever you answer, you owe me an account of what to say about the intuition that a ship is the sort of thing that has to be in one piece to be there at all.

Two further things a careful reader will throw at me, and which I need handled honestly rather than in a clause. First, the double-counting problem: if in case (b) both candidates are Theseus's ship, then there are two ships where there was one, and I want you to say whether that is a genuine cost, a reductio, or something we already accept elsewhere without flinching. Second, the process option — the thought that a ship is closer to a flame, a river or a club than to a rock, something whose identity conditions are those of an ongoing activity rather than of a portion of matter arranged a certain way: tell me whether that dissolves the puzzle or merely relocates it, and if it relocates it, say exactly where it goes. I would also like you to resist, or to earn rather than assert, the cheap version of the no-fact-of-the-matter response; if you end up in that neighbourhood, give me a version with teeth, one that specifies who is entitled to say what in each case and what follows for the registration, the insurance and the temple. Close by running your principle through one case from my beat: the century-long temperature series, whose instruments and sites have been replaced repeatedly, each replacement requiring a correction to keep the numbers comparable. Is it one record, and if so in virtue of what — and what would have to happen for it to stop being one?
