Write a short story (1,200–1,800 words) of literary fiction, in the third person, past tense, with a title. Aim for something that works as a story first: concrete scenes, particular characters, sensory detail, and no closing commentary or afterword in which the ideas are explained.

The story should dramatize a problem in Tolstoy's theory of art, set out in his essay What Is Art?, completed in Russian in 1897. For your purposes, his view can be summarized as follows. Art is not about beauty; it is a means of communication by which one person, having once experienced a feeling and recalling it within himself, transmits that feeling to others by external signs, so that they are infected by it and feel it too. The degree of infection is the measure of a work's artistic quality, and infection is heightened by the maker's sincerity, that is, by the extent to which the maker actually undergoes the feeling conveyed. Transmission is also supposed to be possible without special training: Tolstoy held that genuine art remains intelligible to unlettered working people, and he mocked the obscurity and professional hermeticism of the art of his day. He further distinguished works that merely divert or amuse from works whose transmitted feeling serves the religious perception of the age, which he understood as the consciousness of the brotherhood of human beings; the highest art accustoms people, under imaginary conditions, to feelings of solidarity and love that they then carry into real life. And he listed four recurring marks by which art is commonly counterfeited: borrowing, that is, reassembling devices and images already used by earlier works; imitation, that is, descriptive realism that reports the surfaces of things instead of conveying feeling; effectfulness, that is, reliance on the merely striking; and diversion, that is, aiming to entertain rather than to unite.

Set the story in a near future in which a public body — call it the Registry — certifies works of art for subsidy, applying a test derived from this theory. Its three prongs are transmission (measured by portable telemetry on panels of ordinary viewers, across languages and levels of education), sincerity (verified by a correspondence audit: an attempt to match the physiological traces of the maker during production against the feeling the work conveys), and tendency (whether the feeling transmitted draws people toward solidarity or merely amuses them). The case before the Registry is a three-minute audiovisual piece produced entirely by a generative system that was optimized directly against the Registry's own transmission metric. It scores better than almost any human work ever tested: viewers describe the same uncomplicated feelings in the same words regardless of language, and in follow-up interviews a measurable number of them have done something costly and conciliatory in the week afterward. On the correspondence audit and on the marks of counterfeit art, it scores catastrophically: it was assembled from the fragments of a million earlier works, it aims at nothing but effect, and there is nobody who felt anything while making it.

Into this, build one further discovery, which should be the turning point of the story. When viewers are told before the screening that the piece was produced by a system, the transmission index collapses; when they are told it was made by a person in grief, it rises well above its baseline. The Registry's own instrument, in other words, cannot separate the power of the work from the audience's belief that some other mind is addressing them. Your protagonist should be a junior officer of the Registry — give her a name, a specific personal history, and a reason to care — who uncovers this and who must decide whether, or how, to sign the certificate.

Two further constraints. First, keep the argument inside the fiction: the characters may use in-world terms such as the Criteria or the correspondence audit, but they should never name Tolstoy, lecture about him, or discuss his theory as a theory. Second, do not resolve the case. The story should end with the question of whether the piece is art — and of what the instrument is really measuring — left genuinely open, though not vaguely so.
