I got interested in this after a fight at our school about whether freshmen should still have to recite a Latin grace out loud at formal dinner: everyone I know thinks it is faintly ridiculous, everyone says so in private, and yet nobody wants to be the person who visibly stops doing it, because the people whose approval we actually want are the ones running the dinner. That felt like it might be the same shape as the thing we just read for philosophy club — the argument, associated with Bernard Williams and with Kwame Appiah, that practices like duelling and slavery did not end because somebody finally produced a refutation of them, but because honour and shame turned against them, and that the arguments people remember were mostly written down afterwards, as a record of a change that had already happened in what people found embarrassing. I am a senior, I run the club, about fourteen students aged sixteen to eighteen, one fifty-five-minute block on Thursdays after sports, a room with a whiteboard, index cards and some poker chips, and no grades or money to hand out. Everyone in the room says they believe this view when we discuss it, and then immediately says things like "obviously slavery was wrong, anyone could have seen that," which is precisely the hindsight the view says is an illusion. So I want an activity that makes them feel it rather than agree with it: the practice has to seem normal at minute ten and be dead by minute forty, and it has to die without me ever telling them it was wrong.

My rough idea is to set it at a fictional boarding school very like ours — I have been calling it Ashgrove — that has a formal practice I have been calling "the Answer." If someone insults you or something that is yours in front of witnesses, you are supposed to issue a challenge within two days and then the two of you stage a supervised physical contest in the quad; if you do not issue it, you are "open," which means anyone may treat your word as not counting. I give every player a role card, and the key thing I want from you is that each card carries two things that can come apart: what the character materially gains or loses from the practice continuing (a scholarship, a recommendation, protection from someone bigger, a place in the dining hall) and what the character would be mortified to be caught doing. Several characters should privately want the practice gone while depending on it for their standing. Please also give some of them genuinely good objections to it — I am imagining one about how the contest settles nothing about whether the insult was true, and one about how the whole thing protects the popular kids and eats the quiet ones — and then rig the rules so that the objection is publicly conceded in open session and still changes nobody's behaviour that round. I have been calling that the "concede-and-continue" problem and it is the single thing I most want them to walk into: the experience of making an airtight argument and watching it do nothing.

Then I need the mechanism that actually kills the practice, and I want it to be something other than argument. My guess is a version of what I have been calling the "giggle threshold" — the point at which a practice stops looking formidable and starts looking faintly silly to exactly the people whose approval you were trying to keep — possibly triggered by a visiting team from a school where the practice has already died, who react with polite contempt rather than fear. Tell me whether that staging actually works, what the visitors should concretely do and say in character, how many rounds to run and what a player does on a turn, and how the tally on the whiteboard should be kept so the collapse is visible as it is happening rather than announced at the end. I also want a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific parts of the view, and I want the names Williams and Appiah and the phrase "moral revolution" withheld until the debrief. Finally, be honest about two things: everything that will predictably go wrong the first time I run it with this particular group — players staying ironic because it is a game, someone refusing to play along, the quiet kids' character getting genuinely picked on, players concluding that norm change is just cowardice — and which feature of the real phenomenon the game cannot reproduce at all, so that I know what I have to supply myself in the discussion and what I should expect the simulation to get wrong.
