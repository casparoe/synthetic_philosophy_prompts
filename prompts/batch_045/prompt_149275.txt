Last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether the physical size of a brain could be morally relevant in its own right — not merely as evidence for what a being can feel, notice, do, remember or want. My friend holds that neural tissue matters only as the substrate of psychological capacities, and that once those capacities are held fixed, no fact about how much machinery implements them can make a further moral difference. I tried to resist this and kept losing the thread. I'd like you to make the case I could not make.

The crux we arrived at is a stipulated pair. Call it the gated-channel case. Ada and Bo are indistinguishable by every test we can devise: the same discriminations, the same verbal and non-verbal reports, the same learning curves, the same memories, preferences, plans, affective responses and forms of practical reasoning. The only difference is subpersonal. Ada's sensory systems resolve far more fine-grained detail than Bo's, and in Ada that extra detail is passed to the systems that subserve conscious experience; it is then stripped out by a bottleneck before it reaches anything involved in report, memory, choice, learning, affect or action. Ada's brain is correspondingly larger — the surplus machinery is what the extra resolution costs. My friend granted, for the sake of the argument, that Ada therefore undergoes more experience from moment to moment than Bo does, there being more detail present to her and nothing for her. She denied that this surplus does any moral work.

Please argue for the claim that it does: that the surplus experiential richness in a gated-channel case is morally relevant non-derivatively, and that, because what generates it here is neural scale rather than any capacity, brain size is morally relevant in its own right and not merely as a proxy for capacities. Write it so that a capacity orthodoxist could in principle be moved by it — the argument should locate the surplus inside some general account of what makes anything matter at all, rather than simply asserting that more experience is more of a good thing.

Three things my friend said that I could not answer, and that I want handled. (a) The inert-surplus objection: a difference that makes no difference to anything the being can do, undergo over time, care about, or be benefited or harmed by is not the kind of difference morality tracks; the surplus is, by construction, motivationally and functionally inert, so counting it is like counting the number of unused pixels on a sensor. (b) The sign-indeterminacy objection: even granting that richness per moment matters, the measure you want has no determinate direction — larger brains are often slower brains, a slower brain might package experience into fewer conscious moments per unit of clock time, and fineness-per-moment and moments-per-second could trade off against each other, so 'more brain' has no stable moral sign and cannot bear the weight you are putting on it. (c) The proxy objection: if surplus phenomenal richness is what matters, then the view is a view about phenomenal quantity with an unfortunate name, and it has not answered the question about brain size at all. Address (a) and (b) at minimum, and (c) if you can do so without weakening the rest.

Two constraints. First, I do not want the surplus defended as a hedge — please do not argue that it should be given weight because some theories of value count it and we should aggregate across our uncertainty. Argue that it matters, full stop. Second, don't lean on the literature: no citations, no named authors, no papers, allusions to specific published positions, or reading suggestions. I've read around this and I want the argument made from the case and from first considerations. If you think the thesis is false, make it as well as it can be made anyway and confine your own verdict to the field I've set aside for it below.

Give me the argument as continuous prose of roughly 700–900 words, in British English, and then a single JSON object — valid JSON, no commentary outside the string values — with exactly these fields:

- "thesis": the claim being defended, in one sentence.
- "load_bearing_premises": an array of three or four strings, each one premise of the argument, ordered by how much work it does.
- "case_doing_the_work": which hypothetical or comparison the argument leans on hardest, and what would collapse if that case were withdrawn.
- "objections_answered": an array of objects, each with "label" (one of "inert-surplus", "sign-indeterminacy", "proxy") and "reply" (a two-sentence summary of the reply).
- "weakest_link": the one step you would attack first if you were my friend, and your own verdict on the thesis in the same field.
- "concessions": what the capacity orthodoxist is right about and the defender should simply give up.
- "practical_upshot": one or two sentences on whether anything follows for how we weigh real animals of very different brain sizes, and whether it follows given that we cannot detect gated richness in practice.
- "line_for_next_time": the single sentence I should have opened with last night.
