My brother-in-law writes backend code, and ever since he found out I've been reading about biology on my own he keeps pushing the same picture: the genome is literally a codebase, the cell is the machine that runs it, genes are functions, all the regulatory stuff is the if-statements, and CRISPR is just going in and editing source. He leans on it pretty hard, too — he says it follows that the sequence genuinely says something about the organism in a way the rest of the cell doesn't, that the "information content" of a genome is a real quantity and not just a poetic way of talking, and that in principle you could get the animal back from the sequence alone, so de-extinction is basically a sequencing problem. My friend who works in a chem lab says the whole thing is metaphor: nothing in a cell is reading anything, nobody meant anything by the sequence, bases have shapes and stickiness, and everything else is us telling a story backwards from the result. Her version of the test is that a code needs a coder, a decoder, and a convention, and evolution isn't any of the three. I definately don't have the background to settle it, and honestly they both sound kind of right to me.

Two things keep making me think the picture is doing more work than it should. First, he sent me one of those articles about using DNA as a hard drive, where people encode a file into base pairs and read it back out, and it seems to me that in that case there really is a code — somebody picked the mapping, somebody builds the reader — which makes me suspect the natural case is missing exactly whatever that case adds, but I can't say what. Second, someone in a comment thread said that if a sequence counts as carrying information because of what it was selected for, then a fish's genome carries information about water just as much as about fins, since water is part of what shaped it, and then "information about the organism" isn't special. That sounded clever but I have no idea whether it's a real point or just comment-section nonsense.

Can you audit the analogy properly for me? I'd like the mapping spelled out explicitly — what plays the role of the programmer, the compiler, the machine, the input, the output, the comments, the runtime environment, and the bugs — and then where it breaks. My own guesses at the disanalogies are: the "machine" is made of the same stuff as the "program" and is itself built by it, so there's no clean code/machine split; there's nothing standing outside the system doing the reading; you can't cleanly say which parts of the sequence are instructions and which are just chemistry; and the output seems to depend on the environment in a way a program's doesn't, since the same genome gives you a caterpillar and then a butterfly, or a male turtle or a female one depending on the temperature of the nest. Which of those actually matter for his three conclusions — that the sequence says something, that information is a real quantity, that the animal is recoverable from the sequence — and which are just loose ends? Does anything survive once the disanalogies are on the table? And is there a better analogy, or is every analogy going to quietly smuggle in a reader? I've been toying with two: "a recipe whose ingredients also help write the recipe and whose oven decides which lines to follow," and "a punch card for a loom where the loom itself was woven by earlier punch cards." Which one carries more, and what does each one hide?
