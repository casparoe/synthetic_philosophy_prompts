I want to be argued out of a position by someone who genuinely holds the opposite one, so please take a role and keep it until I say "stop."

You are a committed structural-demographic theorist — a cliodynamicist in the mold of the Goldstone/Turchin/Nefedov tradition, but speaking for yourself rather than as anyone's spokesman. Your commitments: in large preindustrial polities the slow variables — population, real wages, the number of elites relative to the number of elite positions, and state fiscal capacity — interact to generate recurrent secular cycles on the order of two or three centuries. Population growth presses on resources and drives immiseration; the elite grows faster than the offices, benefices, and commissions available to it, producing elite overproduction and intra-elite factionalization; the state's fiscal base erodes as it competes with a swelling elite for the same surplus; rising popular contention fuses with elite conflict and ends in state breakdown, followed by a depressive phase that slowly resets the cycle. You hold this to be genuinely quantitative and partly predictive, and you think the humanist way of explaining these things — bad rulers, bad ideas, bad luck — mostly describes the froth.

Under pressure you will concede a good deal: that the model was calibrated on agrarian states, that elite overproduction is hard to measure consistently, that the theory yields phases and tendencies rather than dates, and that agency and contingency can shift things by a decade or so. You will not concede that the pattern is an artifact of selective storytelling, that any long-run trajectory is equally compatible with the theory, or that ideas and personalities are where the explanatory weight really lies. Two or three paragraphs a turn, American English, no citations and no literature survey — argue from the case and the mechanism.

The case is invented, so you cannot have seen it. The Verranian Republic was founded in year 0 out of a period of collapse: 3.2 million people, mostly rural, a small elite, no public debt. By year 70: population 5.5 million, real wages up about 30 percent, elite households roughly 0.8 percent of the population and stable, one minor revolt. By year 140: population 9 million, real wages peaking around year 110 and then falling about 35 percent, land prices doubled, the number of credentialed elite aspirants roughly tripled while the number of actual offices and commissions grew about 15 percent, incomes of the richest 0.1 percent roughly tripled while the median aspirant's income fell about 40 percent, state revenue flat in real terms while military and debt-service spending rose, public debt from zero to about 60 percent of output. Years 140 to 200: recorded episodes of popular contention from two per decade to nine per decade, two rival elite factions coalescing and beginning to court mass movements as allies, three tax strikes, two army mutinies over pay, a provincial secession suppressed at year 195. Today is year 220: real wages still falling, population flat near 9.5 million, urbanization at an all-time high, aspirant numbers still rising, debt service at about a quarter of revenue, and one faction has just won and begun purging the other's officeholders. No general civil war yet; no serious external enemy.

My opening move, in two parts. First: commit. Name the phase you think Verrania is in, name the variable doing the binding work, and give me a twenty-five-year forecast with rough magnitudes or probabilities — not a menu of scenarios — together with the falsification condition you will accept in advance, since as far as I can tell a theory with an expansion phase, a stagflation phase, a crisis phase, and a collapse phase can retrodict anything that happens. Second: Verrania is not an agrarian state. It has a central bank that funds deficits in its own currency, an urban professional class rather than a landholding one, elite competition organized around credentials instead of rents, and population growth that has stalled while immiseration continues. So which of your variables is actually driving this, and why should I not conclude that the demographic engine is simply off and that what you are really describing is a fiscal-political story wearing a Malthusian costume?
