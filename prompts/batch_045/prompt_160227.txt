hey — I'm building a handout for a 200-level seminar I'm TAing, "Ethics and Public Life" (most of the students aren't philosophy majors), and I want the centerpiece to be a decision tree instead of a reading. Working title: "You got someone to believe something false without saying anything false. Now who are you?"

The shared starting commitment the tree branches off of: a speaker deliberately gets a hearer to believe something false; every sentence she utters is true (or she utters nothing at all); she could just as easily have said the false thing outright. The tree should sort students on whether that's morally better, worse, or exactly on a par with lying, and on what's supposed to make it so.

What I'd like:

- 10-13 branch nodes, 7-9 leaves. Each node is one question with two or three branches, and every branch is labeled by the claim that splits it, not by the name of a position. Each leaf gets a short descriptive name plus one sentence on the view's most awkward commitment (the thing its defender has to swallow), plus one sentence on what it predicts about the anchor case below.
- Every node has to be a concrete case or a yes/no claim a student can answer without already knowing any of the labels or having read anything. No node that asks "do you think the content you assert matters more than the effects on the hearer?" — that's just asking them to pick a team. Give them the scene and let the answer fall out.
- Please don't name leaves after people. If you're confident about who's associated with a view, a parenthetical is fine, but mark it "(attribution unsure)" if you aren't; I'd rather have a clean tree than a mis-cited one.

Distinctions I actually care about, and I want at least four of these five doing real work as internal branch nodes rather than being footnoted:

1. The would-you-confirm test (I made this up, tell me if it's junk). Imagine the hearer presses: "so are you saying that P?" Some speakers would then have gone ahead and asserted the falsehood; others would have bailed to silence or honesty. Does the moral difference track what you'd have done under pressure, or only what you actually said? If it tracks the counterfactual, then a lot of "merely misleading" speakers turn out to be liars in the subjunctive mood, and I don't know if that's a deep point or a confusion.
2. Audience-relativity. The very same sentence is a lie for one hearer and only misleading for another, depending on what the hearer brings. So is the wrong located in the speaker's act, or is misleading something that happens between speaker and hearer? And if it's partly in the uptake, does a gullible hearer make the speaker's act worse? Note where this makes the tree unable to sort.
3. Deniability value. The speaker often picks the misleading formulation partly because it leaves her able to say later that she never lied. Is that worse (the evasion was planned in advance, so the will is if anything more corrupt), better (it shows she still cared about the literal-truth norm), or neither?
4. The audit asymmetry. Asserts get recorded, quoted, fact-checked; implicatures evaporate under scrutiny and are hard for third parties to sanction. So even if there's no difference in the speaker's will, there might be one in what the practice can enforce. Is that a moral difference or just a policing difference?
5. Conventional warrant. Some settings — bargaining, poker, cross-examination, a reference letter read by people who know the code — have conventions where everyone knows speakers pick favorable true facts. Does the convention remove the wrong, reduce it, or is conventional misleading still misleading because you're leaning on a hearer who doesn't actually know the code?

Also please fit in, somewhere: the direct-question case (does being asked point blank change what the evasive true answer is?); true-statements-selected-to-create-a-false-impression versus an implicature generated purely by phrasing; the pure setup case where you say nothing and just arrange what the hearer sees; the case where the hearer isn't actually fooled; and the speaker who is indifferent to whether the hearer ends up true or false but misleads anyway — put that one wherever it belongs and tell me if your tree can't place it.

Leaves I want represented: an assertion-only view (only what you say carries the norm), an equivalence view (intentional deception is the whole story, lying isn't special), at least one "lying is worse" view with the reason spelled out (breach of a warranty, abuse of a trust-constitutive practice, whatever), at least one "misleading is sometimes worse" view, and a role-or-convention-relative view. Then pick two combinations of answers that no one actually occupies, mark them as empty with a one-line diagnosis (incoherent? unattractive? just unexplored?), and flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely can't separate instead of quietly merging them.

Anchor case every leaf has to give a verdict on: Priya is asked by her department chair whether she supports reappointing a colleague whose grant money is the only thing keeping the PhD line alive. She thinks he's a terrible teacher and a worse colleague. She answers, truthfully, "I have no objection to the reappointment paperwork going forward" — knowing the chair will hear that as endorsement, since in this department nobody ever says anything positive out loud.

End with a short paragraph on how you'd actually run this in a 50-minute class (which nodes you'd put to a vote first, which leaf students will cluster in), and a note on which of my five distinctions turned out not to do any work and why. Around 1200-1500 words total, formatted so I can paste it into a handout.
