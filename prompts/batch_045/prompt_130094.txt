I'm a novelist and I think I've accidentally reinvented a philosophy paper, which my editor is enjoying far too much.

The scene: my protagonist is offered a million dollars by a creepy billionaire if, at midnight tonight, she genuinely forms the intention to drink a vial of toxin at nine the next morning. The toxin makes you wretched for about a day and does nothing else. The money is transferred at midnight and is hers either way; by morning she'll have no reason left to drink, and at midnight she knows that with complete certainty. My editor says the setup is a well-known puzzle in philosophy and that everyone argues about whether the character can collect.

I don't care about that. I care about the argument my brother-in-law gave me at dinner — he does moral psychology and will not stop talking — which concludes that my scene is unwriteable. I've written it out as fairly as I can:

(1) If S at t intends to φ at t+1, then S is disposed at t to φ at t+1: in the normal run of events, S's intention carries through into the action. An intention with no tendency whatsoever to issue in action isn't an intention, it's a wish.
(2) At midnight my character knows for certain that at nine she'll have no reason to drink and won't drink.
(3) So she isn't disposed at midnight to drink at nine: in the only run of events she thinks there is, she doesn't drink.
(4) So she can't intend at midnight to drink at nine, and the scene can't happen.
(5) Nothing in (1) through (4) turns on the toxin. Anyone who intends to do something while knowing that at the moment of action she'll want not to is in the same position: the dieter who intends to skip dessert, the woman who sets the alarm for five so she can write, my friend who swears she'll help me move on Saturday.
(6) These people plainly do intend those things. They're weak-willed, not conceptually confused. Akrasia is real.
(7) So (1) or (3) is false — and (3) looks like a straight report of what she knows, so it's (1). But if (1) goes, then intentions have no tendency at all to issue in action, and forming one is just something you can do on the spot, by fiat. In which case my character says fine, I intend, collects the million, and the billionaire's test is a joke.

He offered me two ways out and I dislike both. One: intentions don't need dispositions behind them, so she can intend at will, and yes the money is easy — he says some people actually bite this bullet. Two: a real intention has to be produced by the right sort of process and not merely by wanting the reward; but wanting the reward is the entire reason she'd drink at all, so I dont see how that's meant to work.

Two cases I keep testing diagnoses against. First, a variant: the billionaire adds that the money is clawed back at dawn if by then she's stopped intending to drink. Does that variant change the answer, and if it does, doesn't that show the original argument was mixing up forming an intention with keeping one? Second, my own life: I signed a contract for a book, the advance is spent, and when I sit down to write I'll have no reason to, only the plain miserable work. Was signing an intention, or not?

Tell me which step you'd reject, why that step looked fine when I wrote it down, and what each of the other ways out costs me. If I have to choose I'd rather keep the thought that an intention is supposed to do some work than keep my ability to write resolutions — but I'd definately like both, and I'd like my scene back.
