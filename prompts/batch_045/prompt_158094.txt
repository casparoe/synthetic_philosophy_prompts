i'm a backend engineer — I've spent the last few years on abuse and rate-limiting work at a mid-sized social platform, and I've got basically no philosophy background beyond blog posts and arguments in pull request threads. At work we keep going round in circles about how far to go on deplatforming, and someone dropped the paradox of tolerance into the thread the other day: the Popper thing from his 1945 book, where a society that tolerates everything gets eaten by the intolerant. I sort of get the shape of it, but it feels like it's doing several different jobs at once, and I'd like to see it stated in the vocabulary I actually reason in.

So: translate the argument, as faithfully as you can, into system-design terms. The vocabulary I'm fluent in: threat models and adversarial actors, abuse vectors, Sybil and resource-exhaustion attacks, admission control versus ranking and amplification, allowlists and denylists, rate limits, classifier precision and recall with asymmetric error costs, policy as code versus learned moderation models, escalation paths, who holds the kill switch, appeals and audit logs, A/B tests and the fact that some changes simply can't be A/B tested, and capture of the layer that enforces the rules (the way a regulator ends up captured by the industry it regulates). In those terms, what is unlimited tolerance as a property of a system, what is the intolerant movement as an attacker, what exactly is the failure mode being predicted, and what does the proposed fix — some enforcement mechanism with the authority to exclude — look like when you treat it as a component with an interface? Where do the standard objections land, for instance the stricter line that you only get to act when the threat to liberty is concrete and demonstrable rather than speculative?

Then the bit I definately want: the residue report. Which claims translate cleanly, which only survive if you distort them a bit, and which have no expression in my vocabulary at all? And I've got a half-baked distinction I'd rather you stress-test than flatter: I think what people call tolerance is really two seperate levers that the usual argument quietly conflates — the delivery layer (does the message get through at all, i.e. admission control) and the reach layer (how much ranking, recommendation and reply-graph amplification push it). If those really are separate, does the paradox generate obligations at the reach layer even if you grant that the delivery layer has to stay permissive, and does splitting them change what the argument actually says? Related worry: detecting intolerance is itself a classifier whose errors are asymmetric and unequally visible, and whose training labels come from the same institution holding the ban button, so maybe part of what gets expressed as a moral paradox is really a fight over who gets to own the enforcement mechanism. For anything that won't translate, tell me whether that's a hole in my vocabulary or a sign the original claim is vaguer than it looks — and say so plainly if my two-lever idea is just a relabelling that buys nothing. Last thing: given there's no ground-truth label and no canary deployment available for any of this, is there anything that could count as evidence, in my sense of the word, that a tolerant system is actually collapsing?
