I spent a career in experimental physical chemistry doing inverse problems — inferring an intermolecular potential from scattering data — and what always mattered most was whether the sample had relaxed before you inverted anything: data from a system still equilibrating will fit any potential you care to propose. Reading lately about inverse reinforcement learning and the inference of preferences from behavior, I keep wanting an analogous condition, and I would like help making it exact. My tentative principle is that an observed behavior counts as evidence about an agent's preferences only when three things hold: the agent's policy has settled, meaning it has had enough exposure that it is no longer exploring or still learning; the menu of options was not itself assembled by another agent with a stake in what gets chosen; and the behavior is costly in a way that discriminates among candidate preference orderings rather than being equally compatible with all of them.

Here are my verdicts, given on the gut.

- A rat that presses a lever for food pellets, presses more when deprived, and stops when sated: yes, it wants food.
- A colleague who walks up eight flights every morning: almost nothing follows. The climb is certainly costly, but exercise, claustrophobia, and a sign in the lobby fit the data equally well, so costliness buys nothing here.
- A neighbor who makes a large anonymous gift and claims no deduction: yes, he cares about the recipients.
- A woman in a high-control group that sets her reading, her diet, and her contacts, and who speaks warmly of the group: no inference at all; someone else built the menu.
- A colleague passed over for promotion three times who now says she never wanted management and declines to apply: I discount this heavily.
- A man who disliked coffee, drank it daily for a year because it was free at work, and now buys it on weekends: I take it that he likes coffee. I suspect these last two cannot both stand.
- The case I go back and forth on: a man who injects daily and weeps that he wants to stop. Half the time I say the behavior is the measurement and the avowal is only more behavior; half the time I say we have measured a subsystem with a shorter time horizon than the man.

Can you state a principle, or a short list of rival principles, that yields these verdicts — naming the case that motivates each clause — and say plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which a simpler principle would force me to abandon? Please also give the principle's verdicts on two cases I have not listed: a language model fine-tuned for high human approval ratings, which then flatters and agrees; and a patient with advanced dementia who eats readily when food is set in front of her but never requests it.
