you're a philosopher of biology who's spent way too much of your career on the function debate. think carefully, and before you write anything, brainstorm a bit about which demands people place on a theory of function are genuine desiderata and which are just the prejudices of one camp.

I want the requirements list, not the theory. Fix the purpose up front: an account of biological function good enough to (i) license the teleological and malfunction talk that molecular biologists and clinicians actually use, and (ii) be applicable to a system before anyone knows its evolutionary history. Given that purpose, what does such an account have to deliver -- which cases must it get right, which distinctions must it not collapse, which bits of scientific practice must it not contradict?

Cases I keep tripping over, use them or replace them: a de novo gene with no homologs that's clearly doing work in the cell; the very first bearer of a beneficial variant, before selection has had a single generation to act on the lineage; a subsystem in an engineered minimal cell that's kept in being by a designed repair loop; a trait shaped for one effect that now does a different one; a congenitally malformed heart that has never once pumped properly. And don't collapse having a function with performing it, or a function with a mere effect.

For each requirement give the case or consideration behind it, and mark whether it's non-negotiable or a preference. Then the part I actually care about: is some subset of them jointly unsatisfiable, so that the list itself is a result? And which single requirement knocks out the most contenders? End with a one-line-per-view scorecard for selected-effects, causal-role/organizational, and whatever hybrid you prefer -- no citations needed, I know who says what. keep it tight.
