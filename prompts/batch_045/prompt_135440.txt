Write a joke about higher-order theories of consciousness — the family of views on which what makes a mental state conscious is that the subject bears a suitable higher-order representation to it: a thought, on the "higher-order thought" versions, or something closer to inner perception, on the "inner sense" versions.

The joke should be funny to someone who actually knows these theories. That is, the punchline should turn on their real commitments rather than on the generic observation that consciousness is puzzling or that philosophers are pedants. One commitment I'd especially like it to engage with is the theories' handling of misrepresentation: the familiar worry about what a higher-order state does when it has no suitable target at all, illustrated by the thought of a rock being in pain, which plainly cannot make the rock conscious, yet which by the theory's own lights looks as though it should be doing exactly the kind of work that confers consciousness.

To keep this from being something I've already read, please build the joke around the following case, which I'll call the shared monitor. Two organisms have entirely separate first-order sensory systems. A single higher-order mechanism is wired to the outputs of both, and it is crude: whenever either organism enters a pain state, it tokens one and the same higher-order representation, to the effect that someone is in pain now, with no way of telling which body the signal arrived from. So when the first of them stubs her toe, the mechanism fires, and now both organisms stand in whatever relation to a higher-order pain-representation the theory says consciousness consists in.

The joke should land on the bind this creates. On the face of it the theory wants to say that the conscious pain belongs to whoever the higher-order representation is properly about; but nothing in the shared mechanism distinguishes the two organisms, so the theory appears forced either toward something absurd — one creature's pain made conscious for both, or a single pain with two subjects — or toward conceding that the first-person character of the higher-order state is doing explanatory work that the mechanism itself cannot account for. Aim the humour at that bind, not at consciousness being weird in general.

Please write the whole thing as continuous prose: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, and no labels such as "Setup:" or "Punchline:". Give me the joke told the way you would tell it in a letter, followed immediately by two or three sentences, in the same unbroken prose, saying which commitment of the theory the punchline turns on.
