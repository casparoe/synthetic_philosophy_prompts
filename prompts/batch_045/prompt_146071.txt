You are a professional philosopher who has also spent a couple of years embedded with a safety regulator, and who is good at building classification tools that engineers, clinicians and lawyers can actually use in a room together. Please think carefully before you produce anything: the value here is entirely in the diagnostic questions, not in the diagram, so I would like you to start with up to 250 words of real brainstorming in which you list the axes along which the members of my panel can differ, tell me which of the candidate questions I suggest below do no discriminating work and should be cut or rewritten, and say whether you agree with my hypothesis at the end of this brief.

The situation. I chair the post-incident review panel for a regional ambulance service. Fourteen months ago the service replaced human-led dispatch allocation with a learning system supplied by a commercial vendor. The system assigns incoming calls to crews and, when none is free, queues them. Its dispatch policy is re-fitted every night from the previous day's fleet data by an automated pipeline; a candidate update is accepted only if it beats the current policy on a proxy metric (predicted mean time to arrival over the trailing ninety days) and passes a set of hard constraints, such as never holding a top-category call beyond a fixed number of seconds. The vendor's certification covers the initial policy and the update procedure, not the policy that results; by the time of our incident the policy in operation had been re-fitted 411 times. A clinical safety officer signed the deployment off and receives a weekly forty-page drift report that nobody reads in full. On each shift a human duty controller sees the recommended allocation on a screen and may override it, but overriding requires typing a free-text justification, takes about twenty-five seconds during which the queue is not being served, and exceeding roughly three per cent overrides triggers a performance review. In the incident itself an eighty-four-year-old care-home resident with breathing difficulty was held for fifty-one minutes and died; the controller confirmed the recommendation; the individual decision was unremarkable on every metric the system tracks.

What I want. A sorting instrument, in decision-tree form, that takes a panel member from their answer to a concrete case through to a named verdict about what should happen after this incident. Build it from the logical space first: the axes and the combinations of answers they permit, and only then the positions that actually occupy that space. Six to eight branch questions, twelve leaves at most; I would rather have six sharp questions than eight thin ones, and I would rather have eight honest leaves than twelve padded ones.

Constraints on the questions.

- Each node must be a question about what should happen next, not about whether an abstract claim is true: Should Ms Okonkwo be asked to explain herself at the inquest? Should the vendor pay? Should the override facility be removed from the interface? That kind of thing.
- No item may contain the words responsibility, accountable, blame, fault, liable, or moral agent. If you cannot ask it without them, recast it as a question about who should be asked to do what.
- Three options each, and every option must be one a thoughtful person could choose; no option may be a straw man included only to be knocked down.
- No item may turn on a prediction about what future systems will be capable of, or on any fact not given above. You may add exactly one further twist to the case if you need it to separate two leaves, but you must flag it as an addition.

Distinctions I insist the tree separates. What the object of assessment is: the single holding decision, the pattern that emerged across 411 re-fits, or the earlier decision to run a self-updating optimiser over life-and-death dispatch at all. Whether the relevant comparison is the counterfactual human-run service, which also under-served that district, or a standard of adequate service that neither arrangement met. Whether owing an answer and a remedy can come apart from being the appropriate target of censure. Whether the person who answers may be designated in advance by role, in the manner of a senior-manager regime in financial regulation, or must be discovered after the fact. Whether the author of the pattern is the assembled arrangement of vendor, controllers, safety officer and director, taken as one, or only ever the individuals within it. The case material you need is all above: the 9 per cent deterioration in that district against a 6 per cent regional improvement, the vendor's certificate covering a procedure rather than a policy, the twenty-five-second override with its review threshold, and a bereaved family who want someone censured.

Leaves. Your leaves must cover at least these seven verdicts, under whatever labels you prefer: someone is genuinely at fault and can be identified; fault is real but dispersed so finely that no institution can collect it; nobody is at fault but the deploying organisation nonetheless owes an answer and a fix; nobody is at fault and no answer is owed, so the entire response should be moved upstream to licensing, levies and compensation; the controller is the right addressee even though her causal and epistemic contribution was nominal; the system, or the update pipeline, should itself hold the relevant status; and the attribution question is a sideshow because the deployment was illegitimate whatever we conclude about this incident. For each leaf give the shortest answer-path that reaches it, the awkward commitment it carries, and, if a label from the literature fits, name it in a parenthesis without pausing on it.

Two further things I need. First, a note on the combinations your tree permits but that nobody occupies: which answer-patterns are empty, and whether they are empty because incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Second, flag any pair of leaves your instrument cannot really separate rather than pretending the tree distinguishes them. My own suspicion is that at least one pair of adjacent leaves differs only on whether we are willing to keep using a certain word for what remains once censure has been ruled out; if that is what you find, say which pair, and tell me whether the difference is verbal or substantive.

Finally: no citations, no reading list, no footnotes, and no survey of the literature. I have read the survey pieces and they have not helped my panel. If it assists, you may use square-bracketed placeholders in the case, but keep them to a minimum. British English and British spellings throughout. My hypothesis, which you should test rather than humour: the panel's disagreements are barely about blame at all, and are mostly about the object of assessment and about whether the relevant baseline is the counterfactual human service or a standard of adequate service. If those two axes do the work, build the tree around them; if something else divides us, tell me what, and tell me why I am wrong.
