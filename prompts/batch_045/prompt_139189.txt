I'm writing up my position on mental content for a reading group and I want it stress-tested before I present. The package is below, stated in my own terms because I don't want to argue about labels.

General commitments:

(a) What makes a neural state a representation, and gives it the content it has, is its biological proper function. A state represents whatever it is that state's function to carry information about, and its function is fixed by the history of selection that produced and maintained the mechanism generating it.

(b) The reason I want a theory in this family — honestly the only reason — is that it makes room for misrepresentation. A frog's detector can be *about* flies and still fire at a moving pellet. Any account that identifies content with whatever in fact currently causes the state, or with whatever the state in fact reliably tracks, erases the difference between getting it right and getting it wrong, and I take that to be fatal.

(c) The standard worry about (a) is that there are indefinitely many true descriptions of what a mechanism was selected for, and they yield different contents. My fix: content is fixed by the property whose detection was causally responsible for the mechanism's differential reproduction — the property that did the explanatory work in the actual selection process. And I want that spelled out in purely causal terms, with nothing semantic or intentional smuggled in.

(d) I don't want content to be idle. It is supposed to be the thing that explains what the organism does: the frog snaps *because* its state is about a fly. So content properties have to be properties that do causal-explanatory work here and now, and I hold that two systems that are physically identical at a time have identical causal powers.

Case verdicts I'm also committed to:

(i) A ground-nesting bee has a looming-detector that triggers a dive. It fires at predatory wasps and also, constantly, at falling leaves. The dive is cheap and missing a wasp is fatal, so what selection actually rewarded was firing across the whole class of looming things. I want to say the bee's state is about wasps, and that when a leaf sets it off the bee misrepresents the leaf as a wasp.

(ii) Content arises fast. A child tokens, for the first time, a concept of *someone who can be trusted with a secret*; a grandmaster's visual system comes to respond to a board configuration it has never encountered. These states have content of the same kind as the frog's, not a second-rate or derivative substitute for it.

(iii) Suppose some chance process assembles, molecule for molecule, a duplicate of a frog in the act of snapping at a pellet — no lineage, no designer, no selection anywhere in the causal history. I find it preposterous to say the duplicate's state isn't about anything. I want to say it is about a fly, exactly as the original's is.

(iv) Words, diagrams, and the sentences I am currently thinking have content too, and I don't want a separate story for them. Whatever account I give for the frog should extend to these without a second, unrelated mechanism.

What I'd like: an audit, not a lecture. Which of these are genuinely in tension, and how badly — rank them rather than flagging everything as equally problematic. I have a vague suspicion that (b), (c) and (i) cannot all stand together and that (a), (d) and (iii) cannot either, but I can't tell which commitment is doing the work and which one is expendable, and I'd rather you didn't assume I'm attached to (a) just because I listed it first. Say explicitly which pairs merely look like they conflict and dissolve once you spell out machinery a view like (a) already has available. For each real tension, say what would have to give and what giving it up costs me elsewhere in the package. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most.
