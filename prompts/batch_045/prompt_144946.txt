ok random one: say Rawls hadnt died in 2002 and instead had to watch the last twenty-odd years of religion-and-politics happen — the postliberal/integralist Catholics saying the neutral liberal state is just a comprehensive doctrine in a disguise and that the state should be aimed at the common good including the true religion, the huge rise of the religiously unaffiliated in basically every rich democracy, and the endless fights over religious exemptions where a shrinking devout minority asks for carve-outs from generally applicable laws (wedding vendors, religious employers, that sort of thing).

Imagine he writes a 2025 sequel to the 1997 Revisited paper. I want the actual content of that paper, not a summary of what he already said. Two made-up cases I want it to handle:

(1) A church we'll call the Assembly of the Second Word. Completely orthodox theologically — sin, grace, an afterlife, the works — except that it teaches that God commands its members to give only publicly accessible reasons when they advocate politically. Every political position it takes comes with a secular argument its members sincerely endorse. No scripture in the public square, ever, not even alongside.

(2) A purely non-theistic movement, call it The Reckoning, with a canonical text, weekly assemblies, a creed, formal procedures for excommunicating heretics, and a decent supply of martyrs. It wants the legal accommodations and exemptions that religions get, and its members say their commitments are every bit as load-bearing as a monk's.

So: would Rawls treat (1) as the model of a reasonable religion or as a pathology? If a religion fully internalizes the proviso he added in the 97 paper — nonpublic reasons are fine as long as proper political ones show up in due course — does it stop being a religion with any independent political voice, and does he care, or is that a feature to him? For (2): does the work get done by the category of comprehensive doctrine, or does he need something special about religion, and would he even think the accommodation question is the sort of thing public reason can settle?

And the bigger one: which of those three 21st-century developments would actually force him to revise? Would he revise the idea of the reasonable — does reasonable track what people in a society actually believe, so that once the nones are a majority traditional theism starts sliding into the unreasonable column — or would he say the whole apparatus was never meant to be hostage to sociology? And does it matter that the guy was writing an intensely religious senior thesis as an undergrad and then lost his faith in the war?

Two final things. First, mark which moves come straight from what he actually wrote (say roughly which work) and which ones are you extrapolating, and put a rough confidence on the extrapolations. Second: if I've got Rawls wrong somewhere — the proviso doesn't do what I think it does, or he actually addressed accommodations somewhere I've missed, or one of my two cases is just confused — say so plainly instead of politely playing along. And please actually commit to what he'd argue rather than giving me a both-sides summary.
