>
> Put more carefully: the child's data are consistent with the target grammar and with many others that differ from it systematically, and nothing in the data — no correction, no explicit rule, no statistical pattern in the sample — distinguishes them. Therefore the child must contribute something: a built-in restriction on which grammars are candidates at all. That contribution is what "Universal Grammar" names, and it is what makes the argument nativist.
>
> This is in essence a formal result rather than an empirical one. Gold's identification-in-the-limit theorem shows that a learner given only positive examples cannot converge on the correct grammar for the relevant class of languages; some restriction on the hypothesis space is unavoidable. The poverty of the stimulus is that theorem applied to children, with "the class of languages" read as the class of grammars compatible with experience and "positive examples" read as the child's sample. And since nothing in that result depends on which sentences any particular child hears, the argument is a priori: it concerns what is learnable in principle, not what happens to be in a corpus.
>
> That is also why the machine-learning objection fails. A neural network is not acquiring language from nothing. It comes with an architecture, a training objective and an optimisation procedure, and together those are a bias over hypotheses. Showing that a learner with a bias succeeds does not show that a learner without one would; if anything it illustrates the argument.
