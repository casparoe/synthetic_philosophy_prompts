I am a retired systematic zoologist: forty years of species delimitation, character-based diagnoses, type material, operational taxonomic units, and revision cycles. I have been reading the claim that psychiatric categories are not indifferent natural kinds but interactive — human — kinds, moving targets, because the people classified can learn how they are classified and change in response, which then feeds back into the classification (Hacking's looping effects); together with its principal rival, that categories such as schizophrenia are stable kinds grounded in neural mechanism, our present syndromes being crude symptom-based proxies for them.

Please restate both positions as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I know: syndrome versus clade; splitting on visible characters versus splitting on genealogy; a diagnosis as a delimitation hypothesis subject to revision; and, for the feedback itself, populations that shift because they are studied — trap-shyness and habituation, fisheries-induced evolution, resistance under sustained selection. Then the residue report: what translates cleanly, what survives only under distortion, and what cannot be said in my vocabulary at all.

Two points matter to me especially. First, whether the vocabulary is itself loaded: does it build in the assumption that a taxon's job is to track something the organisms are indifferent to, so that the question is settled by fiat rather than by argument? Second, whether the NIMH's Research Domain Criteria programme — re-anchoring classification in neural circuits and dimensions rather than in reported symptoms — is, in these terms, an attempt to anchor the taxa in something the classified cannot see, and whether that could actually escape looping or would only displace it. Where the residue is large, tell me whether that indicts my vocabulary or the psychiatric debate.
