I'm retired — used to run a water quality lab — and I've got into philosophy lately, mostly through a reading group at the public libary. I also staff a table there twice a week: people bring in hospital letters, lab printouts, insurance forms, a contractor's quote, a supplement ad, a text saying their account has been suspended, and ask what it actually says and whether they should do anything. Twenty-odd of those a morning, two or three minutes each, no one to consult, and a library rule that we don't give medical or legal advice (the coordinator handles anything that turns into a complaint).

I've been reading about lying versus merely misleading: you can leave someone with a false belief without saying anything you take to be false, and a lot of people want to say that's a real moral difference, with the lie being the worse thing. I half believe that and half don't, becuase the cases I actually face don't sort neatly. What I need is something I can use at the table. Please turn whatever is actually solid in that literature into a decision procedure: tests in the order I should run them, in plain words my fellow volunteers could run too (none of them have read any of it, so no jargon — I'd have to explain it to them); what counts as a good enough answer to each test; where the procedure just says no; and where it has to stop and hand me a values call instead. Tell me too which distinctions you had to drop to make it short, and whether losing them costs me anything.

The things I keep tripping on:

- I often know more than they do about how these documents work, so their question may be the wrong question, and staying quiet is itself a choice.
- Words I use precisely — significant, normal, certified, safe — mean something looser to them, so I can say something I'd defend to a colleague and still send them off believing something I think is false.
- Sometimes they want it written down or emailed so they can forward it to a son or a landlord. Then nobody can ask a follow-up and the sentence has to do the work on its own.

Then run the procedure on three cases:

1. A man of 82 slides a lab printout across and asks "is everything normal?" The page says "within normal limits," but the reference range printed on it is the one for adults under 40, and his number only looks fine against that. He hasn't asked me anything about ranges.
2. A woman brings in a water filter box with a big certification mark and asks whether it takes the lead out. Say the small print says the mark only covers what the unit adds to the water, not what it removes. "It's certified" is perfectly true.
3. A regular asks me to email her two sentences about the flood map for her street, to forward to a nephew who's buying a house there — someone I'll never meet and who'll skim it.

At least one of them should come out with an answer I won't like. I suspect it's the one where my habit of technically-true phrasing turns out to be worse than plain lying. And say what the procedure can't do at all.
