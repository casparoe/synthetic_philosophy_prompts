Two members of a regional flood-risk committee asked me to referee an argument I could not settle. They were deciding how to discount benefits and costs of a storm-surge barrier with a 400-year horizon. (1) The philosopher said the analysis must set pure time preference to zero: discounting a future person's welfare merely because it occurs later is temporal discrimination, so the only permissible discounts are for extinction risk and the opportunity cost of capital, and neither of those is pure time preference. (2) The economist replied that this ignores who actually bears the cost: the current, consenting population must pay the tax now, while future beneficiaries cannot vote, reciprocate, or hold anyone accountable, and a positive pure rate is just the political expression of that enforceable asymmetry rather than a claim that future welfare matters less in the abstract. (3) The philosopher answered that the accountability point proves too much; by the successor mirror test — the test that we must apply to our successors the same discounting rule we would have accepted from predecessors who could not be held accountable by us — it would be wrong to discount later generations, just as we condemn predecessor generations for discounting risks that fell on us. (4) The economist said the mirror test is the wrong consistency condition, because the planner is not a single transtemporal agent behind a veil of ignorance but an agent of an existing political community with a defeasible special obligation to its own members, so preferring present constituents is like a parent permissibly favoring her own child: it is a permission to weight a special relationship, not a denial of the child's or the future person's equal worth. Please referee the exchange. For each of the four moves, say whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, and briefly justify the rating; then say who has the better overall argument and what, if anything, both parties are missing.
