I teach a third-year seminar in formal epistemology, and I have a seventy-five-minute slot in which to get van Fraassen's Reflection principle in front of students who handle conditional probabilities fluently, who have been through the synchronic Dutch-book argument for probabilism, and who have been taught conditionalization as the default updating rule — but who have never met a diachronic argument and have never been asked to treat their own future credence as something they can now have a conditional attitude toward. A third of the room are joint honours students in computer science; they will have met dynamic consistency in sequential decision problems and will try to assimilate Reflection to it. Do not explain Reflection to me. I want a plan for how I should teach it in that slot.

Give me the order in which the components must appear, and for each, what it depends on: conditional credence as a present attitude rather than a ratio one computes later; the buying and selling of conditional bets across time; the statement of the principle; the diachronic Dutch book; the Ulysses-shaped worry about a future self you can see coming; and whatever else you think belongs in the sequence. I am genuinely undecided about where the Dutch book goes: presenting it first makes the principle feel like a requirement of rationality, but I suspect it also invites the inference that exploitability and irrationality are the same thing, which I would then have to undo. Tell me what the order buys and what it costs.

I want one case to carry the session, and I want it vetted rather than merely supplied. My candidate: a physician who knows that tomorrow she will be handed a screening result that she currently has the base rate to interpret correctly and will, through exhaustion, fail to use — so that she knows in advance the direction and roughly the size of a change she now regards as unwarranted. Tell me what such an example must have and what it must lack in order to carry the principle, its motivation, and its failure mode all at once, and whether mine has the wrong features. In particular: in my own notes I have been sorting ways a future self comes apart from a present one into leaks, where the future self simply has less of your evidence and lands on the mixture, and noise, where the future self has all of your evidence and processes it worse. My sense is that Reflection looks close to compulsory for leaks and close to false for noise, and that if students feel that asymmetry I will have taught them the thing rather than the formula. Is that asymmetry real, is it the right axis to build on, and if not, what should replace it? I have tried building this session around Sleeping Beauty and it has never survived the hour.

Here are the misconceptions I expect, and I would like you to tell me which are real, which are artefacts of the way I propose to present the material, and at exactly what point in the sequence each should be headed off — before it can form, at the moment it forms, or after: that Reflection simply says your current credence must equal your expected future credence; that it tells you to adopt your future credence now; that it is conditionalization under another name; that it forbids changing your mind; that the Dutch book shows violating it costs you money for sure against any bookie; and, from the computer scientists, that it is the same claim as dynamic consistency. Give me also the one I have not listed.

Then a check: one question for the last five minutes that separates a student who can state the principle from one who sees why it is contested, and say what an answer would have to contain to count.

Tell me too what I should openly decline to cover in seventy-five minutes, and how to decline it without leaving the impression that the omitted material is settled.

Finally, separate in your plan what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this — the formalism and what the principle does and does not entail, as against whether it is a requirement of rationality, whether the diachronic argument supports it, and how known-future-irrationality should be handled. Where your recommendation is a pedagogical bet rather than something the literature settles, mark it as such. I do not need pointers to further reading; spend the whole answer on the sequence, the example, and the misconception map.
