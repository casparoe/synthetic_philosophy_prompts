I've been reading about punishment on my own, with no formal background in philosophy, and I keep running into the same stock contrast: the retributivist punishes because the offender deserves it, the consequentialist punishes because of what punishing brings about, and every example I find has the consequentialist as the harsher of the two — punishing someone innocent, or punishing harder than anyone deserves for the sake of deterrence. I want the case where it runs the other way, built so that it doesn't cheat.

So: one paragraph, in an ordinary setting a real manager, board, or headteacher could face, in which both theories agree the person is guilty, genuinely culpable, and should be punished — and in which the retributivist nonetheless demands a heavier sanction than the consequentialist. Some constraints, because the cheap versions are easy to build: the consequentialist may not go soft because of doubt about guilt, because punishment would be ineffective here, because of harm to innocent third parties (no dependents, no service collapsing), or because of good the offender would do if left in place, or because he has reformed or is remorseful; and the retributivist may not be punishing him for something extra he did, or out of a taste or character trait of his own. The extra severity has to fall out of what counts as an amount of punishment on each view.

My hunch about the mechanism is that desert may have to be measured in what the punishment actually costs the person undergoing it, while the consequentialist measures the same sanction by what it does to everyone else — so an offender who is unusually unbothered by the sanction that would ordinarily fit, for a reason an employer could actually know rather than guess, forces the retributivist to reach further up the ladder while the consequentialist is already satisfied. If that mechanism does not work, get the result another way.

After the scenario: state what each theory recommends and the reasoning that gets it there; name the single feature of the case doing the work; and show that both theories collapse back onto the same sanction if that feature is removed, so I can see the disagreement was generated by the theories rather than stipulated into the setup. Finally, I want to try this on a retributivist who will resist it: give me the strongest objection available to him that changes none of the facts of the case, and say whether you think it works.
