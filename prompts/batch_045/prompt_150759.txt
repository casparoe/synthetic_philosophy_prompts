I am teaching a graduate seminar this term on Nietzsche's 1874 essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, and I am preparing the closing lecture, in which I want to do something slightly imprudent: run his threefold distinction between the monumental, the antiquarian and the critical uses of the past, together with his remarks on plastic power, on the bounded horizon, and on the necessity of the unhistorical, against a case he could not possibly have foreseen, namely the training of large language models on the digitized textual past. The seminar is joint-listed with computer science, and one of my colleagues there keeps pressing me with a question I cannot settle on my own: if Nietzsche's complaint is that a culture can sicken from an excess of history, from an indiscriminate appetite for knowledge of the past that overwhelms the capacity to act and to forget, then surely a system trained on essentially everything ever written, with a steadily growing share of that material written by earlier systems, is the most extreme instance of that sickness ever built. I find the claim rhetorically attractive and intellectually too cheap, so I would like you to reason as a careful Nietzschean would and tell me what he would actually be expected to say, granting that he accepts the general framework of the essay and is being asked about our situation.

To make the question concrete, imagine that a philanthropic foundation with what it calls a duty to the human archive had invited him to chair its advisory board and to rank five competing training regimes, and that the technical facts were explained to him as follows. Canon Ten would discard everything except a curated list of roughly ten thousand works judged the greatest human achievements, with the consequence that the resulting systems are impressive inside the genres of the canon and nearly useless outside them, and that the list itself is made by an unelected committee. Cold Storage would train only on material at least twenty-five years old, would preserve that material byte for byte in perpetuity, and would never revise it; the systems it produces speak in the idioms and norms of a vanished period and cannot address anything current. Excision would keep the unbounded, ever-growing corpus but would continually identify and delete every lineage of thought judged to have done harm, with the result that the deletions are permanently contested and that the material left behind frequently loses the context needed to understand it. Present Tense would refuse the archive altogether and train only on the last eighteen months of text; because so much recent text is itself model-generated, its systems progressively lose contact with human usage and degrade. And the status quo, which the foundation calls Total Capture, trains on whatever text is available, including the rising tide of synthetic text.

What I want is a prediction of his verdict rather than a commentary on the essay. Please tell me how he would characterize Total Capture in his own terms, whether he would see the self-digesting quality of Present Tense as a symptom of the sickness or as a cure for it, and how he would rank all five regimes, best to worst, by the degree to which he would expect each to serve life rather than merely accumulate the past, with a single sentence of justification for each placing and, for each regime, an indication of where it tips from a serving use of history into a damaging one. I would also like to know whether he would expect a system that cannot literally forget to have any plastic power at all, whether he would suspect that the whole question is badly posed because no such system is the kind of thing that can have a horizon, and which of the five, if any, he would recommend funding. Please write this as continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, in the manner of a letter to a colleague who has asked you for a considered opinion; a few paragraphs of connected argument, not a list with commentary.
