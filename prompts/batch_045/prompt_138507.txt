My old supervisor has been sitting on a manuscript about Gongsun Long for something like ten years — the School of Names, the White Horse dialogue, and a chapter that I think is going to be about the hardness-and-whiteness bit — and I'm finally writing a response to it. Before I do, I want to guess what she'll say about a case she's never ruled on, becuase I'd rather not walk into the seminar and have her settle it in thirty seconds flat. Her verdicts so far, from the manuscript and from years of reading group:

- a white horse is not a horse — she's completely happy with it, and gets irritated when people say it's just an equivocation on two senses of "is". In the translation we use, the two moves she leans on are "'Horse' is what designates shape. 'White' is what designates color. What designates color is not what designates shape" and then the seeking bit: "If one seeks a horse, yellow and black horses may all be delivered. If one seeks a white horse, yellow and black horses may not be delivered."
- a brown horse is not a horse — also fine; she generalizes past the one example without blinking.
- point at an actual white horse standing in a field and ask "is that a horse?" and she says yes, obviously — the thesis was never that white horses don't exist or that they aren't horses, and she thinks the objector basically gets her to concede that much.
- a horse is not a white horse — also fine; she says the "not" runs both ways.
- the hard white stone: the hardness and the whiteness are separate, you get one by touch and one by sight and neither of them is the stone, and she insists this is the same point as the horse case rather than a different one.
- a horse is not a horse — here she stops and says no. The paradox needs two different names to get going, and she thinks the Hui Shi paradoxes people always wheel out are the same insight pushed to the limit, not a licence to say any old pair of terms is a "not".

Here's the case I want to put to her. Imagine a kingdom where every single horse is white — not by any law of nature, just because the whole breeding stock goes back to one white stallion, and there are brown horses over the border. In that kingdom, ask for a horse and you get a white one; ask for a white horse and you get exactly the same animal; there is no way of going and fetching something that answers to one request and not the other. Does "a white horse is not a horse" still hold there?

What I'm after isn't really the answer, it's the machinery. As far as I can tell at least two quite different rules fit everything she's said so far: one where the compound term picks out form-plus-color and that's just never the same thing as the bare form, so the verdict shouldn't care what the local horses happen to look like; and one that runs off the seeking test, so that once the two requests can't come apart in what would satisfy them, the "not" ought to collapse. Plus maybe a third that's just tracking whether the two terms pick out the same class of things. So: which rules actually fit her whole track record and not just the headline case, which way does each of them go in the white kingdom, which one would you bet she's running and why, and — the bit I keep going in cirlces on — what's the one further case that would flush out which rule it is?

Two candidates I've got, and I honestly can't tell whether either is the right probe: (i) the same setup but where horses just are white, necessarily, no brown ones possible anywhere; (ii) does the thing run upwards — is a horse not an animal, on her view? Tell me which of these (if either) actually separates the rules, which pair of rules each one separates, and if neither does the job, what case would.
