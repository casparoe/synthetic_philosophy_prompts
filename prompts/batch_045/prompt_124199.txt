I'm a PhD student in early modern philosophy, and this term I've been taking an independent study on Hume while also running a small graduate reading group on his account of testimony and miracles. We met last night to work through Essay X of the 1748 Philosophical Essays Concerning Human Understanding, and we got stuck on the following passage, which I'd like help understanding. Hume writes:

"A wise Man, therefore, proportions his Belief to the Evidence. In such Conclusions as are founded on an infallible Experience, he expects the Event with the last Degree of Assurance, and regards his past Experience as a full Proof of the future Existence of that Event. In other Cases, he proceeds with more Caution: He weighs the opposite Experiments: He considers which Side is supported by the greatest Number of Experiments: To that Side he inclines, with Doubt and Hesitation; and when at last he fixes his Judgment, the Evidence exceeds not what we properly call Probability. All Probability, then, supposes an Opposition of Experiments and Observations; where the one Side is found to over-balance the other, and to produce a Degree of Evidence, proportion'd to the Superiority. A hundred Instances or Experiments on one Side, and fifty on another, afford a very doubtful Expectation of any Event; tho' a hundred uniform Experiments, with only one contradictory one, does reasonably beget a very strong Degree of Assurance. In all Cases, we must balance the opposite Experiments, where they are opposite, and deduct the lesser Number from the greater, in order to know the exact Force of the superior Evidence."

As I read it, Hume is giving a general rule for proportioning belief: conclusions supported by uniform experience count as proofs, while more contested matters of fact yield only probability, and in probabilistic cases we are to set the opposing experiments against one another and subtract the smaller number from the larger. The reading group's confusion—and the reason I'm writing—came when one member proposed a hypothetical that I have not been able to stop thinking about. Suppose a remote valley produces detailed, apparently independent testimony from a thousand eyewitnesses to a resurrection: each witness gives a coherent and internally consistent report, and there is no sign of fraud, coercion, or ordinary error. Unbeknownst to the witnesses, however, all of them were exposed to a rare fungal toxin that reliably causes vivid, highly specific shared hallucinations of precisely this kind, so that the seeming independence of the reports is illusory and the true common cause of the reports is the toxin rather than the alleged miraculous event. A little later in the essay Hume characterizes a miracle roughly as a violation of a law of nature brought about by a particular volition of the Deity or by some invisible agent, so this is meant to be a case in which, if the toxin hypothesis were false, the witnesses would be reporting a genuine miracle.

What I would find helpful is an explanation of how the passage itself, rather than later Hume scholarship, should be applied here. In particular, how should Hume's contrast between proof and probability classify the two sides of this case? The uniform experience that people do not rise from the dead looks like a proof on Hume's own terms, while the testimony looks like an enormous number of experiments on the other side—except that, because of the hidden common cause, those reports are not independent trials and may really be one experiment repeated many times over, or one large confounded observation. When Hume instructs us to balance the opposite experiments and deduct the lesser number from the greater, does the hidden toxin mean the thousand reports should be collapsed into a single contrary experiment, or even discounted entirely, so that the proof against the miracle remains overwhelming? What exactly does an experiment have to be, on the passage's view, in order to count on the side of the testimony: a sincere report, an independent report, or something else? I would be very grateful if you could walk through the passage's logic carefully and tell me what verdict about the rationality of believing the resurrection follows from reading Hume's balancing procedure in that way. Please don't give me a bibliography, a reading list, or a survey of the secondary literature; I only want help understanding what the quoted passage commits Hume to and how a Humean ought to think through the valley case.
