I'm in a metaphysics module and we've spent about three weeks on Goodman's new riddle of induction, and our seminar has got properly stuck — three sessions now where everyone says the same thing slightly louder. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict, and I've got a hypothesis that nobody will engage with.

Setup so we're on the same page: grue applies to a thing iff it's first observed before 2030 and green, or it's never observed before 2030 and blue. Every emerald we've checked is green, so every emerald we've checked is grue too; both generalisations fit the data perfectly, and we all go with green. Fine.

The seminar splits three ways:

(a) the entrenchment people: green wins only because green is better entrenched in our language and practice; grow up speaking a language with grue and bleen as basic and you'd project grue and be none the worse off, and there's nothing deeper to say.
(b) the naturalness people: green picks out a real respect of similarity, grue is gerrymandered, and the relevant metaphysical fact is doing the work — the sort of thing people gesture at with talk of "eligible" referents, or of carving at the joints rather than merely describing.
(c) the pragmatist / learning-theory people: projectibility isn't a property of predicates at all but of a whole inductive strategy given our aims, and green wins because a learner biased towards green-shaped hypotheses does better over the sequence of situations we actually end up in.

My hypothesis: this isn't one disagreement, it's three different questions under one name, and the (b) bit is secretly partly empirical and nobody is treating it as such. When someone says green is natural and grue isn't, are they claiming something about the world, something about human similarity space, or something about the track record of strategies? Those have completely different tests, and I think people slide between them.

Then my tutor threw a spanner in by asking what happens if we drop the time threshold, and we made up two variants:

- grex: applies to a thing iff it's green and north of the equator, or blue and south of it. All our observations were made in the UK, so all the data fits "all emeralds are grex" just as well as "all emeralds are green" — and nothing changes colour anywhere, there's no 2030 switch, the two hypotheses just disagree about unexamined emeralds abroad. If green still feels obviously more projectible here, then whatever is going on isn't really about time or about objects changing.
- grell: green for absolutely everything except ten specific named emeralds sitting unexamined in a vault somewhere, which are blue. No time index, no change, finitely specified in advance, and it differs from green in only ten places.

I think these break different things. grell kills any answer that leans on grue being open-ended or infinitely disjunctive, and it makes the entrenchment story awkward, since grell is basically as entrenched as green, while pushing hard towards some kind of simplicity answer — which I'm suspicious of. grex makes the naturalness story awkward, because north/south is as real a distinction as green/blue if you happen to be a geographer, so "gerrymandered" can't just mean "a distinction I don't happen to care about". And (c) starts to look like the only one tracking anything, since grex would be fine for prediction inside the UK and a disaster the moment you go abroad.

What I want from you:

1. What kind of stalemate is this actually? Is my three-questions-under-one-name thing right, or is the disagreement substantive and I'm just impatient? Which bits are verbal — are people really fighting about the words "projectible", "natural", "entrenched" — and which are differences about what counts as data (does the fact that we do project green and do succeed count as evidence, or is it the very thing to be explained)? Which bits are bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for?
2. Is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work? I have a vague sense that there's a result in computational learning theory — the you-can't-learn-without-an-inductive-bias sort of thing — that is either exactly the new riddle in formal dress or totally irrelevant to it, and I cant tell which. Don't name papers, I can't look anything up; just describe what such a result would have to say to matter here, and what it wouldn't settle.
3. Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a change of question, an experiment — or is this one of those where the honest outcome is that there are two or three answers and we teach all of them?

Oh, and if one of my two variants is a bad test case because it secretly smuggles in what's at issue, tell me which and why. I'd definately rather find that out now than in the essay.
