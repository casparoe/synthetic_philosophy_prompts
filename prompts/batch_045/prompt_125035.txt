so a few of us at work started a very dumb lunchtime philosophy club — mostly backend engineers, one designer, nobody with any actual philosophy training — and we have gotten completely, embarrassingly stuck on Buridan's ass. The donkey exactly midway between two identical piles of hay, no reason to go left or right, starves. We've now spent three weeks on it and I can't tell whether we're stuck on something real or whether we're just four people using one word for four different things. The group split roughly like this: Dan says the case is empty, there's always *some* difference — a gust of wind, which pile he saw first, the flea on his nose — so the whole literature is arguing over a fake case and hard cases make bad law. Priya says the real question is whether a will can act without a sufficient reason at all: if it can, that's what freedom is, and if it can't then we're machines and the donkey's death is just what determinism looks like when reasons balance. Two of us say fine, obviously you can just *pick*, but then we argue about whether that pick is rational or merely caused — whether 'I flipped a coin' is a reason or an admission that there wasn't one. And someone else says any old tiebreaker counts, so once you let 'pick the left one' in as a reason there was never a tie to begin with, and the puzzle was never about choice at all but about what gets to count as a reason. I also whiteboarded a distinction of my own that nobody has commented on: what I called flat ties (the options really are identical in every respect), noisy ties (they differ but I have no way to tell which is better), and silent ties (I know they're equal and I still just want the left one). It felt useful at the time but I have no idea whether it's a real distinction or whether I've just invented three words for one thing.

My own guesses, offered as guesses: (a) it's verbal, and the whole fight is between 'reason' as in *a consideration that justifies the choice to someone else* and 'reason' as in *whatever actually moved the mechanism*, and both sides are right about their own thing; (b) it's a hidden empirical question that's already been answered, because nothing in nature actually freezes — my dog does not starve between two identical bowls of kibble, and if the answer is 'agents don't in fact paralyse, they just act,' then the interesting residue is purely normative; (c) it's free will in a costume, since every time I look anything up online it turns into a free will argument within about two clicks, which makes me suspicious; (d) there's a regress hiding in it — if you're allowed to pick arbitrarily, are you also allowed to pick arbitrarily *which* arbitrary procedure to use, and does that go down forever or does it stop somewhere? There's also the thing that makes me most suspicious of the whole debate: in code a tie is a non-problem, you break it deterministically or you pick at random and nobody thinks the sort function was irrational, so maybe what we're really fighting about is what an explanation of an action has to look like, or what we want 'rational' to mean.

So I'd like a diagnosis, not a verdict and definitely not a survey of positions. Which of my four guesses is closest to what's actually going on, which is a red herring, and are Dan and Priya even answering the same question under the word 'choice'? Tell me where the disagreement is substantive and where it's about words, whether there's a factual or formal question doing the work, and whether my flat/noisy/silent thing helps or whether it quietly smuggles in the answer. Then — given whatever you think the diagnosis is — say what would actually count as progress: a distinction somebody should draw, a formal result, an experiment, a change of question, or an argument that we should expect no progress and why. Two constraints: I don't have library or journal access and I'm not going to chase references, so please don't give me a reading list or names to look up, just argue it from the positions themselves; and please be blunt about anywhere I've got hold of the wrong end, since I have to go back to these people on Thursday and I'd rather be corrected now than in front of them.
