Something I keep going back and forth on and would like help getting straight.

A bunch of the debate spaces I hang around in have a rule that you're not allowed to attack a position until you've stated it back in a version the person who holds it would agree to. The strong version says you should be able to set out the other side's case well enough that someone reading it cold couldn't tell which side you're on, and some places treat passing that test as a precondition for having an opinion worth hearing at all. I think the same idea shows up in fancier settings too, like citizens' assemblies where the background booklet is supposed to be signed off on by people on both sides before anyone deliberates.

Meanwhile the same people who enforce that rule will also tell you that you should basically never take an opponent's stated reasons at face value: look at their incentives, assume the reasoning is post-hoc, remember that they're performing for an audience. And plenty of institutions build that second attitude in — cross-examination, auditing, red-teaming, trust-but-verify.

What I want is the implicit theory of the first practice. What does requiring charitable reconstruction actually presuppose, about what a political disagreement is and about whether the other side argues in good faith? Concretely, does it need any of these three claims, or can it get by without them: (i) the people on the other side are sincere and believe what they say; (ii) they'd move if the evidence moved; (iii) they aren't picking their framing to win rather than to be accurate. My guess is it needs less than people think — maybe refuting a weak version of a view is just uninformative, and that's the whole justification — but I'd like the real presuppositions separated from the stuff it merely goes naturally with.

Two more things. First, the failure mode I keep seeing: the version that passes the test is often a synthetic thing nobody actually holds, a cleaned-up philosophical cousin of a position whose real support base is much messier, so you end up defeating an artifact you built yourself. Is that predictable from what the practice presupposes, or is it just people doing it badly? And second, is the first practice really in tension with the second, or does it only look that way? It feels like one assumes we share enough to recognise an accurate statement of a view when we see it, and the other assumes we dont.

Last bit: if we swapped the implicit picture for something darker — say, that political belief is mostly identity expression and hardly moves in response to reasons — what would the practice have to turn into, or would it just get dropped?
