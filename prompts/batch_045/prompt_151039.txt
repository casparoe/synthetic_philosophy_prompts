I am in the Lower Sixth, and next week I am giving a fifteen-minute talk to our school's Engineering and Philosophy Society, followed by questions. My thesis is that some artefacts are political in themselves and not merely in the uses to which they are put: an artefact is political when swapping it for a functionally equivalent alternative would redistribute who can do what to whom, and when that redistribution is durable rather than a matter of whoever happens to be holding it today. I want to go beyond the usual textbook case of motorway overpasses built too low for buses, which I will mention only in passing, and defend a lifecycle version of the claim: an artefact can acquire or lose a politics after it is built, through its maintenance regime and through its defaults. My two examples are a pedestrian underpass whose lift is allowed to break and stay broken, so that within a year the route is effectively stairs-only and closed to wheelchair users and anyone with a pushchair, and a homework portal whose submission deadline defaults to nine in the evening and which almost no teacher ever changes, so that pupils with evening jobs or caring responsibilities lose out although no one ever decided that they should. I close with the claim that design and maintenance are therefore political acts and ought to be contestable in the way that rules are.

The audience: our Director of Design and Technology, a chartered engineer before he taught, who holds that a tool is neutral and only the user's intentions are political; our Head of Computer Science, who holds that any politics lies in who owns and writes the code; a politics teacher who accepts that technology serves class interests but thinks calling the artefact itself political is mystification that conceals the owners; and a group of Lower Sixth economists who think the market selects which designs survive, so an artefact's politics, if any, is just revealed preference.
