Now the project.

Background, and why this is live for me. Until last year I spent six years as a research engineer building and defending large simulation models — idealized systems that stand in for messier real ones, where the standing-in has to be argued for case by case. Since starting doctoral work I have found that I cannot unsee a structural parallel: a great deal of scientific reasoning proceeds from a *surrogate* — an imagined or implemented system that is not the target — and the interesting question in both cases is what licenses the transfer. I have become convinced that the epistemology of thought experiments has been built to fit one surrogate shape and quietly generalizes from it, and that this is why the debate keeps returning to the same impasse about whether intuitions about imaginary cases can be reliable.

My half-formed idea, which I need you to turn into a proposal. The template most accounts assume is this: you imagine a single concrete case, you register what you take to happen in it, and the epistemic question is whether that judgment is trustworthy or merely a re-description of what you already believed. That fits thought experiments whose imagined scenario is an *arbitrary instance* of the generalization under test. But a large and philosophically celebrated class works differently: the imagined scenario is a boundary point of the theory's space of models, reached by driving a parameter to a limit — frictionless planes, perfectly rigid bodies, infinitely slow reversible processes, a chain draped over a frictionless wedge, an observer traveling alongside a light beam, a being that sorts molecules one at a time, a cat in a sealed box. These vehicles are frequently not even nomologically possible, and they are certainly not representative instances of anything. So for this class the question is not whether my intuition about the scenario is reliable; it is whether the quantity, relation, or concept the thought experiment probes is well behaved at the boundary. And that question looks different in kind: it is a claim about the structure of the target theory — whether the relevant limit exists, whether the idealization is regular or singular, whether the limiting model retains the feature the argument turns on — and theories can differ on it, and it can be checked.

I want to push this into a stronger and less comfortable thesis than I can currently defend: that when a *limiting-case* thought experiment yields knowledge, the credit belongs to a background structural claim about the theory, the imagination does at most heuristic work, and the historical cases that genuinely surprised people are precisely the ones where the limit is singular — so that the thought experiment is really a probe of whether the theory has a well-defined limiting behavior at all, rather than a window onto what would happen in the imagined situation.
