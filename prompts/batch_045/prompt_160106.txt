hey — please take this seriously and act as a professional philosopher who works on aesthetics and literature: think it through carefully, do a bit of unstructured brainstorming before you settle on an answer, and flag the places where you're genuinely torn instead of papering over them. I'd rather have a messy honest answer than a tidy confident one.

I'm a novelist, between books, and I've got a dumb-but-not-dumb problem I keep going in circles on. For two years I've had a side project: I take two out-of-print books — a 1938 scout handbook and a wholesale drug catalogue, that kind of pairing — and run their text through a script that interleaves their sentences according to a rule I fix in advance (three sentences from A, one from B, or whatever), with a second rule that I never edit the output. Whatever comes out is the piece. Some of them are strange and genuinely moving, and when I've shown them around people say things like "your control of tone is unnerving" and "the way you withhold information is masterful." But I don't control the tone. I don't withhold anything. The line somebody admires might just be whatever sentence happened to follow a heading in the catalogue. Now two offers landed in the same week: a small press wants to publish a selection in their fiction line under my name, and a gallery on my block wants to hang the same pieces as printed sheets on a wall with a wall label. And the thing that's eating at me is that the identical pages seem to be one thing in a room with a label, another thing between covers with my name on the spine, and presumably nothing at all when they're just two decaying books in a library stack — so I dont even know what I'd be claiming if I said yes to either.

My writing group split in a way that hasn't helped. One member says the worry is misplaced: I chose the two source books, I chose the rule, and I decided in advance that I'd stand behind whatever came out — he has a name for it, "delegated authorship": you can hand the small choices over to a procedure and still be the author, so long as you own the result before you've seen it. He says the urinal thing works exactly this way and nobody thinks the guy who did it carved the porcelain. Another member says authorship isn't the interesting question at all; her objection is about what the object asks of a reader. A book in a fiction line invites you to linger on word-level choices, to ask why this word and not that one, and my pieces punish that — there's nothing to find there, so offering them as fiction sets readers up to do something the text can't reward, whereas on a gallery wall nobody reads that way and so nothing is being withheld. I think both of them are onto something real and neither is answering my question. One last wrinkle, the part I keep coming back to: someone I've never met posted a long, careful close reading of one passage online, arguing that the recurrence of a certain phrase builds a cumulative dread and ties it to the rest of my work. It's a good reading — perceptive about the effect — but the recurrence is just arithmetic, and the phrase is a dosage instruction that happens to repeat. I could print the method in an appendix, but I suspect people would read the appendix and then read exactly the same way anyway. So: is publishing this as fiction under my name a kind of fraud, or just what writers do at one remove? Does it matter at all that readers are really moved? Is the gallery version a different thing or the same thing? Do I owe that stranger anything? And underneath all of it — please work out what the actual question is, because I'm fairly sure I haven't asked it right, and then answer that one.
