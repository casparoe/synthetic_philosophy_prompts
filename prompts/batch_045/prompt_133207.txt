Hey — I'm the community member on my hospital's ethics committee (I write software, which I assume is why they asked me to sit on it), and I need a short decision memo I can circulate before Thursday's meeting.

We're trying to write a standing rule for how we spend a fixed budget for a new drug. Two live cases are forcing the issue.

Case one: the same money funds either three patients with a progressive disease — each gets roughly six years of decent life, and without it they decline fast — or about 300 people with a mild chronic condition, each getting maybe three weeks of modest symptom relief. Our health-economics person says the 300-person option actually wins on total QALYs, though barely. Most of the committee thinks that's obviously the wrong answer and wants the three. One member says we're just falling for the identifiable-victim effect and should stick with the arithmetic.

Case two: a screening change that adds about twenty minutes of waiting and a very small extra infection risk to 40,000 patients a year, and is expected to save around two lives a year. Everyone, including the people who hated the QALY math in case one, wants to approve it.

Here's where I'm stuck. Our chair keeps muttering about "the separateness of persons," which I gather is a real philosophy thing but I've never seen it turned into a rule anyone could apply. The version I sketched is: you can't justify one person's loss by adding up a pile of smaller gains to other people — you have to be able to point at someone whose gain is bigger than that one person's loss, and you can't spend the same gain twice. But when I trace it through, that seems to block case two as well as case one, which makes me think I've stated it wrong.

Can you draft the memo? I need a recommendaton at the top, a rule with a baseline and a threshold we could actually apply at the table, verdicts on both cases, and an honest go at the strongest argument against whatever rule you land on — the identifiable-victim worry if you go separatist, or the "then you can't justify any public health measure" one if you don't. Keep it under two pages; the clinicians won't read more.
