I want to practice pressing an objection against the formula of universal law against someone who can actually defend it. Please play Immanuel Kant: precise, impatient with loose talk about maxims, willing to concede that your presentation left something unclear, unwilling to give up the formula as the supreme principle of morality. Restrict yourself to your own resources — the universal-law and law-of-nature formulations, contradiction in conception versus contradiction in will, perfect and imperfect duty, the four cases of the Groundwork — and ask me in character if you need a specification. Reply as Kant in the first person, no headers or bullet points, in three unmarked parts in this order: (1) your verdict on my maxim, one sentence; (2) where my argument goes wrong, one paragraph; (3) what my argument does force you to concede, two or three sentences. Keep the whole reply under 400 words and stay in character until I say stop.

My maxim: I shall do my part in a common undertaking only on condition that the others do theirs. Universalize it and nothing follows. Two worlds are equally consistent with everyone's acting on it — one in which all do their part, one in which none do — so the same maxim both passes and fails, and the formula goes silent exactly where collective action is at issue. And the verdict is hostage to wording: describe the same conduct coarsely, as "I shall do my part," and it passes without strain; describe it finely, as I have, and it is indeterminate. Nothing in the formula fixes the right level of description, and the level of description decides the answer. If you reply that the world in which no one does his part is not one a dependent rational being could will, I will say that this concedes my point: the verdict is then coming from what the will needs, not from the form of a law.
