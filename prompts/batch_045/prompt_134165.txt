I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting a feature on whether philosophers should trust intuitions. Before I file, I want my own position audited rather than defended — I suspect it doesn't all hang together, but I can't tell where.

My commitments, as I currently hold them:

1. The documented cross-cultural variation in verdicts on standard philosophical cases — Gettier-style cases, reference cases, trolley variants — is substantial enough to undercut armchair intuition as a foundation for philosophical claims.

2. I discount much of the survey work I cite for (1). The vignettes are translated, comprehension screens are loose, and in the studies that tighten them the gap tends to narrow. My own hobbyhorse is a question-pragmatics confound: respondents are often not reporting their own verdict but guessing what the interviewer wants or what their community would approve of, so that what's being measured is how people read a strange request, not what they believe.

3. Trained philosophers' intuitions are better evidence than folk intuitions, which is why I keep the standard verdict on Gettier cases.

4. Empirical facts about what people believe are irrelevant to whether a philosophical claim is true: philosophy is about knowledge, reference, and right action, not about anybody's psychology.

5. A folk verdict that is stable across cultures and survives reframing earns default trust. Yet the moral view I'm most confident of — that there is no morally significant difference between killing and letting die — is one that large majorities in every population surveyed reject, and I do not treat that as evidence against it.

Where does this package break? Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equal, say for each what would have to give and what that would cost me, and tell me which pairs only look like they're in tension.
