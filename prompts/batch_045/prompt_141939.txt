Context: I run a 90-minute seminar called Concepts, Categories, and Analogies — 14 upper-level undergrads, mostly cog sci with a handful of philosophy majors. Room has a whiteboard, index cards, markers, no laptops, and I'd like to keep it that way if you can.

The thing I want them to run into is what I've been calling *edge room*: how much structure a domain leaves you with at the end of an ordered scale. Four flavors I've been playing with:

- dead end: alphabet strings, nothing after z and nobody really talks about wrapping
- wrap: pitch classes, after B comes C
- tier jump: digits, after 9 comes 10, i.e. a new notation tier
- nested edge: martial arts belt ranks — after brown comes black, and then the scale goes finer-grained inside black (1st dan, 2nd dan, ...)

My hunch, and the thing I want them to feel rather than be told, is that what makes a concept slip isn't that the rule is unclear or that they don't know it. It's that the domain runs out of edge room, and the same rule then quietly becomes a different rule — successor becomes predecessor, or wrap-around, or "just copy the letter over." Second axis, which bites even before you reach the edge: it's underdetermined which chunk of a string the rule is supposed to apply to at all. Given abc → abd, does aabbcc go to aabbcd or aabbdd? Does iijjkk go to iijjkl or iijjll? I think of that one as chunk slippage rather than successor slippage.

What I need from you:

- a runnable session: minute-by-minute timeline, materials, whatever cards or handouts I have to prep the night before
- the problem sequence itself. I'd rather participants generate some of it than be handed it, if you can make that work. Use my seeds above or better ones if you have them.
- a points or stakes scheme that doesn't involve real money. Ideally they're wagering on what the rest of the room will answer, not on being right — I want the gap between how certain they feel and how much they actually agree to be something they run into, not something I announce.
- one phase where a group has to commit an explicit, context-free rule to an index card before seeing the test string, and a different group then applies it blind. That's where the "any rule you can write down is both too rigid and too permissive" point should land.
- a debrief mapping specific moments in the session onto the pieces: edge room, chunk vs. successor slippage, and rules being underdetermined by their instances. A few of the philosophy majors will have seen rule-following material before; I don't want the session to lean on that, but it'd be great if the activity set it up so it clicks for them at the end.
- what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle it on the spot
- where the activity just can't reach and I have to say the thing out loud myself

Constraints: no lecture up front. I imagine groups working in different domains and then comparing notes, but if there's a better architecture use it.
