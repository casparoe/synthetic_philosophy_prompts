So here's a pattern I keep running into, and I have no word for it.

Two people play a prisoner's dilemma exactly once. Same city, no repeat, no reputation, no contract, no outside enforcement, and after it's over they never meet again. The only wrinkle: instead of choosing an action, each of them hands a sealed computer program to a referee. The referee lets each program read the other one's source code, then runs both, and whatever they output is the action. Standard game theory says both defect and that's that. But consider the program that says: cooperate if and only if the other program's source text is character-for-character identical to mine. If both sides submit that, both cooperate — and neither wants to switch to plain "always defect", because then the sources differ, the other side defects back, and the switcher is worse off. So cooperation is stable, in a one-shot game, with no enforcement at all.

I've noticed the same shape in a second place. Two firms each deploy a pricing algorithm. Neither firm talks to the other, no meetings, no messages, but each algorithm can inspect the other's code. Apparently you can end up with both charging high prices and neither having an incentive to cut — collusion without any agreement, where what makes it work is that each program makes its behavior hinge on the *structure* of the other's decision procedure rather than on a forecast of what the other will output.

What I can't get straight is how fragile this is, and whether the different failure modes have names. Three variants I've played with:

1. The text-identity version above. It falls apart if either side reformats, renames a variable, or adds a line of unreachable code. Two programs that behave identically in every possible interaction still defect against each other over pure cosmetics. That feels absurd, like the cooperation isn't really about the strategy at all.
2. A simulation version: don't compare text, just run the opponent's program against a copy of itself inside a sandbox with a step limit, and cooperate iff that simulation cooperates. This survives reformatting. But what if the opponent's code checks whether it's being simulated and behaves differently when it is? And what's the right default when the simulation hits the step budget — is that defection, or just an unknown?
3. A proof version: cooperate iff I can find, within a fixed step budget, a proof in some fixed formal system that the opponent cooperates with me. This seems immune to both reformatting and sandbox detection. But when both sides use it, it seems to hit a wall that smells like self-reference — and I genuinely can't tell whether that's a proof-length blowup (an engineering problem) or a Löb-style impossibility (a principled one), or whether those collapse into the same thing once you fix the budget.

So: does the general trick have an established name, and — more importantly — do the specific distinctions I'm groping at have names? I mean the difference between conditioning on someone's exact source, on a simulation of their behavior, and on a proof about them; and the specific fragility in variant 1 where behaviorally equivalent programs fail to cooperate. If there are established concepts near any of this, I want to know how what I described differs from each of them and which one really fits, not just a label dropped in my lap. If some piece of it has no name, coin one, define it, and give me the boundary cases — what would and wouldn't count as an instance.
