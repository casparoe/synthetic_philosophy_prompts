For several years I have followed the argument about land-use regulation — in the policy literature, in planning hearings, and among people I know who disagree in good faith — and it has settled into a shape that makes me doubt it is a single argument. I am not asking you to decide who is right. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement it is, with reasons, and without surveying the field.

Set out from the inside, the "build" side says: where housing is scarce and expensive, blocking new construction does not shelter existing residents from change, it shifts the pressure onto people who are not in the room — renters priced out elsewhere, would-be arrivals, people forced into longer commutes — and a procedure that lets a small group of insiders veto who may live nearby hands those insiders power over the lives of outsiders. The "block" side says: a neighbourhood is something its residents have jointly made and invested years of their lives in; they are the ones who absorb the noise, the crowding, the pressure on schools and transit, and sometimes the loss of their own building; and they are entitled to a say over the place where their lives are actually conducted. Both sides also trade empirical claims — that new market-rate supply eventually filters down, that filtering is too slow and too thin to matter, that upzoning capitalises into land values and so raises displacement pressure, that displacement is driven by speculative land-holding rather than by the permission itself — and both sides have studies. My observation is that the studies move no one: a result is waved through by those who already agreed and audited for flaws by those who did not.

My hypothesis is that three or four disagreements are stacked and run under one heading. First, a disagreement about who has standing: existing owner-residents, sitting tenants of the building to be replaced, renters citywide, prospective arrivals, or future residents. Second, a disagreement about the counterfactual against which a project is to be judged — build here versus build nowhere, versus build here versus build somewhere else in the same metropolitan area — where each side quietly switches counterfactual to reach the conclusion it wants. Third, a disagreement that may be bedrock: whether a collectively produced place, or expectations formed under an earlier zoning map, generate any moral claim at all, and whether "the character of a neighbourhood" names a good that can enter a calculation or a sentimental gloss on asset values. Fourth, a possibility I cannot rule out: that one side is making an outcome-based case and the other a procedural or consent-based one, and that neither recognises the other's argument as an argument.

I tried a device on my interlocutors to separate these. Suppose every household within the affected radius held one transferable consent right over an upzoning, saleable on a market, so that blocking costs the blockers the value of the option they refuse — with the result that the people who most want to block are the ones who end up holding the rights, and the empirical question of who benefits is settled by prices rather than by argument. I expected the disagreement about burdens and benefits to dissolve under that arrangement. It did not: several of the people I respect most on the block side still object, and say the market itself is the insult. That residual objection is the datum I most want explained — is it a rights claim that no allocation of money can satisfy, a claim about what a neighbourhood is such that pricing it destroys it, or something else?

So: is the stacking hypothesis right, and if so which disagreement is load-bearing? Where, if anywhere, is the dispute merely verbal — about "displacement", "affordable", "community", or about who counts as a resident? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the real work, one about the elasticity and geographic scale of supply or about whether benefits and burdens fall inside a single housing market, such that settling it would actually move people? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction to be drawn, a quantity to be measured, an institutional design that peels the question of standing apart from the question of value, or a change of question? And if you think no progress should be expected, tell me why, and tell me what that implies about the question of who has a right to the city.
