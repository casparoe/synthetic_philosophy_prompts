I'm in a graduate seminar on the foundations of quantum mechanics, and over the semester I've drifted, somewhat against my own expectation, into defending a position I originally took to be indefensible: that the probability problem for Everettian quantum mechanics is basically solved, and that what's left over is metaphysics of personal identity rather than anything specifically probabilistic.

The version I hold has four parts. First, branch counting is dead: no scheme that gets probabilities by counting successors can handle unequal amplitudes, and the continuous structure of the state space was always going to force a measure rather than a count. Second, the decision-theoretic and self-locating-uncertainty derivations show that an agent who knows she is about to branch, and who cares about all of her successors, must set her credences equal to branch weight (squared amplitude) — so the Born rule is recovered as a constraint on rational action rather than postulated. Third, the objections I keep hearing — that the derivations are circular, that nothing is genuinely probable when every outcome occurs, that the derivations only fix betting behavior and not belief — either presuppose what they are meant to establish or would bite equally in non-quantum cases: ordinary duplication, deterministic cosmology with self-location uncertainty, fission cases generally. Fourth, so the Everettian owes us a story about persons and branches, but not a story about probability.

I want the case against this, and I want it ranked. Give me the defeaters ordered from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one say whether a committed defender of my position would concede it as counting against them or would dismiss it, and why. I'm especially interested in exposure rather than restatement: what does this package predict or entail that its rivals don't, and is there anything — an experiment, a formal result, a case — that could actually break it? Three things worry me most, and I'd like them treated directly rather than folded into the general verdict.

One: the derivations are supposed to be general principles of rationality, not principles about amplitudes. So apply them outside quantum mechanics. If I'm told that tonight I will be scanned and reconstituted a hundred times in one city and once in another, do those principles return 1/101 to being in the first city? Whatever they return, does it match what I antecedently think, and does the Everettian have a non-question-begging way of saying why the quantum case is different?

Two: branch weight is standardly glossed as how much of the agent there is, or how much existence a successor has. But consider two branches of equal weight in which one successor goes on to a long, experiencially rich life and the other is destroyed a microsecond later. Weight apparently doesn't track amount of experience, or amount of anything else I care about. Is the gloss therefore empty — a metaphor propping up an intuition — and what argument or case would establish that it is empty rather than substantive? If it is substantive, what would count against it?

Three: decoherence is approximate, and the decomposition into branches is not given by the formalism in any exact way. So the measure looks primitive — a thickness or mass attached to quasi-branches, doing exactly the work the Born rule used to do. What would show that this is a genuine addition to the ontology rather than the Born rule restated in new vocabulary? And if the Born rule is derived from the dynamics, what precisely would a detected deviation — departure from squared-modulus frequencies, or collapse-type noise — refute: the premises of the derivations, or the interpretation as a whole?

Finally, be honest with me about insulation. I can't shake the suspicion that once the Born rule is a rationality constraint, there is no possible course of experience that could refute it: an agent who weighted all branches equally would be declared irrational rather than unlucky. If that's right, say so, and then tell me whether it's a defect here — whether a view of this kind is allowed to be unfalsifiable in that way, or whether it thereby forfeits the claim to have explained anything. For what it's worth, the thing I would personally find most persuasive is a case where the derivation's principles, run outside quantum mechanics, return advice that almost nobody would endorse; I'd rate that above any purely interpretive objection.

Assume I can follow arguments in terms of amplitudes, decoherence and branching structure — no need to rehearse the basics.
