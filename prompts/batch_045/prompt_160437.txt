I'm putting together a philosophy-through-film night for a group of first-years who've just done two weeks on presentism vs. eternalism, and I want to use Arrival (the 2016 Villeneuve film, from the Ted Chiang novella). My co-facilitator keeps saying it's "basically a block universe movie" and I think that's way too fast, but I can't quite say why. Could you give me an actual reading of it?

A few things I want you to get into, because these are the bits I keep tripping over:

1. As far as I can tell the film never really says anything about which times *exist*. Louise comes to know the future. But it seems to me a presentist could accept that — the future just isn't there yet, and it can still be true what's going to happen. (Or can't it? I've got a vague memory that some people think future-tensed claims aren't true or false yet, which if so would break my point.) So is the film's big emotional move — she knows how it ends and has Hannah anyway — actually neutral between the two views? If it isn't neutral, what exactly has to be added to make it non-neutral?

2. Separate question: there's a version of eternalism where every time exists but only one of them has the extra property of being *present*, with the "now" moving along the series — so it's eternalist about what there is and still dynamic. Do the heptapods fit that better than flat, all-slices-equally-real eternalism? Or maybe neither: the fact that they experience the whole thing at once looks like a claim about their psychology or their access, not about the ontology. A block universe doesn't hand anybody simultaneous awareness of the whole block. Am I splitting hairs here or is that a real distinction?

3. And the film leans hard on the language stuff — learn the language, get the time perception. That's usually sold as the strong Sapir-Whorf claim linguists think is basically bogus. Is the film making the language do metaphysical work, or is it just a device for getting Louise her knowledge? Does the answer change the reading?

I want this fair to both sides: don't just use the film as a clip illustrating eternalism, and don't bend the philosophy to make the film look profound. Tell me where the film resists the reading, where it's just muddled, and — if it does work — what it shows about this debate that the standard arguments (especially the relativity-of-simultaneity one) don't.

Also please be clear about what people who actually work on philosophy of time would agree on versus what's genuinely contested. I don't want to stand up in front of forty undergrads and say eternalism is settled because special relativity settled it, if that's actually an open fight. No citations or reading list needed, I just want the argument.
