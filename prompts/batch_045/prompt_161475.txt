I am defending a paper at a comparative church and state law workshop in three weeks, and I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the thesis. Assume the paper is sound and tell me where it will break.

The thesis: the standard objections to religious establishment — coercion of conscience, state endorsement of a faith, unequal citizenship, corruption of religion — all mislocate what is objectionable about an established church, a state-collected church tax, or tax exemption for religious bodies. What is objectionable is administrative. These arrangements require the state to determine, record and update who belongs to which religious community, and thereby convert a contested spiritual status into a legally administrable one. Call this the administration objection. The argument runs in three steps: first, that each of the standard objections is either over- or under-inclusive across the four jurisdictions I compare; second, that what these regimes all involve, and what makes them a single family, is state maintenance of a roster of religious belonging; third, that this is bad not because it coerces anyone but because it gives the state the last word on a question the community exists to answer for itself, and because exit then becomes a bureaucratic procedure whose form the state controls. The payoff is a ranking that inverts the conventional one: the German church tax, collected through the tax system with exit by formal declaration before a state office, comes out worse than the United States regime of exemption for religious bodies, which comes out worse than English establishment, where the parish duties attach to residence rather than to belief. I also argue that laïcité is not the clean case its defenders take it to be, since the separation settlement left in place a local regime in one eastern region in which several historically recognized faiths have salaried clergy, and since the state still has to decide which associations count as religious in the first place.

The room: a German public-law scholar who works on the constitutional law of religion and treats the church tax as a benign mechanism of member self-financing; a United States First Amendment scholar whose working vocabulary is endorsement, coercion, neutrality, and the contrast between exemption and subsidy; a French public-law scholar steeped in laïcité, for whom the indivisibility of the republic and the freedom of conscience from organized religion are the governing ideas; an English constitutional lawyer who finds the whole genre of church and state theory overwrought and will ask what any of it changes; and a political theorist of multiculturalism who will want to know what this implies for Muslim communities currently seeking the standing that Christian bodies already enjoy.

Please draft the prep sheet as follows. Give me the eight objections I am most likely to get, ordered by expected damage rather than by likelihood, each attributed to one of these five people and phrased in that person's own terms: I want to hear the German scholar's vocabulary of public-law status and corporate personality, the United States scholar's endorsement and subsidy contrast, and the French scholar's suspicion that a procedural criterion cannot see what matters. For each objection give me a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds, and one sentence on where that reply is soft or where I should concede rather than push. Then tell me the one question I am hoping nobody asks, with the honest answer I would have to give if it came. Do not include citations or further reading. If any objection turns on a jurisdictional fact you take me to have gotten wrong, say so in the soft-point line rather than revising the paper. Keep the whole sheet under 1,000 words, and no single reply longer than 90.
