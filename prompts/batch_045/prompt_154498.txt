I have been arguing about dispositions with someone in my reading group for months, and I have built an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept. I will state the moving parts so you do not need a library: by the simple conditional analysis I mean the claim that a thing is disposed to give response M to stimulus C if and only if it would give M if it were in C; by a fink I mean a case where the stimulus itself destroys the disposition, as with a wire that is live but wired to a mechanism that would de-energize it the moment it were touched; by the revised conditional analysis I mean the simple analysis plus the clause that the object retains its basis throughout the stimulus, which is what is supposed to dispose of the finks. Assume no access to any literature: reason from the cases as I give them.

(1) Mask cases are genuine. A fragile glass packed in foam is still fragile, and it would not break if struck while packed. The disposition is present and the manifestation absent.
(2) So the simple conditional analysis is false, and so is the revised one: the packed glass keeps its microstructure the whole time, so the basis-retention clause that rescues you from finks does nothing here.
(3) What makes a mask a mask, as opposed to a change in the object or a mere absence of the stimulus, is precisely that it leaves the intrinsic basis intact while blocking the response.
(4) Masks are cheap to construct. For any disposition and any stimulus you like, I can produce an interfering factor that leaves the basis alone and prevents the manifestation: a field that stiffens the glass's surface on contact, a mechanism that catches the glass before it lands, a second drug that cancels the first downstream without touching its chemistry.
(5) So the failure in (2) is not a special feature of a few exotic setups. No disposition, however specified, entails any counterfactual about what would happen. The conditional analysis fails everywhere or nowhere.
(6) If a disposition never entails what would happen, dispositions cannot be conditional or counterfactual properties at all; they have to be their intrinsic bases, or at least supervene on them, with the manifesting behaviour a downstream product of basis plus environment.
(7) But if a disposition just is its basis, masks stop being interesting. A mask leaves the basis intact, so of course it leaves the disposition intact, and fragile but would not break if struck collapses into has such-and-such microstructure and would not break if struck, which the conditional analyst can assert as happily as anyone. The mask argument has closed the very gap between disposition and manifestation that it needed in order to get started.
(8) So either masks are no threat to the conditional analysis, contrary to (1) and (2), or they are a threat everywhere and drive you into a categoricalism that makes them no threat at all. The mask argument argues itself out of a job.

Two cases I cannot slot in. First, the symmetric one: two poisons, each lethal on its own, each neutralized by the other, swallowed together, and the subject survives. Which is the disposition and which the mask? It looks to me as though the answer depends on how the stimulus is specified, and I cannot see a non-question-begging way to specify it. Second, the converse one: an object with no disposition to shatter, which would nonetheless shatter if struck, because the striking triggers a separate mechanism, say a charge wired under the table. Call that a mimic. If mimics are possible, the conditional fails in both directions, and I cannot tell whether that strengthens my argument or quietly refutes it.

If forced to choose I would rather keep (1) and (3) than (6): masks seem to me real, and a genuine embarrassment for the naive conditional story. But I want to know what I am paying, and I cannot tell whether what breaks is the generalization in (4) and (5), the definition in (3), or something earlier.
