I'm a graduate student in philosophy working on a seminar paper about the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis, and I want to check that the package of claims I'm planning to defend is stable before I build a paper around it. My supervisor thinks the topic is settled and that the only interesting questions are technical, while a friend in the theory group says the whole issue is "just engineering" and that philosophy should leave it alone; I disagree with both, but I've started to suspect that the particular combination of things I believe is doing more work than I can honestly defend. So I'd like an audit rather than a summary.

Here is roughly where I've landed. (1) The Church–Turing thesis — the identification of the pre-theoretic notion of an effective or mechanical procedure (finite, deterministic, step-by-step, carried out by following explicit rules, with no appeal to insight or guesswork) with Turing-machine computability — is a substantive claim with a determinate truth value. It is not a mathematical theorem, because one side of the identification is informal; but it is also not a stipulation, a definition, or an explication of a vague notion by a precise one, and it is not a category mistake to ask whether it is correct. There is a fact of the matter about which procedures are effective, and the thesis says something true or false about all of them. (2) Its epistemic status is nevertheless strong, and stronger than "no counterexample has turned up in the better part of a century." What carries it, I think, is the convergence of independently motivated formalisms — Turing machines, the lambda calculus, general recursive functions, register machines, cellular automata — on one and the same class of functions, plus what I'll call a bounding argument: any procedure we would count as effective is finitely describable, proceeds by discrete local steps, uses finite-but-unbounded memory, and never lets one step depend on more than a bounded amount of what came before; and one can argue, without simply assuming the thesis, that anything meeting those structural constraints is Turing-computable. The informal notion is thus squeezed between a lower bound and an upper bound that coincide. (3) I also want to say that the pre-theoretic notion is vague at the edges: there are procedures we would not know whether to call effective, and I take this as a feature of our concept rather than as a discovery about computation. (4) Separately, there is the Physical Church–Turing thesis — the claim that no physical process, device, or system computes anything beyond the Turing-computable functions — and I regard that as a genuinely different thesis: contingent, empirical, and possibly false. (5) Correspondingly, I hold that a successful physical hypercomputer would not refute (1). Suppose someone builds what I'll call a deep-well machine: it exploits a spacetime in which one observer's infinite proper time falls inside another's finite interval, so the machine can run an unbounded search — say, for a divergence witness in the halting problem, which no Turing machine can decide for arbitrary inputs — and return an answer to us in finite time. Or suppose someone builds a device that stores an uncomputable real in a continuously varying physical quantity and reads off its digits. Such a device would be a counterexample to (4), but not to (1): a supertask or an infinite-precision readout is not something a finite agent does by following rules, so it is not an effective procedure at all, and the logical thesis is immune to that kind of refutation. (6) At the same time, when I ask myself what actually grounds my confidence in (1), I keep coming back to something more mundane — that over decades nobody has exhibited a procedure we recognise as genuinely effective that computes a Turing-uncomputable function — and I find it hard to deny that a working deep-well machine would be at least some evidence against (1), on the grounds that it would show our intuitive sense of what "we" can do in principle had been parochial.

What I want from you is a consistency check on this set, written for someone who will have to defend it in a seminar. Go through the commitments in pairs and say which are genuinely in tension and how severely, which only look like they are, and for each real tension what would have to be given up and what that would cost me — including whether the cost is contained (I lose a rhetorical flourish) or structural (I lose a claim the rest of the paper needs). I have a nagging feeling that parts of this package are not all pulling in the same direction — something about how a vague notion can support a determinately true identity, something about whether the bounding argument's premises are the right kind of thing to bear on an informal notion, something about how a device can refute one thesis and leave the other untouched — but I can't isolate which worry is the serious one and which is a pseudo-problem, and I don't have a view about which commitment I'd abandon. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and finish by saying which single revision would clean up the most.
