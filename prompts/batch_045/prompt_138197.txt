I'm a retired experimental physicist — thirty years in condensed matter, mostly noise measurements — and since I packed it in I've been sitting in on the philosophy colloquia at my old department. Because I'm the token physicist they've asked me to chair the Q&A after next Thursday's talk. The speaker is a philosopher with a new book defending the block universe, and the spine of his case, from what I'm told, is the Rietdijk–Putnam argument: for any two events at all there's an inertial frame in which they're simultaneous, so if the events simultaneous with me here-and-now are real, then everything is, everywhere and always. I've drafted a list of questions and they're all things a man in his position has answered in print to many times before. Give me five better ones instead, each with a follow-up keyed to the reply he's most likely to make, and nothing he can dispose of just by restating the B-theory.

1. It looks to me like the argument needs more than the relativity of simultaneity — it needs something like "what's real can't be a frame-relative matter" or "being real is transitive across simultaneity relations." I want him to say exactly what the extra premise is. Likely reply: no frame is privileged, so a frame-relative verdict about what exists can't be the whole truth. Follow-up: relativity is stuffed with frame-relative quantities — length, kinetic energy, simultaneity itself — that nobody concludes are illusory, so why isn't temporal existence one more of those?

2. Our actual universe isn't Minkowski: there's a favoured-ish slicing, the comoving frame in which the microwave background is isotropic. My pet hybrid is that the real events are just the ones on the comoving slice through here-and-now, so Andromeda-paradox-style distant events exist and my tomorrow doesn't. Make him say what's actually wrong with that beyond "nobody holds it." Likely reply: the argument only needs one frame, so a preferred frame can't remove any slices. Follow-up: then is the block universe a contingent, empirically decidable thesis that cosmology could in principle have refuted, or is the comoving frame merely convenient and not metaphysically privileged at all?

3. If the payoff is that future events are already there, the "already" is doing work he can't literally mean — in a block, already just means earlier-than-this-utterance. Ask what the claim that the future is real comes to once that's cashed out tenselessly, and what positive account he gives of the asymmetries we live inside: memory runs one way, traces run one way, we deliberate about the future and not the past. Likely reply: those all reduce to the thermodynamic asymmetry plus a low-entropy boundary condition. Follow-up: does he then owe us an explanation of why a boundary condition on a static block is experienced as passage from the inside, or is he committed to calling that experience an illusion — and what does that do to the rest of his epistemology?

4. Press him on the "the future is real but deliberation still matters" line. Likely reply: my deliberating is part of the causal process leading up to the outcome; openness is epistemic, not metaphysical. Follow-up: if the block contains exactly one branch with my decision in it, what work is "could have done otherwise" doing, and what grounds the counterfactuals — and does a block with no temporal production in it leave any room for genuine causal production, or only for patterns?

5. Ask him what empirical finding would count against the view. Likely reply: nothing much — it's the metaphysical reading of our best physics. Follow-up: if a relativistic dynamical-collapse theory, of the kind that has to posit a preferred foliation to work at all, were confirmed, would that be evidence for objective becoming, or does a preferred slicing leave the block untouched? And is the so-called problem of time in quantum gravity — a formalism with apparently no time in it — evidence for him, or an embarrassment, given that a timeless formalism doesn't obviously give you a block with temporal relations in it either?
