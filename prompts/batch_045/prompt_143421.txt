**Three Molochs (working title)**

The word "Moloch," in the rationalist-adjacent writing that popularized it, gets used for roughly this: lots of agents each doing something locally sensible, an outcome everyone hates, and no one able to stop unilaterally without being disadvantaged. My thesis is that the word is doing at least three different jobs, that the three want different remedies, and that mixing them up is why people reach for "we just need to coordinate better" when coordination isn't the binding constraint.

**M1: the equilibrium trap.** The static case. Mutual cooperation 3/3, unilateral defection 5/0, mutual defection 1/1; defection dominates; everyone defects. The diagnosis is about the payoff structure of a single game played at a time, and the remedies are the standard toolkit: change the payoffs (tax, fine, subsidize), change the game (make it repeated, bundle it with other games, introduce a correlated signal), change the beliefs (assurance).

**M2: the selection trap.** Here the interesting failure isn't anyone playing their game badly. It's that agents, firms, or institutions differ in how willing they are to exploit, and the exploiting type grows as a share of the population — or simply survives while the scrupulous type dies out. The outcome persists even though no individual chose it and most individuals regret it, because the composition of the group changes underneath them. Diagnostic I like: ask whether the bad outcome would persist if you replaced every current agent with a wholehearted cooperator. If yes, it's M1. If no — if the cooperators would be outcompeted or displaced within a few turnover cycles — it's M2. Remedies: change the gradient (cap the advantage exploiters get), slow the turnover, lengthen the horizon over which selection acts. Note that "coordinate" is nearly useless here, because the relevant defectors aren't in the room; they're the ones who will exist later.

**M3: the intra-agent trap.** This is the bit I think is under-theorized. Selection also operates *within* agents. In a person: habits, impulses, attention-grabbing routines compete for realization, and the ones that win tend to be the ones with fast, local, legible payoffs; they degrade the person's shared internal commons — time, attention, sleep, savings, reputation, capacity for deliberation. In an organization: subunits compete for budget and headcount, the winners are those producing measurable short-cycle output, and they eat the shared pools — slack, maintenance, institutional memory, morale. It's multipolar in the same formal way: many small processes, each capturing a benefit it fully enjoys while paying only a fraction of the cost into the pool. What makes it *distinct*, I want to argue, is timescale. The selecting environment runs faster than the agent's own deliberation, so "decide to stop" isn't available in the way it arguably is in M1. That shifts the remedy from resolve to architecture: precommitment, changing what's legible (i.e., what gets measured), internal prices, friction. Upshot I toy with: akrasia starts to look less like a failure of will and more like an M3 trap, i.e., a design problem rather than a character problem.

**Second axis: slopes vs. cliffs.** In a *slope* trap, harm is a smooth function of total defection and each increment is small; no single increment is detectable or blameworthy, so any intervention that waits for visible damage arrives late. In a *cliff* trap, harm is near zero until a threshold and then collapses; the striking feature is that the agent who crosses the threshold makes all the difference but has no way of knowing they're the one, and isn't behaving worse than anyone else — pure moral luck pointing the wrong way. Slopes want continuous price-like mechanisms and feedback made faster (leading indicators). Cliffs want buffers, redundancy, and governance of the distance-to-threshold rather than of the behavior itself. And the two get systematically confused: people treat slope problems as if they had a cliff (waiting for a crisis before acting) and cliff problems as if they had a slope (assuming incremental reform is safe because it's been safe so far).

**Third: the ratchet, and why exit isn't the mirror of entry.** The standard asymmetry is that entry is unilateral while exit must be multilateral. True but incomplete. The deeper asymmetry: entering a trap manufactures a constituency with a *concentrated* interest in staying in — people whose income, status, or identity is now bound up with the defection — while the costs of staying remain diffuse. So the coalition for exit has to be built against organized resistance, not merely against apathy. The corollary, which is probably the most practically useful claim in the paper: to get out, you usually have to manufacture a concentrated beneficiary of *exit* — buyouts, grandfathering, compensation for first movers, procurement that rewards early adopters. Coordination alone won't do it. Reverse-Moloch needs its own Moloch.

**A worry I can't settle.** Maybe "Moloch" names nothing over and above "collective action problem plus selection." My tentative replies: (a) even if it's extensionally just a subset, the term does heuristic work by pointing at gradient-shaping rather than equilibrium-shaping interventions; (b) it picks out a family of cases that the standard toolkit systematically mishandles; (c) it's best understood as a research heuristic, not as a kind of thing in the world. I'm not sure any of these is strong enough, and I suspect (a) is the weak one.
