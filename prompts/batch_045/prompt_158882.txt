Im trying to settle an argument with my sister, who just started as a loan officer at a small community bank and keeps insisting that when her bank makes a loan it "creates money" and that this is basically a good thing. Then I ran into this bit in Hume's essay Of Money (1752) and now I don't know who's confused:

That provisions and labour should become dear by the encrease of trade and money, is, in many respects, an inconvenience; but an inconvenience that is unavoidable, and the effect of that public wealth and prosperity which are the end of all our wishes. But to endeavour artificially to encrease such a credit, can never be the interest of any trading nation; but must lay them under disadvantages, by encreasing money beyond its natural proportion to labour and commodities, and thereby heightening their price to the merchant and manufacturer. And in this view, it must be allowed, that no bank could be more advantageous, than such a one as locked up all the money it received, and never augmented the circulating coin, as is usual, by returning part of its treasure into commerce.

Help me get clear on three things. One: what is the mechanism he thinks he's describing, and what is money's "natural proportion to labour and commodities" supposed to be a proportion of — is he saying anything more precise than "prices just scale with the money stock"? Two: is the harm he's pointing at really inflation in general, or specifically that the merchant and manufacturer get undercut abroad (he says the price is heightened "to the merchant and manufacturer," and in the paragraphs before he worries about poorer states underselling richer ones) — i.e. is he making a claim about the price level or about the relative price of traded goods? Three, the part I most want: does his argument depend on assumptions a modern fiat, fractional-reserve system breaks? He seems to picture banks as intermediaries that can only relend metal coin that already exists, so more credit means more coin in circulation one-for-one, and he's writing before anyone thought a central bank would offset that. If lending today creates deposits and a central bank then leans against the resulting spending, does his conclusion collapse, invert, or survive as a different claim — say, one about who gets to spend first rather than about how much money there is? And is that "bank that locks up all the money it received" a position he actually endorses, or a reductio he's stuck with?

Answer in American English, plain and concrete. Also tell me where your reading is most likely to break down and what in the passage would make you switch to a different interpretation — I'd rather know which sentence the whole thing hinges on than be told it confidently.
