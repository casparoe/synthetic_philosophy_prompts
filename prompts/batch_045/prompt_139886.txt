Hey — I could use some help building a case for the third section of a paper I'm working on. The topic is rational irrationality, hitchhiker-style: the guy stuck out in the desert promises the driver a big sum to get him to town, and once he's in town he's got no reason to pay, so if she'd known that she'd never have picked him up — being the kind of person who pays is worth more to him than paying is, but he can't make himself into that kind of person on the spot. I'm going after the standard way of defusing this, the two-tier move where you say rationality recommends the disposition (or recommends causing yourself to have it) rather than the act, so the conflict was never real. My angle is cases where the irrationality isn't downstream of any rational recommendation in the first place.

So: build me a case where the irrationality pays, and where every version of that move is shut off. Constraints:

- Realistic: a hospital, a lab, a courtroom, a small firm — something my committee won't rol their eyes at. No desert roads, no guys with guns, no trolleys, no sci-fi. Scenario itself in one paragraph; the analysis can be as long as it needs to be.
- Two people: the agent, and a counterparty who controls something the agent wants and will hand it over only if genuinely confident she'll stay with it once it gets hard.
- The counterparty reads people about as well as a sharp, experienced human does. No perfect predictor, no magic lie detector. So she can't just announce that she'll stick it out; the confidence has to come off something the counterparty really sees.
- Close the usual exits: no contract, no repeat play or reputation, no third party who can enforce anything, and no precommitment of any kind — no pills, no hypnosis, no handing her keys to a friend, no penalties.
- The thing doing the work has to be a false belief about a matter of fact. Not a desire, not a character trait, not weakness of will, not an exotic preference. And one specifiable false belief, not general muddleheadedness.
- It has to be load-bearing for her own good: given her evidence the belief is clearly false and unsupported, and she comes out ahead of an otherwise identical agent with the true belief, aims and evidence held fixed.
- Interior optimum: a little of the distortion helps, more of it hurts. Slightly off and she never takes the opportunity at all; way off and she takes it and crashes. Tell me where the peak sits and what the two mechanisms pulling against each other are.
- It has to stay false. No version where it turns out she was right all along — at the end of the story the belief is still false and she's still ahead.
- The belief is already in place before the situation comes up, formed for unrelated reasons or just a standing cast of mind, and there's nothing she can now do about it. This is the one I care about most: I want a case where rationality never got the chance to recommend the irrationality.
- The counterparty's cooperation is rational for them too. Not a sucker, and not misled about anything they could reasonably check.

What I need back: the paragraph; then a node-by-node walk of what the standard act-by-act expected-utility story says at each decision point, where exactly it says she's being irrational, and roughly what the irrationality is worth to her in whatever units you pick; then the counterfactual, what the true-believing version of her does at each node and why she ends up worse; then the feature of the case that's doing the work and what happens if you pull it out (if the counterparty couldn't read conviction, say, or if she could precommit); then three attacks from someone who thinks none of this is a problem — (a) it would have been rational to cause herself to be like that, (b) so much the worse for the theory of rationality, revise it so this comes out rational, (c) rationality was never in the business of tracking what's good for you — and an honest verdict on which one actually lands and which is hand-waving. I'd rather hear it from you than from whoever gets assigned to comment.

One last thing: if you don't think all of these can be met together, tell me which one you'd drop and why. What I can't use is a case that quietly stipulates the verdicts — the payoffs have to generate them.
