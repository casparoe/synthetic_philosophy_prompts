Hey — I'm in the first year of an MPhil and have to hand in a dissertation prospectus by the end of next month. The thesis itself would be about 15,000 words, written between roughly March and October, and my supervisor has said (unhelpfully) that he'll support anything as long as the question has a determinate answer and I can finish it. I keep circling back to the old statue-and-the-clay problem and I'd like to build the prospectus around it, but not in the form it usually gets discussed in.

Here's the half-formed thought I can't get straight. Every version of the debate I know seems to smuggle in what I'd call a matter-first bias: we're given a statue and the lump of clay it's made of, and the lump is quietly treated as the metaphysically basic occupant of that region — the thing that was already there before the sculptor showed up, the thing that survives squashing, the thing whose identity conditions are the demanding ones — so the whole question becomes how the statue relates to it. Identical to it? Merely constituted by it without being the same thing? A second object coinciding with it? A stage or temporal part of something longer-lived? But there are loads of artifact-ish objects where nothing like that holds, because there's no privileged stuff they're made of and no pre-existing portion of matter that got arranged: a piece of software that's been ported between languages, a committee whose members all turn over, a chess opening that gets named and then refined across decades, a fictional character who turns up under different descriptions in different novels. For those, the "what is it made of" question looks downstream of the identity question rather than upstream of it. So what I want to know is what happens to the constitution puzzle if you start from cases like those instead of from clay. Does the same pressure to posit two coinciding things — one with relaxed identity conditions, one with fussy ones — reappear, or does it evaporate? And if it evaporates, what does that tell us about the classic case: was it ever really about constitution, or was it about something else that clay happens to hide, like our practices for counting and reidentifying artifacts over time?

My two worries are that this is either just the standard three or four positions with new examples bolted on, or that it's secretly three different questions I'd spend 15,000 words conflating. So what I need is an actual prospectus, not encouragement. Pin down a single question that's genuinely open and that someone could fail to answer; say which of the usual options it presses hardest on — coincidence, constitution-without-identity, mereological essentialism, the perdurantist treatment, the sortal/conventionalist deflation of the whole thing — and why those don't already settle it; say what I'd actually do, chapter by chapter and roughly month by month, and why that method rather than the obvious alternatives (e.g. just running the standard arguments on the new cases, or doing a straight survey); and be explicit about what the thesis would have established if it works and what would count as it having failed, including the possibility that the honest upshot is "the clay case was never a case of constitution in the first place." Also be straight with me about where this would blow past 15,000 words. And please skip any bibliography or literature review with names and dates attached — I'll fill those in myself, and I'd rather the argument stand up without them.
