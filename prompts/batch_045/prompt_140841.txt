Me and two other parents judge the projects at our regional middle-and-high-school science fair every spring, and I also help moderate a neighbourhood Facebook group where people are constantly posting health and wellness claims, so I end up doing the same thing over and over: deciding whether something somebody is very confident about is actually the kind of claim that evidence could ever touch, or whether it only looks like it is. I got nerd-sniped into reading about Popper and falsificationism online a few months ago and honestly it's made things worse rather than better — I now catch myself wanting to fail half the kids' projects for being untestable, and at the same time some of the woo in the Facebook group seems to survive the "what would prove you wrong?" question just fine, because the poster will cheerfully name some result and then quietly explain it away when it happens. So I've been messing around with a few rules of my own and I want someone to tell me which of them are worth keeping and then build the actual procedure around them. The first is what I call the Tuesday test: keep "nobody could ever test this, even in principle" strictly separate from "this particular twelve-year-old can't test it before Sunday with the equipment in her garage," because those feel like totally different problems to me and I keep conflating them. The second is the pre-mortem: before anyone runs the experiment, make them write down in advance which outcome would make them abandon the claim, and then check whether that written-down outcome is one they'd actually accept or one they'd reinterpret. The third is rubber claims — my name for claims that stretch to absorb whatever happens, where the giveaway is that after the result is in you still can't say what would have counted against them; I think the right test for a rubber claim is whether the person can describe a result that would lower their confidence by a specific amount rather than just "make me think about it more." I've also read somewhere that any test actually checks a whole bundle of assumptions at once rather than one idea on its own, which worries me because it seems like it would let anybody wriggle out of anything by blaming their equipment, and I don't know how to fold that in without making the whole thing useless.

What I need is a procedure, not an essay. Something the three of us can run in about ten minutes per project, written in plain language with no philosophy jargon in the final checklist (I can translate, but the other two judges won't read anything with the word "epistemology" in it), with the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as a good enough answer to each, the point where you just stop and say no, and the point where you hand it to a human because it's a values call and not a facts call. It has to end in a verdict we can defend to a parent who complains to the school board, and — this matters — it needs a repair step, because with twelve-year-olds we can't just say "your topic isn't science"; we need to be able to say "not testable as you've written it, but here is the nearest testable version" and still give them most of the marks. Please also be straight about which steps are really judgment calls wearing a procedure costume.

Then run it on three cases. First: a girl's project where she talks kindly to one set of bean plants and ignores another set, and the talked-to ones come out taller. Second: a post in the Facebook group selling a copper bracelet that rebalances your energy field, where the seller says it obviously doesn't work on people who are skeptical. Third, and this is the one where I want you to be blunt even if it annoys me: I've believed for about fifteen years that my migraines come on ahead of storms, I've half-kept a diary about it, and I really don't want to be told I'm doing pseudoscience — so tell me honestly what your procedure says about my headache theory and what I'd have to do to make it come out the other way. Finally, list the kinds of claims your procedure just can't handle (historical one-off stuff, statistical things, whatever else) so I know when to stop using it.
