I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy. A popular book on mind uploading that I read recently summarizes Derek Parfit's teletransporter case (from *Reasons and Persons*, 1984) roughly like this:

Parfit imagines a scanner that records your brain and body, destroys them, and rebuilds an exact copy on Mars out of new matter. He grants the obvious part — the person who steps out on Mars is not you, you died back on Earth — and then argues that this doesn't matter. What matters in survival, on his view, is just psychological continuity: your memories, character, and desires being carried forward in someone. Since identity is not what matters, Parfit concludes that there is no deep further fact about whether a person persists over time; strictly speaking there is no persisting self at all, only a series of mental states that resemble one another, which is why he treats questions about personal identity as empty. He pushes the same point with the branching version, where the machine fails to destroy the original and produces two copies: there, Parfit concludes, you literally survive as both of them, because nothing settles which one is really you. The upshot the book draws is that fearing death is irrational — if identity isn't what matters, dying is fine so long as somebody psychologically like you is around afterward.

I can't tell which parts of this are Parfit and which are the author's gloss, and I'd rather not build my intuitions about uploading on a distorted version. One thing I can't get past: from the point of view of the person pressing the button, experience just stops, so isn't this death with extra steps? Please go through the summary claim by claim and tell me what a philosopher who works on personal identity would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong — including whether the claim attributed to Parfit in the branching case and the conclusion about death follow even from the corrected version.
