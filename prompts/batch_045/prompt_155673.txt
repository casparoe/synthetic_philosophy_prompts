Hi — I'm a journalist, I cover science and ideas, and I keep running into a shape of argument I dont have a word for. Tell me whether there's an established concept for it, or whether I'd be inventing one. Please don't give me a reading list or footnoted citations, I'll never chase them — I just want to know whether what I'm seeing has been carved out somewhere before, and if it hasn't, how you'd carve it.

Here's the shape. Two people disagree about some empirical claim: how fast a new virus spreads, say, or how much of a wage gap is discrimination. They agree on the data. They agree on what the data would look like if each competing story were true. And they agree that whatever is left over — the part the data doesn't settle — should be filled in by what you'd have expected before any of it came in. The prior, I think that's the word. So the disagreement gets located: it isn't about the evidence, it's about the prior.

Then the part I can't name. They ask whose prior is the right one, and they agree on a rule for deciding — that a starting point should be as uncommitted as possible, say, or that it shouldn't depend on which way you happen to write the quantity down. And they disagree again, about which starting point satisfies that rule. So they go up a level and agree on a rule for choosing among rules, and disagree again. It doesn't shrink. Three rounds in they're as far apart as ever, but at each round they've agreed about one more thing and disagreed about one more thing.

What makes it more than "they disagree" for me: each person's preferred rule one level up is the rule that hands back the starting point they already liked at the bottom. Nobody's being paid. They're not obviously lying. But the higher-level principle always seems to have been picked to ratify the lower-level one, not the other way round.

So does this have a name? I've heard "deep disagreement" used for cases where two sides don't share a way of arguing at all, and that's not this — these two share all the machinery and still can't land. It also feel different from a regress-of-justification thing, because nothing here is being propped up by the level above it; the support seems to run downward, from the conclusion to the principle. And it isn't just "everyone is biased," because what I actually want to know is whether the same shape could turn up between two people with no stake, no motive and unlimited patience — if it could, then calling it bias misses it.

One more thing: I've been told there's a result, Aumann something, saying two people who start in the same place and who know each other's conclusions shouldn't be able to keep disagreeing. Is that even compatible with what I'm describing? Because if it isn't, one of my two stories is wrong, and I'd rather know which before I write it up.

If there's no established label, coin one and define it, and give me the boundary cases: at what point does this stop being itself and become an ordinary disagreement about priors, or plain motivated reasoning, or just the dull fact that two people can start in different places. I'm not after a word, I'm after the shape and its edges.

And be straight with me, since I may quote you: say how confident you are in each of the main claims you make, and flag the single point you're least sure of. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give me the reasoning — no preamble, and don't restate my question back at me.
