My friend and I spent like two hours on this last night and I still can't find the hinge, but I'm fairly sure his argument cheats, because it seems to convict people it shouldn't.

Setup so we're using the same words: suppose a person's confidence isn't a single number but a range, represented as a set of probability functions (people call this a credal set). Say her credence that a coin lands heads is the interval [0.3, 0.6]. Her lower probability for heads is 0.3, her upper is 0.6. So the most she'll pay for a ticket that pays $1 if heads is $0.30, and the most she'll pay for a ticket that pays $1 if tails is $0.40 (that's 1 minus her upper probability for heads). OK. His argument:

(1) We find out what rationality requires of a credal state by looking at betting: a state is irrational if there is a package of bets, each of which the agent's own attitudes say she should accept, that together guarantee her a loss. That's the whole content of the standard Dutch book argument, and it's the reason anyone thinks credences have to obey the probability calculus.

(2) For the agent above, her own state licenses paying up to $0.30 for the heads ticket and up to $0.40 for the tails ticket.

(3) So a bookie can sell her both tickets for a combined $0.70. Exactly one of them pays $1 whatever happens, so she nets $0.30 guaranteed — and she won't take it. Her own attitudes say to take it and she doesn't, which is the same self-condemnation the sure-loss book involves. The Dutch book shows sharp-but-non-probabilistic credences are irrational; this "sure-gain book" shows imprecise ones are.

(4) Nothing turns on how wide the interval is. [0.49, 0.51] gets booked just as easily as [0.1, 0.9]. So imprecision isn't something you can have a bit of, calibrated to how thin your evidence is — any of it at all is as bad as total ignorance. It's binary.

(5) Nothing turns on where the imprecision came from either. Whether you couldn't be bothered to think, or you stared hard at an urn whose composition is genuinely unknown to you, one exact number is demanded and everything else is irrational.

(6) So rationality requires a sharp number for every proposition, including ones you've never thought about, and including unproven mathematical claims: a mathematician who hasn't settled some conjecture and won't name a precise number is being irrational.

(7) That's absurd. Rationality is a standard a person can actually meet, and when the evidence is silent or ambiguous, picking a number — 0.5? 0.51? — isn't something the evidence could tell you. It looks like a demand for logical omniscience dressed up as a demand for coherence.

(8) So one of (1) through (5) is wrong.

Two things bother me. First, the argument seems to prove too much: swap (2) for a person with perfectly sharp credences who just doesn't gamble, or who has any bid-ask spread at all because of transaction costs or plain caution. A bookie offers her a bet at better than her own fair price, she declines, and there's a guaranteed gain she left on the table. Same argument, same verdict — and I don't think my friend wants to say every bet-refuser is irrational. That makes me think the argument was never about imprecision in particular. Second, when I said that, he said no: the sharp-credence decliner isn't irrational, because refusing a bet isn't a doxastic commitment, whereas the imprecise agent's own attitudes positively favour the package. And then he added: if I don't like any of this, I owe him an account of what's wrong with just averaging over the functions in the credal set and being done with it, which he says is always available.

I'm attached to (1) — it's the reason I believe probabilism at all — and I'd rather give up almost anything than (7).

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost? If I reject (3), do I have to say it's fine to leave free money on the table, and does the reply that saves the sharp-credence decliner end up saving the imprecise agent too? If I reject (1) instead, do I lose probabilism, or does some thinner Dutch book argument survive? Is there a reading of "the most she'd pay" on which the two tickets are worth more together than apart — the pair is a guaranteed dollar, after all — and does that break the argument or just relocate it? What's the right thing to say about the mathematician in (6)? And if you think his argument is basically sound, tell me what I'm supposed to do about (7), and about the averaging move he ended on.
