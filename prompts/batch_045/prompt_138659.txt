Hi — I'm a PhD student in philosophy (I work mostly on explanation and counterfactuals in the historical sciences) and in three weeks I'm giving a 25+15 paper at a one-day workshop on big questions in global history. The room will be roughly fifteen economic historians and global historians — several of whom work on China and think the why Britain and not China? question is perfectly respectable, just under-specified — plus three philosophers of history and philosophy of science who are sympathetic to me temperamentally but will want the argument to be much sharper than it is, and one comparative-historical political scientist who will want to know what my view implies for how you'd actually design a comparison (cases, controls, units, all of that). My supervisor, who is a historian, is chairing and will ask the first question, and I know from a grad conference last year that she thinks the whole thing is a philosopher telling historians their question is confused.

My paper is on whether why Britain and not China? — the Needham-style version of it, and the broader Great Divergence framing — is even a well-posed contrastive question. I'm not taking the lazy line that it's meaningless. My thesis is that as usually posed it bundles together at least three quite different explanatory demands (sustained per-capita growth; coal-and-machines industrialisation; the emergence of modern science) that have different candidate explanations and different natural spatial and temporal units, and that it also fails a test I want to propose, which I'm calling the partition test: a contrastive question of the form why X rather than Y? is well posed only if the contrast survives re-partitioning the units on both sides — i.e. you can't make the contrast evaporate, or reverse, just by redrawing X and Y into different sub-units (regions instead of polities, say) or by scaling up or down. The last five minutes are a repair manual: unpack it into a family of explicitly contrastive questions, each with a named outcome variable, a fixed unit, and a stated contrast class, and give up the idea that there is one single thing out there to be explained. So the payoff I want is methodological, not a verdict on which explanation is actually right, and I'm not totally sure yet whether I have an objection or just a complaint.

What I need is a Q&A prep sheet for this audience specifically — not the objections I'd get from philosophers in my own department. Give me the objections in the order I should expect them (think likelihood times damage, not just damage), phrased the way these people would actually phrase it, in their own vocabulary (unit of analysis, identification, selection on the dependent variable, Galton's problem, research programme rather than proposition, ex post ergo propter hoc, whatever fits), with a short reply I could actually give in about ninety seconds, and an honest note on where each reply is soft. Please include: the so-what objection (that I'm dressing up the observation that the question is shorthand, which everyone already grants); which two objections have enough force that I should concede something in the talk itself rather than in Q&A; one objection I should answer by changing the paper rather than the performance; and the objection I'm secretly hoping nobody asks. Also flag anything I should just flatly not say in front of this crowd. My own worry, which you can use if it's useful, is that the partition test either has no bite or proves far too much — it looks like it might also dissolve why the Western Roman Empire fell when it did or why the Allies won, and I don't want to give those up.
