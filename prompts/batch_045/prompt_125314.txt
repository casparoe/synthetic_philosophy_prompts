My office-mate Rae works on speech act theory and I work on syntax, and I've been keeping a list of her off the cuff verdicts on wether some utterance really did the thing it names. So far: an actor playing a registrar saying "I now pronounce you married" to two actors — no marriage; a real wedding where the officiant's license had quietly lapsed and nobody found out for years — she said yes, a marriage, a defective one; a groom who was visibly drunk and told his friends afterwards he didn't mean it — married; a line of dialogue in a novel, "I promise I'll come back" — not a promise at all; "I bet you ten bucks" to a friend who just laughs — no bet; "We apologize for the outage" on a company's official account but written word for word by an intern — she said the company apologized and the intern didn't.

Rae has started calling things like this "orphan performatives" — right words, live channel, nobody behind them who means them or can answer for them; also a dead colleague's auto-scheduled email, an account whose whole team was fired. Would she say the appeal was granted in words there, or only that a system recorded an outcome some other process produced?
