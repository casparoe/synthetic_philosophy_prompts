I never studied philosophy or history beyond what the school required, and I have been reading on my own since I got involved with a tenants' union in my city. I keep running into one sentence — "the arc of history bends toward justice" — and I have now heard it used for two exactly opposite purposes.

At a meeting last month, when some of us proposed a rent strike that could have got people evicted, an older member told us not to force things: the arc bends toward justice, he said, housing will be recognised as a right eventually, and we should not spend our neighbours' goodwill on a fight we will win anyway if we are patient. A week later, at a vigil for a man who died in the county jail, I heard the same sentence from a speaker who used it the other way round: nothing bends on its own, she said, and the arc only bends because people with no power grab hold of it and pull. Both of them spoke as though they were reporting a plain fact about the world, and I cannot tell whether either of them was.

So I would like a menu rather than a single answer. Please set out the different precise theses that sentence could be expressing, and for each one: state the claim in a sentence; say what kind of claim it is — a plain empirical forecast about measurable indicators, a statistical tendency that permits long reversals, a metaphysical claim that history has a direction or a goal, a mechanism claim about injustice producing the resistance that undoes it, a claim about which facts we select when we decide to call something progress, or a normative claim about what we owe the future that has been put into the grammar of a prediction; say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or straightforwardly false; and say whether, if it were true, it would support the man at the meeting, the woman at the vigil, or neither of them.

I tried to sort the readings myself with three questions, and I cannot tell whether they actually separate anything:

The graph question. If you plotted justice against time over five hundred years, is the claim about where the curve ends up, about its average slope, about whether the slope is ever negative, about how rough the line is from one century to the next, or about the total area under the curve — the amount of justice actually lived through, rather than the level reached at the end? These strike me as different claims that could come apart: a curve could finish high while almost everything underneath it is misery.

The off-switch question. If everyone now pushing stopped pushing, would it still bend? A reading that answers yes and one that answers no seem to me to be doing quite different work, and it looks like only one of them could be used to counsel patience.

The reset question. Suppose the population collapsed, the archives and institutions were lost, and people rebuilt from scratch. Would the rebuilders arrive at something we would call justice faster than the first time, at the same speed, or not at all? I made this up to separate views on which the arc is driven by accumulated knowledge and institutions from views on which it is driven by something fixed about human nature, or by the structure of rational development itself.

Two further things I would find useful. First, when people repeat this sentence in speeches and on placards, which of the readings are they most likely to mean, and is there any reading on which repeating it is actively misleading — a way of making present injustice feel less urgent? Second, are these readings rivals, or can a sensible person hold several at once? And when I try to use the sentence myself I notice I slide between three different things — what the law says, what people believe about who counts, and how much suffering there actually is. I have seen it claimed that more people are living in slavery now, in absolute numbers, than in any earlier century. If that is right, it seems to pull those three apart, and I would like to know which of the readings survive the separation.

I would be grateful for something I can copy out and take back to the union.
