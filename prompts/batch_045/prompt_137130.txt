I record an interview series on moral philosophy, and our next guest is a consequentialist who has spent much of her career replying to Williams. Her central move is what she calls deliberative subsidiarity: the claim that a fundamental principle of rightness need not be usable as a decision procedure, and that it is a virtue rather than a defect in a theory if it is not. Her strongest and most recent chapter extends this from deliberation to auditing: not only may the agent act from love rather than from the impersonal principle, but the theory can positively forbid him from auditing his own commitments, since auditing has costs of its own and is itself an act the theory must assess.

The episode matters to me because I spent most of last night arguing about this with a friend, and the argument stalled in a way I could not repair. My friend holds that the whole force of the one thought too many objection is that it is raised from the first-personal standpoint: the complaint is not that the husband reaches the wrong verdict but that a theory which makes his love hostage to a verdict cannot be the theory he lives by. I kept insisting the objection is instead about what the agent may be able to think *while* acting, and that this is a different claim. We talked past each other for two hours over whether there is a real distinction between deliberating on a reason and merely having one.

I would like eight to ten questions that test my reading rather than let her restate the book, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Please build them around the following pressure points.

First, the timing question: whether the thought Williams objects to is corrupting only when it occurs in deliberation, and permissible when the agent is asked to justify himself afterwards. She will say something like this, so I need the follow-up: what fixes the time, and in what sense is a reason that may only be thought outside the moment the reason the agent acts on?

Second, her audit chapter. If the theory can forbid the audit, what distinguishes her agent from one whose commitments are simply unjustified, and does the self-effacement stop anywhere or generate a regress of higher-order checks? She is likely to answer that auditing is governed by expected costs; the follow-up should ask what happens over a whole life, in which projects sour and people change, so that a permanently unaudited commitment drifts toward complacency while the audited one drifts toward the fanatic.

Third, the mirror case: the husband who never registers the stranger's claim at all, the agent with one thought too few. If the extra thought corrupts, why is the missing one not also a fault? The follow-up has to separate the absorbed husband from the merely dismissive one, and to press her on whether the only way to separate them is by a disposition to have the thought when circumstances shift, which would make the thought mandatory again.

Fourth, second-personal uptake. Ask her whether the sentence the husband can address to his wife afterwards, that he saved her because she is his wife, comes out true on her view or merely useful for her to hear. The follow-up: if merely useful, the wife's complaint is managed rather than answered, and the objection has quietly become a claim about what we owe each other in the articulation of reasons rather than about the agent's private psychology.

Fifth, scope and the concession she makes in chapter six, where she grants a modest agent-centered prerogative to favour one's own projects. Does that concession dissolve the objection, or relocate it, so that the offending thought becomes whether one's project is important enough to trigger the prerogative? And if the objection does generalize to any theory with a standing reason to reconsider one's commitments, is she conceding that Williams's target cannot really be maximizing or agent-neutrality specifically?

Please end with one question about what would count against her: whether the integrity objection is, on her reading, an empirical claim about what deliberation does to us or a conceptual claim about reasons, what finding would move her if it is the former, and what she would say if it turned out that a defensible account of love requires the lover to be able to cite the relationship as a reason that is not derived from the impersonal one. Avoid anything biographical or about how she came to the view.
