Write a piece of satire in the form of an official guidance document issued by a fictional Office of Ontological Assessment that has begun levying an annual per-object tax on the contents of a household: a preamble plus numbered rulings, roughly 700 words, in the deadpan register of real tax guidance, including at least one worked example, one table of assessable and non-assessable items, and one line instructing taxpayers what they may not write in the margins. Title it as a revenue ruling on the Composite Object Return.

The humor has to come from getting the metaphysics right, so build the rulings around the following and exaggerate nothing that is not already in the position:

The Office has drafted its statute by asking the special composition question and receiving three competing answers: that any plurality of things whatever composes a further object; that nothing ever composes anything, there being only simples; and that composition occurs just when the joint activity of the parts constitutes a life. Each must be applied to an ordinary household inventory and shown to generate its own administrative absurdity rather than being strawmanned: the first by making the tax base literally inexhaustible andforcing the Office to tax such items as the sum of a taxpayer's left sneaker and a distant landmark; the second by compelling it to accept that there is no table, only things arranged tablewise, and to rewrite its assessment forms accordingly; the third by assessing the cat while refusing the table and then struggling with organic but non-living items.

The Office's escape attempt via a de minimis rule, whether by size, by value, by contact, by fastening, or by cohesion, should quietly reproduce the standard complaint that moderate restrictions draw the line in an arbitrary, gerrymandered place, and the Office should defend itself with the equally standard reply that any line looks arbitrary if you keep asking why here rather than who this serves.

End with a case the Office cannot rule on cleanly and settles by appeal to the purpose of the tax: a table one of whose legs has been replaced, and a composite whose parts are separately owned by two taxpayers, which the Office calls a split-tenure sum. A reader should come away having felt, without being told, that the composition question may have no answer except relative to some interest.

Constraints: name no real philosopher and cite nothing; let the positions be recognizable from their content alone. No character may notice the absurdity and no ruling may wink, and the satire must be fair enough that someone who works on the question would concede that the joke is on the view and not on a caricature of it.
