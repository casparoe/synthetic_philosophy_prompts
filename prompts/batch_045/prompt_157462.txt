I sit on the funding committee of a small foundation that moves somewhere between ten and fifteen million dollars a year. We rank causes with the importance–tractability–neglectedness framework that circulates in this part of the philanthropic world: score a cause by the value of solving (or substantially reducing) the problem, multiplied by the share of the problem you can expect to eliminate per million dollars spent, multiplied by how little is already going into it. One of our programme officers has written an internal paper arguing that the framework cannot underwrite a durable funding strategy. I cannot find the mistake in it, and I also cannot accept its conclusion. Reconstructed as cleanly as I can:

(1) Our score for a cause C is S(C) = I(C) × T(C) × N(C), where I is the value of eliminating the problem, T is expected problem-eliminated per dollar at current spending levels, and N is (inversely) the resources already devoted to C.

(2) S(C) is not a property of C alone. Both T and N are functions of what other funders are doing, so S depends on other agents' behaviour. Call any quantity with that feature crowding-sensitive. This step is not in dispute; it is why we re-run the ranking each cycle.

(3) A decision procedure is admissible as the basis of a standing strategy only if widespread compliance with it does not systematically destroy the conditions in virtue of which it issued its recommendation. A procedure that is self-defeating in that sense cannot guide action at scale, however well it serves whoever moves first.

(4) ITN is self-defeating in precisely that sense. Its top-ranked cause is by construction one that few others are funding. Acting on the recommendation moves money and people into that cause; that raises the resources going to it, which lowers N, which lowers S; and if enough of us act on it, the cause stops being top-ranked. The recommendation erases the ground it stood on.

(5) None of this turns on the details of ITN. Take any crowding-sensitive quantity, build a rule that says do more of whatever currently scores highest on it, and the same thing happens: successful compliance moves the quantity and withdraws the reason for complying. So no crowding-sensitive framework can support a standing strategy for a community of funders; it can at most give a one-off edge to whoever moves first and quietly.

(6) But (5) is absurd, and I want to treat that as a reductio. It indicts choosing the shortest queue, arbitraging a mispriced asset, triaging an emergency room by urgency, and allocating wherever marginal return is highest. Every one of those is crowding-sensitive; every one of them is fine. A principle that condemns all of them has condemned optimizing under scarcity as such. So something in (1)–(5) has to go.

(7) Hence we must restrict or replace the framework. The paper offers three options: treat ITN as a private heuristic valid only for actors too small to move the aggregate; drop N and rank by I × T alone; or replace N with an estimate of the total resources the cause will eventually attract, and fund only where we expect to move that total rather than join it.

Two things about our situation that I think constrain the answer. First, we commit on a three-year cycle and some of the causes we care about are thirty-year projects, so a framework whose verdicts flip when others move is a real problem for us and not a theoretical one. Second, we cannot measure T directly for most of what we fund — no trials, no clean counterfactuals, and where we do have estimates they are contested. Neglectedness is in the framework largely because it is the best available evidence about where the cheap opportunities are, not because anyone thinks crowding is intrinsically valuable. Assume that stays true.

There is also a worry I cannot tell whether it belongs to this argument or is a separate one. For a cause to be uncrowded may itself be evidence that better-informed funders looked at it and passed — the sidewalk-money thought. On that reading N is not a proxy for untapped opportunity but a proxy for adverse selection, and multiplying by it is not merely redundant with T but sign-flipping. Does that change the diagnosis, or is it an independent complaint that I should handle on its own?

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? Please name the step, explain why it looked innocent, and price the alternatives rather than only the one you pick — in particular, tell me what I lose if I give up (1) rather than (3) or (4), since I would rather keep (2) and (6) than (1). If you intervene at (3), give me the corrected version of the principle and say whether ITN survives it. And if the right answer is that ITN is fine at any given moment but unstable across time, tell me what a committee in our position should do with that instability. One thing I would find unhelpful: being told that ITN is only a heuristic and that heuristics are fine. I know that; it does not tell me which of the three options in (7) to adopt.
