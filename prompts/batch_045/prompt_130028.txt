I teach a graduate seminar on self-knowledge and I've been going in circles on the KK principle, so I thought I'd just dump my gut verdicts on a pile of cases and see whether anything reasonably tidy falls out. Quick flag on where I'm starting: I'm happy to give up luminosity — I don't think being in a mental state automatically puts you in a position to know you're in it, and the usual sorites-style argument against it seems right to me — but I don't want to end up saying that knowing-that-you-know is just one more piece of hard-won empirical knowledge, because that feels like it loses something. Here are the cases: (1) A subject shivering in a room at 2 degrees: she knows it's cold, and she knows that she knows. (2) Same subject, same discrimination ability, but she's in the middle of a slow-warming series and the coffee in front of her is at 92 degrees and genuinely scalding: I want to say she knows the coffee is hot but does not know that she knows — the sorites structure is doing the work, even though the first-order fact is completely clear. (3) Same as (2), except someone now tells her the temperature and hands her a printout of her own reliability profile, which is good above 85 degrees: verdict flips, she does know that she knows, even though nothing about her discrimination changed. (4) A blindsight-ish patient who guesses where the stimulus is and is reliably right: I want to say she doesn't know at all, so there's no KK failure — and I want that to fall out of the principle rather than be stipulated. (5) A grandmaster who plays the safe move instantly and can't give a reason: I'm inclined to say she knows, in some sense, that the move is safe, but doesn't know that she knows, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's a real counterexample to KK or just an equivocation on know. (6) My friend who overheard the platform announcement but was half asleep and now can't remember whether she heard it or dreamt it: she knows the train leaves at nine, but she doesn't know that she knows — and note this is a pure higher-order-evidence case with no sorites in sight. (7) The colleague who verified a proof twenty years ago, has forgotton the details, but clearly remembers having checked it line by line: he knows the theorem, and I want to say he knows that he knows, which sits badly with (2) since his access to his grounds is if anything worse. (8) An anesthesiologist who knows the patient is under, but whose monitor is showing awareness-like cortical activity: she doesn't know that she knows; variant in which the monitor is miswired to another patient: she does know that she knows — same evidence, different verdict, which I take it forces something externalist on me.

The thing I keep playing with is a nested-margin idea: roughly, KK holds for a subject just in case the margin for error on her first-order belief strictly contains the margin for error on her second-order belief about it, so that there's room for the first-order state to be safe while the second-order one isn't. I suspect that handles (1), (2), (4) and maybe (8), and I can't see how it handles (3) or (6) at all, since there the first-order margin never changes. So: is there a single principle that gets most of these, and if not, which of my verdicts should I be most willing to overturn? I'd rather you work the cases than walk me through the literature, and I'd like you to say what your account predicts for two cases I haven't listed — someone who knows a perceptual proposition but has been given a drug that makes her uniformly and uninformatively overconfident, and someone who is currently dreaming and wonders whether she's awake. Finish with a single line: \answer{the principle, stated in one sentence}.
