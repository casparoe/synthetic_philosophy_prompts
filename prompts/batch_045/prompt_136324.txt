I've been arguing about the doomsday argument with the same two friends for something like two years, no one has shifted by a millimetre, and I've stopped wanting to know who's right — I want to know what kind of disagreement this is. The classic version (you're a random draw from everyone who'll ever live, your birth rank looks suspiciously low, so probably not many more of us) always gets bogged down in the same three escapes: we don't know the prior over how long a technological species lasts, we don't know who's in the reference class, and it's not obvious "I could have been born later" is even coherent. The doom part is basically decoration anyway. So I built a version that blocks all three, and the three of us split exactly the same way as before, which makes me think the real disagreement was never in any of those places.

Here's the set up. A machine flips a fair coin. Heads: it creates exactly 200 observers. Tails: it creates exactly 200 million. Everyone is made in an identical sealed room, told the rules, and handed a card with a serial number assigned by a physically random lottery with known probabilities — no births, no evolution, no wondering whether you personally might never have existed; the sampling mechanism is sitting right there in the open. You wake up, read the rules, and your card says 137.

One friend says the posterior on heads is essentially one: the likelihood ratio is a million to one, obviously you update. The other says no, it's still a fair coin, because your prior should already have been tilted a million to one toward tails — a world with 200 million observers has a million times more slots for you to turn up in one of — and the two factors cancel exactly, so you stay at fifty-fifty. Me, I think we've now pinned the whole thing to a single move, but I can't tell whether that move is a substantive claim about rational credence, a disagreement about what a probability is for (my credence, versus what I should bet, versus what's a good policy for the group of all the observers), or a straight verbal fight about what the datum even is: "my serial number is 137" versus "someone exists with serial number 137" versus "I exist at all, and there are more ways for that to happen in the big world." I lean toward that last framing — that it's a fight about whether your own existence is the sort of thing that calls for an explanation, dressed up as a fight about numbers. Though I also wonder whether there's a hidden question about what would happen if the machine were run over and over, i.e. whether "probability" here is secretly supposed to cash out as long-run frequency across runs, which would make part of this empirical after all.
