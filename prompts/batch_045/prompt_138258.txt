I chair the grants allocation panel of a medical research charity — five of us, roughly forty applications a year, a charter that says we may fund a proposal only if (i) the scientific rationale is sound, (ii) the target cohort can realistically be recruited within the stated recruitment window, and (iii) the budget is realistic for the work proposed. Our last meeting deadlocked over a phase-II application, and I need to settle the procedure before Friday. The individual judgements came out like this: Adeyemi — (i) yes, (ii) no, (iii) no, so against; Bryce — (i) no, (ii) yes, (iii) no, so against; Chandra — (i) no, (ii) yes, (iii) yes, so against; Duval and Eriksen — yes on all three, so both for. Told premise by premise, the proposal clears every hurdle: (i) carries 3–2, (ii) carries 4–1, (iii) carries 3–2. Taken as a whole, it fails: three members voted to reject and two to fund. One of our number, who read philosophy before she read medicine, says this is the discursive dilemma and that we simply have to decide in advance whether we are a premise panel or a conclusion panel, because we cannot coherently be both; another says that on technical questions a majority of experts is more likely to be right than any one expert, so the premise tallies are the ones that carry the epistemic weight and the conclusion vote is just noise.

Three features of this particular case make me doubt that the choice is as clean as that framing suggests. First, Adeyemi's two negative votes are not really two doubts: she thinks recruitment will fall short, and she thinks the budget is unrealistic only because it assumes recruitment will succeed — so our agenda may have carved one disagreement into two votes and then counted it twice, and had we written the sub-questions differently the paradox might not have appeared at all. Second, Chandra is our lay member; she has told me privately that her "no" on (i) was not a scientific judgement, that she could not follow the assay validation, and that she voted no only because she was told a blank would be recorded as assent — which means the thinnest premise majority is the one carried by the votes of the two members who do understand the assay plus one who does not. Third, there is an outside audience: our reasons statement is capped at two sentences, it goes both to the applicant and to the trustees, and the applicant may appeal on the ground that the reasons we gave do not support the decision we reached — so an internally inconsistent statement is a real risk and not merely an embarrassment. Duval has proposed a way out that struck several of us as promising: treat the conclusion vote as binding on the decision, but only after a second round of discussion at which the premise tallies are disclosed, so that the panel can see whether its rejection survives contact with its own reasons. Our secretary, who enjoys this sort of thing, told me over lunch that there is a body of work showing that every obvious rule — premise-based, conclusion-based, and one that selects the outcome closest to the members' overall pattern of judgements — violates some condition you would want it to satisfy; I have no way to check that, so please do not lean on it or on anything else you cannot establish from the case as I have described it, and do not send me references.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to the other four before Friday. It should recommend a definite procedure for this application and for the rest of the year, apply it to reach a funding recommendation here, state exactly what our two-sentence reasons statement should say and whether the premise tallies ought to be disclosed to the applicant and the trustees, and give the strongest and fairest version of the case for the procedure you are rejecting rather than a caricature of it. Begin the memo with a single sentence giving the bottom line — the procedure and the decision — then give the reasoning, with no preamble, no restatement of my question and no throat-clearing; keep the whole thing to about 800 words, and write it in British English.
