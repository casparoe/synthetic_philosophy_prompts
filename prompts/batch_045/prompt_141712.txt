I'm a PhD student in history and philosophy of science, and I've been asked to give a short "what is philosophy of science for?" talk to a physics department — the kind of audience where someone will repeat the line usually attributed to Feynman, that philosophy of science stands to scientists as ornithology stands to birds, and treat that as settling whether science needs philosophy. I'd rather use the line as the spine of the talk than dodge it, so I need an audit of it as an argument.

Give me the mapping explicitly: what plays the role of the ornithologist, of the birds, of the study relation and of the usefulness relation, and what conclusion the analogy is being used to license when working scientists deploy it — roughly, that philosophy gives scientists no guidance about how to work or what to expect, and so should stop telling them how to work.

Then the disanalogies, sorted by whether they matter for that use. Three I want addressed. First, Weinberg's anti-philosophy chapter — the one arguing that philosophy's benefits to physics have been largely negative, shielding physicists from the preconceptions of other philosophers — reaches for analogies of its own, comparing philosophy's value to that of early nation-states to their peoples and physicists to hounds rather than hawks; those seem to concede philosophy a causal role, which the ornithology line excludes by construction, so I suspect the quip is stronger than the sceptic's own considered position and may prove too much. Second, ornithology takes the birds themselves as its object, whereas much philosophy of science takes theories, models and evidence relations as its objects; if the mapping is strict, the analogy quietly recasts philosophy of science as a social science of scientists, which is arguably the one construal on which its uselessness comes for free. Third, usefulness to whom: ornithology does nothing for birds directly but a great deal to birds through third parties — conservation agencies, legal protection, birders — so tell me what the corresponding third parties would have to be in the science case (funders, journal editorial standards, replication-crisis method reform, evidence grading in medicine, graduate curricula, expert testimony) and whether the argument from the analogy depends on their absence. Birds, unlike scientists, are not themselves trying to get things right about the world; say whether that does any work.

Finally: does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it? And is there a better analogy a sympathetic physicist would accept — grammar to speakers, cartography to navigators, music theory to composers — or does every candidate smuggle in a verdict about whether philosophy's object is the scientist or the world?
