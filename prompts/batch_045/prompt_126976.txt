I'm working on a feature for a magazine about whether moral judgment can be outsourced — to friends, to institutions, to clergy, and increasingly to systems that will tell you, in confident prose, what you ought to do. The piece keeps collapsing into either a shrug (of course you should listen to people who know better) or a scold (you have to think for yourself), and I want a case that breaks the tie rather than one that merely illustrates it. As I understand the debate, there are three positions in play. Pessimists hold that taking someone's word on a moral question can at best hand you a true moral belief, not the kind of moral understanding you need in order to act well, and that whenever deference looks good it is really because the testimony carried a non-moral fact. Optimists hold that moral testimony isn't epistemically special and can transmit genuine moral understanding. And a third view says the difficulty was never testimonial at all: there is no moral expertise to defer to, so the only real question is whether you have reason to think this speaker got it right. I need a case that pushes all three toward the same practical verdict — the agent does the right thing, and does it well rather than by luck, precisely by taking someone else's word about what she ought to do — while denying the pessimist her favourite explanation.

What I want from you is the scenario, followed by the demonstration that it does what I've asked. Design constraints: set it somewhere ordinary, in one paragraph, with no trolleys, no futuristic machinery and no AI oracle; the agent already possesses every relevant non-moral fact before the conversation begins, so nobody can say the testimony was informative only about how the world is; the agent is experienced rather than naive and is under no time pressure, so this can't be a case where deferring is just a labour-saving shortcut; and the speaker's reliability can't be established by the agent in any of the usual ways — no track record she can check, no argument she can follow and assess for herself, no corroboration from other people, no credential that settles it. It also has to survive being read, in the opening three hundred words of the piece, by someone who has never heard of this debate. After the scenario, give me the three readings: what each position says about the case, what it costs each of them to say it, and which of the three resists hardest and why.

Then be honest with me about the machinery. Name the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and tell me what the case degenerates into if that feature is removed — does it collapse back into a boring case of deferring about the facts, or into a case where deferring is plainly the wrong thing to do? Then give me the strongest version of the pessimist's counterattack: the precise way she would accuse you of having smuggled in a non-moral fact, or of having stipulated the verdict instead of deriving it from the view. And one last question my editor will ask, which I should be able to answer from the case itself: if the speaker had turned out to be wrong, would the agent still have done the right thing in deferring — and does your answer to that change which position the case is evidence for?
