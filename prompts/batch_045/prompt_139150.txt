I am assembling discussion materials for a graduate seminar on identity, individuality, and quantitative concepts, and I would like a dialogue I can hand out alongside the basic statement of the principle of the identity of indiscernibles. I would be grateful if you would write it.

Format and setup. A dialogue of roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words, with speaker tags and at most a few brief stage directions, set in the twenty minutes after a departmental colloquium, over coffee. The two speakers are colleagues who have clearly argued this through before and who respect each other's competence. Call them Dr. Ruhl and Dr. Amadi. The piece should be self-contained: a student who knows only the bare formulation of the principle should be able to follow it, so any technical notion has to be introduced and defined in the course of the conversation rather than presupposed.

The positions. Ruhl defends the identity of indiscernibles by appealing to weak discernibility: two things count as discerned if some irreflexive relation holds between them, so that two perfectly similar spheres standing at some distance from one another are discerned by the symmetric, irreflexive relation of spatial separation even though nothing asymmetric or monadic distinguishes them. Amadi holds that numerical difference can be primitive: there can be distinct individuals that no qualitative or relational difference distinguishes, and the irreflexive relation Ruhl leans on already presupposes the distinctness it is supposed to underwrite. Neither may be a caricature. Amadi may not simply gesture at primitive thisness and stop; he has to say what work such facts do and why positing them is better than Ruhl's alternative. Ruhl may not dismiss the two-sphere world as impossible or appeal to a perfect creator's lack of reasons; for the purposes of the exchange the case is stipulated to be possible, and the argument has to happen inside that stipulation.

The case they must work through. Rather than stopping at the classic example of two intrinsically identical spheres alone in an otherwise empty universe — symmetrically placed, standing in no asymmetric relation to one another, so that the cheap manoeuvre of saying that the first is two kilometres from the second but not conversely is unavailable — I want them to spend most of their time on a variant I have been using in seminar. At t1 the two spheres are as described. At t2 an indeterministic process, with objective chance one half, confers on exactly one of them an intrinsic property the other lacks: a uniform rise in temperature, say. The point of the variant is to force the question whether there was, at t1, a fact of the matter about which of the two would warm.

What each must press. Amadi should press that if there is such a fact, then the chance function distributes over outcomes that are qualitatively identical beforehand and whose difference remains unobservable afterwards, since seeing a warm sphere does not tell you which of the two it is; he should explain why he can live with that, or why he thinks Ruhl's own account of objective chance is committed to it anyway. Ruhl should press that if there is no such fact, then Amadi owes a story about the transition from t1 to t2: one object became two; or there was only ever one sphere and it fissioned; or there were never two objects but a single symmetric structure that lost a symmetry at t2. And she should make clear why the weak-discernibility reply does not by itself adjudicate among these, given that the separation relation holds both before and after the change.

Both should also face two questions head on: whether weak discernibility can ground the count claim that there are exactly two things here, and whether it can ground singular reference or demonstrative thought — this one, and not that one, will warm; and whether a relation's being irreflexive already takes distinctness for granted. Each speaker must make at least one real concession rather than merely reasserting a position: Ruhl, for instance, that separation does not tell you which is which and so cannot be the whole account of reference; Amadi, that the facts he posits do no work in any explanation or prediction and answer to no possible test.

Do not resolve the dispute. End instead with each speaker having identified, in the other's view, the one question that would have to be answered before the variant case could be settled. No footnotes, no citations, no mention of authors, papers, or titles; the argument should stand on its own.
