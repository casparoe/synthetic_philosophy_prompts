I teach a graduate seminar on rational choice, and I have found myself unable to give my students a number on a question that I am sure admits of one. The proposition I want priced is this: expected utility maximization is a genuine requirement of rationality, in the sense that an agent who systematically and reflectively violates it is thereby defective as a practical reasoner, rather than merely unconventional or merely optimizing something the axioms were never designed to capture. To fix what would count as resolution, I propose the following: count the proposition as true if at least one of three argument-strategies survives the two restrictions I specify below and delivers a norm that an agent could in principle violate while remaining blameless on every other count — count it as false if the most that can be established is that EU is one elegant option among several, or that violating it is imprudent rather than irrational, or that the appearance of a requirement is an artifact of how the theorems are stated. The three strategies I have in mind are: (i) synchronic sure-loss arguments, of the Dutch-book or sums-of-bets variety, which I take it get you to probabilism fairly directly but seem to need a substantive extra premise — roughly, that the agent prices a compound bet by addition across its components — before they get you all the way to expected utility rather than just to a probability function plus something else; (ii) diachronic exploitation or money-pump arguments, which appear to presuppose that the agent chooses myopically and lacks the capacity for resolute, plan-governed choice; and (iii) what I have been calling the repeated-exposure argument, which is the one I have never seen stated cleanly and would most like assessed: the thought is that if an agent's future decision problems are draws from a stable distribution and she applies the same choice function to each, then a violation of EU makes her realized payoff worse than what she could have secured by committing to the EU plan, with probability approaching one, so that the norm is really a claim about the long-run performance of stable dispositions rather than about coherence at a time. The two restrictions I want imposed, and which I think are not standard: first, in assessing (i), the bookie must post the entire book at once, in a form the agent can inspect before accepting any of it, and may not revise prices in response to what she accepts — I want to know whether the argument survives when the book must be legible in advance, or whether it depends on the agent being led through the bets one at a time; second, in assessing all three, the agent deliberates in a small world, in Savage's sense, and I want credit given for the problem of transferring a norm proved over a coarse partition to the grand world in which the agent actually acts.

What I want from you is a credence in the proposition, and the derivation of it rather than the number alone. Give me your own figure at the top, then decompose: the sub-questions your credence rests on, a rough probability for each with one line of justification, and then an explicit combination, together with a note on which single term your final number is most sensitive to. I do not want to be told that it depends on what you mean by rationality or on which decision theory you accept; make that a numbered term with a probability attached — the probability that there is a substantive, determinate notion of rationality capable of underwriting a requirement here at all — and let it do its share of the work in the product. Among the terms I would expect to see, though you should add or substitute as you think right: the probability that the representation theorems are sound as mathematics (I take this to be near one and do not need it belabored); the probability that the extra structural premise needed to get from probabilism to expected utility is itself rationally required rather than a convenience; the probability that diachronic arguments survive an agent capable of resolute choice; the probability that the repeated-exposure argument is valid on its own terms, granted an i.i.d. or otherwise stable distribution of future problems, and the separate probability that the distributional assumption is true of any agent we care about; the probability that small-world coherence transfers to the grand world; and the probability that no rival is rationally permissible, where the rival I have in mind is an agent who maximizes expected utility with respect to a strictly increasing but non-convex transformation of the cumulative probability of rank-ordered outcomes, thereby reproducing something close to Allais-pattern behavior while retaining transitivity and a great deal else.

Two further things. First, I am trying to locate a disagreement rather than win one. I sit at roughly 0.55 and a colleague in the decision-science group sits at roughly 0.15, on the view that the coherence literature is elegant mathematics plus a stretched analogy between inconsistency and incoherence; on your decomposition, which one or two terms are most likely where our difference actually lives, as opposed to where we would say it lives if asked? Second, I would like a short closing section on what would move your number, but not of the form more empirical work or further reflection. Say what specific result would move it and by how much: a proof, a counterexample, a class of agents, a change in what the theorems are taken to range over. A table for the decomposition is welcome, and I would be grateful if you kept the whole thing under eight hundred words; if you think one of my three strategies collapses into another, or that the real argument for EU is a fourth thing I have not listed, say so and price that instead.
