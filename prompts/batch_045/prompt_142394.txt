I'm writing a paper on replacing elections with lotteries — lottocracy, or whatever the proper term is — for my senior government class, and there is a feature of the actual proposals that I keep tripping over.

The schemes I have read about, the citizens' assemblies convened on things like climate policy, all seem to do several things at once. Members are chosen by lottery, so that in principle anyone might end up serving. The draw is then adjusted so that the group comes out matching the country by age, sex, region, income, education and so on, which means some people are deliberately much likelier to be picked than others. The longer-running ones usually replace anyone who drops out by drawing again from that person's demographic "cell," so the group keeps its shape. And members serve a single term they cannot repeat, with no election at any point.

Here is my problem. "Everyone gets an equal shot" and "the body should be a miniature of the people" look like two different ideas of equality, and the replacement rule suggests the second one is really the one doing the work. But if it is, I do not see why randomness is needed at all rather than simply choosing people on purpose, and I do not see what is supposed to make such a body accountable to anyone if it can never be voted out.

So: what does this package of practices actually presuppose about political equality, legitimacy, and accountability? Which parts of it do those commitments strictly require, which parts merely fit comfortably with them, and where is the design philosophically underdetermined, so that two quite different theories would still produce the same institution? I would rather have the disagreement laid out than be told which side is right.
