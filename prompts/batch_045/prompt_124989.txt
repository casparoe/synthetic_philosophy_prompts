I should explain where I am coming from, because it shapes what I want. I spent most of my working life in experimental surface science — growing epitaxial films and then arguing with diffractometers about what was actually on the substrate — and since retiring I have been sitting in on a Thursday-morning reading group in the philosophy department, which this term is working its way through the literature on metaphysical grounding and fundamentality. I find it exhilarating and also, I suspect unfairly, faintly comic: every time the reading insists that grounding is not causal, that it is not empirical, that it is hyperintensional, that derivative facts obtain in virtue of more fundamental ones, and that it is nevertheless the thing all genuine explanation runs on, my hands start looking for an instrument. I have been boring my friends about this for weeks, and I would like to turn the reaction into something disciplined rather than another grumble at the faculty club.

So: write me a piece of bureaucratic satire — an official Technical Bulletin of an invented standards body, call it the Office of Ontological Metrology, announcing that the Office is now able to certify, for a fee, the fundamentality rank of any submitted entity and the grounding relations among any submitted pair of facts. Give it a bulletin number and a date, write it as numbered clauses, and close it with a schedule of fees. Say 700 to 900 words. The voice throughout should be the flat, helpful, faintly weary voice of a public agency explaining a new regulatory service: no narrator, no asides to the reader, no puns on ground or depth or levels (I have already made all of those myself), and nothing that breaks the frame. The target is not a person but a research programme and a style of discourse — the practice of writing as though grounding were a single unified relation with stable formal features, and fundamentality a rank that could in principle be read off a hierarchy.

What I want the clauses to land, each by exaggerating something that really is in the literature rather than something invented for the joke: hyperintensionality, so that the intake form must distinguish formulations that are necessarily equivalent, and a substitution of co-referential descriptions voids the certificate or incurs a reformulation surcharge; non-causality, so that every ordinary detection method is solemnly ruled inadmissible while the Office insists it still measures something; asymmetry, irreflexivity and well-foundedness, so nothing may ground itself, loops are declared void, and some officer is answerable for chains that do not terminate; the question of what grounds the grounding facts, handled as a deferred certification or as a per-link fee that never quite ends; purity, the requirement that facts about derivative entities be grounded in facts about the more fundamental ones and not conversely; and the slogan that derivative entities are nothing over and above the fundamental ones, which the fee schedule should render with straight-faced literalism as a zero charge. Somewhere in the middle, let the forms require the applicant to declare whether grounding is a relation or an operator, with a note that the choice makes no difference to the fee. Then, after the schedule, include a short letter of complaint from an invented body — say the Association for the Replacement of Grounding by Identity, Causation and Law — whose real objection is the familiar one that grounding does no explanatory work beyond identity, causal and nomological facts, but stated in the idiom of a consumer who has been billed for a service that did nothing. Please do not let the Office get the last reassuring word: I would like the final line item to be the certification of the Bulletin itself, billed at nothing because it is absolutely fundamental, or else marked deferred indefinitely — your choice, but it should leave a reader who knows the literature aware that the deepest worry about grounding is the one the Office has just administered its way around. Two constraints: it should be funny to a specialist because the joke is fair rather than because it is rude, and it should be legible to the retired chemist and the civil engineer in my reading group who have read none of this — so no citations, no named authors, and no in-group shorthand that only works if you have read the papers.
