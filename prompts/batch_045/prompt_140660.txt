I am writing a novel set almost entirely within a single tower block in south-east London, and one scene in the middle of it has now defeated me three times running. Two characters — Nadia, who has spent twenty years as a pest control technician on that estate, and Marcus, a welfare biologist who used to advise on insect husbandry in laboratories — spend an evening disagreeing about what a humane response to an infestation actually consists in. Every draft I produce turns one of them into a straw man, because I can only ever hear one side of the argument properly. So I would be grateful for a careful comparison of the two positions I set out below, and for your judgement, at the end, about which is the stronger and why.

The concrete case is this. A German cockroach infestation is running through the service ducts of the block. The options on the table are: a residual pyrethroid spray; a gel bait (indoxacarb or fipronil), which the insects feed on and then carry back into their harbourages, where most of them die out of sight; an insect growth regulator such as pyriproxyfen, which barely touches the adults at all but disrupts moulting and reproduction, so that the population thins over months rather than overnight; and non-chemical work — proofing, sealing, monitoring. Assume the residents want the infestation gone, and assume nothing else about who is in the right.

The first position, which is Nadia's and which I have been calling the exclusionary view, holds that the proper object of pest control is not the death of animals but the terms on which a dwelling is shared. On this view the moral core of the job is removal rather than killing, and there is a real difference — a difference in what the technician is answerable for — between taking a life and bringing it about that a life ends. It therefore favours proofing, monitoring and growth regulators over poisons, treats the absence of corpses as a genuine achievement rather than as evidence of nothing, and says that a technician who reaches for the spray first has solved a problem for the residents by making a problem for herself.

The second position, which is Marcus's and which I can only half hear, holds that none of this is to the point: what matters is what happens to the animals, and whether a death is caused or merely permitted, visible or hidden from the person who permits it, is no part of any animal's good. On this view it is a genuinely open question whether proofing and release are kinder than a fast-acting poison, and there is a special difficulty with growth regulators: if the lives they prevent would, on balance, have contained more welfare than illfare, then suppressing a population by preventing those lives is not the moral free lunch Nadia takes it for.

I would like you to do the following.

First, state each view in the strongest form you can manage, including the strongest version of the objection the other side presses against it, and rename either or both if my labels distort them.

Second, there is an asymmetry I cannot get straight. Deaths caused directly tend to be visible: a flushing spray drives insects into the open where they can be counted, whereas a bait kills them in the cracks and voids, and proofing produces no bodies at all. Nadia's preferences track what she can see; Marcus says that is a moral illusion dressed up as a principle. How much work should this epistemic asymmetry be allowed to do? Is there something real in the distinction between doing and allowing here, or is the exclusionary view simply a rationalisation of what happens in front of us?

Third, a complication from the ecology. Because nothing any of these methods does alters the carrying capacity of the duct, the population is likely to be replaced by recruits from elsewhere; the number of animals alive in two years' time may be largely independent of how many are killed now. Does that help or hurt either view? My own sense is that it wrecks the welfare view's hope of preventing future suffering while leaving the exclusionary view untouched, but I suspect I am missing something.

Fourth, the growth-regulator case, which is where I get properly stuck, since it is an intervention that prevents lives rather than ending or displacing them. How should each view handle it, and does the answer turn on whether a prevented cockroach life would have been worth living?

Fifth, nobody knows whether insects are sentient in any sense that matters. The welfare view's verdicts seem to scale with one's credence on that question and to fall silent if the credence goes to zero, whereas the exclusionary view is entirely indifferent to the facts about the animals. Is that a point in its favour or a scandal?

Please argue from the considerations themselves rather than from authorities; I have no library within reach, and a bibliography would improve neither of my characters. I would rather you kept the disagreement alive than dissolved it too quickly — I need the hinge of it, not a verdict in the first paragraph — but I do want a verdict at the end, together with a sentence on what would have to be true for you to change your mind. Around a thousand words, in prose I can set a scene against.
