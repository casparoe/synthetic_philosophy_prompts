I host a fortnightly podcast on poetry and translation, for listeners who read poetry casually and who mostly do not read French. My guest for the next episode is a poet and translator whose new book consists of 140 English versions of a single short poem by Clément Marot: the get-well verse usually titled À une damoyselle malade and nicknamed, from its first line, Ma mignonne. It is twenty-eight lines long, three syllables to a line with the stress on the final syllable, a run of rhyming couplets, a switch partway through from the formal vous to the familiar tu, the poet's own name set into the middle of the poem, a teasing suggestion that the addressee made herself ill with sweets, and the opening line repeated word for word as the last.

She uses all this as the centerpiece of a general argument about translation: that a poem's form is not the vessel of its content but a large part of what the poem is; that in translating poetry the formal constraint should therefore be honored first and the wording negotiated afterward; that a version which keeps the sense but drops the meter and the rhyme is not a translation of the poem but a report of what the poem says; and that constraint is generative rather than merely restrictive, so that binding herself to the three-syllable, final-stress, couplet-rhyming shape is what allows her to reach solutions she could never have reached by first deciding what she wanted to say.

I need eight to ten questions that go beyond inviting her to restate that position. For each question, give me the answer you expect from a defender of this view, and one follow-up keyed to that answer. Please build the set as a sequence that tightens rather than as an inventory of separate topics, and make sure it covers at least the following pressure points.

First, the vous-to-tu switch. English has no grammatical T–V distinction, so she must either drop the shift or reproduce its social effect with some other device. Ask what she takes herself to be preserving when she preserves it; then press on the point that the French device and her English device are different linguistic objects with similar effects, so it is not obvious that the form has been preserved rather than replaced by a functional substitute. I suspect this opens into whether there is any language-neutral description of a poem's form at all, and I want a question that forces her off the fence on that.

Second, the embedded name. Marot puts his own name into the poem, and she must decide whether to keep Clément or substitute her own name to make the rhyme work. Whichever she does has a consequence worth exposing: if she substitutes her own name, her version becomes a poem in which the translator speaks in her own person; if she keeps his, her poem contains an untranslated proper noun doing rhyming work. I want a question that makes her say which of these is closer to translation, and a follow-up on what the answer implies about whether a proper name belongs to form, to content, or to neither.

Third, the counting problem. Whether a line has three syllables, and whether two words rhyme, are settled differently in French and in English: the languages differ in what they elide, and in how much a rhyme may be carried by grammatical endings rather than by sound alone. Press her on whether the constraint she is honoring is really the constraint the original satisfies, or only the nearest English analogue to it; and if she answers by appealing to the effect on a competent reader, test whether effect-preservation can bear the weight she puts on it, given that the original's readers and hers have different competences.

Fourth, ranking within the constraint. If many versions satisfy the same constraint, the constraint cannot be what separates the good ones from the bad ones. Ask her what does, and specifically whether she has a criterion she could state before producing a version, or whether quality here is only ever recognizable after the fact.

Fifth, a worry of my own that I would like put to her rather than smuggled in as my editorial line: should a translation be as constrained relative to English as the original is relative to French? Twenty-eight lines of three syllables with final stress is a demanding shape in English, but for some source forms and some language pairs the formal fidelity would cost almost nothing, and it is not clear that honoring a constraint one could satisfy in one's sleep is worth much. Ask whether difficulty of reproduction is part of what makes formal fidelity valuable, and whether she accepts the consequence that the same degree of formal fidelity can be worth more for one language pair than for another.

Sixth, the occasion. This is an occasional poem: a playful get-well message to a particular person, closing by wishing her health. Several of my guest's versions are addressed to other things altogether — a failing laptop, a stalled car, a department in crisis. Ask whether, on her own account of form as content, the speech act is also part of the content, and whether those versions are translations or new occasional poems in Marot's shape.

Seventh, the problem of the many. Her book gives 140 versions of one poem, and the 1997 book by Douglas Hofstadter that made this poem a case study collected dozens more. Ask what makes them all versions of one poem rather than a family of English poems with a common ancestor, and what she says to someone who argues that the sheer number is itself evidence that no single one of them is the translation.

Eighth, and I want this near the end: what would count against her method. Ask what evidence would show that form-first translation fails — for instance, if readers who know no French consistently prefer the freer versions, or if her most formally exact versions are also her least affecting — and what she would treat as a genuine cost rather than a price worth paying.

Constraints on what you write: no biographical or how-did-you-get-into-this questions, no invitations to summarize the book, and no questions that depend on the listener knowing French or any theory of translation. Each question should be askable aloud as it stands, with any technical term either avoided or glossed in the question itself. Do not frame questions around quotations I would have to look up, and do not supply citations, reading lists, or suggestions for further reading: I do my own briefing.

Finally, two things about your own confidence. For each question, flag in a line or two any assumption it makes that she could reasonably reject, and say what answer would make you drop or rephrase the question. Then add a short closing paragraph: which of these lines of questioning you expect her to handle most comfortably, which you think is most likely to collapse into slogan, and which single argument from her would most change your own mind about whether honoring the constraint first is defensible as a general method rather than as a personal working habit.
