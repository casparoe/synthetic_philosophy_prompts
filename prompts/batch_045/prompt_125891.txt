I am a novelist some twenty years into my career, a long stretch of which was spent teaching in an MFA program, and I am at work on a book whose narration has backed me into a technical dispute I am not equipped to conduct. I would like you to restate that dispute in the vocabulary I actually work in, and then to report honestly on what the translation fails to carry.

The novel is set between 1968 and 1971 in Barrow's Mill, an invented mill town in northern New England, and is narrated in the present tense first-person plural by the voice of a single street: a choral "we" that reports the town's doings in the town's own idiom. In its first sentence that voice applies a period slur — call it W — to the Franco-Canadian families who live at the lower end of the street. In chapter nine the "we" narrows to the consciousness of one of those men, Aubin Ducharme, and the free indirect narration applies W to his own people, his own household. In chapter fourteen a character declines to say W aloud, and the narration marks the refusal without printing the word, which is the only place in the book where W is genuinely absent. The final chapter is narrated by Ducharme himself, and he uses W of himself once.

My editor's position is that the choral frame means the word is exhibited rather than used: the street says it, the book reports it, and the difference between those two things is the whole of fiction. An early reader's position is that the word lands on her irrespective of the frame, and that the frame is a pass the book has written for itself. My own defense leans on exactly the distinction these two are contesting, so I went looking for the argument and found that philosophers have been having it for some years, in terms I can't use.

There appear to be four broad families of account. One holds that the derogation is part of the word's encoded meaning, so that no amount of framing, negation, or reported-speech marking removes it, even when the utterance is perfectly reportable. A second holds that the derogation derives from a standing prohibition on the word: the content is roughly that the targets are licensed objects of discrimination, and anyone who utters it — even in quotation, even to condemn it — invokes and thereby reinforces the authority of that prohibition. A third treats the derogation as expressive rather than descriptive, an act of contempt performed by the utterance rather than a proposition it asserts, which is why it survives negation, questions, conditionals, and explicit disavowal, and why saying "I don't endorse this" does not switch it off. A fourth treats it as a pragmatic inference: hearers read contempt off the speaker's having chosen a marked and offensive term, which suggests that where there is no real speaker and no real hearer, something importantly different is going on.

Translate each of those, one at a time, into the vocabulary of prose fiction. I am fluent in it and do not need it explained: point of view against focalization, who speaks against who sees; free indirect style and the borrowing of a character's idiom by the narration; diegetic against non-diegetic; the implied author; reliable and unreliable narration; dramatic irony; mimesis against diegesis, showing against telling; ventriloquism and an ear for period register; flat and round characterization; psychic distance; code-switching; paratext, by which I mean the jacket copy, the epigraph, and any author's note; and the split between the fictional addressee inside the book and the actual reader holding it.

Then give me the residue. For each account, say what translated cleanly, what had to be bent to fit, and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all — and say, where you can, whether the residue marks a poverty in the craft vocabulary, an artifact of the philosophers' assumption of a single intending speaker addressing a single hearer at a single moment, or a genuine vagueness in the position itself. I am especially interested in three suspicions I can't settle: that my vocabulary has no way of registering a speech act that goes through but misfires; that it treats every word in narration as doubly attributed, to the character and to the implied author, in a way none of the four accounts model; and that it treats derogation as an effect accumulating across three hundred pages rather than as something a single utterance does once.

Please do not let the translation quietly import my framework's assumptions and call them equivalents — in particular, do not settle the dispute by appeal to reader effect unless the account in question really does make reader effect the whole story, and do not resolve it by reminding me that these are craft choices, since that is precisely what is at issue. I am not asking you to tell me whether to cut the word. If one of the four accounts, once stated in my terms, evidently dissolves the disagreement between my editor and my reader rather than deciding it, say that, and say which.
