I am a fourth-year PhD candidate in philosophy working on normative ethics and decision theory, and I am assembling a bank of practice questions for my cohort's comprehensive exam in moral philosophy. I can write serviceable questions on material I know cold; what I need is one I could not answer by summarizing a debate I have already read. The topic I want is decision-making under moral uncertainty, and I want the question to bite on the part of that topic where the machinery, not the slogans, is doing the work.

Here is what I want it to test. Candidates should be forced to engage the aggregation proposal in its most defended form: rescale each moral theory's utilities for the options on the table by dividing through by the standard deviation of the values it assigns across that menu, then maximize the credence-weighted sum of the rescaled values. They should have to show, rather than assert, that this procedure is sensitive to the shape of the menu in objectionable ways: that adding or deleting an option no candidate would ever choose, or splitting one option into two near-equivalent variants, can reverse the ranking between the two options originally in question. They should then have to say what the rescaling step presupposes about comparability of value across theories, and whether anything internal to the theories themselves settles the scale factor, or whether the choice of a scale factor is a substantive moral commitment smuggled in under a technical-looking convention.

Second, I want the question to separate the two things candidates routinely conflate: the worry about tiny probabilities of astronomically large payoffs within a single theory, and the quite different worry about how to combine theories. The case should include a long-shot option whose expected value under the aggregative theory is enormous, so that a candidate has to work out what the rescaling does to that outlier, and whether the result is a principled cap on the pull of huge stakes or an artifact showing that the apparatus has quietly adopted a view about stakes it cannot justify.

Third, the question should contain a two-stage element, so that candidates must address the sequential-choice argument against simply acting on whichever theory has the highest credence: whether that argument shows the favourite-theory approach is exploitable over time, and whether the argument itself presupposes the cross-theoretic aggregation it is meant to establish. A strong answer should also say whether the credence-weighted approach can ever recommend an option that every theory in the candidate's credence set ranks below some alternative, and whether that is a reductio or a cost to be accepted.

Constraints: closed-book, ninety minutes, twenty marks, and the question must be self-contained. State the aggregation rule and give explicit numbers for a menu of three or four options within the question itself, since candidates are marked on argument and may not appeal to the literature. No part of the question should be answerable by naming a view or recalling who defended it.

Please structure your response in four sections, in this order:

1. Question — about 250 words. The exam question itself, with subparts, ready to hand to a candidate.
2. Model answer — about 1,000 words, written as a strong candidate would write it under exam conditions: worked arithmetic where arithmetic is called for, no meta-commentary, no citations.
3. Marking scheme — a table with one row per must-hit point and columns for the required move, the marks awarded (twenty in total, weighted so the two hardest moves are worth the most), what a competent but recall-dependent B answer does, what an A answer adds, and the one way candidates typically get the point wrong; followed by about 150 words on how to separate an A from a B overall.
4. Common evasions — about 200 words listing the moves that read well but should earn no marks, so that I can police my own practice answer honestly.
