I work as a backend engineer (Go and Postgres, mostly on a payments service), and my only formal exposure to logic is a first-year course on automata and formal languages that I remember only vaguely. Three of us from the platform team have been working through *Gödel, Escher, Bach* in a lunch reading group, and we've gotten stuck on the MU puzzle — not on the solution itself, but on what solving it is supposed to show. The book keeps gesturing at "jumping out of the system" as if it were one thing, but in our discussion it seemed to be doing at least three or four different jobs at once, and I'd like those separated out and their relations explained.

To pin things down, here is the case I keep turning over. Take the MIU system in its usual form: axiom MI, and rules allowing you to append U to a string ending in I, to double everything after the M, to replace an occurrence of III by U, and to delete an occurrence of UU. Inside the system you can shuffle strings around forever without ever settling whether MU is producible; from outside you notice that the number of I's starts at 1, that doubling and subtracting-three both preserve non-divisibility by 3, and that the other two rules don't touch the count, so a string with zero I's is unreachable. Now change only the third rule to IIII → U. Then MU *is* reachable (MI → MII → MIIII → MU), but my colleague and I convinced ourselves — correctly or not, please tell me if we've botched it — that the reachable counts of I are exactly 1, 2, and the multiples of four including zero, so that MIII is still unreachable, and that the argument for this needs a backwards, inductive step rather than a one-line modular observation. That variant is what made me suspicious that "find an invariant" is being presented as a single trick when it really isn't.

So, concretely, I'd like the following explained, in terms I can map onto things I already know (type systems, sound over-approximations, abstract interpretation, invariants on a state machine, the difference between running a program and reasoning about all its runs):

1. What exactly is the jump here? I can tease apart (a) moving from searching a space of derivations to proving a statement *about all* derivations, (b) moving to a meta-language in which the original system's strings and rules are objects that can be quantified over and negated, (c) mapping the system into some other structure — here, arithmetic modulo 3 — such that every rule is sound with respect to that mapping, and (d) moving to a theory of greater proof-theoretic strength. Which of these is actually load-bearing in the MU proof, and which are merely incidental? Is the modulo-3 argument essentially the same object as a sound finite abstraction in program analysis, or is there a difference that matters?

2. Relatedly: is the finite-abstraction route always available? For a general string-rewriting system, if every genuine non-reachability fact were witnessed by some computable finite invariant, then reachability would be decidable — and I'm fairly confident it isn't in general — so there must be cases where no single finite abstraction settles the question. What does "jumping out" buy you there, and does it degenerate into an endless ladder of ever-stronger systems rather than one decisive jump?

3. Is the MU puzzle really a miniature of Gödelian incompleteness, as the book suggests, or does the analogy break exactly where it matters? My sense is that the two cases fail in opposite directions: MIU cannot so much as *express* "MU is not derivable" — it has no negation, no quantifiers, no way to talk about its own derivations, and its strings have no truth-conditions until someone interprets them — whereas a system like Peano arithmetic can express its own undecidable sentence, which is true under the intended interpretation but unprovable in the system. If that's right, then MU illustrates an expressive limitation and Gödel illustrates a limitation in proof-theoretic strength, and the "toy model" framing may be hiding the role of self-reference. Am I overstating the disanalogy, or is it the standard way to draw the line? And if the meta-theory needed for the MU argument is extremely weak, what exactly was gained by leaving the system — expressive power, or strength, or something else? I'd rather have the conceptual structure with a couple of worked illustrations than a survey of positions, so please argue for the reading you find most defensible.
