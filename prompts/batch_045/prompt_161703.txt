I'm an undergraduate in a seminar on Buddhist philosophy and personal identity, and I work about fifteen hours a week as a care companion on a locked memory-care unit. The thing I'm actually required to do, several times a week, is answer families who ask some version of "is this still her?" It comes up in corridors and at the edges of care conferences, I have a minute or two, and the question is almost always a decision in disguise: whether to keep visiting, whether a promise made ten years ago still binds, whether to consent to a procedure. Our charting system has just added two mandatory fields after every family contact: a yes/no box reading "Resident is still themselves," and a one-line "who is this resident now" statement capped at 150 characters. The charge nurse reviews both and sends back anything that reads as evasive, so whatever I say has to be short, plain, and defensible to someone with no philosophy background.

What I want is a procedure I can run in about ninety seconds, in my head, that turns what the no-self view actually commits us to — no permanent, independent self standing behind the changing stream of body, feeling, perception, dispositions and consciousness; the person as a conventional designation over that stream; and crucially, none of this entailing that there is no one there or that continuity is an illusion — into a sequence of checks I can run on a family's question. Please preserve three distinctions in the translation: denying a substantial self is not denying continuity; a change in psychological continuity is not automatically a change in what is owed to someone; and there is a difference between speaking conventionally, which is legitimate and often the only appropriate register, and making a metaphysical claim, which is almost never what the family needs. For each step, say what would count as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the question back to the family as a matter of values rather than fact. It must tell me what to type into those two fields, including what to do when the yes/no box is ill-posed, and it must be honest about where a ninety-second version distorts the view it came from. Use no Pali or Sanskrit terms and no jargon a colleague couldn't follow on a busy shift.

Please give the procedure itself as a numbered list of exactly five items, the five steps in order, each one or two sentences, with no preamble before item 1 and no summary after item 5. Then, in a separate short block, run it on three cases: a daughter who says her mother, while still sharp, made her promise that machines would never keep her breathing, and who now asks whether she is still bound; a husband who has decided to stop visiting because his wife no longer says his name and who wants me to confirm that the woman he married is gone; and a son whose mother was anxious and guarded before her diagnosis, now hums all day, hugs strangers and does not recognize him, and who wants the chart to say that she is suffering and would hate this. Close that block with a single sentence on what the procedure cannot settle. Please write in American English.
