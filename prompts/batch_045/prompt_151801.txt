Hey — hoping you can settle an argument on my team, or at least tell me what's actually at stake, before it turns into a design meeting where everyone argues from vibes.

Context: six of us maintain a CI scheduler — you hand it a bunch of jobs with dependencies and machine budgets, it hands back an order to run them in — and for about a year now we've been proving its core properties in a proof assistant, with the idea that the scheduler itself gets extracted from the proof rather than hand-written, so there's no gap between what we proved and what actually runs. Right now maybe a third of our proofs go through by contradiction or by splitting on some either/or that isn't really decidable, and the tool flags all of those as noncomputable, so those parts don't extract at all. So we're arguing about whether to bite the bullet and rewrite everything so that every "there exists" comes with an actual procedure for producing the thing, and every case split is on something we can genuinely decide.

Two escape hatches have been thrown into the argument that I definately can't evaluate:

1. Our one team member with a logic background says we don't need to bother for the main theorem, because it has the shape: for every job graph there is an order that meets the deadline, and whether a given order meets the deadline is mechanically checkable. He says there's a conservativity result (his words: some translation trick) showing that for statements of exactly that shape, if a classical proof exists then a constructive proof exists too — so extraction is safe and the rewrite buys us nothing.

2. Someone else says: don't rewrite, just add a rule that whenever you've refuted "no witness exists", you're allowed to write a search that keeps going until it finds one. He says this is harmless because the case where it spins forever can never actually come up.

Both smell like they're smuggling something past me, but I can't say what. There's also a thing I care about that nobody's addressed: I don't want the extracted code to depend on which proof of the same statement we happened to write, because then our "verified" scheduler is really just an artifact of proof style.

So please lay out the most important implications of taking the constructive route seriously — and of each of those two escape hatches — for what we can actually get out of our verification work. Concretely: (a) do we still get a real, running scheduler out the other end, and will it terminate; (b) which shapes of statement can we keep proving classically with no loss and which ones break; (c) what the rewrite actually costs us day to day — proof size, the statements we're allowed to make, and anything we'd have to just give up on. Be blunt about where 1 and 2 are right and where they're wrong.
