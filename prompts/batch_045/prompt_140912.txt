A friend of mine who reads a lot of popular science argues that discoveries are inevitable, and he puts it like this: independent discovery in science is just convergent evolution. Two lineages that never met both end up with camera eyes, or with wings, or with the same enzyme trick, because the environment makes that the good solution and keeps selecting for it — so calculus was going to happen in the late 1600s and natural selection in the 1850s, and which person happened to arrive first is like which lineage happened to throw the useful mutation: a fact of natural history rather than something to admire or reward. He uses this to support two conclusions at once, that the genius theory of invention is a myth and that it does not really matter who gets the credit.

I am not sure the comparison carries that much, and two things keep bothering me. First, the two versions of calculus do not look interchangeable to me — different notation, different starting ideas — and the notation that won seems to have shaped what later mathematicians could then do easily; whereas convergent traits are usually alike in function but different in structure and not swappable, which makes me think the analogy quietly assumes the duplicates were the same thing when they were not. Second, evolution seems to have no counterpart to a shared literature: lineages cannot inherit each other's acquired advances, but scientists read one another and pass formal tools along, so the independence may be thinner than it looks.

Could you audit the analogy for me? I would like an explicit table of what is supposed to correspond to what — the environment and its pressures, a mutation, selection, a trait, a lineage — then the disanalogies, split into the ones that actually bear on those two conclusions and the ones that are just noise, and then a verdict on whether either conclusion survives once the mismatches are on the table. If the analogy is doing less work than it appears to, is there a better one? My own attempt is that it is more like two parties reaching the same summit by different routes, where the route taken determines what is climbable next — but I worry that just begs the question in the opposite direction. I would also value your view on a point we got stuck on: the famous thought experiment about rewinding the tape of life is normally told as a story about contingency, yet he says it actually supports him, since the tape gets rerun and life finds eyes again anyway. Who has the better of that?
