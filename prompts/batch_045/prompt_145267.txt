I serve on a body that has to recommend a design for publicly funded parental leave, and I find that I have firm reactions to individual cases and no settled account of what they add up to. I am not asking for comparative data, statutory surveys, or citations; I want the shape of the justification, stated precisely enough that I could defend it to someone who disagrees. Here are my verdicts, given on cases as they come to me.

(a) A dual-earner couple, six months of paid leave available. I want three months reserved to each parent and non-transferable, and I hold this even where both parents freely agree that the mother should take all six. I dislike the freely divisible version a lot, not slightly.

(b) The same couple, except that the second parent has died in childbirth, or has been absent since before the birth and cannot be located. I want the full six months available to the surviving parent. I can see this strains against (a): if I am willing to override a couple's own agreement in (a), why does the absent parent's share become available on the same facts plus an absence?

(c) A parent takes the full paid leave and resigns in the week after it ends. I oppose any clawback or repayment condition. The benefit is theirs to keep, and I hold this even though the leave plainly failed to preserve the attachment to that employer.

(d) A self-employed or platform-work parent with no contribution record and patchy earnings documentation. I want a floor entitlement funded from general revenue, available on something like self-certification. I hold this despite thinking the verification problem is real, and despite the fact that the case for an insurance-style benefit seems weakest exactly here.

(e) A parent who places the child in full-time daycare for the whole of the paid-leave period and returns to work. I recoil; this strikes me as misuse. A parent who works remotely for their employer during the leave period strikes me as worse still. I cannot say what rule I would write to catch either one.

(f) A parent who spends the paid-leave period in full-time retraining, while the other parent provides the care. I have no objection at all. I notice that in both (e) and (f) the recipient is not providing care, and that in (f) the recipient is not even at home, and yet my reactions differ sharply.

(g) The replacement rate. I want earnings-relatedness in the middle of the distribution, a cap at the top so that a very high earner receives a lower replacement rate, and a floor at the bottom high enough that a low earner can actually afford to take the leave — which means the benefit is not insurance-like at either end. This sounds to me like three different principles bolted together, but I am unwilling to give up any of the three.

(h) A stillbirth at thirty-eight weeks. I want the leave to stand in full, undiminished.

(i) The adoption of a seven-year-old from foster care. I want more leave than for a newborn, not less, even though there is no pregnancy and no infant care.

(j) A gestational surrogate who is not a legal parent of the child. I want no parental leave for her, but paid time for physical recovery from the birth. I hold both halves confidently and am not sure they are consistent.

The case I keep reversing on: a household of three adults raising the child — two legal parents and a live-in relative who is the child's primary daytime caregiver. Should the reserved quota divide into three non-transferable shares? Dividing it seems to follow directly from whatever makes me endorse (a); refusing to divide it seems right because I do not want the state deciding how many caregivers count. I go back and forth within a single conversation.

My own attempt at a principle, which I suspect is wrong: paid leave is justified exactly insofar as it neutralizes two distortions — the statistical discrimination that leads employers to discount a young woman's career, and the intrahousehold bargaining that pushes the lower-earning parent out of the labor market — and no further. This handles (a) beautifully and predicts my reaction to (c), but it seems to imply that once the discrimination were gone the policy should collapse into an unconditional cash transfer, which I reject, and it has nothing obvious to say about (h) or (i).

What I would like from you: a principle, or at most three candidate principles, stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause; its verdicts on cases I have not listed — a parent who was not in paid work before the birth and never intended to be, a second child born eleven months after the first, and a parent who uses the leave to care for a newly disabled partner rather than for the child; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate, especially among (a)/(b) and (c)/(e)/(f), where I expect I am holding something indefensible. If the principle has implications for who should bear the cost rather than only for what parents should receive, say what they are.
