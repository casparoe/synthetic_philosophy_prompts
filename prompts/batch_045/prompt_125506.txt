I am putting together a two-hour graduate seminar on decision-making under moral uncertainty, and I need a diagnostic flowchart rather than a survey. Starting point: the reader assigns non-trivial credence to two or more moral theories that disagree about what they should do. Each branch node should be a single question, phrased so that someone can answer it without already knowing which view it leads to, and each leaf should carry the name of a position (or be marked unnamed if nobody actually defends it) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of theories: first the questions and the answer combinations they allow, then the real positions located in that space. At minimum these distinctions should be doing the branching work: whether moral uncertainty is to be handled by the same decision rule as ordinary empirical uncertainty or is sui generis; whether intertheoretic comparisons of value are made directly or only after some calibration of each theory's utility scale; whether choiceworthiness is assessed act by act or over policies that include one's own future choices; how the rule behaves when one theory assigns astronomically large values to very low-probability outcomes; whether the credences plugged in are the agent's own or something like an aggregate of informed opinion; and whether what is aggregated is choiceworthiness or only permissibility.

Three cases I want usable as nodes: a case where the option that is best on the agent's highest-credence theory forecloses an option that a lower-credence theory treats as mandatory later, while a compromise option is second-best on both and preserves access; a case where one theory's scale is compressed near the top (nearly all options tie) while another's is spread out, so that mixing them raw lets the spread-out theory dominate; and a case pitting a certain modest benefit against an astronomically large benefit at vanishingly small probability.

Constraints: seven branch questions and nine to eleven leaves. Leaves must include myopic expected-choiceworthiness maximization, a non-myopic or policy-based variant, a rank-based or Borda-style hedging rule, a variance-normalized variant, an anti-fanatical or tempered variant, and a non-cognitivist option on which non-moral attitudes rather than moral credences get weighted. One leaf should be a hybrid I have been toying with, which I want located on the tree rather than merely listed: maximize expected choiceworthiness, but treat any option within a fixed tolerance of the maximum as tied; among tied options prefer the one permissible on the greatest total credence; break any remaining tie by whichever option preserves more of what each theory regards as the best available future choice. Tell me where it lands, which neighbors it collapses into, and whether its cell is genuinely unoccupied or merely unattractive. Finally, flag any two leaves your tree cannot actually separate and say what further case would separate them, and for at least two empty cells say whether they are empty because incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored.

Please write your answer in American English.
