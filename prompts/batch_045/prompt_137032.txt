I am writing the opening section of a paper on the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis and, before I commit to a position in print, I would like an audit of the package of views I have ended up holding, because I cannot locate where it is under strain. Here are the commitments, stated as flatly as I can.

First: the mathematical thesis, that every effectively computable function is Turing-machine computable, is true, and I want to say that we know it with a confidence greater than that of any empirical generalization. But it is not a theorem, and I do not think it could be turned into one, since "effective procedure" is not a mathematically precise predicate and so cannot figure as such in a proof.

Second: it nevertheless has genuine content. It is not a stipulation or a disguised definition of "computable," and it is revisable in principle. To make that concrete, here is the discovery I take would force us to abandon it: someone publishes a finite description, followable by anyone competent, of a device that can be built and run by different people in different places with the same results, and that returns correct answers to arbitrary instances of the halting problem, by drawing on some unbounded physical resource — a quantity measured to unlimited precision, say, or an infinite process compressed into a finite elapsed time.

Third: my main reason for believing the thesis is the convergence of the formalisms. Turing machines, general recursive functions, lambda-definability, register machines, Post's production systems, cellular automata — proposed independently of one another, and all of them picking out exactly the same class of functions, where the co-extension is itself a provable mathematical fact. I am inclined to think this robustness would retain its evidential force even if we had no independent account at all of what a procedure is.

Fourth: the physical version of the thesis, that no physical system can compute beyond the Turing limit, is a distinct claim, and an empirical one, and I think it is probably false. I see nothing in the physics we actually have that rules out the arrangement I described above.

Fifth: none of which threatens the first commitment, because finiteness belongs to the notion of an effective procedure itself. Finitely many steps, on finite discrete configurations, each step fixed in advance, locally checkable, and surveyable by whoever carries it out. A working version of my device would not be executing an effective procedure, and I take that to be a substantive claim about what procedures are, not a verbal maneuver to protect the thesis.

Sixth: at the same time, I think the real weight of the case lies in the analysis of what a human being can do when following a rule — bounded attention, one local operation at a time, marks on a page — rather than in the equivalence results; the convergence strikes me as remarkable, but not as what convinces me. And I want the negative results to keep their full force: when we say that the halting problem is unsolvable, or that there is no algorithm that decides which Diophantine equations have solutions, I take us to be saying something absolute and not merely that no Turing machine manages it, and it is the thesis that licenses that step.

What I want from you is an audit of these six taken together. Which of them actually pull against one another, which only appear to, and for each real tension, what exactly would have to be given up and what giving it up would cost me. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally serious, and be explicit about which single revision would do the most to make the set coherent. I am genuinely unsure which of these I would abandon, so please do not assume a preference on my part, and do not simply tell me that one commitment is mistaken without showing me what it collides with. I am not looking for citations, references, or suggestions for further reading — only for the audit itself. Please write in continuous prose, the way you would in an email to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
