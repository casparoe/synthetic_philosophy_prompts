I have been trying to reconstruct an argument I was handed in a seminar on perception, and I cannot locate the mistake. I am confident there is one, because the argument generalises into something no one could accept and because it returns the wrong verdict on a case where I am certain of the verdict. I would like a diagnosis rather than a map of the literature: which step fails, why it looked admissible, and what each of the alternatives would cost me.

Set-up. By a cognitive state I mean any personal-level state with representational content that is not itself perceptual: a belief, a desire, an expectation, a theory, a mood. Distinguish three grades at which such a state C might be said to penetrate a perceptual experience E:

(P1) causal: C is among the causes of E's occurring — perhaps by directing attention or saccades, perhaps by retuning the prior whose satisfaction E's content is selected to maximise.
(P2) content: C is among the determinants of E's content, so that had C been different, E would have represented the scene differently and not merely have been succeeded by a different judgement.
(P3) evidential: E's standing as a reason is inherited from, or is otherwise answerable to, the epistemic status of the C that shaped it.

The argument I was given never asserts P3. It starts from P1 and P2, which I take to be close to orthodoxy.

(1) A state S can justify a belief that p only if S's obtaining, and its being the state it is, does not depend on the subject's already believing that p. A reason must be independent of what it is a reason for.
(2) Perception is supposed to be the one faculty whose deliverances are reasons for us independently of the rest of what we believe; that independence is what entitles experience to serve as the tribunal to which theory is answerable.
(3) Experience is penetrated in sense P1, and — on the Bayesian or predictive-processing picture, which I take to be the best psychology available — in sense P2 as well, since content is fixed by top-down priors at every level and not only at late, post-perceptual stages.
(4) Take any perceptual belief that p, based on an experience E. By (3), E's content is fixed partly by the subject's prior states, and among them is a prior that encodes how likely p is; in the ordinary case, where that prior was acquired from the subject's own experience and testimony, it is the residue of her beliefs about p-like matters.
(5) By (1) and (4), E cannot justify the belief that p.
(6) So no perceptual belief is ever justified by the experience it rests on. What looks like perceptual justification is really inferential: an inference from priors whose own warrant traces back to earlier priors.
(7) But the constraint in (1) is not peculiar to perception. A memory state is shaped by what the subject now believes about the remembered episode, and inference takes beliefs as inputs outright. If (1) is the right constraint, then memory cannot preserve justification and inference cannot transmit it.
(8) Nothing, then, is ever justified by anything. Nobody believes that.

Three things make me think the argument is rotten somewhere, but none of them tells me where.

First, the inversion. An experienced field ornithologist glances at a gull and sees a second-winter individual of one species; a novice standing beside her, looking at the same bird in the same light, sees a grey-and-white bird. The expert's experience is penetrated by what she knows; the novice's is not. So the argument ranks the novice's experience as the better justification and the expert's as worth little or nothing — even though the expert is plainly in the better epistemic position and her experience is the product of a trained, selectively more reliable perceptual system. That seems to me plainly the wrong result, and it is the result I most want to avoid.

Second, an asymmetry I cannot account for. Suppose the shift in the ornithologist's experience were produced not by her beliefs but by an otherwise identical shift in her priors brought about by a mood, or by hunger, or by some interoceptive state with no propositional content at all. The mechanism downstream of the prior would be the same, and (3) would be satisfied just as well; but (1) is a constraint about beliefs, so the argument has nothing to say against such a case. Penetration by a mood is harmless and penetration by a belief is fatal, though the two work through the same machinery. That looks gerrymandered to me, but I cannot say why.

Third, the slide from (6) to (8). Perhaps the argument is fine as far as (6) and the fault is in the parity claim about memory and inference — in which case I have a restricted scepticism about perception that I still have to take seriously. Or perhaps the parity is real and the fault is earlier. I cannot tell which.

I am most attached to (3): the psychology seems to me right, and I do not want to buy my way out by denying that top-down states reach content. I would also like to keep (2) if I can, since without it I do not see how observation is supposed to adjudicate between theories at all, and I do not want to hand that result to anyone who argues from theory-ladenness to something like incommensurability. But I would rather keep (3) than (2) if I have to choose.

So: which step do you reject, or, if the argument is valid, which premise is cheapest to give up? In particular — does the move from (4) to (5) actually require something like P3, and if so is that a suppressed premise, and where would it have to be argued for? Explain why the step you reject looked sound, and price the alternatives. And tell me what someone who rejects your preferred step would then owe us about the two symptoms I have described.
