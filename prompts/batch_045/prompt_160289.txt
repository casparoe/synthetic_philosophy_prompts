I am a PhD student in philosophy working on decision theory and collective action, and I run a 90-minute graduate reading group for 12 PhD students in philosophy and economics; they are comfortable with Nash equilibrium, the prisoner's dilemma, and weak dominance, but they have not encountered Hofstadter's superrationality experiments. I would like you to design a classroom activity that makes them feel the force of superrationality before the idea is named, using a two-stage variant I have devised, the Platonia–Luring Relay, which combines the Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery.

In the original Platonia dilemma, a prize is awarded to an agent only if that agent is the sole claimant among N symmetric, anonymous agents; two or more claims, or none, void the award. Individual reasoning appears to make claiming weakly dominant, so universal claiming wastes the prize; the superrational reply is that, because every agent occupies the same evidential position, the optimal symmetric mixed strategy is to claim with probability 1/N, which maximizes the chance of exactly one claimant, p(1-p)^{N-1}, at roughly 38 percent for N = 12 and gives each agent an individual chance of about 3.2 percent. In the Luring Lottery, a fixed prize is divided by the total number of entries and one entrant is drawn at random; submitting more entries raises an individual's chance while diluting the prize, so symmetric restraint at one entry per person would preserve a larger prize, yet an individual who expects others to submit one entry does better by submitting more.

Stage 1, the Luring stage, uses a nominal prize of 120 units to be awarded to one entrant drawn uniformly at random from all entries submitted, where the value of the prize is 120 divided by the total number of entries. Each of the 12 participants secretly chooses to submit either 1, 2, or 3 entries by writing an anonymous ID on that many slips; submissions are simultaneous and there is no communication. Submitting zero is permitted in principle but is weakly dominated, so I do not expect it. If all 12 submit 1, total entries T = 12, the prize is 10, each has a 1-in-12 chance, and the expected value is about 0.83. If all submit 3, T = 36, the prize is about 3.33, each still has a 1-in-12 chance, and the expected value falls to about 0.28. But if the other 11 participants submit 1, an individual who submits 3 faces T = 14, has a 3-in-14 chance, and the prize is about 8.57, giving that individual an expected value of about 1.84 while diluting the prize for everyone else.

Stage 2, the Platonia stage, uses a separate nominal prize of 120 units, payable only if exactly one claim is submitted. Each participant now chooses one of three options: Claim outright, Abstain, or Join the Randomizer Pool. If K participants join the pool and K > 0, the facilitator randomly selects exactly one member of the pool as the designator; the designator's claim is the only claim submitted from the pool. Any participant who chose Claim outright also has a claim submitted. The prize is paid only if the total number of submitted claims is exactly one. Thus, if K > 0 and nobody claims outright, a winner is guaranteed and each pool member wins with probability 1/K; if anybody claims outright while the pool is nonempty, at least two claims are submitted and the prize is void; if the pool is empty, the prize is paid only if exactly one person claimed outright and everyone else abstained. If all 12 join the pool, each wins with probability about 8.3 percent and the prize is never wasted; if all 12 claim outright, 12 claims are submitted and the prize is void.

Please design the full 90-minute session around these two stages. I need: a minute-by-minute timeline; the exact materials, such as index cards, envelopes, and a randomizer such as a 12-sided die or a sealed envelope; a payoff scheme that uses only a small real stake, such as a 10-to-20-dollar voucher or equivalent participation points, and that scales the nominal 120-unit prizes down to that stake without changing the strategic structure; a facilitator script for staging both games in which participants do not hear the terms superrationality, evidential decision theory, or correlation device before the debrief; and a debrief that maps specific moments onto the components of the idea. The debrief should connect the disappointment when the Luring prize is diluted to the clash between individual dominance and symmetric restraint, and the voiding or success of the Platonia prize to the mixed-strategy calculation, the difference between independent randomization at probability 1/12 and the correlated Randomizer Pool, and the sense in which superrationality asks an agent to treat its own choice as evidence about what the other symmetric agents will do rather than as a cause of their doing it.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it: for example, participants over-entering out of confusion in Stage 1, someone claiming outright in Stage 2 to test the mechanism or out of skepticism, attempts to communicate, misunderstanding of what the Randomizer Pool does, or taking the small stakes unseriously. Finally, be frank about which feature of superrationality the activity cannot reproduce and which step the facilitator must supply in words. In particular, the correlated Randomizer Pool is a device I am adding to the original Platonia dilemma; it is not generated by pure reasoning, cannot be replicated by the participants without the facilitator, and shows the benefit of a correlated selection procedure rather than the original independent mixed strategy. Say clearly how much of the felt lesson depends on that device and how much, if any, survives without it.
