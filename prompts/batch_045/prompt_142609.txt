Hi — I'm the (fairly reluctant) ethics and compliance lead at a mid-sized port logistics firm, and I need help turning some half-formed ideas into something my colleagues can actually run. We operate in six countries, mostly West Africa and South-east Asia, and our parent is listed in London so the Bribery Act is permanently hanging over us. Every month I get maybe twenty "small stuff" queries from country managers with no legal training and, honestly, no patience for theory, and I have to answer yes or no in writing, usually within a working day. The stakes on one side are prosecution and personal criminal liability; on the other side are losing a contract, wrecking a relationship that took years to build, and occasionally putting a local member of staff in a genuinely awkward position with people who can make his life hard. My board wants an auditable rule. My country managers say any rule I write will get us laughed out of the market. Here are the actual cases I keep seeing: a customs officer asks us to cover his daughter's boarding school fees; a village head wants a "community contribution" before our trucks can use a road we already hold a permit for; a client's procurement director sends our manager a crate of very good whisky at New Year and we send one back, which locally is just what relationships are; a regional governor offers to release a licence we're legally entitled to anyway, for a facilitation payment of about £200; and someone nominates a charity we've never heard of for a donation that would be, let's say, timed.

I have three half-baked distinctions I keep circling but can't get to do any work. First: that a payment which buys a *decision* is different from, and in a way less corrupting than, a gift which buys a *relationship* — because a decision is over when it's made, whereas a relationship keeps generating obligations, and you can never tell when the bill arrives. Second, something I call the "who can refuse" test: a practice isn't extractive if any party could walk away, or say no, at no cost to themselves, and I suspect an awful lot of gift-giving fails that test even though no money changes hands and nobody threatens anybody. Third, what I think of as the reverse-direction problem: benefits flowing *upward*, to the official, get caught by every policy I've ever read, while benefits flowing sideways or downward — to his village, his mosque, a third party he names — sail straight through, even though they seem to me to operate in exactly the same way. There's also a nasty practical wrinkle: the more carefully we document a gift, the more it starts to look like evidence of intent, but not documenting it is itself a breach of our own policy.

So — please be a professional philosopher who works on corruption, clientelism and political philosophy generally, and think carefully about this before you commit to anything. Start with no more than 150 words of brainstorming on which distinctions between bribery, clientelism, extortion and ordinary gift-giving actually matter for a decision procedure and which are philosopher's clutter, then give me the procedure itself: the questions to ask in order, what counts as an adequate answer to each, the places where it just stops with a hard no and no discretion, and the places where it has to hand the decision to a named human because the question underneath is one of values rather than facts. Write it so a country manager can run it in twenty minutes without me on the phone, and tell me where any step quietly imports a Western, Weberian picture of office, impartiality and the public/private split that my colleagues in Lagos or Jakarta would be entitled to reject — I really don't want to smuggle in an assumption and then congratulate myself on being principled. Then run it on three of the cases above, including at least one where the procedure gives me an answer I'm not going to like and I'll have to defend anyway, and finish with a short section on what the procedure can't tell me. Hard limit: 150 words of brainstorming and no more than 800 words for everything after it — I will count, so keep it British English and keep it tight.
