I'm a graduate TA for a first-year seminar on persons, property, and virtual worlds — I'm also taking a metaphysics seminar this term where we keep using the word real in ways I can't pin down, which is part of why I'm worried about this session. Next week I have one fifty-minute section to cover whether virtual objects and virtual harms are real. The assigned reading, which I didn't choose, runs the two questions together, and the students will arrive with two reflexes pulling in opposite directions: half of them will say the objects are real because they were paid for with real money and can be bought and sold, which settles a legal question and short-circuits the metaphysical one, and half will say nothing in a game is real because it's all just code, so that demolishing someone's in-game building is like tearing up a photograph of their house — bad only because of how it makes them feel. What I want them to leave with is the ability to hold apart three things: whether a virtual object is a real object; whether damage to it is a real loss; and whether that loss is someone's fault, and hence a harm that someone can be wronged by. So please don't explain the topic to me; plan how I should explain it. Tell me what has to come first and what genuinely depends on what — my instinct is that I can't say anything useful about virtual objects until they have some grip on an object that is publicly observable, causally active, and mind-independent but not made of the stuff we normally count as physical, something like a pattern or process implemented in a computational system and realizable in many different substrates — but I'm not sure whether to introduce that contrast before the case, inside it, or as a cleanup move after the students' own two reflexes have been used as foils.

I also want your judgment on the example that has to carry the whole session, and on what such an example needs to have and to lack. I've been toying with a player-built structure in a persistent multiplayer sandbox world, run in two variants: one where another player spends four hours demolishing it with tools the game itself provides, and one where the studio wipes it in a server migration, with an apology and no wrongdoer. I like that pair because the first looks like something done to someone and the second like a loss with no agent at all, which is exactly the seam I want them to see. But I worry it smuggles in questions I don't want to answer — effort and desert, whether the builder owned the thing, whether the game's own rules made the demolition permitted — and I'd rather the case force the three-way separation than settle it by intuition. Tell me whether that's the right carrying case, what it should be stripped of, and, if there's a better one, what makes it better.

Then the misconception map and the ending. I've guessed at four misconceptions: that real means made of matter; that if the object is real then damaging it is straightforwardly theft, on a par with smashing a window; that because the underlying bits persist, or can be restored from a backup, nothing was really lost; and that the only candidate for harm here is hurt feelings, so the whole question collapses into how sensitive the victim happens to be. Rank them by when in the session they'll actually bite rather than by how important they are, give me the move that defuses each at that moment, and tell me if I'm missing a fifth that first-years reliably produce. Finally, give me one question for the last five minutes that would show me they've got the three-way separation rather than just agreeing with me — I was thinking of a variant where the studio silently restores the structure from a backup five minutes later and only one of the two players ever notices — and a short list of what I should openly refuse to settle at this level, saying out loud that I'm refusing it. My current list is the legal and end-user-license question, the question whether an entire virtual world could itself be real in the way ours is, and the reading's own detour through taboo content, where it makes much of an asymmetry between how players treat virtual killing and virtual sexual content involving children; I have fifty minutes and no training in moderating that discussion. Tell me if that's the right set of refusals, or if I'm declining something I should actually be using.
