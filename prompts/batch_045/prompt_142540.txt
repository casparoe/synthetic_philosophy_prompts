I'm writing the "what should we ask of an account?" section of a paper on the epistemology of conspiracy theories, and I want the requirements list settled before I score any actual view against it. The purpose of the account is fixed: it should be a standard that a careful non-specialist — an editor deciding whether to run a story, a citizen deciding whether to keep digging — could apply to a live claim, before anyone knows how it turns out, in order to decide whether to investigate further, to assert, or to dismiss. Write the desiderata: what any account fit for that role has to deliver, with the case or datum behind each requirement, in a table with columns for the requirement, what motivates it, and whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp.

The phenomena I keep coming back to, which the list should be built to handle:

- Real conspiracies occur and are sometimes officially acknowledged years after being dismissed, so the account cannot build unwarrantedness into the definition of a conspiracy theory, and cannot grade a past judgment by an outcome that was not available when it was made.
- The escalation structure: a theory that converts every denial, retraction, or absence of evidence into further confirmation. Say what is wrong with it — or when it is not wrong — beyond the bare charge of unfalsifiability.
- Scale sensitivity: a plot involving three people over a month and one involving hundreds over a decade differ in the traces they should be expected to leave. The requirement is that the account treat these differently rather than treating conspiracy as one thing.
- The adjudication gap: the claim is often precisely that the institutions which would normally arbitrate it are complicit, so deference to expert consensus or official testimony is unavailable. Say what is supposed to replace it, if not a general suspicion of everything.
- Planted or fabricated evidence: leaked documents and recordings can be forgeries, and forging them has become cheap. A smoking gun no longer settles a question by itself. Say what distinguishes a genuine leak from a plant, and what it costs the account to concede that it sometimes cannot be told.
- My own wrinkle, which I want taken seriously rather than smoothed over: evaluating the proposition and evaluating the inquiry are different tasks, and an account must say which one it is doing. A claim can be true and reasonably supported while the community that circulates it reliably degrades its members' long-run accuracy, and the converse is possible too. Say whether an account owes us verdicts at both levels, how the two interact, and what should be said when they come apart — that divergence is the case I most want handled.
