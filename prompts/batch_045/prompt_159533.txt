I'm a PhD student in philosophy — my area is autonomy and the ethics of paternalism — and this year I'm the graduate-student representative on my university's Retirement Benefits Advisory Committee, which has to vote in two weeks on a proposal from HR. I'd like a draft written opinion I can circulate to the committee beforehand, since I'm expected to say something more useful than "the literature is divided."

Under the current plan, contributing requires an active election, and take-up is 41% among continuing staff but only 12% among people on appointments of a year or less — graduate research assistants, postdocs, adjuncts. HR wants automatic enrollment at 6% of salary for all new hires, annual escalation of one percentage point up to 10%, and a one-click opt-out available at any time. What the committee is actually split over are three further features. First, the opt-out screen would not be a bare confirmation: it would display the projected balance at sixty-five under 6% and under 0%, and require a second click to complete the exit. HR calls this a deliberative exit; two members call it pressure by salience, on the ground that the comparison is calibrated to work on people who have not asked to see it. Second, HR proposes to exempt, automatically and without asking anyone, any employee whose payroll account shows an overdraft or a short-term salary advance in the preceding twelve months; those employees stay opt-in. I am personally in that group, and I genuinely cannot tell whether the exemption is the respectful part of the design or the most paternalistic part of it — whether it tailors the intervention to people's actual circumstances or sorts the financially fragile into a category where the institution has decided saving is not for them. Third, the faculty-senate representative wants a letter mailed thirty days before any automatic enrollment takes effect; HR responds that a pilot it ran with library staff last year found pre-notification reduced enrollment from 91% to 64%, and that the drop was concentrated in the lowest-paid quartile.

The objection I find hardest to answer, and which I want treated seriously rather than dispatched, comes from another member of the committee: she says the mechanism works by ensuring that people do not notice it, so a high take-up figure is evidence of a failure of respect rather than of a benefit, and the better the intervention performs the worse it looks — an institution that got 91% enrollment has mostly succeeded in getting people to do something they never decided to do. My own case seems to support her, since I have known for two years that I should be contributing and have still never elected to, and I suspect that if HR simply enrolled me I would let it stand out of inertia rather than out of any view about retirement. Please write the memo I can table: a recommendation on each of the three contested features separately — keep, drop, or keep with specified modifications — a concrete implementation timeline (I think a twelve-month pilot restricted to one employee class is the realistic option, but tell me if you disagree), and a view about what the pilot should measure, given that HR's proposed metric is the enrollment rate, which seems to me to assume the very thing my colleague denies. Give the strongest version of whatever position you end up rejecting, in a form the person holding it would recognize as her own argument, and keep the whole thing to something a committee can actually vote on.
