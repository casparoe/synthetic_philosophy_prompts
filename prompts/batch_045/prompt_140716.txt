I'm writing a two-thousand-word essay on Denis Villeneuve's *Arrival* for a philosophy magazine's film series, and I want the philosophy of language in it to carry real argumentative weight rather than serve as set dressing. The Quine material — the field linguist with nothing to go on but native utterances and a shared environment, and the point at which rival translation manuals fit every scrap of evidence and there is no fact of the matter which is right — feels like the right spine. I'd like a reading of the film through it that is faithful in both directions: don't reduce the film to an illustration, and don't distort Quine to make the film look deep.

Four things I want worked through.

First, the crux scene. The Chinese team reads the aliens' utterance as a call to use a weapon; Louise argues for tool, or means; the film treats the disagreement as one that further evidence and better linguistic judgment settle. As I understand Quine, that is precisely not his claim. Indeterminacy is supposed to survive any possible behavioral evidence, so a case you resolve by getting more data is a strawman of the thesis — ordinary ambiguity or ordinary mistranslation, not inscrutability. Am I right that the film dramatizes the wrong thing? And if so, is there a subtler reading available, on which what is really at issue is not the extension of one symbol but the shape of the whole manual?

Second, and this is the wrinkle I most want help with: the heptapod written language appears to be semasiographic — logograms produced all at once, no word order, and apparently no singular terms and no apparatus of individuation or identity. Quine's route to the inscrutability of reference runs through exactly that apparatus; it is the availability of singular terms and bound variables that lets the referential trade go through, so that what the native term picks out could equally be rabbits, undetached rabbit parts, or rabbit stages. So does a language with no devices of individuation make the inscrutability worse — nothing fixes reference at all — or does it mean the question never arises, because there are no reference relations there to be inscrutable? Relatedly, radical translation gets off the ground by pairing observation sentences with shared stimulation, using assent and dissent. Aliens with a different sensory apparatus and no evident assent–dissent repertoire offer no such foothold. Is Louise's procedure Quinean in any sense, and should that bother me?

Third, an objection to the setup that I haven't seen anyone raise. Quine's argument holds the translator's home language fixed: English is the medium of translation, not a variable. The film breaks that. On its own premise, learning Heptapod rewires Louise's cognition — her experience of time becomes non-linear — so the medium of translation is deformed by the translation itself, and the manual must be stated in a language its user no longer quite occupies. Call it auto-radical translation, if you like: the case where the linguist is herself among the translated, and the metalanguage is not inert. Does Quine's thesis have anything to say about that, or does it silently presuppose a fixed background language? If you get a regress — manuals for manuals — is that a genuine philosophical problem or merely science-fictional exotica?

Fourth, Louise's evidence base. Her fluency arrives with premonitions: she has access to her own future utterances and, through the aliens' atemporal cognition, to theirs as well. Quine's claim is not that we lack evidence but that no evidence settles the manual. Add the informants' entire future verbal behavior and the translator's own future speech to the evidence base: does the underdetermination persist on Quine's own terms, or does it collapse? I want to know whether the film has accidentally built a genuine counterexample, or whether it has confused having more evidence with there being a fact of the matter.

Then the part where I expect the reading to fail, and where I want you to say so plainly. The film's dramatic engine is a strong linguistic determinism — the language gives its learners a different experience of time — and that sits poorly with Quine's behaviorism about meaning, which has no obvious room for a language reshaping perception. The film also needs communication to have worked: the ending is a coordination triumph, and Louise's persuasion of General Shang depends on her having gotten the message right, where a Quinean can only say she got a workable manual. Is the film therefore better read as a drama about charity and coordination under radical uncertainty, in the interpretive tradition that grows out of Quine and softens him, than as a drama about indeterminacy? If you think so, argue it — but don't quietly substitute a friendlier philosopher for the one I asked about. I want to know specifically what Quine would say the film leaves out.

Give me the two or three scenes you would actually build the essay around and what each does for the argument — no plot summary, I've seen it many times — and end with what, if anything, the film adds to this debate that the arguments alone don't. One exclusion: I don't want the teleological-physics thread, the business about least-time principles and knowing the end in advance. That's a different essay.
