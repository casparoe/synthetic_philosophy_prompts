So I've slowly talked myself into the growth-optimal / Kelly-ish picture of decision-making: the thing to maximize is expected log wealth, i.e. your asymptotic growth rate, and the plain expected-value instinct is a mistake for a single agent who can't average over parallel copies of itself, because the time-average growth rate is what you actually get and the arithmetic expectation is propped up by jackpot branches that essentially never happen. I've been half-living it too — modest leveraged index position sized around half of what the formula says, and I keep telling a friend who bets sports that anyone staking bigger than Kelly is guaranteeing their own ruin. But it's started to feel too comfortable, and two things somebody said at a party cracked it open. First: if a bunch of us each Kelly-bet independently on unrelated things and then pool and re-split the whole bankroll every round, the pool grows at the *arithmetic* mean rate rather than the geometric one, and with enough people in it the stake each of them should use seems to slide toward everything they have — so whether geometric rationality is even the correct criterion looks like it depends on whether the relevant agent is one bankroll extended over time or a commune that can share across simultaneous bets, and nobody in the ergodicity framing ever quite says that out loud. Second: with a fixed goal and a fixed date (clear a specific debt in five years, say), what I actually want is to maximize the chance of hitting the target, and I don't believe Kelly is optimal for that, especially for a target that isn't far away — overbetting seems to buy real probability of getting there, paid for out of the long-run growth I'm supposedly committed to.

What I want is an honest map of what would count against the view, and not of the "but have you considered risk aversion" variety. Which observations, arguments, formal results, or cases should actually move someone holding it, ranked from fatal down to merely awkward, with the ones a committed growth-rate maximizer would concede themselves kept seperate from the ones they'd be right to dismiss. Include the stuff that's presumably already on the table — finite horizons, and the fact that almost-sure asymptotic dominance is perfectly compatible with being behind a more aggressive bettor for a very long time; the "why log?" objection, i.e. why the growth rate is normative rather than just one utility function among many; the nasty asymmetry where overbetting by roughly a factor of two appears to wipe out the growth advantage entirely while underbetting costs you only mildly, which makes the whole criterion hostage to how well you know your own edge — but also anything better I haven't thought of. My own guess is that the most interesting defeater is about what counts as the relevant agent: the pooling case above, or the fact that everyone who actually uses this thing lands on fractional Kelly, which looks less like a theorem and more like an admission that pure growth-maximization is unliveable. Tell me if that guess is right, and tell me whether the view has been quietly qualified into unfalsifiability — "it wins in the long run" is compatible with losing for an arbitrarily long stretch, after all — and if it has, whether that's a genuine problem for a claim of this kind or just what a long-run criterion is supposed to look like.
