I'm running a reading-group session next month on whether the psychology of left and right tells us anything about who is right, and what I need is a decision-tree handout rather than yet another summary of the six foundations — assume everyone in the room already knows care/harm, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity and liberty, and already knows the usual finding that conservatives respond fairly evenly across them while liberals concentrate on the individualizing ones. Start people at "yes, MFT is roughly right as a description of the variation" and lead them to a named position on the epistemic question.

Give me five to seven branch questions and five to seven leaves. The splits I actually care about: whether the foundations are meant to be tracking anything at all or are just causal vocabulary; if they do track something, whether the argument is a coverage argument (the side responsive to more foundations sees more of the moral domain, so it's less likely to be blind to whole kinds of reasons) or a sensitivity argument (one side's intuitions are better calibrated because of what shaped them); whether the binding foundations pick out genuine reasons or coalitional heuristics whose value is only evolutionary; and whether the debunking is symmetric — if cultural evolution explains both sets of intuitions, does it undermine both equally, or does one side being responsive to more inputs break the symmetry somehow.

The node questions have to be cases someone can answer without knowing the labels: a guy who cleans his toilet with the national flag in private, a family firm hiring a less qualified cousin, a village that insists on burying its dead within a day, whatever works. I want at least one leaf where the wide-sensitivity psychology comes out epistemically better and at least one where it comes out worse — more inputs versus more noise — and both should be reachable by answers an honest person could give. Also please include a leaf for the view I keep half-defending and cant find a name for: the psychology tells us nothing about which side is right but tells us something about how to structure deliberation or institutions, so it's a fact about procedure rather than a premise in first-order ethics. Give it a name and one awkward commitment, same as the named positions.

Mark any two leaves your tree cant really seperate, and any combination of answers nobody occupies, with a line on whether it's incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored. Last thing: would the tree look any different if the empirical finding had come out the other way — liberals even across all six, conservatives narrow? If it would look the same, I want that said rather than hidden.
