I write fiction — two novels about a family in upstate New York, and now a third, set partly inside a small research group that trains software creatures to walk, fetch, and find their way around rooms by giving each creature a number after every attempt and letting that number change what it tries next. I've been sitting in on the group's meetings for about a year. A few months ago, mostly so I wouldn't be writing about work I'd never done, I built a toy version on my own laptop: a few hundred lines, a creature with four joints, a score that goes up when it reaches a marker and down when it walks into a wall. It's a hobby project with no use to anyone. But it has started to bother me, and the bother has turned into a decision I actually have to make, so I'm asking for help with it rather than for reassurance.

Here is the argument I keep having at home. My brother, an engineer, says the whole thing is arithmetic: a table of expected scores being nudged upward or downward, no more capable of being upset than a ledger. My daughter, who is seventeen and about to apply to study philosophy, says that whatever the machinery is made of, I have arranged for something to be made to steer away from a state of affairs; that steering-away is the core of what we mean by something being bad for a creature; and that I should either stop running it or rebuild it so that nothing is ever subtracted. Three things complicate this for me, and they're why I'm asking you instead of just picking a side. First, I tried her fix: I deleted the penalties and let the creature collect only gains — and it went on avoiding the wall just as efficiently, because what moves its numbers is not the score itself but the gap between the score it expected and the score it got, and a shortfall below expectation seems to do the very same work the penalty did. I've read that something roughly similar holds for the learning signal in animal brains, which makes me think that removing the minus signs may not touch whatever the moral problem is supposed to be. Second, I noticed that in my system the minus sign does nothing at all except change future behavior: if I let the creature register the bad number but block it from learning from it, it stops avoiding anything. So everything the penalty does, it does as a teacher, and I can't find any work left over for the hurting part to do — which pushes me toward saying there is nothing there, while also making me suspicious of myself, since the same would be true of me if you cut the connection between my pain and anything I did about it. Third, my setup lets me choose whether the creature's accumulated numbers carry over from one run to the next or reset to zero each time, and I genuinely cannot tell whether that should matter to anyone's conclusion; and it would cost me almost nothing to run a thousand copies of the creature at once, which makes me distrust the entire line of thought, because a worry that grows that fast with the number of copies feels like it might be a bug in the reasoning rather than a discovery.

So: what would have to be true of a system like mine for the penalties — or the shortfalls — to carry any moral weight at all, and is there any way of telling whether it is true? Is my daughter's proposal even aimed at the right part of the machinery, or would rebuilding the thing without minus signs change nothing that matters? Is the fact that the concern multiplies so cheaply a reason to take it seriously or a reason to back away from it? And concretely, for the remaining months of research for this book: should I keep running the toy, change it, or switch it off — and what should the character in my novel, who is facing a far larger version of the same choice, decide? I'd rather have the argument, including the parts that tell me I'm being sentimental, than a verdict that settles the thing comfortably.
