Two epistemologists — one a conciliationist about higher-order evidence, one a steadfaster — have been going at each other for a year about peer disagreement and have finally agreed to shut up and run a proper adversarial collaboration. Write the joint statement they'd come out with: a numbered section of claims they can both sign (about what higher-order evidence is, about what's at stake, about which standard cases are supposed to show what), then a disagreements table where each disagreement is stated twice, once in the conciliationist's own preferred wording and once in the steadfaster's, then the cruxes, with for each crux a concrete case both would accept as bearing on it and each side's prediction written down in advance.

There's one case-type I want them to actually deal with, and as far as I can tell neither camp has handled it seperately: a disagreement that goes all the way up. You and your peer disagree about some first-order question, but you also disagree about the right response to peer disagreement — you think you should move towards her, she thinks you should hold your ground. So part of your higher-order evidence is evidence about her meta-view, and part of hers is evidence about yours. Does the conciliationist owe some weight to the fact that a peer is a steadfaster, and is that self-undermining or is it actually no problem at all? Can the steadfaster keep both her first-order belief and her meta-view, or does the meta-level row undo the steadfastness?

Two more things I want in there: whether higher-order evidence can ever make epistemic akrasia the rational state rather than just an excusable one, and how much of their remaining row is verbal — say wich part and why. Keep it tight and don't pad it with waffle.
