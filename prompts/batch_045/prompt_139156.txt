You are a professional philosopher with a specialism in the philosophy of cognitive science and artificial intelligence, and you have been asked to clerk for a statutory panel that has to rule on two live applications. Take your time with this. Begin by brainstorming, before you commit to anything, the full range of criteria that could be generating the panel's past verdicts; then eliminate the ones that any single verdict rules out; then work with what survives. Reason carefully, and say explicitly where you are guessing.

The Panel on Cognitive Status was created by the Adaptive Systems (Designation) Act. It decides whether a system is an "intelligent system" for the purposes of the Act. Designation cuts both ways, and the panel insists its criteria are direction-neutral: it brings the deployer under a package of obligations (registration, audit trails, incident reporting, a duty to keep and disclose a record of how determinations were reached) but it also brings a permission, since only a designated system may make a determination in a statutory process without a human in the loop. Some applicants want designation, some fight it. The panel has repeatedly said it is making no finding about consciousness, sentience, or moral status, and that its reasons must be reviewable by a court, so gesturing will not do.

The Act's test is deliberately thin. A system is an intelligent system if it "acquires the competence it has rather than having it installed in it" and can "bring that competence to bear on problems of a kind it was not prepared for."

The precedents, with the ground each decision actually gave:

P1. A Deep Blue–class chess engine: hand-crafted evaluation function, brute-force search, superhuman, no learning of any kind. Not designated. Ground: the competence was installed by its programmers and it does nothing else.

P2. An expert system encoding a tax code as hand-written rules, good enough to pass the relevant professional exam within its domain. Not designated. Same ground.

P3. A system that learnt three different board games from their rules alone by self-play, reaching superhuman standard in each, needing tens of millions of games to do so, and unable to do anything at all outside those games. Not designated, by four votes to three. Ground given: it is domain-bound, and it needs more experience than any animal would. The three dissenters thought domain-bound-ness was the wrong thing to object to.

P4. A general language model trained on a very large text corpus: no hand-written rules, zero-shot transfer to a wide range of tasks it was never trained on, competent on professional examinations, prone to confident confabulation, no goals of its own, no persistence across sessions, and — this was expressly addressed — weights frozen at deployment, so that it learns nothing from any case it sees. Designated, by five votes to two. The majority said the panel does not require that a system go on learning. The two dissenters said it has no goals, no world, and nothing at stake, and is merely a very large statistical table.

P5. A five-gram language model trained on a comparable corpus, which does well at the task it was trained on and nothing else. Not designated. Ground: its competence is exhausted by surface statistics, and it collapses when the phrasing of a problem is changed.

P6. A New Caledonian crow. Designated unanimously, with little discussion.

P7. A two-year-old human child. Designated unanimously. The panel expressly rejected the submission that the child scores below P3 and P4 on every benchmark the sponsors use.

P8. A hospital's sepsis-detection pathway — a statistical score, a set of ward protocols, and the nurses who acted on it. Not designated: "the intelligence in this pathway is the nurses." The panel has never had to decide whether a system that is partly a model and partly hand-written code can itself be intelligent, because until now no applicant has asked it to look inside the wrapper.

The two applications now before the panel:

Application A: "Ardent", a benefits-determination system. Its sponsor took a model of the kind designated in P4 and distilled it — training a much smaller model on the larger one's own outputs over roughly two million worked cases that human experts had generated and checked — into a compact system that makes initial determinations on one class of welfare claim: whether a claimant's stated circumstances meet the statutory conditions, and whether further evidence should be requested. Stipulated facts. On its own task it now matches or beats its teacher, including on genuinely novel cases: it has been shown to read a statutory amendment it has never seen and apply it correctly the same day, and to reason sensibly about claim histories of a kind absent from its training. No human wrote or could state the rules it applies; they exist only as weights. Its weights are frozen at deployment. It was given a calibrated abstention mechanism, trained by engineers on labelled data, so that it declines and refers to a human when uncertain, and it does so appropriately. It produces short justifications that reviewers find plausible, but in a measurable minority of cases the justification does not reflect what actually drove the output. Outside the determination workflow it is not merely poor but incoherent: asked to summarise the very text it has just applied, it falls apart.

Application B: "Ledger", a case-management deployment. It uses a model of the kind designated in P4, weights likewise frozen, wrapped in orchestration code written by the deployer's engineers: a planner that decomposes objectives, tool interfaces to a case database, a calculator and a code interpreter, an external memory store that persists notes across sessions, a standing objective supplied in the wrapper, and a retry loop with hard-coded validity checks. In operation it pursues objectives over several hours, asks for clarification when a case is underspecified, notices and works around tool failures, and catches some of its own arithmetic errors by re-doing them against the calculator. Nothing in the weights ever changes; everything that looks like learning is accumulation in the memory store, whose format the engineers designed and whose contents they can read and edit. The deployer argues against designation, on the footing that the model inside is a frozen statistical table and the rest is ordinary software. A competitor argues for it.

What I need from you:

1. Set out the two or three criteria that fit all eight precedents — stated precisely enough that they can genuinely come apart — and say for each which verdicts it explains cleanly and which it strains to explain. Do not give me one criterion; the whole difficulty is that the track record is consistent with more than one.
2. Say what each surviving criterion predicts about Ardent and what it predicts about Ledger, and where, if anywhere, they agree.
3. Give me your recommended decision on each application, with the closest precedent identified and a reason for thinking that precedent closer than the obvious rival. For Ardent, tell me whether the closer analog is P2 (narrow, statutory, no transfer) or P4 (learnt, no hand-written rules, robust within its domain), and why.
4. Tell me honestly where the precedents underdetermine the answer, and for each way of resolving it, state the principle the panel would thereby be adopting — in a form a future panel could actually apply, not a slogan.
5. Name one further case, not before the panel, that would separate the surviving criteria, and say what each verdict on it would tell you. (My own candidate: a system with Ardent's narrow but rule-adaptive competence whose weights were written by hand by engineers who had studied the teacher's behaviour rather than distilled from it. Tell me if that is the right probe, and if not, what is.)
6. Give me a confidence rating on each recommendation, and tell me what the two dissenters in P4 will say about both applications and whether their position has become more or less attractive given what has come in since.
