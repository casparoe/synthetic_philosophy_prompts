I teach an upper-level seminar on representation and logical empiricism, and I am writing the final exam. I need one essay question — 90 minutes, 1,000 words, 25 marks — on Otto Neurath's picture languages: the Isotype system developed at the Vienna social and economic museum, with its rules that a quantity be shown by repeating one standardized pictogram rather than by enlarging it, that perspective and incidental detail be suppressed, and that raw figures be transformed before anything is drawn.

The question must defeat two stock answers. The first treats Isotype as mere popular outreach for the unity-of-science movement — attractive pictograms for workers, continuous with the campaign against metaphysics. The second treats it as a refutation of Neurath's own epistemology: a medium meant to cross linguistic borders seems to presuppose exactly the neutral, self-interpreting representation that his holism and his tolerance of vagueness rule out. Both are half-right in ways I want students to separate out.

Set the question around the following scenario, which students will receive verbatim:

Suppose designers extend Isotype with three new signs: one for causal influence; one for a quantity as it would have been under a different policy; and one for the margin of uncertainty in a figure. The signs nest, so the resulting charts can claim that unemployment would have been lower under a public works programme and that we are moderately confident of this. Call it counterfactual Isotype. Untrained viewers misread it. After two hours of instruction they read it accurately, and then report that the charts are obvious — that they can simply see what is being claimed. The charts then enter a political dispute about which policies caused what.

Ask students to argue whether counterfactual Isotype completes Neurath's project or reduces it to absurdity, and why.

Deliverables: the question as students will see it; a model answer of roughly 700 words; and a marking scheme built on five must-hit moves totalling 25 marks, each with a one-line note on how a strong student typically falls short on it. Make the A/B boundary explicit: I want real credit for noticing that Isotype's own conventions — repetition, suppression of detail, transformation — are learned and already interpretive, so that the live issue is not whether pictures can replace words but whether a visual display can be at once learnable and non-question-begging, and what follows for Neurath's politics of enlightenment if it cannot. Say also how to reward a student who argues that part of the dispute is merely verbal. Mark on argument alone: a defensible answer should require no quotation, citation, biographical detail, or source beyond the scenario.
