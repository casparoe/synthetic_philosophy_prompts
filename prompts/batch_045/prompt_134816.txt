hey — i want a comparison of two different worries about cognitive penetration of perception, and i want it at the level where you actually have to think rather than restate the debate.

so we're talking about the same thing: cognitive penetration is when what you believe, want, expect, or have been trained to notice changes what your perceptual experience is like — not merely what you judge afterwards. the stock examples: a hill looks steeper when you're tired or hauling a heavy pack, an object in the hands of someone you hold a stereotype about reads as more weapon-ish, a radiologist or a chess master takes in the scan or the board differently from a novice.

now the two worries. call the first the laundering worry: if your prior is what shaped the experience, the experience can't be used to check the prior — perception hands your own hypothesis back to you stamped with the authority of "i saw it," like a witness who happens to be your friend. call the second the drift worry: penetration pushes experience away from how the world actually is, so its a plain reliability problem.

here's what i want from you:

1. show where they come apart and why that matters. penetration can make you *more* accurate (the radiologist's training is what makes the lesion show up for her at all) and still be laundering; penetration can make you less accurate without laundering any particular hypothesis (the hill). so: are these two independent worries, or is one of them the real one with the other as a special case? pick a side, say which, and say what you'd be blind to if you only had the other one.

2. the laundering worry seems to come with an obvious fix — quarantine the episode, don't treat that experience as evidence for the belief that produced it. but can you ever tell from the inside which belief did the penetrating? or does identifying the penetrating state always need a third-person story (experiments, mechanism, what happens when you vary the belief), and if so, does the first-person epistemic problem turn out to be unsolvable in practice even if it's fine in principle?

3. the drift worry needs a baseline: away from the truth relative to what? if the predictive-processing picture is roughly right and every perception is prior-shaped to some degree, there is no unpenetrated perception to compare against. does the drift worry collapse under that, or can it be rebuilt as a claim about the *wrong kind* of prior doing the shaping (motivated, self-serving, stereotype-driven) rather than about shaping as such? and does the laundering worry fare better or worse once you accept that nothing is unpenetrated?

4. the bit i actually want tested. i've been toying with a distinction between penetration of *content* and penetration of *insistence*. content: you come to see the thing as F, where otherwise you wouldn't. insistence: what the experience represents doesn't change, it just grabs you harder — it's more vivid, more compulsory, harder to look away from, harder to doubt, it jumps out. my hunch is that the laundering worry is mostly a worry about insistence and not about content, and that this matters, because insistential penetration is also exactly what perceptual expertise feels like and probably what makes perceptual learning possible at all. so: is the distinction any good, or does it collapse — does insistence always drag some content change along with it ("this matters," "this is dangerous," "look here")? and if it holds, does it follow that some laundering isn't merely tolerable but constitutive of having any perceptual skill?

end by telling me straight which of this is settled among people who actually work on perception and which is a live fight — i don't want a contested position dressed up as the consensus. flag in particular where you're least sure, and whether my content/insistence distinction is just me reinventing something that already has a name. no references or reading lists, just the concepts.
