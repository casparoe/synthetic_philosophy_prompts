hey — ive been chewing on a short paper (2500 words max, for a grad seminar) about pain asymbolia and what it actually shows about the badness of pain, and im at the point where i cant tell if my main move is clever or if ive just built a strawman with extra steps. my supervisor is some kind of experientialist about pain's badness, so shes the audience im most worried about. could you read the notes below and argue with me? please dont give me citations or a reading list — i cant get to a library right now, so anything you say has to be arguable from the cases themselves.

the standard setup as i understand it: pain has (at least) a sensory-discriminative side — where it is, how intense, what quality, burning vs stabbing vs cramping — and an affective-motivational side, the unpleasantness and the get-me-out-of-this pull. in the textbook asymbolia cases, after certain kinds of brain damage the sensory side seems intact: patients locate the sensation, describe its character, rate its intensity, distinguish it from pressure or warmth. but the aversive part is gone. no withdrawal, no apparent distress, sometimes they make light of it, and they generally dont go asking for painkillers.

the argument im pushing back on:

P1 in asymbolia the sensory component survives and the affective one is gone
P2 the asymbolic's pain is not bad for them
P3 so the badness was coming from the affective component all along; the bare sensory character isnt bad-making by itself
C experientialism about pain's badness is false, something attitudinal or desire-based is true

i think P2 is doing all the work and its smuggling in two assumptions:

(A) authority — that the subjects lack of distress, plus their say-so, settles that the state isnt bad for them. this assumes badness-for is transparent to the subject: if it were bad for you, youd be bothered by it, and youd be in a position to tell.
(B) affect exclusivity — that occurrent felt aversion is the only thing that could make a state bad for you.

my move is a hypothetical variant, call it lucid asymbolia. same deficit in generating affect, but add: intact memory of what pain felt like before the damage, intact practical reasoning, and no global apathy — they still care about their other projects. this person feels the pricking/burning, feels zero distress about it, and on the basis of memory and testimony judges that this is a state they have strong reason to be out of. they calmly ask for analgesia. afterwards they say something like: it wasnt distressing at all, but it was bad for me while it lasted and im glad you stopped it.

i claim the variant is coherent — desires can be driven by memory and judgement rather than by occurrent affect, the way you can calmly act on prudence when you arent feeling anything urgent — and that if it is coherent then (A) is false: absence of distress doesnt settle whether the state was bad for the subject.

then the bit im least sure about, the dilemma. i want to say the attitudinalist faces a choice. either bad-for just means something like "S wants it to stop / has reason to end it", in which case "asymbolic pain isnt bad for them" is near-trivial — of course it isnt, they dont want it to stop — and the empirical cases add nothing, plus in lucid asymbolia the desire is present without the affect, which suggests desire rather than affect is the real bad-maker, which is a different thesis than the one asymbolia is normally wheeled out to support. or bad-for requires occurrent felt aversion, in which case the conclusion is substantive but the theory has basically built the answer into the account of welfare, and the asymbolia data starts to look less like evidence and more like a bullet they have to bite — theyd also have to say the lucid asymbolic is doing fine, which seems wrong to me.

last section, which might be nothing: instead of picking a side i gesture at a view where pain's badness is a role property with more than one filler — felt unpleasantness is the normal filler, a judgement- and memory-backed standing desire is another — and the two arent additive, so a state with both isnt bad twice over. then i end on a question i genuinely dont know how to answer: should we still give an asymbolic patient analgesics? everyones intuition says yes, but on the attitudinal story there seems to be nothing in it for them. my best guess is that the reason is evidence-relative or has to do with the persons earlier and ongoing autobiographical concerns rather than their occurrent welfare, and im worried that just smuggles experientialism back in through the side door.
