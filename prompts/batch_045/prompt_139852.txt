I'm a third-year PhD in philosophy (Chinese philosophy, philosophy of language, depends who's in the room) and somehow I've ended up as the 'philosophy person' on our university museum's Interpretation and Visitor Experience Committee — I think it was punishment for complaining about the labels in the last show. My supervisor thinks its a monumental waste of my writing-up year and she's probably right, but here we are. There's a small exhibition opening in about six weeks, mostly on loan from a partner museum in Xi'an, on naming and language in early China: about a dozen objects, and two of the wall texts are completely stuck. I have to bring a recommendation to Tuesday's meeting, because the texts go to the printer on the 14th and after that we're locked in whatever happens.

Object 7 is a Tang white-glazed ceramic horse. The curator wants the label to end: 'Gongsun Long would have said this is not a horse.' Our head of learning says that if we tell a group of nine-year-olds that a white horse isn't a horse we'll get letters from parents and possibly a visit from a councillor, and she wants: 'a white horse is not the same thing as horses in general.' I think both are slightly wrong, and my own hobby-horse is that the interesting claim is about names rather than animals — the name 'horse' picks out a shape-kind, 'white' picks out a colour-kind, and the compound name fits neatly under neither, so what's really being denied is that the name 'horse' correctly applies to a white horse as such, which is a claim about correct naming and not about zoology at all. Object 9 is worse: a carved white marble, with the other bit of Gongsun Long, where the argument is that you see the whiteness and you feel the hardness, so the two come apart, and the stone itself ends up as a third thing over and above them. The drafted label says the whiteness and the hardness of this stone are two different things and the stone is a third. Our head of learning says that's both false and incomprehensible. Complicating all of this: the partner museum's English catalogue, which they've already printed and asked us not to contradict, states the claim flatly with no hedging whatsoever; the learning team have dwell-time figures showing the baffling labels are exactly the ones visitors stop at; and the museum's plain-language policy says labels must not be 'deliberately obscure'.

What I need is the memo I'll circulate on Monday night: around 800 words, bottom line first, recommending a concrete decision with the actual words you'd put on both labels, plus a principle short enough that the curator and the head of learning could apply it to the other ten objects without ringing me, plus one paragraph giving the strongest version of the position you're telling me to vote down — and if you think that position actually wins, say so and be straight with me about what my names-not-animals reading is costing me. Please write it in British English; house style is -ise endings and single quotes.
