The project is on sortition — filling political offices by lot rather than by election. Here's my half-formed thought. People say "the lottery is fair," but I think that covers at least two different claims that get run together. One is a blindness claim: the procedure is insensitive to properties that aren't reasons for preferring anybody, so there's no canvassing, no faction-building, no one's claim being honored because they're better liked or better funded. The other is an equal-chance claim: everyone eligible gets the same ex ante shot at the office, which seems to matter when the office is something worth having. These can come apart — you could deliberately over-represent some group in the sample (unequal chances, still blind), and you could allocate by something blind but not random, like rotating alphabetically or by birthday. The wedge I want to use to prise them apart is what I've been calling the urn problem: real sortition can only draw from people who agree to be in the urn, and saying yes correlates with exactly the things the lottery was supposed to be blind to — education, confidence speaking in public, party identification, having free time, not being worn out by politics. So actual citizens' assemblies are lotteries over volunteers. The obvious comparison is jury duty, which is compulsory and where nobody worries that the pool is self-selected; but the practice of deliberative minipublics went the other way, toward consent plus incentives plus stipends. I want to know whether the case for sortition, properly stated, actually requires a compulsory, population-wide urn — jury-style — and what that would cost the volunteer-based model that nearly every real reform uses; and whether the blindness rationale and the equal-chance rationale give different answers here, because my suspicion is that blindness survives a volunteer urn reasonably well and equal-chance doesn't, and that a lot of the rhetoric about ordinary people is quietly trading on the second while the mechanism only delivers the first.
