I am teaching an advanced seminar on early modern skepticism and the origins of cultural relativism, and I want to pair Montaigne's essay Of Cannibals with Shakespeare's play The Tempest. I am not looking for a source-study, a bibliography, or a survey of criticism; I know the scholarly background well enough for my purposes. Florio's 1603 English translation of Montaigne's essay appeared shortly before the play and is often taken to lie behind Gonzalo's description, in Act 2, Scene 1, of an ideal commonwealth without traffic, magistrates, letters, property, contract, sovereignty, or labor, in which nature spontaneously yields all abundance. The courtiers immediately mock the speech, observing that its ending forgets its beginning and that Gonzalo would still be king, and the name Caliban is often linked etymologically to cannibal and taken to allude to Montaigne's figure of the noble cannibal. I want a philosophical reading of the play in terms of Montaigne's relativism, not a demonstration of borrowing.

To make the reading precise, I want to distinguish two strands in Montaigne's essay that are easily conflated. Call the first deflationary or critical relativism: the skeptical argument that European judgments of barbarism are parochial, and that contemporary European practices—religious warfare, torture, cruelty in punishment, and the conquest and exploitation of the peoples of the New World—are at least as barbarous as the ritual cannibalism Montaigne describes. This strand is diagnostic; it unsettles the standpoint from which we call others barbarians. Call the second strand primitivist utopianism: the stronger and more constructive claim that a people without property, commerce, magistracy, contract, letters, or coercive sovereignty—a people living by nature's spontaneous provision—would be not merely no worse, but happier, more innocent, or closer to nature. Gonzalo's commonwealth is the staged version of this second strand.

Please read The Tempest through that distinction, and be willing to say where the play resists the reading. In particular:

Does Gonzalo's speech commit the play to primitivist utopianism, or does the mockery of Sebastian and Antonio show that Shakespeare treats the abolition of magistracy, contract, and sovereignty as comic or conceptually unstable? Notice that the commonwealth still seems to require a king; is the point that the primitivist ideal cannot eliminate sovereignty, only disguise it?

Where, if anywhere, does the play instantiate the deflationary relativist strand? Montaigne uses the cannibal to shame European vice, so one would expect Caliban, the island's enslaved native inhabitant, to serve as that mirror. Shakespeare does give Caliban a speech about the island's noises and dreams, in which the isle is said to be full of sounds and sweet airs that give delight and hurt not and that make him dream of riches. Does the play thereby use Caliban to expose Prospero's domination, and European civilization generally, as the true barbarism? Or does the play withhold that redemptive role? Caliban is also represented as deformed and slavish, as plotting against Prospero, and, in Prospero's account, as having attempted violence against Miranda; he is finally subordinated and left on the island. Does that complexity mean Shakespeare rejects Montaigne's deflationary relativism altogether, or only the primitivist utopianism that Gonzalo voices?

Finally, what does the ending imply? The play closes with the restoration of legitimate rule: Prospero returns to Milan, the Neapolitan court is reconciled, and Caliban is ordered to obey or is pardoned and left behind. Does that restoration reinstate a civilizational hierarchy that Montaigne's essay would resist, suggesting that some coercive sovereignty and civil order are necessary after all? Or does the play qualify the restoration, leaving Prospero's authority looking imperious and the restored order fragile, so that the relativist critique survives even though the utopian fantasy does not?

I want a reading that is faithful in both directions: it should not reduce The Tempest to an illustration of Montaigne, nor distort Montaigne to fit the play. Please tell me what, if anything, the play adds to the philosophical debate that Montaigne's essay alone does not—for instance, whether staging the primitivist commonwealth as mocked dialogue, and embodying the would-be noble cannibal as an enslaved and resentful subject, tests the relativist position in a way argument cannot. Please do not rely on verbatim quotation; refer to scenes and speeches by paraphrase and give me the conceptual analysis I can use in the seminar.
