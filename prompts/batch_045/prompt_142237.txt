I'm in a little reading group — four of us, Sunday evenings — and weve spent something like eight sessions on whether the separateness of persons is a real objection to aggregating well-being across people, and we are completely stuck. Two of us aggregate, two dont, and somewhere around session five it stopped feeling like an ethical disagreement at all.

Heres where we actually are, so you can see why I think that. Everyone agrees suffering is bad, everyone agrees well-being is the only currency in play here (no desert, no side-constraints out of nowhere), everyone agrees numbers matter at least sometimes, and both sides converge in policy on a lot of ordinary cases — we all accept imposing small costs on some for large benefits to others, speed limits and quarantines and so on. We diverge on exactly one shape of case: a large concentrated burden on one person that buys a slightly larger total of very tiny benefits spread over very many people. The aggregators say: more well-being, everyone counted once. The separatists say: nothing has been done for the one person, and there is no one for whom the trade is a good deal. And when we ask why it isnt a good deal for the group, the separatists say the group isnt the kind of thing that can be dealt with, and the aggregators say that just is the conclusion restated.

So we built what we thought was a discriminating instrument and it turned out not to discriminate. A continuum:

1. One person across eighty years with total amnesia every decade — biological continuity only, no psychological continuity across the breaks.
2. Ordinary fission: one person, two simultaneous successors, each continuous with the original in whatever way normally settles questions of survival.
3. A relay population: a thousand people each living one day, day n's person inheriting all of day n-1's memories, projects and character, so full psychological continuity runs along the chain but a new person each midnight.
4. A thousand ordinary strangers with no continuity between them.

Aggregating across the stages of (1) is prudence and nobody flinches. Aggregating across (4) is exactly what the separatists object to. We put our money on (2) and (3) deciding it. Instead each side just redescribed them: the separatists said continuity without identity means (3) is still a thousand separate people and nothing has been settled; the aggregators said (3) shows separateness doesnt track anything that carries moral weight, so it cant be doing the work the separatists want. Same continuum, both readings, no movement — which is itself my main piece of evidence that this is several disagreements stacked under one label.

My own hypothesis, offered as one: that the fight isn't about persons or identity at all but about what a reason is addressed to — the separatists are running a picture on which a justification has to be someones justification, addresseable to an individual who can complain, and the aggregators are running a picture on which reasons are just impersonally there and the only question is how much. On that reading our spectrum couldnt discriminate because it varies identity and continuity, and neither of those is the variable that matters.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it verbal — the two sides using compensation and good deal for different relations? Substantive first-order morality about whether numbers go inert when they're spread across lives? Borrowed metaphysics of personal identity that ethics is illegitimately leaning on? metaphysics of reasons in the sense above? Or a methodological fight about what counts as data — whether the shared verdict on the concentrated-burden case is evidence or a trained response we should discount? Rank them for me, tell me which one is really doing the work and which are noise, and tell me which would have to be settled before any of the others could be.

And then, given your diagnosis: what would count as progress? If there's a distinction that dissolves part of it, give me the distinction. If our continuum is rigged — wrong variable, wrong endpoint, threshhold where there shouldn't be one — say what's wrong with it and what case would actually separate the two sides. If the honest answer is that it bottoms out and shouldn't be expected to move, say that, and say exactly where the bedrock is. I know the literature, so please no reading lists or names to look up; I want your diagnosis of the structure of the thing, not a map of who said what.
