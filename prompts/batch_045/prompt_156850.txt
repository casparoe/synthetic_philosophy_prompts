I want to stress-test a position I have been defending, and I would rather you attack it than summarize it.

The position has two parts. First, a criterion: to say of a system that it believes that P is to say that the intentional stance is the best predictive strategy available for that system — more predictive, more cheaply, over a wider range of contexts, than the design stance or the physical stance. Nothing about substrate, training objective, phenomenology, or mechanistic realization can defeat a well-confirmed intentional-stance prediction; if the stance is the best game in town for a thing, then attributions of belief and desire to it are true, and that is all their truth can consist in. Second, an application: for current large language models the stance is in fact the best game in town across a large and growing range of behaviors, so, in whatever sense anything has beliefs, they do.

Three pieces of machinery, stipulative, so that we are discussing the same things. Call a belief attribution's *surplus predictiveness* the predictive edge it retains, for a fixed interpreter, over the best available non-intentional description of the same system — and measure it against the hardest case, an interpreter with full access to weights, activations and training data and arbitrarily much compute. Call a system *attributionally decoherent* when belief attributions are locally optimal but globally unsatisfiable: for each prompt context there is a tidy assignment of beliefs and desires that predicts the continuation well, but no single assignment survives across contexts, and the assignments that work in different contexts contradict one another. And call the *playback problem* the fact that a device that merely replays recorded answers produced by a real person appears to satisfy the criterion just as well as the person does.

What I want is a map of the position's exposure: what would have to be true, or to have already turned out true, for it to be false. Sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me for each whether it strikes at the criterion itself, at the application to language models, or only at some particular belief attribution. I am especially interested in three things a defender of this kind of view would have to say something about: whether surplus predictiveness plausibly survives the idealized-interpreter test, and what it would mean for the view if it does not; whether attributional decoherence — the persona-relativity that a pure next-token predictor trained on the output of millions of disagreeing authors seems almost designed to produce — is a counterexample to the criterion or merely a complication in applying it; and whether the position quietly collapses into instrumentalism or fictionalism if surplus predictiveness turns out to be indexed to an interpreter's ignorance rather than to a pattern in the system. Say explicitly which of these a committed defender would concede as counting against them and which they would dismiss, and say why.

Two further things. First, tell me what the position predicts that its rivals do not — I take it to predict something like a displacement: as mechanistic interpretability succeeds, surplus predictiveness should decline and belief talk about these systems should wither, which is not what a fictionalist or a realizer-based functionalist predicts. If that is a bad reading of the view's commitments, correct me. Second, give me your candid assessment of how much the view actually risks: the word *available* in the criterion floats with the interpreter's evidence and resources, and any failed attribution can be re-described as a mis-attribution, so I want to know whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a defect in a view of this kind or merely what a criterion for belief attribution should look like.

For what it is worth, I currently rank the worst-case interpreter condition and attributional decoherence as the serious threats, and the familiar empirical findings — sycophancy, prompt-order sensitivity, verbalized reasoning that does not track the process producing the answer — as the least threatening, since they seem to me to show only that we are often wrong about which beliefs to attribute. Tell me if I have misranked these, and what would have to be added to the empirical findings to make them bite against the view rather than against particular attributions.

Finally: attach a confidence estimate to each of your main claims, and single out the one you are least sure of.
