I sit as a lay member of a citizens' assembly — 24 of us, selected by sortition, meeting over six weekends — charged with recommending whether our health region should close three rural birthing units and centralise maternity care in a single hospital. Our remit letter instructs us three times not to substitute our own judgment for "the will of the people," and never says what that phrase picks out. In the same month I have heard it used three other ways: a campaign against the closures insists the will of the people was already expressed in a 2019 referendum and that our assembly has no business reopening it; an organiser at a public meeting used it to close down an internal dispute — "the people have decided, we are not relitigating this"; and one of our own members uses it to mean, roughly, whatever a body like ours would recommend after hearing the evidence and deliberating.

What I need is a disambiguation, not an introduction to populism. Please set out the distinct precise claims that "the will of the people" could be making, as a menu ordered from the thinnest reading to the most demanding, and for each reading state: what it claims; what kind of thing it takes the will to be (a tally of individual preferences, a function of them, an idealised counterfactual, an ascription by whoever successfully speaks in the name of the people, or merely a title conferring authority on some decision-maker); who counts as "the people" under it; what procedure or observation would settle what the people will; and what would count against the claim.

Five constraints, because these are the parts I cannot work out alone.

First, I want the readings separated into those on which the phrase purports to identify a determinate content — a policy, a ranking of options — and those on which it only settles who is entitled to decide, or only marks a decision as legitimate once made. Say which of my four uses needs the first kind and which could get by with the second, since I suspect the organiser's use relies on sliding between them.

Second, the constituency question. Our decision imposes concentrated costs on a small, place-bound population and diffuses benefits over a much larger one, and its effects will run for decades. For each reading, say whether "the people" has determinate membership, who sets the boundary, and whether that boundary question has to be answered before the will can be ascertained or is answered by the same act of ascertainment — i.e. which readings face a regress in which the demos must already be constituted in order to constitute itself. Say explicitly whether any reading can include people who do not yet live in the region or are not yet born.

Third, the referendum-versus-assembly conflict. Is there a reading on which an earlier, less informed expression of the will binds a later, better informed one? Which readings make the relevant will a counterfactual one — what the people would will under specified conditions of information and discussion — and what happens to the phrase's political force, and to its ability to bind officials, once it is read that way?

Fourth, test a tradeoff I think I can see: that the readings determinate enough to settle which policy to adopt are the ones no procedure could refute, while the readings a procedure can settle (vote totals, referendum results) settle only who decides and not what is to be decided. Tell me whether the tradeoff is genuine, where it bites hardest, and which readings escape it and at what cost.

Fifth, for each of the four uses, identify the reading doing the work and judge whether that reading is defensible on its own terms rather than merely in the mouth of the person invoking it.

Close with your verdict on whether any reading survives as something an assembly like mine could actually apply to a decision, or whether the phrase is best understood as an honorific whose function is to immunise a decision against further challenge. Please also flag any reading that is close to the familiar contrast between a general will and the mere aggregate of everyone's private wills, but do not stop there — the interesting readings are the ones that are neither. Argue for your verdicts; do not give me a reading list, and do not lean on named theorists or citations.
