The idea I want them to run into is the Gould–Lewontin critique of the adaptationist program: the charge that if you carve an organism up into discrete traits and ask of each one what it's for, you can always produce a plausible selective story, and so the program has no way to fail — plus the positive alternatives the critique points at, the stuff that isn't selection: developmental constraint, growth allometries where a trait rides along with body size, phylogenetic inertia, exaptation, a trait being a side-effect of something else that was the actual target. My own half-baked version, improve it or throw it out: I write one-paragraph descriptions of two or three invented species that share a body plan, and print a pile of index cards each carrying one trait of one of them. Unbeknownst to the students, half the traits I lifted from real field-guide descriptions of actual animals and half I generated by mashing a random adjective, a random body part, and a random number — so something like "asymmetrically thickened left rear molar, 3.1 mm," pure noise. Small groups get a subset and score points for the most convincing adaptive explanation per card. Then I reveal which were noise. The problem I can already see is that this only teaches "sometimes the story is wrong," which is boring and isn't the point at all. So I want the scoring rigged somehow — a payoff matrix, a betting round, a fold option — such that the rational move under the rules is to *always* claim a selective explanation, and they discover afterwards that the thing they were being rewarded for was unconstrained. Maybe a second round where the same trait comes back with a sister taxon that lacks it, or with a note about how it scales with body size, and they get to revise their bets.
