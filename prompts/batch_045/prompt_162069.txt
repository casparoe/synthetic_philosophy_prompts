I advise the amnesty committee of a truth commission in a country emerging from a decade of dictatorship. Our enabling statute says almost nothing beyond two conditions — that the act be associated with a political objective and that the applicant make full disclosure of all relevant facts — so we have been writing the doctrine case by case, loosely along the lines of the South African and Argentine processes. Please act as a philosophical adviser specialising in transitional justice and the ethics of amnesty, and work through this carefully: begin with a short brainstorm of the candidate principles that would generate the pattern of our decisions so far, and only then settle on a recommendation.

Our decisions to date:

1. Granted to a police sergeant who confessed in detail to a killing he carried out on orders and named the officer who ordered it.
2. Denied to a captain who gave a long account but quietly omitted his own presence at an incident we could document independently.
3. Granted to a former guerrilla who disclosed an operation in which two civilians died; the act was clearly connected to her movement's campaign.
4. Denied to a state agent who disclosed a killing fully but afterwards stripped the house and sold what he took; we called that a private motive.
5. Granted, over the objection of some victims' families and after a hearing they attended, to an applicant whose account was the only reason those families learned where their relatives were buried.

The application now before us: the applicant was a nineteen-year-old conscript assigned as a driver. He drove the truck that carried the men who killed three people that night and carried the bodies away; he killed and planned nothing. He has given the fullest account anyone has given of that night — names, route, burial site, his own role — and it corroborates. Under questioning he also said that he would have driven the truck anyway without the threat, because he needed the money and would have taken any driving job, that he had no view on the politics one way or the other, and that he had guessed, before getting into the truck, that people were going to die. He is the only applicant in this matter; the men who did the killing are dead or have not applied.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Which of our five decisions is the closest analogue, and in exactly which respect? If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, say so plainly and tell me what principle we would be entrenching with each available choice — amnesty earned by disclosure, by political motivation, by the service the account does to victims, by the applicant's diminished agency, or something else — and finally name the single further case that would best tell those principles apart.
