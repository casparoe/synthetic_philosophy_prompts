I'm working on a paper about Ulysses contracts — the individual kind (Ulysses and the sirens, the guy who hands his car keys to a friend before the party, advance directives in mental health law, the app that locks the fridge at 10pm) and the collective kind (constitutional entrenchment, supermajority requirements, one parliament trying to bind the next one, clauses that constitution drafters declare unamendable, sunset clauses as the compromise version). Before I write another page of it I want the spec, not the survey. What would a theory of precommitment actually have to get right to count as adequate — as adequate, specifically, for deciding when an earlier decision should be allowed to bind a later agent, and how rigid the binding mechanism should be? Give me the requirements with the case or consideration behind each one. I care far more about you getting the criteria right than about scoring views, so keep the scorecard to a few lines at the end.

What I want in it:

- The cases it has to handle, especially the ones people skip. The begging Ulysses: he's tied, the song is playing, he screams to be let go — and suppose that by then he's genuinely reconsidered and means it. The crew's problem, which I think is the crux: from outside, how do they tell "the music has deranged him" from "he has thought about it and changed his mind"? The ship-burner: Ulysses ties himself, Cortés (or whoever) burns the boats — is removing an option the same move as just making it expensive, or a different one with different justification? The enforcer problem: nearly every real precommitment needs someone else to hold the rope, and that someone has interests of their own (the crew can be bribed, the court can be packed, the fridge app can sell your data). The second-order case: Ulysses has to bind the crew not to untie him — so who ties the crew? Does that regress stop anywhere, or doesnt it, and if it doesn't is that a reductio or just the normal shape of any enforcement story? The ratchet: a chain of precommitments where each self binds the next, every step is locally justified, and the chain as a whole walkes somewhere that no self in it would have chosen. Asymmetric friction: rules that make one direction cheap and the other costly — degenerate version of precommitment, or the ordinary case?
- The distinctions it can't collapse. Binding versus merely influencing. Changing your mind versus finding something out. Weakness of will versus a revised view about what's worth wanting. The past self's authority versus the past self's better information. Binding yourself versus binding other people who happen to be your future. Precommitting to an outcome versus to a procedure for deciding. Revocable versus unamendable. And the one I suspect is doing the most damage: precommitment where the binder and the bound are numerically different agents. The "dead hand" worry only really bites in that case, so a theory that explains individual Ulysses contracts by appeal to personal identity over time has nothing to say about constitutions, and a theory built for constitutions may have nothing to say about why it's fine to tie yourself to the mast.
- What it can't contradict: people systematically discount the future and mispredict their own later tastes, and that fact is most of the reason precommitment is ever rational; and the dull empirical point that very rigid entrenchment does sometimes lock in a mistake for decades.
