I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy background — my only formal logic was the discrete-math course everyone takes — and I sit in a small reading group at work where we take turns presenting something outside our field. My turn came after a colleague's talk on formal verification, in which he said that Gödel's incompleteness theorems are just the halting problem restated in the language of proof, and then used that identification to argue for a fairly strong conclusion: because no mechanical system of reasoning can settle everything, a recursively self-improving system can never fully verify its own successors, so there is a hard and principled ceiling on how far automated mathematics and self-improvement can go, and anything that looks like progress past that ceiling must be either unsound or secretly stuck in the same place. A second member of the group took the same analogy and ran it the other way: if every fixed formal system leaves something out, and human mathematicians can apparently see that the leftover sentence is true, then whatever mathematicians are doing can't be mechanical at all. What struck me was that both of them treated the mapping as obvious and neither ever wrote it down, and when I tried to write it down myself I got stuck almost immediately.

So I'd like an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on the theorems themselves. Please give me an explicit table of correspondences — what plays the role of the machine, the input, the halting versus looping outcome, the decider, and the diagonal construction on each side — and then say which rows are exact, which are loose, and which are decoration. In particular I can't locate the following on the map: (i) the halting problem is about one fixed procedure deciding a property for every program-input pair, whereas incompleteness is about a fixed computably axiomatized theory strong enough to encode arithmetic failing to decide some one sentence, and I'm not sure the sentence corresponds to a program's behaviour or to something else entirely; (ii) the Gödelian limitation looks like a package deal of three properties — mechanical axiomatizability, consistency or omega-consistency, and completeness — so that a complete arithmetic is available the moment you give up soundness, and a sound and complete one is available the moment you give up being mechanical (the set of all true sentences of arithmetic); a system that merely conjectures and is sometimes wrong seems to opt out of the package altogether, which makes me suspect the ceiling argument is smuggling in a soundness requirement nobody defended; (iii) not all independent sentences seem to behave alike — the Gödel sentence of a theory, the assertion that the theory is consistent, and set-theoretic statements known to be undecidable from the standard axioms (which nobody agrees have a determinate truth value) all get lumped together as truths we can never prove, and I suspect that lumping is carrying much of the weight; (iv) the group also got onto the move where you notice the undecidable sentence is true, add it as a new axiom, and start again — one person says this shows the mind outruns any machine, the other says it shows the limit is a treadmill rather than a wall, since adding-and-restarting itself looks mechanical. Is there anything to say about what happens if you keep iterating and then take the union of everything you obtain, and is there an analogue on the halting side, along the lines of always being able to write a new analyzer that catches the case the previous one missed?

Finally: does either conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if the halting-problem framing is the wrong one, is there a better analogy from computing — Rice's theorem, the impossibility of a static analyzer that is both sound and complete for a language expressive enough to encode arithmetic, or something else — that carries the real content without importing the extra claims? If the honest conclusion is something narrower, such as that there is no mechanical, sound and complete arbiter for arithmetic, I'd like to be told exactly how narrow that is, and why it does not by itself yield either a ceiling on machine intelligence or a proof that mathematicians are not machines.
