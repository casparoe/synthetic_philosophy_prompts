Anyway. I wrote an argument for my response paper and the conclusion is absurd, and I'm also worried it hands a free victory to the forgetting side, which is exactly what we spent the semester resisting. Here it is:

(1) If we have a duty to remember the victims of an atrocity, what explains that duty is that being forgotten is a misfortune for them — that forgetting them is a wrong done to them. This is what is supposed to make remembering different from extracting the useful lessons and moving on.

(2) Forgetting can be a misfortune for someone only if they can be harmed: only if it can be bad *for them*, not merely bad, period.

(3) The dead cannot be harmed. Old symmetry argument: once the subject is gone there is no one left for it to be bad for.

(4) So the duty is not owed to the victims. It is a duty regarding the living — a duty to secure whatever forward-looking goods remembering produces: deterrence, moral vigilance, the identity of a mnemonic community, whatever.

(5) If it is a duty to secure those goods, then it is a duty to secure them as effectively as possible.

(6) Here's the case I invented to test this. Call it the confabulating successor community. Four generations after the atrocity, the commemorative practices are fully intact and more motivational than ever — the rituals, the days, the education, the refusal of denialism. But the content has drifted: first the names are lost, then the numbers, then the events get fused with a partly fictional narrative of national suffering. Nobody is a denialist, nobody is indifferent, and the forward-looking goods in (4) are produced *better* than they were when the memory was accurate. And suppose that in one variant, accurate memory would actually produce less vigilance than the drifted version, because the accurate version is more divisive.

(7) A duty that is better discharged by getting the past wrong is not a duty to remember. Remembering is essentially answerable to how things actually were — take that away and you have myth-production with good consequences.

(8) So there is no duty to remember. It collapses into a duty to manage the future.

(9) But (8) is unacceptable, for two reasons. First, forgetting is precisely what the perpetrator aims at: the argument seems to concede to atrocity the very thing it wanted. Second, it generalizes — by (2) and (3) we'd also have no duty to keep a promise to a dead friend, no duty to honor her last request, no duty not to slander her, no duty to finish her work. That's crazy.

A second case, since I think it's a separate fault line: the sealed archive. Everything is preserved perfectly, in a vault nobody will ever open, and no living person remembers or will ever remember anything about the victims. If you think the duty is discharged there, I think you've separated memory from storage and you probably have to explain why a hard drive can be a moral agent's substitute; if you think it isn't discharged, you need to say what remembering adds beyond the existence of the record.
