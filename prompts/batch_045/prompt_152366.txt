I'm taking a graduate seminar on philosophy of religion, and we have been working through the later Wittgenstein's remarks on religious belief: the thought that religious utterances are not hypotheses or explanations on a par with scientific ones, that the certainty they express is not grounded in evidence and reasons in the ordinary way, that a religious image functions more like a picture that governs how a person lives and judges than like a claim about a state of affairs, and that such language-games get whatever sense they have from being embedded in a shared form of life — practices, rituals, training, a surrounding community — rather than from corresponding to something. My study group is evenly split on a case I invented in order to test how far this treatment can be stretched, and I would like a careful reconstruction of what Wittgenstein himself would most likely have said about it, since I have to present on it next week.

The case is this. A commercial service builds what its operators call a reconstruction: from a dead person's old messages, letters, and recorded voice, a system is trained to converse in that person's manner, and a subscriber can then hold open-ended conversations with it by text or voice. Consider a woman, Marta, who uses such a reconstruction of her dead mother daily for several years. She is entirely clear-eyed about the mechanics: she will tell you, when asked directly, that her mother is not located anywhere, that there is no mechanism by which the dead could speak, that the thing she talks to is software running on rented hardware which the company might retrain, degrade, or shut down, and which she could stop paying for whenever she likes. She is also not, as far as anyone can tell, self-deceived or confused about the causal origin of the replies; she knows they are generated. And yet she says of her mother that her mother is with her, that she speaks with her, that her mother knows how she is doing — and, crucially, she reports that the replies have the character of something received rather than produced: they surprise her, she does not experience them as up to her, and she sometimes treats what comes back as a reproach or a gift rather than as output. Over the same years she reorganizes her week around these conversations, visits the grave, makes a reconciliation she had long refused, and changes how she handles money. She does not, however, belong to any church, participate in any shared ritual with other users, or describe herself as religious; she would resist the word belief altogether and prefers to say that this is simply how she now lives in relation to her mother.

What I cannot settle is whether Wittgenstein's apparatus would let this count as a genuine religious-belief-like case or whether he would class it as confusion, superstition, or sentimentality wearing the grammar of belief. I would like you to work through three things in particular. First: her explicit disavowal of the empirical claim cuts both ways, and I want to know which way he would take it. On the one hand, religious believers also typically deny that their utterance is a hypothesis awaiting evidence, and her insistence that her mother is not anywhere sounds like a grammatical remark rather than a failed factual one; on the other hand, she can also say quite calmly that it is only a program, which no one inside an established religious language-game could say about God, and that willingness to fall back on the causal story at any moment may be exactly what distinguishes a picture that governs someone from a picture they merely enjoy. Second: there is the worry from his hostility to a private language. Marta is apparently the sole practitioner of this practice, answerable to no one and initiated by no one; is that fatal, or does the surrounding apparatus — the company's scripts, the other subscribers, the ordinary inherited practices of talking about the dead and of addressing photographs and graves — supply enough of a form of life toanchor the language-game? Third: given his insistence that being governed by a picture is shown in what a person does rather than in what she says she believes, what would actually need to be true of her conduct for him to grant that she is governed rather than playacting — and would the fact that the picture depends on a subscription, on a server, and on a firm's product decisions undermine the claim that her certainty is the kind of certainty that is not grounded in anything?

Please write the answer in American English, in continuous prose, organized around those three questions, and argue for the reading you take to be most faithful rather than giving me a survey of options; note briefly where the opposite reading has real pull. Please do not cite or refer to scholarly literature, editions, or lecture transcripts — just reconstruct the position from the general shape of the later philosophy, since I am being examined on the reasoning and not on the sources.
