I run a drop-in ethics discussion group at a public library — adults with no philosophy background, most of whom have met karma mainly through pop culture and yoga studios — and I have 75 minutes to get them to see why karma is most charitably read as a *theory of moral desert* rather than as the slogan that suffering is always caused by the person who suffers it. Two or three of the regulars are Buddhist and will know the tradition better than I do. Don't explain karma to me; plan how I should explain it.

What I need from you is the plan, in four parts. First, the order in which the components have to arrive and what each depends on: what has to be in place before the word "desert" can do any work at all, given that the group will hear it as "they had it coming," and where the distinction between desert and mere causation has to be drawn relative to that. Second, the one case to carry the whole session, and what it must contain and must lack: I want a sufferer whose suffering is traceable to no act in her remembered life, an agent who appears to get away with a serious wrong, and no judge, court, or institution anywhere onstage. I suspect the usual illustrations — the bully who trips in the hallway, the smoker's diagnosis — all fail for the same reason, so tell me what that reason is and what test my example has to pass. Third, the misconceptions, in the order this particular group will produce them, and the point in the sequence where each gets defused. I'm expecting "what goes around comes around," fatalism, victim-blaming, and the quiet addition of a cosmic bookkeeper; tell me which of those I'm wrong to expect and which I've missed. Fourth, what I can ask or do at minute 70 that would show they've actually got the desert-versus-causation distinction rather than having been told that the popular version of karma isn't really karma.

Two constraints, and a complication. I'm not going to adjudicate whether karma is true, and I'm not going into rebirth mechanics, dependent origination, or the Sanskrit taxonomy — tell me what else I should openly refuse to settle at this level and say so out loud. The complication is mine to handle: something in my case connects the payout to the deed only via the theory itself — no memory, no record, no one in the story who can name the link. I don't know whether that is a feature to lean on early or a defect to hold back until the group is committed; say where it goes and why. And then the two participants who know more than I do: what do I do with them, and at which point in the sequence.

One last thing, which I want as a short note at the end of your plan rather than woven through it: tell me which reading of karma the sequence as you've built it quietly commits me to — suffering as a debt being paid, suffering as formative of character, or something else — which consideration would lead you to build the session around a different reading instead, and where you think the plan is most likely to break down with this group in particular.
