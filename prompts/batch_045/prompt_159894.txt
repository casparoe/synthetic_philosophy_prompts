The situation: I'm a PhD student (political theory) and I'm the student rep on my department's curriculum committee, so I get one vote and I'm expected to actually say something. The item on the table is our graduate handbook's rule that any change to degree requirements has to be approved at two separate faculty meetings at least three weeks apart. A couple of people want to cut it to a single meeting plus an email notice, on the grounds that it's pure friction and it once killed a reasonable language-requirement reform by running out the clock before the spring.

I reached for the fence parable: don't tear down the fence until you can say why it was put up. Two people pushed back and I'm not sure either is crushable.

First, a historian on the committee went and dug through the minutes. The rule was adopted in the mid-70s, apparently because two factions were each trying to ram changes through when the other side's people were away at a conference. His line is: fine, we now know exactly why it was put up, the reason was parochial factional self-defence, and travel patterns have changed anyway — so Chesterton's test is satisfied and it licenses removal, not preservation. That seems to me like it might show the parable is more neutral than defenders pretend, but I can't tell whether it also shows he hasn't really understood the rule.

Second, an institutional-economist types says the parable's test is the wrong question entirely. Her view is that the rule is currently doing something nobody ever wrote down — it creates a three-week window in which a proposal can be quietly reworked, it gives junior faculty time to notice they're about to be outvoted, and it screens out changes that only survive on momentum. The thing it "keeps out" is a rare event (a factional capture) that you literally cannot observe in the counterfactual, so no amount of archive-digging will surface it. She also says this is why the comparison to real fences fails: a fence blocks one direction of traffic along a fixed road, whereas a rule like this partly constitutes the practice it regulates rather than just blocking something.

There's a third wrinkle I keep coming back to. Our university dropped the foreign-language requirement years ago; apparently it had been inherited from a nineteenth-century philology model, and after it went people noticed it had also been functioning as a weeding-out device that nobody had ever stated as its purpose. So there seem to be at least four cases — recoverable reason, lost reason, no reason at all (someone copied another university's handbook), and current function that was never anyone's reason — and the fence picture seems to have room for maybe two of them.
