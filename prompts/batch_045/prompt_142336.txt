My roommate brought two people from her philosophy cohort back to our apartment after a department talk, and they ended up arguing at our kitchen table untill like 1am. I'm in biostat so I followed maybe 60% of it, but I keep turning it over and I can't settle it. The topic was epiphenomenalism -- the view that conscious experiences (the what-it's-likeness, qualia) don't cause anything, they're just along for the ride -- and whether the view ends up eating itself. Here's roughly how it went; I've numbered the moves so you can take them apart:

(1) Dana: Epiphenomenalism is self-undermining. My saying 'I'm in pain' is best explained by my being in pain. If my pain causes nothing, then nothing about my pain explains why I said that -- so the theory predicts my reports about my own experience are unreliable. And if those reports are unreliable, then my belief that I have experiences in the first place isn't justified, so I don't even have a reason to accept the theory.

(2) Mira: Too fast. The pain and the report can share a neural cause, so the report reliably tracks the pain without being caused by it. Knowledge needs reliability, not causation. Nothing collapses.

(3) Dana: Reliability is doing the work of causation without earning it. On your own view the quale is an extra ingredient: a creature physically just like me but with no inner life at all would produce exactly the same report. So the report tracks the brain state, and nothing in your story makes it track the quale rather than the brain state. Which means I could be systematically wrong about which quale goes with which brain state -- my 'pain' report might be paired with what is really a pleasant warmth -- and the account has no way to rule that out, because the quale never gets a chance to correct the report.

(4) Mira: The mismatch worry hits physicalists too, so its not a special problem for me. And the tracking complaint has two answers. Either the quale is realized by or necessitated by the brain state, in which case tracking the brain state is a way of tracking the quale -- the dependence is grounding rather than causation, and grounding is what fixes what my judgment is about. Or, if you don't like that: the judgment itself is partly constituted by the experience. The experience is a component of the believing state, so the belief is about the pain in a way tighter than any tracking relation.

(5) Dana: You're opening the gap and closing it as convenient. The conceivability of the physically identical creature with no inner life is what creates room for epiphenomenalism in the first place; if the brain state really necessitated the quale, the whole motivation evaporates. So you can't appeal to a necessary connection at step four that you denied at step one. And the constitution move is worse: if the pain is a proper part of a state that causes things, you need a story about when parts inherit causal relevance -- a passenger isn't driving the car. Either way you've quietly shrunk epiphenomenalism into a claim about which wholes do the causing.

(6) Mira: Fine, take the gap seriously and revise the view instead. Qualia are causally inert with respect to everything physical, but they can still bump into other mental states -- call it inboard epiphenomenalism, since all the causal traffic stays inside the mind. My pain causes my belief that I'm in pain; that belief, being neurally realized, is what causes my vocal cords to move and the sentence to come out. Self-knowledge is safe, nothing mental-to-physical happens, and the paradox is gone.

(7) Dana: Two problems. First, now every mental event has two causal stories running side by side -- the neural one that does the physical work and the phenomenal one that does no physical work -- so once the neural story is complete the phenomenal one is either redundant, and we're back to plain epiphenomenalism, or it's a genuine extra causal influence, and you're an interactionist with all the baggage that brings. Second, even granting it, you've only rescued my knowledge of my own experience. Nothing in anyone's behavior depends on their qualia, so my belief that you have experiences is left with no support at all -- and accommodating other minds was half of what the theory was supposed to do.

(8) Mira: On the first: parallel stories only look like a problem if you assume every event has exactly one complete causal story, and that assumption is precisely what's in dispute. On the second: I'll bite it -- my evidence about your experience was never going to be behavioral anyway. But note what that concedes: move (1) was supposed to show I can't know my own mind, and (6) blocks that, which is all I ever needed.

Can you go through it move by move and tell me who's right about what -- rate each of the eight moves somewhere from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification each -- and then say what, if anything, both of them are missing? I honestly can't tell who won and it's bugging me.
