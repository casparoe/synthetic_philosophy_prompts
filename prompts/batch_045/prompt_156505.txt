I run a tiny reading group — me, two linguistics grad students, one philosophy MA student who is definately allergic to formalism, and a retired classics teacher who shows up purely to argue — and we're spending two sessions on the semantics and pragmatics of slurs. The first session went sideways in a way I want to fix before the next one: two members were obviously disagreeing, but I've since decided they were disagreeing at different levels, one of them about whether the derogation is part of what is said at all and the other about what kind of thing it is if it is. Everyone in the room will grant two things as a starting point: that a typical derogatory use of a slur for group G offends, and that this has something to do with the word itself rather than only with the speaker's tone of voice. Given just those two starting points, I want a decision tree that lands each of us on a position.

What I want specifically: build the tree out of the logical space rather than out of a list of names — give me seven or eight branch questions first, each one a concrete case a person can answer without knowing which theory it leads to (so nothing like is the derogatory content semantic or pragmatic?), and then eight to twelve leaves. Cases I insist on: in-group members using the slur warmly among themselves; an out-group journalist quoting what someone shouted in a courtroom; a slur aimed at someone who isn't in G at all; nobody present as a target, say the word spray-painted on a wall overnight; an actor playing a bigot onstage; an ordinary denial of the form X is not S, said by someone campaigning against the word; a speaker who uses it sincerely but has none of the associated attitudes, out of pure habit; and one I made up that I think does real work — someone who grew up in a city where S got reclaimed in the nineties using it affectionately to a stranger from somewhere it never was, which I think forces you to say whether that is one word used two ways or two lexical entries that happen to sound the same. Leaves that have to be there: the view that the offense isn't content at all but a standing prohibition on the word, the view that it is a purely pragmatic or perlocutionary matter of causing offense, and at least one hybrid view where the derogation rides on a separate expressive dimension alongside truth-conditional content. If a combination your questions allow has no defenders, mark it unnamed and say in half a sentence whether it is incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored; and if two named views collapse at every node you've got, say so plainly and tell me what extra case would pry them apart. Use a standard label only where you're actually confident one exists, and write unnamed plus a one-line description otherwise — I don't want invented names, and I don't need citations or a reading list, since half the group won't be able to look anything up anyway.

And please, no formatting: no headings, no bullet trees, no bold, no tables — I want to paste this straight into an email to the group. I know a tree wants to be a picture, so keep the nesting followable without one, something like if you said yes here the next question is about the graffiti case, if you said no jump to the courtroom one. Each leaf should get its name or description plus one sentence on the most awkward thing that position is stuck with, and I'd like the whole thing to end by telling me which single question does the most sorting work, so I can put it on the board first.
