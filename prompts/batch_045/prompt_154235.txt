I have recently been seconded onto a small team redrafting my foreign ministry's guidance on when to support protection-of-civilians mandates, and the same case keeps being cited at me in opposite directions, so I want it worked through properly rather than gestured at. Take NATO's 2011 Libya intervention and analyze it through the doctrine it was sold under: the responsibility to protect, read together with the just-war categories it leans on (legitimate authority, last resort, reasonable prospect, proportionality, right intention, and a post-war responsibility to rebuild). The facts I want taken as given: Security Council authorization in March 2011 for a no-fly zone and all necessary measures short of foreign occupation, five abstentions including Brazil, Russia, China, India and Germany; Gaddafi's forces advancing on Benghazi after he threatened reprisals; a seven-month air campaign in which the coalition effectively became the rebels' air force; his killing at Sirte in October; then state fragmentation, a second civil war and a militia economy, and a much colder Security Council on Syria afterwards, with Brazil's responsibility-while-protecting formula and the Russia-China vetoes.

Two things I want you to test rather than assume. First, a structural claim I have been toying with and cannot settle: that regime change in Libya was not mission creep or bad faith but was selected by the means the mandate permitted, since air power with no ground component cannot convert a temporary reprieve into a durable one (any ceasefire leaving the incumbent's armour and command intact simply re-creates the threat the mandate exists to remove), so a protection mandate plus stand-off air power plus no boots on the ground adds up to decapitation whether or not anyone intended it. Second, the symmetric hazard on the protected side: once an external air umbrella exists, the party being protected has an incentive to reject ceasefires and to leave its own civilians exposed, because the interveners' credibility is hostage to being seen to protect. Give me the disciplined mapping first: who the mandate-holder is, what the proportionality baseline is against (including the counterfactual massacre that never happened), what would count as success and as disengagement, and what the doctrine says the coalition should have done at each point. Then say which features of Libya R2P explains, which it has no vocabulary for (a protection force that becomes a belligerent, success conditions it cannot specify, a norm whose authority is spent by one selective use), and whether the episode is a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the doctrine never contemplated. Say plainly where the theory is silent.
