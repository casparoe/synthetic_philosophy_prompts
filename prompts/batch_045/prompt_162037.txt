I'm part of a small giving circle — six of us: four software engineers, a nurse and a retired teacher, none of us with any training in philosophy or economics — and every spring we spend two long evenings arguing about where our pooled money (around £40,000 this year, plus a few hours a week each of volunteer time) should go, given that the honest alternative is that we do nothing much at all. For the last two years we have scored each candidate cause on three things and multiplied them: how much good would be done if the problem were solved or very substantially reduced (we call this scale), how much progress an extra pound or hour actually buys at the current margin (we call this traction), and how few other people are already working on it, on the theory that crowded fields have had their cheapest opportunities used up. Our shortlist is the usual mix: insecticide-treated bednets against malaria, unconditional cash transfers to very poor households, campaigns against the worst forms of farmed animal confinement, pandemic preparedness, work on reducing risks from advanced artificial intelligence, and a homeless shelter two streets from where I live.

The multiplication has started to bother me, for two reasons I cannot fully articulate. First, one of our group argues that traction and crowdedness are not independent: if traction genuinely means progress per marginal pound, then the fact that a field is crowded should already show up inside the traction score, and multiplying by a separate crowdedness term counts the same thing twice. Second, our scores vary enormously in spread — scale might range across a factor of a thousand between two causes, while crowdedness only varies by a factor of five — so the final ordering seems to be decided almost entirely by whichever factor happens to spread the most, which feels like an artifact of how we chose to write the numbers down rather than a fact about the world. I have also been toying with a fourth factor of my own, which we have started calling the keystone idea: credit a cause for how much easier it makes it to make progress on other causes later, so that, for instance, preventing a civilisational catastrophe is credited not only with the lives it saves but with preserving the option to solve everything else afterwards.

I would like you to sort the following candidate repairs and additions into tiers, according to how much you would expect each to change which cause we end up funding, and to give me one sentence on each explaining why it lands where it does: tier A if it could plausibly flip our top pick, tier B if it would reshuffle the middle of the list but not the winner, tier C if it is mostly a presentational change that leaves our decision about the same, and tier D if you think it would actively mislead us. The candidates are: dropping the crowdedness term and folding it into traction by defining traction strictly as progress per additional pound at the current margin; scoring every factor on a logarithmic scale and adding the logs with weights instead of multiplying raw numbers; adding my keystone factor as a fourth multiplier; adding a durability factor for how long the benefits last before decaying, being undone, or being absorbed into the status quo; adding a feedback-speed factor for how quickly we would find out whether we are succeeding, on the grounds that a fast loop lets us reallocate next year; adding a would-have-happened-anyway discount that subtracts the share of the good that governments or other funders would have produced within a few years regardless of us; replacing our point estimates with explicit ranges plus a penalty on estimates that rest on long chains of speculative assumptions; ranking by the total size of the prize rather than by progress per pound, on the grounds that some problems require large coordinated pushes and cannot usefully be entered at small scale; treating personal fit, meaning whether any of us actually has skills the cause needs, as a hard filter rather than as a score; and including an information-value term that credits a cause for what working on it teaches us about which causes matter, even when its direct impact is small. Please also flag anywhere you think two of these are really the same proposal in different words. Finally, if you had to put a number on it: of whatever separates our eventual top pick from the bottom of the shortlist, what fraction of that gap comes from the scale term? Give me a single number between 0 and 1 and a sentence of justification.
