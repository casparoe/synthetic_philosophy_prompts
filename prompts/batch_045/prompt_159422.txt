I am a novelist by trade, and I read philosophy only insofar as it happens to be useful to me. I should be grateful for an essay of 1,100 to 1,400 words, in British English, written in continuous prose rather than in bullet points or under headings, on a question that has arisen out of the book I am finishing.

The novel's narrator, a translator in her fifties, begins every working day with an unvarying sequence: she fills the kettle, opens the study shutters in a fixed order, lays out three pencils, and re-reads the closing paragraph of the previous day's work. What interests me is not the routine but what happens when it is broken. If she is interrupted — a telephone call, a delivery at the door — she does not take the sequence up where she left off. She returns to the first item and runs through the whole of it again, and only then does she sit down to work. She is not troubled by this; she calls it tidiness. Her sister, who exists in the book chiefly in order to disagree with her, calls it the behaviour of an insect, and the word she uses is sphexishness.

The parable she has in mind is the familiar one of the digging wasp: the wasp drags its prey to the mouth of the burrow, leaves it on the threshold, goes inside to inspect the nest, emerges, and then drags the prey in; if the prey is shifted a few inches while the wasp is within, the wasp returns it to the threshold and inspects the nest afresh, and the cycle can be repeated. I have met this story only at second hand, in essays read years ago, and I understand its standing as a description of what wasps actually do to be disputed. Please rest nothing on the entomology. Treat it as a parable, and spend no more than a tenth of the essay on it.

The distinction I want tested is one I have been working with in my own drafts. Call a routine re-triggerable when an interruption causes it to begin again from its first step; call it re-plannable when the agent can resume at the point of interruption, reorganise the remainder, or abandon the enterprise altogether without retracing the earlier steps. Marian's morning is re-triggerable. The question I want answered is whether re-triggerability, considered on its own, is any evidence at all that the agent performing it is not acting freely, and the essay should reach and defend a verdict on it. A qualified verdict is acceptable — for instance, that re-triggerability is in itself neutral and becomes evidence of unfreedom only when combined with some further feature — provided the qualification is argued for and not merely appended.

Beyond stating that verdict, the essay must do four things.

First, it must deal with the case in which the reset is what the agent has most reason to do. A great many procedures are safest or cheapest to restart from the beginning, because intermediate states are unrecoverable, unsafe, or impossible to verify: reinstalling a system from a clean image, recounting a till, resterilising instruments, retelling a story from the top to an audience who arrived late. If a return to the first step can be the rationally indicated response to an interruption, what exactly is supposed to be sinister about re-triggerability, and what would have to be added to the case before it became evidence of mechanism rather than of caution?

Second, it must confront the question of level. The routine is rigid; the policy of adopting a rigid routine may be the agent's own, adopted for her own reasons, and revisable when she reflects on it. Marian could, presumably, decide one morning to stop. Which level is the question about freedom properly asked at — the sequence, the policy that installed it, or the agent's capacity to revise that policy — and what reason is there to think the answer is one rather than another?

Third, it must address a paradox I cannot get out of my head, which is a novelist's paradox rather than a philosopher's. Every plotted character is, in a sense, a mechanism: the events of a novel are fixed in advance, a character cannot do otherwise than the plot requires, and yet readers blame characters, hold them responsible, feel the weight of their choices, and are unmoved by the reminder that the whole thing was settled before they began reading. If rigidity of that absolute kind sits comfortably with the practices of praise and blame in fiction, what follows about whether rigidity in Marian's case is freedom-relevant at all? Does the paradox tell us something about freedom, or only something about what readers will put up with?

Finally, the essay must say what would change its mind. Name one further feature of Marian's case — something I could actually write into the book — that would reverse your verdict, and explain why that feature carries the weight you are assigning to it.

Two further constraints. I am not asking for a survey of the free will literature and I do not want one: no citations, no bibliographies, no reading lists, no summary of which famous position is which. Argue the case in your own terms, defining any term of art you find you need. And write as to an intelligent non-specialist: I shall be reading this as a novelist, and I should like prose that moves an argument forward rather than prose that displays a map of one.
