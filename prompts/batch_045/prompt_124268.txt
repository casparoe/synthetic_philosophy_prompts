**The Understanding Debt**

Everyone agrees that outsourcing cognitive work to AI might cost us something. Almost nobody says clearly what. "We won't know things anymore" is too crude: I already don't know the phone number of anyone I care about, and I'm not worse off. So here is a first pass at splitting the worry into pieces, because I think only one of them is scary.

The obvious piece is *displacement*: there was something you could have learned by doing the task, and now the tool does it and you don't learn it. True, but boring, and it has an obvious answer — you were never going to do the task anyway, or the thing you'd have learned wasn't worth the hours.

The piece I actually care about I'll call *decalibration*. The danger isn't that you fail to understand; it's that you stop being able to tell. A fluent output is extremely good at passing the checks a non-expert actually runs: does it look structured, does it use the right vocabulary, does it feel like the thing I would have said if I'd had time. And the standard fixes make this worse rather than better. Asking the model to explain its answer, or show its work, or rate its own confidence, hands you the *feeling* of having verified without any of the substance — because you can't tell a good explanation from a plausible one in a domain you don't know. Definately the most underrated part of this: reassurance features are aimed at the exact feeling that's already broken.

The second piece is structural rather than individual, and I think it's the one that should worry institutions. Call it the *apprenticeship bottleneck*. You acquire real understanding by doing work that is slightly too hard for you, badly, under someone who can see why it's bad. That work is also, by a nasty coincidence, the most automatable work in any given profession — it's the routine, the well-specified, the low-stakes. So automation eats the bottom of the ladder first. Every individual choice to offload is locally rational; the aggregate result is a profession with no pipeline, a kind of competence Ponzi scheme that hasn't crashed yet because the people at the top learned before the tooling got good. [WEAK — is this just displacement with extra steps?]

The third piece is about checking rather than doing. When I check your work with my eyes and you produced it with your brain, our failure modes are mostly independent, and agreement between us is real evidence. When I check AI output with a different AI, or with the same AI in a different window, or even just with my own judgment after staring at its output for a while, the failure modes are correlated: we share a prior, a style, a blind spot. Call this *correlation of the check*. The usual remedy for unreliability is redundancy — get a second opinion — but redundancy does nothing when the replicas fail together, and general-purpose models are precisely the thing that makes all the replicas fail together. This, I want to say, is the deep difference between depending on a calculator and depending on a reasoner: calculators fail independently of you, general-purpose oracles don't.

Now, how would you know if any of this was happening to you? Here's a test I half like. Understanding X, I'll say, is a matter of three separable abilities, and they degrade at different rates:

1. *Generation*: produce the answer from scratch.
2. *Variation*: perturb the case — what if the sign flips, what if the deadline halves, what if this assumption is false — and say what changes.
3. *Adjudication*: look at several candidate answers, including wrong ones, and reliably pick the right one.

The thing that makes AI dependence feel harmless is that it kills (1) while leaving (3) almost perfectly intact. You can still read a model's output, or three models' outputs, and know which one is better. That's a real skill and it *feels* exactly like understanding: you're exercising taste, you're catching errors, you're not a passive consumer. But adjudication without generation is a curator's skill, not a producer's, and it's domain-local in a way that understanding isn't — it works right up until the point where every candidate on offer is wrong in the same direction, which is exactly the situation correlation of the check produces. Call it the *curator's illusion*. [CLICHÉ? this feels like it might be a very familiar point dressed up]

If that's right, the operational test for "do we still understand this" isn't a quiz and isn't a self-report. It's something like: can you teach it to a competent beginner who is allowed to interrupt you, survive the interruptions, and recognise a good correction when they give you one? I like the interruptible-teaching version because it's a live test of variation and adjudication at once, and because you can't fake it with fluency. I also worry it's circular — maybe I've just redescribed "understanding" as "ability to do the thing" and called it a test. [WEAK]

Which brings me to the bit I'm least sure about, so I'll put it at the end and keep it short. Suppose all of the above is true. Is the loss of understanding bad in itself, or only bad because there will be days when the oracle is wrong and nobody is left who can notice? I think the honest answer is the second, and that this changes the shape of the problem. If understanding is a *safety stock* rather than a virtue, then the question isn't "how much understanding must we preserve" but "how often will the oracle fail, how bad is a missed failure, and how much redundancy do we want to buy" — which is an insurance question, not an epistemic one. [CLICHÉ?] And insurance questions have a characteristic pathology: the premium is paid now, the accident is hypothetical, and the people who decide are not the people who'll be in the crash. That's the actual reason I expect institutions to under-invest in understanding even once they believe every word of this essay.
