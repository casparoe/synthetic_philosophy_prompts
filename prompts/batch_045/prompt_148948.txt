I cover AI and cognitive science, and I keep coming back to the "user illusion" way of talking about consciousness: the suggestion that what a subject enjoys is not a view of the machinery but a simplified, action-oriented interface laid over it, the way a graphical desktop stands in for what the machine is really doing. I want to know whether that can be turned into an actual principle, so here are my verdicts for it to be tested against.

- Blind spot: the visual system interpolates colour and texture across the optic disc. I say the subject genuinely sees the completed surface. Conscious: yes.
- Blindsight: the information is demonstrably in the system and demonstrably steering behaviour, since the subject guesses location above chance. I still want to say: not conscious of the stimulus.
- The dashboard: an autonomous vehicle maintains a rich world-model and renders a simplified, accurate summary of it, with alerts, on a screen for its engineers. My verdict is no consciousness, even though the processing underneath is far richer than in the blindsight case and the summary is true.
- Write access: now suppose the same machine's summary is broadcast to its own subsystems and is what its planning operates over, so that it can defer, re-plan, and override its own modules on the basis of it. I still balk at calling it conscious, but I cannot say why, and I suspect this is where my verdicts stop being mutually consistent.
- Confabulation: a split-brain patient's left hemisphere invents a reason for an action initiated elsewhere. The unity of agency is a user illusion, and the patient is conscious nonetheless.
- Phantom limb pain: the interface reports a limb that is gone, and knowing it is gone does not revise the report. Conscious, and painfully so.
- The neuroscience-literate subject: I can know, in the moment, that my sense of being one deliberate agent is manufactured, and the knowledge does not dissolve it. So this is not the kind of illusion that seeing through dispels.

My tentative principle: a system is conscious of X just in case it constructs, for its own use and on a timescale relevant to its own action, a simplified summary of X that is broadcast to many of its subsystems at once and makes a causal difference to what it does next, where the summary may be false, may go unrevised in the face of what the system knows, and need not disclose its own mechanisms.

Test that against my cases and repair it where it fails. I especially want to know which clause is doing the work of separating the dashboard from the blind spot, and whether the write-access case forces me either to accept that consciousness is cheap or to smuggle in something the user-illusion metaphor does not supply. Then give me what the repaired principle predicts about three cases I have not settled: a system with two independent self-addressed interfaces running over a single machinery; a self-model with no valence at all, pure neutral summary; and the write-access machine after years of operating in the world with a memory of having done so. Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts your best principle cannot accommodate and would have me give up.
