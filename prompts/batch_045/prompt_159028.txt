Me and my writing partner are two books into a trilogy about a family and a fertility clinic in Bristol, and we've got into the habit of "re-running" scenes: we take a character, change one thing about how she came to exist, and write the same life again to see what falls out. It's more or less become the structure of book three. The problem is that every week one of us pitches a variant and then we spend forty minutes arguing about whether the person in the variant is still her or is somebody else with her name, and the answer changes the emotional contract with the reader completely — if it's her, the reader grieves; if it's a stranger wearing her face, the reader is meant to feel spooked. I had a look at the Wikipedia page on origin essentialism and its no use to me: it tells me the Kripke-style view, the one where a person couldn't have grown from a different egg and sperm, and then tells me that plenty of people disagree, and that's about it. Please don't palm me off with a reading list or start name-dropping papers at me — I'm not writing an essay and I've got no library access where I live, I need something I can actually use.

What I want is a decision procedure, the sort of thing we can run on a pitch in ten minutes at the kitchen table with no philosophy between us. Roughly: what's the first question we ask about a variant, what counts as a good enough answer to it, at what point does the procedure just say "no, that's somebody else" and stop, and at what point does it hand the decision back to us because it's a choice about the book rather than a fact about identity. It needs to keep whatever is really doing the work in the philosophical view while being usable by two novelists, and I'd rather you were straight with us about which distinctions you've had to flatten to make that work and which ones have to survive or the whole thing falls apart. Three snags I keep tripping over and want the procedure to deal with rather than dodge: fertilisation isn't an instant, it's a process lasting hours, so "which sperm" is sharp but "when she began" isn't, and one of our best variants is the very same embryo, same gametes, frozen for twelve years and implanted later, so she's born into a completely different family; gametes are themselves objects with their own origins, so if we insist on strict necessity at the level of the egg and sperm, why not at the level of the grandmother's, or of the matter the gametes are made of, and where does the procedure stop that regress and admit that it's just stopping; and then the nasty one, a mitochondrial donation case, where the mother's nuclear material is put into a donor egg that's been emptied of its own nucleus, so the nuclear DNA is mum's and dad's but the cytoplasm and a tiny sliver of the genetic material come from a stranger. Is that a different egg or not? Under a rule that says different egg, different person, I genuinely can't tell, and it's currently the opening of chapter four.

Could you also run the procedure on three of our actual variants and show your working. (1) The frozen-embryo one above. (2) Identical twins: same sperm, same egg, one zygote that splits — so if the rule only ever asks about the gametes it can't tell the twins apart, and we need to know whether that's a bug to patch by moving the origin to the zygote or to the splitting event, or whether it's a sign the whole approach is wrong for a novel. (3) A clone: a skin cell from the mother, nucleus put into an emptied donor egg, no sperm anywhere in the story. My editor, who has to sign off on the structure and has already told me she wants the throughline to be one woman's life, is going to hate whatever the procedure says about at least one of these, so please say plainly when the answer is one she won't want, and be honest about the cases where your procedure is mostly a way of dressing up a decision we were always going to have to make ourselves — and about where it will misfire on us, because we'll be trusting it for the next eight months.
