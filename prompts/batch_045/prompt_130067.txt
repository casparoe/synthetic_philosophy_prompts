I'm writing a seminar paper on epistemic communities and have backed myself into an argument whose conclusion I don't want, and I'd like a diagnosis rather than a literature summary. Set the terms stipulatively, so nothing here hangs on how any particular author uses them: an epistemic bubble is a group from which relevant voices and evidence are missing through omission, so members simply never encounter them; an echo chamber is a group whose members' trust has been engineered so that outside sources are actively discredited, i.e. a mismatch between exposure and credibility rather than a lack of exposure. My argument aims to show that the second notion does no work that the first doesn't already do.

(1) Whether an agent is justified in believing p on the basis of a source's testimony depends on whether she is justified in taking that source to be credible about matters like p.

(2) Credibility assignments of the kind at issue are, for most agents, themselves formed largely on the basis of testimony from sources they already trust.

(3) If testimonial justification must ultimately rest on the agent's own non-testimonial grounds — her first-hand evidence that those sources are reliable in the relevant domain — then a community-wide pattern of credibility assignments is justified only if it traces back to such first-hand grounds.

(4) Call a community's discrediting mechanism calibrated when it discredits outside sources exactly when those sources are in fact unreliable. Calibration is just accuracy, and accuracy is not an epistemological defect.

(5) The diagnostic core of an echo chamber is that the mechanism, not the evidence, is doing the work: members' credibility assignments are not supported by their non-testimonial grounds. That is what distinguishes it from a bubble.

(6) But suppose the mechanism is calibrated, as in a community I'll call the rigid chamber: its blacklist extensionally contains every unreliable outside source and no reliable one, yet it would have produced the very same blacklist had every blacklisted source been impeccable. By (4) its members have grounds of exactly the sort (3) demands, so the rigid chamber is not an echo chamber, and (5) is false.

(7) If instead the mechanism is not calibrated, members' beliefs are unjustified, but for the unexciting reason that they have been badly informed; a merely omissive bubble that happened to leave them equally badly informed would be no better off, so the trust-based apparatus is idle.

(8) So the notion of an echo chamber is dispensable: it collapses either into accurate informant-tracking, which is not a pathology, or into plain false belief, for which we already had a name. Since I think the notion does real work, something above is wrong.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I am most attached to (5), and least to (4), so if (4) is the culprit I want to know precisely why it looked compelling — I take it the appeal is that accuracy and justification are supposed to come apart in some way I haven't spelled out — and what I inherit by denying it. In particular: does denying (4) commit me to holding that a community can be in a bad epistemic condition while none of its members' beliefs are unjustified and none of its sources are unreliable, so that the harm has to be located somewhere other than in the falsity or unjustifiedness of what is believed? If so, say what that somewhere is and what would have to be true of the members for the charge to bite.
