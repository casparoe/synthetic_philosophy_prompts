My flatmate and I got into a three-hour argument last night about modal realism — the view that there really are infinitely many concrete worlds, spatiotemporally cut off from one another, that every way a world could be is a way some world is, and that "actual" is just an indexical like "here", so our world isn't metaphysically privileged in any deep way. He's the skeptic, I'm the one who's sort of sympathetic, and he laid out this argument, which I've been turning over since and can't find the hole in. (1) If modal realism is true there's no deep metaphysical difference between being in this world and being in any other: "actual" picks out a world the way "here" picks out a place, without marking anything special about it. (2) So the question "which world am I in?" has the same shape as "which hotel room did I wake up in?" — a bunch of candidate locations, exactly one of which is mine, and nothing available from the inside to tell them apart. (3) In a self-locating problem with N candidates and no evidence distinguishing them, you spread credence evenly: 1/N to each. (4) Now, all you really have to go on is your current local state — what's in front of you this second, plus whatever you take yourself to remember. Plenitude and recombination guarantee worlds containing exactly that local state and then going on any way at all: worlds where the next ten seconds are entirely ordinary, worlds where the wall dissolves into a flock of flamingos, worlds where your apparent past is the only orderly thing in an otherwise random mess. (5) The orderly continuations are a speck next to the messy ones — for every way of going on normally there are vastly, vastly more ways of going on chaotically. (6) So by (3) you should be all but certain you're in one of the messy worlds, i.e. you should expect your surroundings to come apart into noise within a few seconds. (7) That's insane, and no evidence could ever make it reasonable. (8) So modal realism is false, or at minimum it proves far too much: it costs you any confidence that the future will resemble the past.

I keep going back and forth between thinking its step (3) and thinking its step (1), and every place I push seems to cost me something I wanted to keep. I'd really like to keep the indexical story about actuality, the denial that the same individual literally exists in more than one world (counterparts instead), and the idea that plenitude is about variety rather than quantity — one world per way a world could be, no duplicate worlds — since that last bit is the thing I half-remember being the official reply to counting arguments like this. When I say that to him he says the counting step is the only part that isn't negotiable once you grant (1) and (2), and that if I want to save modal realism I should reject (2): the slide from "actuality is indexical" to "there's no deep difference between my world and the others." He also says this is just the Boltzmann brain worry wearing a costume and that it hits every big-world view equally, so it can't be an objection to modal realism in particular — which I want to believe but don't fully.

So: which step do you actually reject, and why does it look fine right up until you press on it? If you had to price the options for a modal realist — giving up the indexical account of actuality, refusing the move to self-locating uncertainty across worlds, refusing to count worlds or take ratios of them at all, or swapping a uniform count for some similarity-weighted measure over counterparts — what does each one cost, and which is cheapest? And is he right that this is one generic problem attacking every large-world theory, or does the specifically Lewisian package (counterparts, no trans-world identity, recombination of possibilia) make it worse than usual or easier than usual to answer?
