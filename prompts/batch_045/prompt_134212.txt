I am working through the debate over the special composition question — the question of when it is true that there is something composed of some given things — and I would like a careful explanation of one particular maneuver in it.

The standard menu of answers is familiar: nihilism, on which composition never genuinely occurs and only mereological simples exist; universalism, on which any things whatsoever have a fusion; and restrictivism, on which composition occurs only under some substantive, non-trivial condition. The best-known restrictivist view is the organicist one defended by Peter van Inwagen, on which the xs compose something just in case their joint activity constitutes a life (simples trivially composing themselves), so that tables, chairs, and clouds turn out not to exist. Restrictivism characteristically faces two complaints at once. First, the arbitrariness complaint: any restriction other than "always" looks like an unprincipled list of favoured cases unless it can be given a general rationale. Second, the problem of the many: for an ordinary object such as a cloud or a mountain there appear to be countless overlapping pluralities of particles that differ only marginally and each of which seems to have as good a claim as any other to be the plurality that composes it, so that either there are very many coincident clouds or there are none.

Suppose someone tries to discharge restrictivism's burden with a view I shall call *contrastive-cohesion restrictivism* (CCR). Let causal integration be a matter of degree: the extent to which the members of a plurality stand in dense, reciprocal, ongoing causal interaction with one another, rather than interacting mainly with things outside the plurality. CCR then holds:

> The xs compose an object if and only if the xs are a local maximum of causal integration among the pluralities that overlap them — that is, if and only if no plurality that shares some but not all of the xs is more strongly causally integrated than the xs are.

Please explain the following, at a level appropriate for someone who already knows the standard menu of views but has not encountered this proposal.

1. **What is structurally new here, and what is it supposed to buy?** In particular, explain why CCR makes facts about composition *extrinsic* and *competitive* in a way that neither organicism nor universalism does, and how that feature is supposed to answer the arbitrariness complaint and the problem of the many simultaneously. I would also like you to address a classification question: is CCR best understood as a metaphysical answer to the composition question at all, or is it really a semantic or contextualist thesis about how claims of the form "there is an object composed of these things" get their truth conditions? Say why that distinction matters for what the view can claim to have achieved.

2. **How the view behaves on cases, treated as arguments rather than illustrations.** Work through at least: (a) my two hands held apart in front of me; (b) the plurality consisting of a living organism together with an inert pebble resting beside it; (c) a case in which two overlapping pluralities are exactly tied in causal integration. For each, state what CCR predicts, show the reasoning from the criterion to the verdict, and say whether the verdict should be counted as a point in the view's favour.

3. **The most serious objection.** My suspicion is that the contrastive move purchases a treatment of the problem of the many only by paying for it somewhere else, and I want that suspicion sharpened rather than merely confirmed. Articulate the strongest version of the objection, and distinguish it from the cheap version (that the view is unfamiliar, or that it makes composition depend on "what else is around"). Then give the best reply available to a defender. If you judge the objection fatal, say what the most attractive fallback position would be for someone who wanted to preserve the view's motivation without its comparative apparatus.

Throughout, I am more interested in the reasoning that connects the extrinsicality of CCR's criterion to its verdicts than in a catalogue of positions, and I would ask you to flag any place where the view's fate turns on a further choice that I have left unspecified in the formulation above.
