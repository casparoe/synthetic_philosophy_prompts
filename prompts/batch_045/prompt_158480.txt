I run a monthly thing at the back room of a pub called "Thinking Out Loud" — usually about 16 people, mixed ages, almost nobody with a philosophy background, one 90-minute session, and whatever I can carry in a bag (index cards, pens, two opaque cloth bags, a tub of red and blue beads, some poker chips). Next month I want to do a session on what to do when someone you basically respect as a thinker disagrees with you about something you've both looked into. I've been reading about this on my own and there seem to be two rough camps: the people who say that when you find out an intellectual equal disagrees you should move toward them, maybe all the way to the midpoint, and the people who say you're allowed to hold your ground when you've got more evidence or you've thought about it harder. I've seen the words "conciliationism" and "steadfastness" floating around but I'm not confident I'm using them right, and I really don't want to open with the vocabulary or a mini-lecture — I want the room to run into the problem before anyone names it.

Here's the sketch I've got, which I'd like you to turn into something actually runnable (or replace if it's broken). Three rounds with the bead jars and a scoring rule that pays people for accurate confidence rather than just for being right — last time I tried anything score-based the room glazed over, so I need a payoff scheme that is a short table, not a formula. Round one: a hidden jar is either mostly-red or mostly-blue, everyone draws one bead in private and writes down a private percentage, then pairs are formed so that most pairs have drawn different colours, each person sees the other's number and can revise. My hope is that splitting the difference obviously pays here and anyone who digs in looks silly. Round two: same set-up but half the room secretly gets three draws and half gets one, so the person with more evidence meets someone who disagrees — I want them to feel that the "meet in the middle" habit that just won them points is now costing them points, and ideally to notice the version where one person has seen everything the other has seen plus more, where moving at all seems wrong. Round three is the one I care most about and the one I can't get to work: everyone sees the same sequence of draws, so the disagreement carries no new information at all, and people still disagree. My idea was to run a quick ten-question trivia confidence quiz at the very start, before anyone knows what the session is about, and hand each person their own calibration score privately at the start of round three — so each person knows how well calibrated they are but not how well calibrated anyone else is, and (without telling them) about four or five of the sixteen are badly overconfident and have no idea. Then the pressure to move toward the group and the pressure to trust yourself both feel strong and they conflict, and nobody can tell which camp they're in. Tell me whether that rig actually produces the effect I want, and give me exact numbers for the jar compositions, the draw sequences and the payoff table. If it helps, I'd also like a final vote where people choose the rule the group should adopt for future rounds before they learn whether they were one of the well-calibrated ones or one of the overconfident ones.

What I need from you beyond the rules and the numbers: a timeline for the 90 minutes; a debrief that maps specific moments in the three rounds onto the different positions (the "their disagreement is evidence" thought, the "I've got strictly more evidence so I shouldn't move" thought, the "we saw the same thing and I still think I'm right, and I know the other person is thinking exactly the same about me" thought, and whatever the rule says when it's applied to itself); a frank list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — people reporting a number they don't really believe, the one guy who argues with everything, someone working out that the beads go back in the jar, collusion between pairs, confusion about the scoring — and how to handle each; and, importantly, an honest note on which part of the problem a game like this simply can't reproduce and which I'll have to say out loud myself in the debrief, because I suspect the hardest part of the real thing is that nobody gets handed their own calibration score, and I'd rather know in advance where my activity quietly cheats.
