I'm a third-year med student on my ICU rotation and I got into an argument with a resident that's been bugging me for a couple of weeks, so I figured I'd ask people who think about this kind of thing for a living.

What set it off was two donation cases within a few days of each other. In the first, a young woman was declared dead by neurological criterea — no brainstem reflexes, no drive to breathe, the whole workup — but she was on a ventilator, warm, her heart was beating, and because she was pregnant the team kept her going on pressors and hormones for weeks to get the fetus further along. Everyone I asked said no, she was dead the whole time, we were just maintaining the body. But what was being maintained sure looked like a lot of what I associate with being a living organism: temperature regulation, wound healing, fighting off infections, growing another human being. In the second case, the family withdrew the ventilator in an operating room, the heart stopped, someone listened with a stethoscope and watched the arterial line for a few minutes, and declared death. And then — this is the part I cant get straight — the surgeons opened her up and, at the hospital I'm at now, they hook a pump up to the vessels in the abdomen and get blood flowing to the kidneys and liver again, while clamping the aorta higher up so nothing can get to the head. So what supposedly made her dead was permanent rather than irreversible in any strict sense: we won't restart her circulation because we've decided not to, and then we partly restart it anyway.

The resident's gloss was that death is just irreversible loss of the capacity for consciousness, or maybe of the organism's ability to integrate itself as a whole, and that both cases fit. I don't think they do fit, and I don't know what the practice would have to assume for them to fit. So: what does the actual institution here presuppose about what death is? It's supposed to be one practice, but it looks to me like it might be two different theories stitched together — a brain-based story doing the work in the first case, a circulation-and-breathing story doing it in the second — or maybe neither, and what's really load-bearing is the dead-donor rule, so that whatever account of death licenses the transplant we want to perform is the one that gets adopted. What I'd like is for you to pull apart what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with: could someone who thinks death is the loss of consciousness and someone who thinks it's the loss of organismic integration both sign off on these exact protocols, and where precisely would they start to diverge — patients in a permanent vegetative state, anencephalic newborns, a body whose brain could in principle be kept perfused but would never be conscious again? And here's the bit I actually care about: if we dropped the dead-donor rule and simply allowed organs to be taken from patients who are dying but not yet dead, what would have to change in the ICU? Would anything really break, or was that rule carrying all the weight and the rest of the talk about death just decoration?
