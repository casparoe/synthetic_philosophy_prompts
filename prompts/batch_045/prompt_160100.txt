I work in deep reinforcement learning — policy gradients, actor–critic architectures, reward shaping, model-based planning and MPC, offline RL, intrinsic motivation terms, credit assignment, discounting, off-policy correction. Please use that vocabulary at working-engineer precision rather than softening it; I do not need RL explained to me. My reading in philosophy of mind is at the level of a curious non-specialist.

What I want translated is the dispute about what makes pain bad, and about what a certain kind of clinical case is supposed to show. The case, as it is usually described: after a brain lesion, a patient still localises pain accurately, still rates it at the same intensity on the same scale as before, still distinguishes burning from stabbing from aching — but reports that it no longer bothers him, is not distracted by it, and takes no steps to avoid or end it. Something like this is what gets called pain asymbolia. As I have seen the argument framed, there are at least three candidate answers about where the badness lives: (i) in the felt unpleasantness, the affective or hedonic aspect of the state; (ii) in its functional role — the way it seizes attention, demands that it stop, disrupts whatever else the person was doing; (iii) in the subject's conative attitude towards it, in its being something he dislikes or wants gone. The asymbolia cases are then used as evidence that whatever is left in them is not bad, or not bad in the way pain normally is.

Translate each of those three answers into my vocabulary as faithfully as you can. I can guess at pieces of the mapping myself — unpleasantness sounds like a negative reward term or a large negative advantage, the functional role sounds like the grip of that term on the policy through the gradient or an interrupt on planning, the sensory dimension sounds like the observation channel, the attitude sounds like a learned value estimate or a head that reports utility — but I don't trust those guesses, and in particular I don't know which of them is supposed to be carrying the normative weight, or whether the three views are even disagreeing about the same slot in the machinery.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. For each view: what translates cleanly, what translates only if you distort it, and what cannot be said in my vocabulary at all. Where RL genuinely has no term and you reach for something adjacent — control theory's error signals and setpoints, predictive processing's precision weighting — flag the import as residue rather than counting it as translation. And don't let the translation quietly settle the question: if the only way to express "bad for the agent" in my vocabulary is "penalised by the objective the designer wrote down," say so explicitly, and then tell me whether that collapse is a defect of my framework, a defect of my fluency, or a sign that the philosophers' notion was doing less work than it looks like it does.

Two further things I want tested rather than assumed. First, take two stipulated variants of the case that I have not seen discussed. Variant B: the person has intact sensory discrimination and entirely intact avoidance behaviour — flinches, takes the analgesic, cancels his plans, reports being motivated — but insists, credibly, that it does not feel bad at all. Variant C: he says it feels awful, exactly as bad as it used to, means it, and would say "I want it to stop" if you asked — but he never acts on it, is never distracted, and would not take a pill placed in front of him. What verdict does each of the three views give about each variant, does any view give a verdict that its own motivations sit badly with, and is there a variant the debate needs and does not have — a fourth dissociation you would design yourself?

Second, audit my own proposed analogue. I had been reading asymbolia as gradient masking: the penalty signal reaches the observation vector and is fully reflected in the critic's output, but a stop-gradient blocks it from shaping the policy, and it is disconnected from whatever produces the agent's summary reports. Is that the same phenomenon, or does it conflate three things the philosophers keep apart — perceiving, valuing, and being moved? If it is the wrong analogue, say precisely where it goes wrong. What I want at the end is a straight answer on whether my intuition that this is all just reward versus policy influence is right, is missing something, or is asking a different question from the one the literature is asking.
