I'm a journalist — I write a column on science and ideas — and right now I'm doing a piece on the boom in "know your risk" medicine: full-body MRIs for people with no symptoms, polygenic risk scores, whole-genome sequencing you buy online. Half the piece is about why people say yes and half is about the people who say no thanks, and I keep going in cirlces on whether there's anything rationally wrong with that second group.

A decision-science guy whose newsletter I half-trust told me the thing to read up on is Good's theorem, and he summarized it for me like this:

"Good proved that information can never make a rational agent worse off. Take any decision problem: the expected utility of choosing after you've seen the evidence is always at least as high as the expected utility of choosing now, and it's strictly higher whenever the evidence would change your mind about something. So a rational agent should always want more information, and the bigger the decision, the more it should be willing to pay for it. That's why any AI built to maximize expected utility will hoover up every scrap of data it can reach, and also why 'I'd rather not know' is never rational when the stakes are real."

I want to use this, but I don't want to print something a decision theorist would laugh at. Can you go through it more or less sentence by sentence — what someone who actually works on this would grant, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong? And for the parts that are wrong, tell me whether they're wrong in a way that matters for my story or in a way that's just sloppy phrasing.

Three things I'm suspicious of, in case I'm right for the wrong reasons. One: the "strictly higher whenever it changes your mind" bit feels off to me — I have a feeling the value can be exactly zero even when the information moves your credences a lot, if it doesn't change what you'd do. Two: the AI claim. Isn't the theorem a claim about an agent that updates by Bayes and then re-picks the action that maximizes expected utility? If so, does it say anything at all about a system with a fixed policy, or a proxy objective, or a compute budget, or that's been told to just answer now? Three, and this is the one I really can't check: a philosopher friend muttered at me that the whole thing is a result about expected-utility agents specifically, and that for agents who aren't Bayesian — ambiguity-averse, dynamically inconsistent, whatever he meant — information can actually have negative value. Is that a real caveat or was he just showing off at a party?

And then the question I actually need for the piece: does this theorem give me any purchase at all on declining a screening test? My instinct is that it's nearly silent — that it's a claim about the instrumental value of information inside a decision problem, and that "I'd rather not know" isn't obviously about that — but i'd like to know exactly where the silence starts. If you can, give me one sentence about the theorem I could safely print in a newspaper, and one tempting claim I should cut. Please don't just point me at the literature — I want the shape of the argument, not a reading list.
