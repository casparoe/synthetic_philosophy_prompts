I've been reading these blogs and forums for a long time, and I've picked up the habit of building debunking schemas: general recipes that take a family of concepts and return the verdict that the family is empty. They have served me well on libertarian free will and on the ontologically simple, deeply persisting self. Pointed at phenomenal concepts, my schema seems to hand me illusionism about consciousness, which is where I wanted to end up. The trouble is that one more turn of the same crank appears to consume illusionism along with every other error theory I hold, and I can't see which step to give up or what giving it up costs.

The schema. Call it GDS. For any concept C:

(a) C is introspectively sovereign: whether C applies, in one's own case, is settled entirely by how things seem to one. There is no further check, no independent route to the fact, nothing the seeming answers to.
(b) C is physically idle: the property C purports to pick out is not among the properties a completed physical and functional account of the subject would need to invoke.

If both hold, C is empty: nothing falls under it, and every judgment deploying C is false. Clause (a) is meant to isolate the cases where the seeming does all the epistemic work, so that once the seeming is reconstrued as an output of the system there is nothing left to make the claim true. Clause (b) is meant to isolate the cases where a physicalist has no independent use for the property and so loses nothing by dropping it.

The argument.

1. Phenomenal concepts — the concepts we deploy when we judge, on introspection, that our sensory states have properties of the sort realists call phenomenal — satisfy (a). Nothing beyond the seeming settles whether the judgment applies; that is the whole of the realist's evidence, and illusionists and realists agree about that much.
2. Under physicalism as I hold it, phenomenal concepts satisfy (b). A completed account of the subject in physical and functional terms would be complete without positing phenomenal characters; this is the premise on which the realist digs in, and I know it is the expensive one.
3. So by GDS phenomenal concepts are empty: no state has a phenomenal property, and every introspective ascription of one is false. That is illusionism — and, in the form illusionists actually defend, illusionism adds that the false ascriptions are the output of a systematic misrepresentation, i.e. that there is an illusion, which is what distinguishes the view from bare eliminativism and what earns it the right to explain the confidence with which the ascriptions are made.
4. Now ask whether the concept of a seeming itself satisfies the schema. Take seeming in the thick sense: there is something it is like for the subject; the subject undergoes an appearance with a character. Clause (a) holds trivially, since whether one is appeared to in some way is exactly what is settled by how things seem. Clause (b) holds by the reasoning of step 2: phenomenal character is precisely what the completed account was said to leave out.
5. So GDS returns the verdict that nothing thickly seems any way to anyone. There are no appearances with character.
6. But the illusionism of step 3 requires appearances with character: an illusion of phenomenal properties is a state in which it seems to the subject as if her states had them. If no such seeming exists, the illusion does not exist either, and step 3 collapses into eliminativism — the view illusionists in the Frankish mold define their position against, and a view with no account of why the ascriptions get made at all, let alone with the recalcitrant conviction they carry.
7. So the schema that was supposed to deliver illusionism refutes it, by illusionism's own method.
8. And it does not stop there. My compatibilism holds that the sense of libertarian freedom — the sense that I could have done otherwise in a way no causal story touches — is an illusion, and it earns its keep by explaining that sense. My reductionism about the self holds that the sense of a single simple subject is an illusion, and earns its keep the same way. If step 5 is right, neither has a datum: there is no such seeming to be debunked, only false judgments with no appearance behind them. A debunking with no datum is not a debunking but a denial, and here not even a statable one, since clause (a) of the schema that produced the result quantifies over seemings.

What I tried. Splinter seeming into a thick notion (something it is like) and a thin one (the subject's being in a state with a certain introspective content, with no phenomenality required), and read step 3's illusion thinly. Two things I can't settle. First, if the illusion is thin, I no longer see daylight between illusionism and eliminativism plus a story about how our introspective systems are wired to emit false phenomenal judgments; the word illusion looks like it is doing rhetorical work. Second, a thin illusion does not seem to explain the datum — the conviction, the felt self-evidence, the fact that the ascriptions regenerate in every generation of undergraduates and in illusionists themselves, which is roughly the so-called meta-problem. A thin state can be a false judgment, but I do not see how it can be the thing that makes it feel like something to be me. If the answer is that the datum too must be reconstrued thinly, then the hard problem was never the problem illusionists advertised themselves as solving, and illusionism begins to look like advice about how to talk.

A case that sharpens this. Imagine transparent introspectors: a community raised, or trained, or fitted from birth with a neurofeedback interface, so that they have no phenomenal concepts at all and introspect their sensory states only under functional and neural descriptions. GDS says their introspective judgments are true; illusionism agrees; and nothing in the world had to change for the error to disappear. So is illusionism a discovery about consciousness or a proposal for conceptual reform — an invitation to become a transparent introspector? If it is the latter, is the realist entitled to reply that we were never disagreeing about the world in the first place? And, relatedly, if we imagine a graded series from us to them, with each generation's introspective concepts a little thinner, at which point does the illusion stop, and what happens to a verdict that has no determinate stopping point?

Where I want to end up. Name the step you reject, or, if you think the chain is valid, the premise you would give up; explain why that step looked fine when I wrote it; and price each of the alternatives rather than just picking one. Concretely: is the thin reading of illusion a rescue or a capitulation? Does illusionism survive as anything more than eliminativism plus a story about introspective wiring? Does it survive as a substantive metaphysical thesis rather than a recommendation about concepts, given the transparent introspectors? And do my compatibilism and my reductionism about the self survive whatever you say about steps 5, 7 and 8? If I have to choose, I would rather keep illusionism as a substantive thesis about consciousness than keep the schema in its fully general form; but I would rather give up illusionism than accept that nothing ever seems any way to anyone, since on that verdict I cannot say what my own introspective reports — the evidence illusionism was built to explain — even are.
