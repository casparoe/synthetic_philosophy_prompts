I teach an upper-level seminar called Minds and Machines — philosophy and cognitive science majors, sixteen students, one eighty-minute session, everyone around a single long table. Next week we are doing Hofstadter's chapter on the location of meaning: the decomposition of a message into a frame message, an outer message, and an inner message; the question of whether meaning is intrinsic to the information-bearer or supplied by whatever receives it; the observation that there is no such thing as an uncoded message, only more and less familiar codes; and the genetic case that anchors all of it — a strand of DNA whose content is realized only inside a cell that already possesses the machinery to read it, machinery that the DNA itself in part specifies.

I do not want to lecture on any of this. I want an activity in which the students collide with the three layers before I name them: some of them should fail to see an object as a message at all; some should see that it is a message and be unable to read it; some should read it fluently and come out with the wrong content; and at least one group should discover that the object in front of them cannot by itself settle which reading is correct.

My working skeleton is a two-by-two: four groups, one and the same physical artifact — a punched paper strip, a knotted cord, a string of coloured beads, whatever you think is best, but it has to be buildable in an afternoon out of cheap materials — and envelopes that vary along two dimensions, whether the group is given a frame (something that makes it visible that the thing is a message, and what kind of thing) and whether it is given an outer message (a decoding key or convention). Only the condition with both recovers the intended content. I am not attached to this structure; if a single arc with staged reveals does the work better, argue for it and explain what the two-by-two gets wrong.

What I would like from you: the artifact and the exact contents of each condition; rules, roles, and at least one role that holds information the others lack, designed so that the asymmetry is a genuine constraint rather than a gotcha; a minute-by-minute timeline for the full eighty minutes including the moment when the groups compare results; and, most important, the underdetermination beat — I want two groups to finish with different, internally coherent contents extracted from the same object and to have to argue about which is right using only what is in the room. If a scoring scheme helps, propose one, though I suspect it does not here.

Then the debrief: map each thing that actually happened onto frame, outer, and inner, including why neither the frame nor the outer message can be carried inside the inner message, and draw the parallel to the cell reading DNA without turning it into a biology lesson. Tell me how to keep the three-layer vocabulary out of the room until the debrief; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — someone cracking the code in two minutes, someone treating the artifact as a puzzle rather than a message, the group with the confident wrong reading refusing to budge, the asymmetric role proving either inert or decisive; and be frank about which part of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and which I will have to say myself. My own worry is that the exercise either smuggles in or quietly falsifies the bootstrapping story, since my students arrive already knowing what a message is and what to do with one. Finally, give me a forty-minute fallback in case the discussion of the reading runs long.
