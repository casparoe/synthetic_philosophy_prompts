I am drafting a short position paper on distributive ethics, and before I build an argument on it I would like an audit of the package of commitments I have ended up with. Here is where I stand.

(1) The basic structure is prioritarian, but strictly as a claim about the moral weight of benefits: a given benefit matters more the worse off the person who receives it is. It is not a claim that any pattern of distribution, equality included, is intrinsically good or bad. I want the view to be welfarist, in the sense that only facts about how well people's lives go, and about how much better or worse they would go, do any of the work.

(2) I take the levelling-down objection to be decisive against any view on which equality is intrinsically valuable. A change that makes some people worse off and makes no one better off is not better in any respect, and the fact that it would leave people more equal is never a reason for it. Any acceptable distributive view has to be able to say this.

(3) How badly off someone is, for me, is a relational matter: what matters is where a person sits relative to the others in the relevant population, and I want the priority weights keyed to position in the distribution — the recipient's quantile in the distribution of lifetime well-being, for instance — rather than to any absolute level. A fixed absolute threshold strikes me as arbitrary in a way that a comparative one is not.

(4) The unit is the complete life rather than the time slice: a person's priority is fixed by how well their life as a whole goes, not by how badly off they happen to be at the moment a benefit arrives.

(5) On top of that I want a chronicity factor: how long someone has already spent badly off adds to the claim they have, over and above how badly off they are now. Two people at the same current level, one of whom has been there for twenty years and one for two, do not have the same claim on a benefit.

(6) Formally I want to stay close to the standard additive treatment: the value of an outcome is the sum of independently weighted benefits, with weights that are fixed and that do not depend on how many people there are or on how the rest of the distribution looks. I also want a total rather than an average view when people are added. But I take it to be unacceptable that a very large population of lives barely worth living should count as better than a small population of very good lives, and I had assumed that the diminishing weight on benefits to better-off people was what protected me here.

(7) A case verdict: between a policy that gives each of a very large number of comfortably-off people a very small gain and one that lifts a handful of people out of catastrophic deprivation, the second wins, and goes on winning however large the first group becomes and however small each individual gain is, provided each gain is real. No aggregation of trivial comforts to the comfortable should outweigh rescuing someone at the bottom.

(8) A second case verdict: in an emergency allocation, one bed and two patients, I would still give it to the patient in acute crisis now, even when the other patient's life as a whole has gone, and will go, considerably worse. This feels as though it brings back the time-slice notion of being badly off that (4) was supposed to exclude.

What I want is an audit rather than a survey. Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look as though they are; where an appearance of inconsistency depends on an ambiguity in how one item is spelled out, say which disambiguation dissolves it. For each real tension, say what has to give, what the cheapest repair is, and what that repair would cost me elsewhere in the list. Rank the problems by severity rather than presenting them as a flat list, and be specific about which single commitment is doing the damage in each case. I do not have a settled view about which item I would abandon, and finding out which one is load-bearing is most of what I want from this. If one revision would remove more than one of the problems at once, say so.
