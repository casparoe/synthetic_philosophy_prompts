I'm in a philosophy-of-science reading group — mostly public-policy grad students plus one retired agronomist who keeps dragging us away from toy examples and toward actual forecasts — and we've now spent three sessions on whether a failed long-run prediction can ever be legitimately called right but early rather than falsified, or vaguely worded, or degeneratively rescued. I keep rendering verdicts that I strongly suspect don't cohere, so I want to lay them out and ask for the principle, or the short list of candidate principles, that they add up to, and to be told plainly which of them has to go. My own tentative rule is something like: a long-run prediction earns the right but early verdict only if (i) it names a mechanism whose operation can be measured independently of the prediction itself, (ii) it states its closure conditions — what is being held fixed, what counts as outside the system — explicitly rather than after the fact, (iii) the delay is explained by a genuine change in something the model treated as exogenous, not by a repair to the mechanism, and (iv) the mechanism's distinctive signature shows up somewhere, at some scale, in some domain. But I don't trust that this returns my own verdicts, especially since it seems to treat two cases identically that I don't feel identically about.

The cases, with my gut verdicts. Yeast in a sealed flask of sugar water grows exponentially, overshoots, and crashes when the sugar is gone: mechanism confirmed, and I'm entirely comfortable saying this is the Malthusian structure instantiated at small scale, even though the ending is a crash rather than the grinding misery Malthus described. Malthus's own late-eighteenth-century argument that population presses against the means of subsistence and is held down by checks: I want to say right but early — real mechanism, industrialization and the Green Revolution postponed the bite — though I notice I can't say by when it should have bitten, and that troubles me. The standard run of the 1972 Club of Rome report, with its resource depletion and mid-to-late-century collapse: I want to say falsified in its trajectories, and I regard the later revivals that re-fit updated data as rescue rather than vindication, even though I concede it is structurally just Malthus with more variables. The famous decade-long wager over five commodity metal prices between an ecologist and an economist, which the ecologist lost as metals got cheaper: I count this as a real disconfirmation of that specific forecast but not of any general scarcity claim, on the grounds that a decade of prices in an economy with substitution is a bad proxy for long-run scarcity — which I realize is close to the move I refuse the Club of Rome. The peak-oil forecast that US production would crest near 1970: this is my most comfortable case, since there's a named mechanism, an explicit auxiliary about new provinces, and a signature that actually appeared. A fictional one I find myself drawn to: a 1930s agronomist, call him Verhoeven, predicting that staple-crop yields per hectare would plateau within a generation at a ceiling set by photosynthesis; yields kept climbing for half a century afterward, but independently derived modern estimates of the photosynthetic ceiling land near his number and yield growth in some staples has flattened — I lean toward right but early here, and I can't say why this differs from the Club of Rome case except that Verhoeven named a quantity that later got measured. Another fictional one: the Kestrel–Oyediran irrigation model, which predicted an agricultural region would collapse by 1990 as its aquifer emptied, and was wrong because of deeper drilling and imported grain; here I feel no pull at all toward right but early and want to say the closure assumption was simply false, even though an aquifer is about as finite as a jar of sugar water — so why does the jar get the mechanism and the aquifer not? A 1960s famine projection for South Asia that arguably triggered the agricultural investments that averted it: I want to call it successful rather than true, and I have no idea whether self-prevention should count as confirmation, disconfirmation, or neither. And a contemporary neo-Malthusian who says the limits will bite later this century, specifying no date, no measurable signature, and no closure conditions: I distrust this completely, while suspecting my verdict on Malthus licenses exactly this vagueness. My partner says the whole discussion is just the demarcation problem wearing a costume; I'd like to know how close that is.

So: give me a committed answer, not a survey. Take a definite position on which principle is correct, tell me which of my verdicts it overturns and why that one rather than the alternatives, identify the clause doing the most work and the case that motivates it, and apply the result to two cases I haven't settled — the widely repeated projection that antimicrobial resistance will cause on the order of ten million deaths a year by mid-century, and the claim that AI capability growth will hit a wall because high-quality training data is a finite stock, which feels structurally just like peak oil. Say plainly which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, flag any case where right but early is itself the wrong frame, and tell me how confident you are in the central verdict you're asking me to give up, plus what sort of evidence would move you off it.
