You are a decision scientist who advises research and development organizations on escalation of commitment, and you are fluent in the formal literature on the dollar auction and wars of attrition. Think carefully before you answer: begin by listing the ways the dollar-auction framing can misfire when it is imposed on a real organization, and only then build the procedure.

I chair the quarterly go/no-go review at a mid-sized biotech, roughly 200 people. We make six to ten continue/kill decisions per quarter in a single half-day, the CEO signs off, and the program leads in the room are the people who built the programs. The standard advice, that sunk costs are irrelevant and only forward-looking value counts, gets quoted and then quietly ignored, because two features of our situation make it look less like a bad investment and more like a dollar auction: the commercial prize is winner-take-most, so second place is worth very little, and much of what we have already spent is unrecoverable in a way that is mirrored on the other side, since the rival still in the race has burned comparable money that their exit would also strand. Our head of business development uses exactly this to argue the reverse of the standard conclusion: if a rival is still funding, that is evidence the asset is worth more than our own numbers say, so killing is the real mistake.

I need a reusable procedure, not a one-off opinion. Please give me, in order: a screening step that sorts each program into the structures that actually matter, meaning a genuine two-sided contest with an observable rival, an ordinary one-sided escalation whose only opponent is our own prior spend, and what I think of as the phantom bidder case, where a rival is invoked rhetorically but is not really still bidding or whose exit we cannot observe; the tests to run after screening, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and what to do when the number a test needs is simply unavailable, as it usually is; an explicit treatment of the persistence-as-evidence argument, covering when a rival's continued funding is information about the size of the prize, when it is only their own escalation, and how to tell the difference without access to their data; the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and a named person must decide, flagged honestly as judgment calls dressed up as procedure; and what the procedure cannot do. Then run it on three cases: a mid-stage asset whose rival just published strong data, a program whose rival left the field two years ago while our team still describes the space as a race, and one where I expect to dislike the answer, our flagship program, the one the company was founded on, now three years behind the leader. Answer in American English throughout.
