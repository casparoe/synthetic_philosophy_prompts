I'm in a small reading group — two anthropologists, an archaeologist, and me — working through the question of whether the shift to farming was a disaster for the people who lived through it, and I've ended up defending a position I don't actually want. We have already granted the empirical case on the affluence side: short working hours and varied diets among foragers, and then after the transition longer workdays, narrower and more famine-prone diets, and a skeletal record showing declining stature alongside rising dental caries and infectious disease. We have also granted the inequality side: stored surplus made wealth heritable and hierarchy durable in a way that foraging did not allow. Grant all of that and my friend's argument still seems to acquit agriculture entirely, which is why I think it proves too much. Here it is:

(1) A transformation is a mistake *for the people who live under it* only if it makes at least one of them worse off than they would otherwise have been: if nobody is left worse off, the outcome may still be regrettable in some impersonal way, but there is no one it was a mistake for. (2) Being made worse off than you would otherwise have been requires a counterpart: for each actual person there must be some person who would have existed had the transformation not occurred, and whose life would have gone better. (3) Farming altered settlement, birth spacing, and who encountered whom; because which child is conceived depends on chaotically sensitive chains, within a couple of generations of a local transition the population alive is disjoint from the one that would have been alive under continued foraging — call this the reshuffle threshold, and note that the people past it outnumber the people before it by orders of magnitude. (4) Past the reshuffle threshold there is no fact of the matter about which counterfactual person any actual person would have been, so no actual person has the counterpart that (2) demands; and because the counterfactual foraging branch would also have differed in size, even a collective comparison of the two populations has no canonical pairing — my one attempt to repair this by pairing people across branches by their rank in the welfare distribution produced a verdict that flipped sign when I varied the assumed population sizes. (5) So for everyone after the threshold, the transition was not a mistake *for them*, whatever it did to their health, their leisure, or their equality; the only verdict that survives is a verdict about the first generation or two, who did have counterparts. (6) Nothing here turns on farming specifically: any transformation whose demographic effects reach past its own reshuffle threshold — industrialization, urbanization, mass schooling, fertility collapse, forced resettlement — is likewise not a mistake for the people who lived under it. (7) So either the question "was it a mistake for the people who lived through it?" has an empty answer for every large-scale transformation, or (1) is false.

I want to reject that conclusion and I am willing to pay for it, but I would rather not pay by giving up (1), which reads to me as close to definitional, and I would rather not deny the reshuffle in (3), which the anthropologists say is just what the demographic models do. I keep circling back to the thought that the inequality half of our complaint survives the argument in a way the health-and-leisure half does not, since hierarchy is a relation among contemporaries rather than a counterfactual comparison, but the group is split on whether (1) governs relational complaints at all, and I cannot make the thought precise enough to settle it. Please tell me which step to reject, why that step looked sound when we wrote it down, and what rejecting it commits me to elsewhere; and if there is a scope shift or an equivocation hiding between two of the steps, name it rather than simply replacing my premises with better ones.

On confidence: I would like you to separate what is settled among people who actually work on these questions — population ethics, the non-identity problem, the archaeology of the transition — from what is a live controversy on which a reasonable specialist could go either way. I do not want a contested view handed to me as the consensus, and I would much rather be told "this is genuinely unresolved" than given a confident answer that papers over a fight.
