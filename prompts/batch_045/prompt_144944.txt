I run an undergraduate reading group on decision theory and I want a one-page printable instrument: a flowchart that starts from a single commitment a student is asked to grant, and sorts them by their answers into a named position on Newcomb's problem. The starting commitment is this: the Predictor has been correct in 99 per cent of recorded cases involving people the student regards as relevantly similar, and the student has no evidence of being one of the exceptions. Everything downstream of that should be the student's own answers about cases, never their choice of label.

Constraints on the instrument:

1. Branches are concrete cases, not theories. Every internal node states a variant of the puzzle in ordinary language and then asks a question of the form What do you take?, Does your answer change if...?, or Would you pay...? No node may ask which theory the student accepts, name a theory, or use technical vocabulary (causal expected utility, screening off, subjunctive dependence, decision procedure) inside the question. A student who has only ever heard the bare puzzle should be able to answer every node without knowing where it leads.

2. Cases. Build the tree out of the space of variants below. Use at least the five marked required, add at most three of your own, eight nodes in total:
   - Required, baseline: opaque boxes, one thousand dollars visible, one million or nothing in the other, no mechanism of prediction specified.
   - Required, the Bypass Predictor: accuracy is achieved through a neurological marker present from birth that causes both the way the boxes are filled and, by producing a compulsion, the choice you end up making; the Predictor reads the marker and never models your reasoning.
   - Required, the Feedback Machine: nothing is predicted; the contents of the opaque box are produced by a device causally downstream of the choice you are about to make.
   - Required, the Open Box: you see the contents before you choose, and the Predictor foresaw that you would see them.
   - Required, the Register: you learn that the ninety-nine per cent figure was computed from a record from which unsuccessful predictions had been quietly dropped, so that the true rate is unknown to you.
   - Optional, the Self-Modification option: for a small fee, paid before the prediction, you may have your own reasoning replaced by a procedure that reliably takes one box, and the Predictor predicts whatever you will then be like.
   - Optional, the Vault: accuracy is achieved by running a complete physical simulation of you up to the moment of choice, filling the box by its outcome, and erasing it afterwards.
   Where the right verdict for a named theory is itself contested, mark the node as contested in a note rather than deciding it for the theories.

3. Leaves. At least six, including each of the following, described by its behaviour across the cases rather than by its name: (i) take both boxes, because the contents are already fixed; (ii) take one box wherever taking it is strong evidence about the contents, whatever the mechanism; (iii) take one box only where the contents depend on what your own reasoning would deliver, and both where the link is merely statistical; (iv) take both boxes when it comes to the act, while holding that there is a standing requirement to arrange beforehand not to be the sort of person who does; (v) hold that the stipulations underdetermine the answer, so no verdict is available until further questions about time, determinism or the Predictor's powers are settled; (vi) at least one leaf you label unnamed. Give each leaf its usual name in the literature where a stable one exists, and write unnamed where none does.

4. Locate, do not merely list. Before or beside the tree, set out the dimensions along which your cases vary and the combinations of answers those dimensions permit; then say where each leaf sits in that space; then give at least two combinations that no position occupies, with a one-line diagnosis of each: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

5. Verification. Add a table with a row per leaf and a column per case, each entry the verdict. Then list every pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, together with the minimal extra case or question that would separate them. If two named theories agree across every case you use, say that plainly instead of manufacturing a distinction to house them.

Assume the reader has no library access: no citations, no bibliography, no reading list, no further reading. I want the apparatus itself, drawn from the cases, on one side of A4.
