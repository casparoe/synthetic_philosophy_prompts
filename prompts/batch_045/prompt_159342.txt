ok so this is a bit of a weird ask but here goes. I'm in a reading group that's been chewing on scientific realism for about a year and a half and we have totally stalled, and I want a diagnosis, not a verdict on who's right.

The loop we're stuck in: realists run the no-miracles argument — novel predictive success would be miraculous if theories weren't at least approximately true. Anti-realists run the pessimistic meta-induction over lists of theories that were wildly successful in their day and are now regarded as false, with central terms that referred to nothing at all. Realists come back with structural realism, or with the keep-the-parts-that-did-the-work-and-drop-the-idle-parts move, plus the complaint that some of those historical cases are cherry-picked. Anti-realists come back with: fine, but nobody could tell in advance which parts were the working ones, and there were always unconceived alternatives nobody had thought of yet. Then we loop. Nobody has moved, and it's not that anyone is being obtuse — every move on both sides seems reasonable in isolation.

My hypothesis, which I floated last session and got groaned at, is that the whole thing is secretly a fight about a base rate nobody can measure. The no-miracles argument only has bite if it's antecedently improbable that a false theory would land novel predictive successes; on this reading the pessimistic induction is just our one empirical handle on that probability, and it's a sample of maybe a dozen contested cases, selected by people with an axe to grind. So we're not really disagreeing about whether electrons exist or about how theoretical terms get their reference — we're disagreeing about the shape of the space of possible theories, i.e. about how often false-but-successful ones should be expected, and both sides are reading their answer off the same tiny biased sample.

Is that a real diagnosis or just a relabeling? Three things I want:

1. Is it a genuine hidden empirical question, or is it verbal — the same stalemate redescribed in base-rate vocabulary, so that nothing is gained?
2. Does it blur two targets that ought to be kept apart: whether our theoretical terms latch onto anything, versus whether our method for choosing theories is reliable? Those look like they'd need different evidence, and I suspect the keep-the-working-parts reply only speaks to the first.
3. The objection I actually got: we can't enumerate the space of possible theories, so this is a priori speculation in empiricial costume. Is that fatal?

And the bit I care most about: given your diagnosis, what would count as progress? My own candidate is that we stop fighting over the dead-theory lists and instead go look for cases where a theory had genuine novel predictive success, was later abandoned, and where we now independently know what the truth turned out to be — so we can check, case by case, whether the machinery that did the predicting survived, got deformed, or was junked. Different evidence base (nineteenth and twentieth century physics and chemistry, maybe model-building in the special sciences), and it could come out either way. Is that the right shape of thing to do, or is it just better bookkeeping on the same biased sample? And if it's hopeless, say so and tell me honestly what the right thing to do with this question is.
