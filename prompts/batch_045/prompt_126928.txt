I convene a small working group on explanation in the human sciences, and it has split into two camps that have spent three seminars failing to engage each other. Camp A — call them the meaning-realists — holds that an agent's action is individuated by the meaning it carries for that agent, that a socially transmitted system of such meanings can be a genuine cause of large economic outcomes, and that Weber's Protestant-ethic thesis states a real finding: a religious reinterpretation of ordinary work as a divinely assigned task redirected economic conduct in directions the material interests of the same agents did not already determine. Camp B — call them the institutionalists — grants the correlation between confession and economic performance but holds that the causal work is done by literacy, legal form, network position, and incentive structure, and that culture is the label we attach to the residue of a causal story we have not finished telling; on their reading the ascetic values are expressive or legitimating of an emerging commercial order rather than productive of it. What I want from you is the document the two camps should have written instead of the seminar: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration.

Stipulate for both sides that neither denies the bare correlation in some early modern European data, that neither holds a crude geographic or racial determinism, that both accept that any causal claim here must be contrastive, and that both accept that Weber himself claimed to be examining one direction of a two-way causal relation rather than denying the other. Everything past that is up for negotiation.

I want the drafting to do real work, so please observe the following. First, the agreed claims must be substantive: a hostile third scholar should be able to imagine denying each one. Second, each side's own formulation of its position must be one that side would actually sign, not a paraphrase the other side would prefer; keep the neutral restatement in a separate column rather than substituting it for either. Third, I suspect part of this dispute is verbal — about the extension of culture, about whether cause means difference-making or productive mechanism or intelligibility-conferring, about whether an explanation can be adequate at the level of meaning while remaining causally unevaluated. Say which parts are verbal, and say what, if anything, survives once the vocabulary is disambiguated. Where a disagreement is genuine, do not soften it into a both-sides-have-a-point.

For the cruxes, record in advance what would settle each, using these stipulated cases; you may discard a case if you explain why it bears on nothing, and you may add a better one.

1. The matched pair. Two neighbouring districts share language, soil, market access, crop mix, and, after a single ruler's administrative reform, an identical legal code; marriage-register signatures show comparable literacy. The ruler then assigns one district to a Reformed confession and leaves the other Catholic, for reasons of dynastic marriage rather than economics. Within three generations the Reformed district shows more artisanal enterprise and markedly lower festive-day consumption. Variant: a second pair in which the confessional assignment was imposed and then reversed twice.

2. The secularization lag. In a Reformed region, church attendance and doctrinal belief collapse within one generation while the work-and-saving differentials persist for a hundred years and only dissolve when the local industry itself disappears.

3. The sect substitution. The same economic differential, of roughly the same size, appears among doctrinally unrelated commercial minorities in the same period — disciplined sects, endogamous merchant networks, and ritual communities that share no doctrine of predestination and no theology of the calling, but do share internal discipline, mutual monitoring, and exclusion from guild and office.

4. The origin probe. In a region where the Reformed confession spread earliest and most completely, the towns that adopted it first were also the towns that were already most commercialised, and the same confession failed to take hold in otherwise similar pastoral districts.

For each case, say why both sides would grant that it is relevant to something, what each predicts, and what each would concede if the result went against it. Among the cruxes I expect you to place: whether a value must be avowable by the agent to do causal work, and what follows when agents systematically violate the value they avow; whether a thesis whose central kind is constructed by the inquirer's own value-relevance can be tested or only applied; and whether a culture that cannot explain its own origin can explain anything else.

Format the document as four sections: a numbered agreed-claims section; a disagreements table with columns for the crux, Camp A's own wording, Camp B's own wording, a neutral restatement, and whether the disagreement is verbal or genuine; a tests table with columns for the case, the crux it bears on, why both grant its relevance, each side's advance prediction, and each side's defeat condition; and a short closing section on what this collaboration could not settle even in principle. Include at least one claim in the agreed section that each side accepts but finds uncomfortable. No citations, no bibliography, and no reliance on the actual empirical literature: the cases are stipulated, and the argument should stand without them. Under 1,600 words if you can manage it.
