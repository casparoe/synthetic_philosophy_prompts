I write about science and ideas for a newspaper, and over the past few years I have covered a run of institutional decisions in which the disagreement was not about the facts, nor about whose interests were at stake, but about which moral framework should govern the choice: a foundation redrawing its grant strategy, a research ethics committee, a university deciding whether to keep a donor-funded centre. In each case the participants, being reasonable people, tried to handle that disagreement by some aggregating procedure — give each framework its due weight, find the option that does best overall, settle on something everyone can live with. And in each case the procedure seemed to me to presuppose exactly what was in dispute. I would like to know whether this has been identified and named, because it keeps recurring and I have no word for it.

Two features, as I see them. First, the menu of options is not neutral. The frameworks disagree not only about which option is best but about how the option space is carved up, about what counts as one option at all. The hedge — split the money, publish the redacted version, run the smaller trial — gets presented as the compromise both sides can accept, but on at least one of the frameworks it is not a smaller dose of the good thing; it is a forbidden thing. So the option that looks like the neutral middle is second-worst on every framework and ruled out flatly by one of them. Second, the weights are not neutral either. To aggregate you need each framework's verdict expressed on a common scale, and the frameworks measure different things — deaths averted, stringency of a duty, expected size of the future — with no exchange rate supplied by any of them. Any conversion you choose is itself a moral judgement that one of the frameworks would reject, and it can reverse the outcome: scale one way and the framework nobody much believes, the one that assigns enormous stakes to the matter at hand, dictates every decision; scale another way and it drops out entirely.

An instance of each. A foundation I wrote about was split between a view on which it must direct its money to the worst-off people alive now and a view on which it must reduce risks to future generations. The first treats spending on the far future while children die of preventable causes as itself a violation rather than a missed benefit; the second treats any dollar that does not go to the future as a catastrophic waste. Splitting the portfolio, which is what they did, was called betrayal by one side and dilution by the other — it was nobody's second choice, which is what a compromise is supposed to be. Separately: a committee weighing whether to fund a line of work was told by one member that a large class of beings the others had never counted has interests that matter enormously. The committee gave that view perhaps five per cent. It then swamped the decision, not because anyone was persuaded of it, but because on the only scale on which the committee knew how to compare the options, that view's numbers were astronomical — and whether that result is a finding or an artefact of the scale seemed to depend on a choice nobody had noticed making.

The wrinkle I care about most is that this repeats one level up. Some of the frameworks in play have views about how this very kind of disagreement should be handled: one says that in doubt you should do what the framework you find most plausible says, another says you should aggregate, a third says that aggregating across frameworks is a category error about what moral reasons are. So the choice of procedure is not external to the dispute; it is one more item the frameworks disagree about — and it is suspicious that the frameworks with an opinion on the matter tend to have a favourable one.

I have been offered several labels and none seems right. Incommensurability and value pluralism seem to concern conflicts among values inside a single framework, not conflicts among frameworks about the inputs to a decision. Hedging and splitting the difference assume the menu and the scale are already given. The precautionary principle, appeals to the burden of proof, and worries about low probabilities with huge payoffs all concern how much weight to give a possibility, not whether the framing of the choice is itself contested. Aversion to unspecified probabilities concerns ordinary empirical uncertainty, where nobody thinks the correct response to doubt is itself up for moral argument.

So my question is whether the phenomenon has an established name and a literature. If there is one, I want it placed: what exactly is the phenomenon at its core, what are its nearest neighbours in the way I have sketched, and precisely where does it stop — what would count as a case where the framing is genuinely neutral and aggregation is unproblematic? If there is no established concept, please say so plainly, coin a name, define it tightly, and give me boundary cases, including at least one case that looks like mine but is not. Finally, and most usefully for the piece I am writing: is there any procedure that is immune to it? I have the embryonic idea that the only defensible recommendations would be the ones that survive every admissible way of carving up the options and every admissible way of putting the frameworks on a common scale, and I would like to know whether that idea has been developed, whether it has a name, and how much of the decision space it typically leaves unsettled.
