I'm a journalist — I mostly cover science and ideas — and I'm midway through a long feature on prediction (working title is something like "The Two-Week Wall", not final). My editor made me write down, on one page, what I actually think about all this before I start calling other people confused, and now that it's on one page I'm worried it doesnt hang together. I don't want a lecture on chaos theory, I want an audit. Here's the list, roughly in the order I wrote it:

1. At the scale of weather and climate, the dynamics are deterministic. Quantum randomness is real but it averages out long before it reaches anything I write about, so for my purposes the atmosphere is a clockwork thing with one future.
2. Chaos means sensitive dependence on initial conditions, and the roughly two-week limit on useful weather forecasts is a hard limit: it's a feature of the system, not a measure of how ignorant we happen to be. Better models and denser observations buy you days, not decades.
3. I nevertheless trust century-scale climate projections, and I think my readers should too. People keep telling me this fights with 2; I don't see it.
4. Probabilities in forecasting are statements about our information, not about physical chances out there in the atmosphere. A 30 percent chance of rain is a credence, full stop.
5. The right way to judge a model is whether it reproduces the right long-run statistics — the climatology, the attractor, the distribution of outcomes — not whether its trajectory tracks what actually happened. Losing the path is fine if you keep the statistics.
6. And yet I do think it's fair to blame an operational forecast center when it misses the one event everybody cared about. Say a shop puts out a seasonal outlook that is, statistically, unimpeachable — perfectly calibrated, exactly the right spread — and still completely whiffs on the single landfalling storm that floods a city. I want to say they blew it.
7. Related but seperate: I've become convinced that the honest test of a model is what I've started calling the doubling-time audit. Take the historical record, initialize the model on past states, and ask whether errors in the model grow at the same rate, and for the same dynamical reasons, as errors in the real system grow — not "did it get the climatology right" and not "did it call the event". I think this is stricter than 5 and more honest than 6, and I'm completely untroubled that a model can pass it and still be useless for the storm that actually arrives.

So: which of these really fight with each other, which only look like they do, and which pairs are fine and I should stop worrying about? Please rank the real tensions by severity rather than handing me seven equal-sized problems, and for each one say which commitment has to bend and what bending it costs me — I'd much rather give up something small than rebuild the whole page. And if there's a single revision that cleans up the most, tell me which one.

My own guess is that the trouble is in 1 and 2, but I've been wrong about this stuff before, so don't take my word for where it is.

One constraint: please don't fix this by pointing me at papers. I can't chase a citation in the middle of a draft and my readers certainly can't — argue it out in plain terms.
