I am a type designer, and I am working on a generative project in the spirit of Letter Spirit: a program that, given a handful of seed glyphs, extrapolates an entire alphabet in one coherent style. The difficulty is that I need a stopping condition. Left to itself the generator will wander indefinitely, and at some point I want both it and my own eye to be able to say: no, that is no longer an 'a'. So I have been collecting my own verdicts on particular glyphs, going by gut alone, and I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

My cases, all concerning the Latin lowercase 'a' unless I say otherwise:

1. A double-storey 'a' — bowl plus hook — and a single-storey 'a' — circle plus stem. Both are 'a', equally, with no hesitation on either.
2. The single-storey shape scaled up and given the proportions of a capital. That is an 'A', not an 'a' — and yet I also want to say that it is the same letter of the alphabet, which I cannot state without contradicting myself somewhere.
3. A young child's 'a': bowl too large, stem too short, baseline wandering, drawn before she could make the shape twice in the same way. Still an 'a', only a poor one.
4. The 'a' of a blackletter face, which I cannot read at all, though readers of that face read it instantly. My verdict: it is an 'a'. My inability to read it is not evidence against it.
5. The 'a' of a recent display face, which I also cannot read, where nothing in the design suggests to me that an 'a' was meant beyond the designer's own labelling, and where nobody reads it as an 'a' unaided. My verdict: not an 'a'; a shape that has been given the wrong name. I am aware that 4 and 5 sit badly together, and that is deliberate.
6. A snowflake icon in a symbol font, drawn exactly like the 'a' of the text font in the same family. My verdict: not an 'a'. It is a snowflake that happens to look like one.
7. A Cyrillic 'а' in a face where it is visually indistinguishable from the Latin 'a'. My verdict: not a Latin 'a', even though no one looking at it could tell the difference.
8. My grandmother's handwritten 'a', shaped exactly like everyone else's 'u'; in her hand, systematically, that is the shape she writes for 'a'. My verdict: she has written an 'a' there, although a careless reader would take it for a 'u'.
9. Someone who has never been taught the alphabet copies the shape of an 'a' accurately from a photograph, having no idea that it is a letter or what it sounds like. My verdict: I want to say no — she has drawn the shape of an 'a' but not an 'a' — but I cannot square that with 3, and it makes me doubt 8 as well.
10. A bowl with its counter filled in solid: a blob. My verdict: not an 'a'. Though where the fill is a printing artefact at small sizes, I would call it a badly printed 'a'.
11. The ligature 'æ'. My verdict: not an 'a'. It is a letter in its own right in Danish and a ligature elsewhere, but in neither case an 'a', despite visibly containing one.
12. A glyph produced by my generator at its wildest setting. In isolation I read it as a '2' or a 'z'; set into the word "cat" in its own alphabet I read it as 'a' immediately, without effort, and I read the word without a stumble. My verdict: I have changed my mind four times about this one.

My own tentative principle: a glyph is an 'a' if and only if (i) it was produced by a process whose function is to produce 'a's — the writer's, designer's or generator's having that letter in mind — and (ii) a competent reader of the script and style in question can distinguish it, in context, from every other letter of the alphabet to which it actually belongs. Condition (i) is meant to handle 6, 7 and 9; condition (ii) to handle 4, 10 and 12, and to explain why I do not require that a glyph resemble a prototypical 'a'.

What I want is for that principle to be tested against my twelve verdicts and then repaired or replaced. Specifically:

- A statement of the principle, or of two or three competing candidates — an essentialist one about bowls, stems and topology; a prototype or family-resemblance one; a purely differential one on which an 'a' is whatever contrasts with the rest of its alphabet; a causal or intentional one — precise enough that I could implement it, with each clause tied to the case that forces it.
- An honest account of which of my verdicts no single principle can retain. I suspect that 5 and 6 cannot both survive, or else 3 and 9, and I would rather be told which to abandon than be handed a principle with an epicycle per case.
- What the principle predicts about cases I have not listed: at minimum, a glyph in an alphabet where 'a' and 'o' are distinguished by nothing but a one-pixel spur; a hand in which 'a' and 'o' are genuinely indistinguishable even to the writer; and a font in which every letter is assembled from the same small stock of parts, so that no glyph is recognisable out of the company of the others.
- Whether any of this generalises. Is the 'a' problem simply the chair problem under another name, or does letterform have a feature — membership of a finite, closed, conventional system of contrasts — that makes a differential account more plausible here than for natural-kind or artefact categories?
