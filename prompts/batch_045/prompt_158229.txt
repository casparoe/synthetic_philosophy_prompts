I'm a novelist, and my fourth book — finished, currently in copy-edit — is about a woman who is given a psychiatric diagnosis at seventeen and spends the next twenty years in its light. Structurally, the chapters after the diagnosis re-read the chapters before it: the same childhood episodes are described twice, first as temperament and then as prodrome. I've started calling this "narrative pre-emption" — the diagnosis arrives and organises retroactively the very material it was supposed merely to describe. I'm about to write an afterword, and to sit on a panel with a psychiatrist and a philosopher of science, and I want my own commitments in order before I do. I'd rather be contradicted now than on stage.

So here, in my own words, is what I believe about psychiatric classification. I'm aware that several of these have famous names attached; I don't care who gets the credit, and I'd rather you didn't explain the background to me.

1. Moving-target kinds. A diagnostic category is not a natural kind with fixed boundaries that we slowly discover. It is the sort of kind whose members are altered by being classified: people come to experience, present, and deliberate about themselves in the terms the classification supplies, and the category is subsequently revised to fit what it has produced. I hold this strongly.

2. Real dysfunction underneath some of them. I don't accept the all-the-way-down version of (1). I think at least some diagnoses pick out a genuine failure of a mechanism to do what it was selected to do — a failure that would be there, and would be costly, in a world with no psychiatry and no vocabulary for it. So I want a dysfunction requirement of roughly the "harmful dysfunction" shape, and I want that failure to be what makes at least some diagnoses apt.

3. Second-order looping (my own coinage). Once the looping effect is public knowledge — and for readers of my sort of book it now is — people stop being merely the objects of classification and start steering. They seek a diagnosis because it unlocks accommodation, community, or simply an intelligible story, and having sought it they come to have the experiences it predicts. I hold that experiences arrived at this way are not second-class: strategic uptake does not make an experience less real, and the self-description that results is not a fraud.

4. Mechanism is the humane framing. When a clinician explains a condition to a patient, the more the explanation is couched in mechanism — brain, chemistry, circuitry — the better. It relocates the trouble from character to machinery and thereby reduces blame. I hold this as a moral claim about clinical practice, not merely as a claim about what is true.

5. First-person authority. A patient's own account of what is happening to them should be treated as authoritative, and a diagnosis the patient refuses should not be imposed. In my book this is exactly what the psychiatrist fails at.

6. Historical mortality. Some diagnoses have had careers and died — fugue states, nervous exhaustion, and the rest — and I take their disappearance to tell us something about the category, not only about the patients who once filled it.

7. Fiction is part of the mechanism. Novels, memoirs and their adaptations are among the channels through which a diagnostic kind becomes available to the people who might come to occupy it; I therefore have some responsibility for the kinds my book makes available. At the same time I insist, flatly, that my protagonist has the condition in the ordinary realist sense: she discovers something about herself. She does not acquire a costume.

8. Keep the manual anyway. We should retain a common diagnostic manual and its codes, and continue to gate access to care and to benefits through them, while continuing to say, as in (1), that these are not natural kinds.

What I'd like from you is an audit rather than a survey. Go through the eight and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly; which pairs only look tense and dissolve once you're careful about what each claim is about; and, for each real tension, what would have to give and what giving it would cost me. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I'm attached to all eight and I'd rather you told me which is weakest than that I guessed in advance, so please don't assume I'm expecting any particular one to fall. Please also say explicitly whether the architecture of my novel — narrative pre-emption, and the arc in which my protagonist is meant to be discovering rather than acquiring — survives the most consistent subset you can find, or whether the book is quietly relying on a confusion.

Two further things. First, be epistemically honest: mark how confident you are in each verdict, say where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and tell me what would change your mind — if any of your verdicts leans on an empirical claim about what mechanism-talk does to blame and stigma, or about how stable any actual diagnosis is, say so plainly, name the claim without citing studies, and tell me which finding, if it went the other way, would flip you. I have no access to journals and don't want a reading list. Second, please write in British English, and please don't open with a primer on looping effects or on the history of the manual; assume I've read the obvious things.
