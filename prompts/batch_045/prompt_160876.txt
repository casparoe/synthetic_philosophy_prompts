You are a professional philosopher who specializes in social ontology and the foundations of game theory, and who has a working command of David Lewis's account of convention: the analysis on which a regularity R in the behavior of members of a population P, in a recurrent situation S, counts as a convention only if it is common knowledge among them that almost everyone conforms to R, that almost everyone expects almost everyone else to conform, that almost everyone prefers that any one further person conform given that the others do, and that some alternative regularity R* would have served the same coordinating purpose nearly as well — so that R is arbitrary among eligible equilibria, not uniquely rationally mandated.

I am a retired condensed-matter physicist — thirty-eight years, most of it on nucleation and metastability — and I have lately been auditing a graduate seminar in social philosophy. I would like you to take the question below seriously rather than dialectically. Please think carefully before committing yourself: in a first pass, brainstorm for a few paragraphs — list the sub-questions you think the answer genuinely depends on, note any concrete case that pulls you in one direction, and mark where your own grip on Lewis's conditions is weakest. The claim I want a credence for is this:

When a convention R in a population P is replaced by a different convention R* governing the same recurrent situation, there is necessarily an intermediate interval during which P's behavior in that situation is governed by no convention at all — R has ceased to satisfy the conditions while R* has not yet come to satisfy them. On this claim convention change is like a first-order phase transition: it must pass through a disordered stage, seeded by a critical nucleus of defectors who make the old expectations false and so unravel the common knowledge that held them in place.

The rival possibilities I can see are, first, *creep*: a chain R = R₀, R₁, …, Rₙ = R* in which every stage is itself fully conventional, each step is small enough that nobody's expectations are seriously violated at any single stage, and the population therefore slides from one equilibrium to another without ever being in disequilibrium. Second, *coexistence and migration*: R and R* are each conventional in partially overlapping sub-populations, and the balance of adherence shifts smoothly from one to the other, with no stage at which the situation as a whole is conventionless.

Give me your credence in that claim as a single number to two significant figures, but give me the derivation with it. Decompose the question into the sub-questions it actually turns on and assign each a rough probability with one line of justification — I would expect terms for whether arbitrarily small steps between neighboring coordination equilibria are themselves available in the relevant situations, whether the coordination problem at each such step is any easier than the original one once the salience that originally solved it has faded, whether the common-knowledge condition can degrade gradually or only collapses all at once, and whether the sub-population route really escapes the claim rather than merely relocating it. Show how the terms combine, tell me which one your final number is most sensitive to, and tell me which one you are least confident about.
