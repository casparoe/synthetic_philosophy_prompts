I am a high school senior doing an independent reading project on predictive processing and the free energy principle, and there is one objection I have not been able to put to rest. I would like a careful explanation of whether it actually goes through. My math stops at precalculus, so I am asking for an explanation at the level of what the quantities mean and how they relate, not at the level of derivations.

Here is roughly what I think I understand. A brain like mine is treated as carrying a hierarchical generative model of the causes of its sensory input. Perception is inference: top-down predictions meet a bottom-up sensory signal, and what gets revised is whichever side of that exchange the system treats as the more reliable, where reliability is encoded as precision, in the sense of inverse variance. Attention is precision-weighting, turning the gain up on some prediction errors and down on others. Action is the second way of closing the gap: instead of changing the model to fit the signal, change the signal to fit the model. And all of this is supposed to follow from one imperative, keeping sensory surprise low, or rather keeping an upper bound on surprise low, since the real quantity is not directly computable.

The familiar version of my worry is the dark room objection: if the imperative is to keep surprise low, why does an organism not simply curl up somewhere dark, quiet, and unchanging forever? The replies I keep finding are that organisms carry priors, set by evolution, that treat the states a permanently curled-up organism would end up in as extremely surprising, and that for action the relevant quantity is not surprise about the present but expected free energy under a policy, which includes something like a term for how much a course of action is expected to teach you. Those replies seem to work, but they seem to me to leave my actual problem untouched, so let me ask the version I care about instead.

My version is about precision rather than about behavior. Precision is not handed to the system by the world; it is something the system sets, and it is set in a way that is cheap, fast, and entirely internal. So consider a system that holds its model of the world fixed, gives up on acting to confirm it, and instead handles every mismatch by declaring the sensory channel untrustworthy, turning the precision of the incoming error signal down toward zero so that no prediction error ever carries enough weight to force an update. Nothing about the organism's situation has to change for this to work; the trick is pulled from the inside. The bound on surprise stays low, the model is never embarrassed by evidence, and the cost is only whatever it costs to keep the gain low. If this is genuinely available, then the imperative to minimize free energy has a degenerate solution that no actual organism exploits, and I cannot see what in the framework rules it out.

So my questions:

1. Is the quantity being minimized in perception the same quantity being minimized in action and in attention? If attention sets precision, and precision decides which errors count, then attention looks like a control variable sitting inside the very minimization it is supposed to serve. How should I think about a system that can adjust the weights of its own objective?

2. Is there an in-principle reason, internal to the framework, why a system would not collapse into this self-blinding solution, or does ruling it out require an empirical claim about the priors and hyperpriors evolution happened to give us? If the latter, how much of the framework's explanatory work is really being done by that empirical claim rather than by the principle itself?

3. Where exactly does expected free energy fit? If the answer to the dark room problem requires adding a term for expected information gain or for keeping one's options open, that looks like an extra commitment beyond surprise minimization. Is it an addition, a consequence, or a restatement in different words?

4. If my version of the objection bites, does it tell us anything about whether predictive processing is a substantive empirical hypothesis about the brain or an extremely general way of rewriting any adaptive system in informational vocabulary? I know that question is far bigger than my project, so please keep the answer to what follows specifically from the precision issue.

I have read enough popular presentations to recognize the standard moves, so please do not spend time restating them. I would rather know where my version is genuinely different from the dark room problem and where it quietly collapses back into it. And if I have misused precision or mixed up levels of the hierarchy, say so directly, because I would rather have the terms corrected than have the answer tailored to my mistake.
