I help run a sci-fi-and-philosophy film night at the public library — volunteer thing, mostly retirees plus a handful of undergrads who show up for the free popcorn — and we're doing Arrival next month, the one with the alien ships and the linguist, based on Ted Chiang's story. I want to use it to get at the block universe and the Rietdijk–Putnam style move: relativity of simultaneity, so what's "now" depends on your frame, so allegedly future events are every bit as real as the chair I'm sitting on. But I definately don't want to stand up there and say something a physicist in row three would wince at.

Here's where I'm stuck. A friend who actually does relativity told me the whole argument only gets you the spacelike-separated cases — the Andromeda-paradox business, things far enough away that some frame's simultaneity slice catches them alongside my present. Anything in my own future light cone is later than me in every frame, so the argument never touches it. But the case the film cares about — Louise's daughter, the life and the illness and the death she walks into anyway — is inside her own future light cone. So if my friend is right, the physics people wheel out for the block universe doesn't cover the thing the film is actually about, and its fatalism has to come from somewhere else. Is that right? And does the argument have a way of generalising past the spacelike case, chaining observers or whatever the trick is, or is it genuinely that narrow?

Then there's a gap I don't have a word for, which I've been calling the availability gap in my notes. Grant the block universe entirely, every event equally real. That still doesn't look like it explains how anybody gets *access* to a future event, and the film needs access, not just reality: Louise has to learn the phone call, the General's wife's dying words, the whole shape of a life she hasn't lived. Co-reality isn't a channel. In the film the channel is the language rewiring how she thinks, which is a story about her cognition and not a fact about time at all. So which mechanism is really doing the work — the spacetime stuff or the Sapir-Whorf stuff — and can the film have both without cheating? Do the two even pull in the same direction?

Last bit, the part the group will actually argue about. Is Louise's choice to have her daughter, knowing, meant as triumph or tragedy, and does the film think there's a difference? The standard move — knowing what will happen isn't being forced — seems to me to undersell what it's doing, because her foreknowledge isn't just compatible with the choice, it's part of the reason for it; she chooses the life *because* of how it ends. That's either compatibilism in a nicer hat or a claim I've never seen argued for. And the aliens asking for help three thousand years out runs the same problem from the other side: if they already see the whole thing, why do they need her to do anything?

What I'd like: which two or three scenes you'd put on the screen for each of these, honestly where the reading breaks down and the film is just doing movie things (I'd rather say "this bit is metaphysics-free" than fake it), and what, if anything, the film shows about living under a block universe that the arguments and papers don't.
