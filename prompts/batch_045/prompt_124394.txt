I'm a retired physical chemist — reaction kinetics, mostly — and I've been pottering around in decision theory since I gave up the lab. The argument I keep running into is the money pump one: if your preferences are cyclic, there's a loop of options where you'd pay a little at each step to move A→B→C→A, you wind up back where you started minus cash, so you must be irrational.

What I can't shake is a resolution worry from my old working life: an effect can be perfectly real in the equations and still be uncollectable, because exploiting it needs a probe finer than the instrument allows. So give me one concrete example — over money or over lotteries, your call — where the incoherence is genuinely there (cyclic preferences, or a failure of independence, whatever's cleanest) but the usual pump can't actually be run: put the agent in front of a finite menu of options, give it a memory of its own transaction history and a smallest stake anyone is willing to offer, so it gets cycled only a limited number of times and the total loss is capped at something trivial. Then tell me honestly what, if anything, is still wrong with that agent. And if the real answer is that the argument was never about getting exploited but about ending up with something you like less than another option you could have had, show me that landing in the same example. One worked case, please, not a tour of the theorems.
