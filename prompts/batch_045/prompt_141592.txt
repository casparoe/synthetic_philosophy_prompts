I run incident review for a mid-sized payments platform, and two of my strongest engineers have spent roughly a year talking past each other about what our postmortem's "root cause" and "contributing factors" fields actually mean. I have to ship an incident document either way, so I want to force the issue as an adversarial collaboration with a written deliverable rather than another meeting. I'd like you to draft the joint statement they could both sign.

I have no philosophy training. As I understand it, the two positions are usually called the interventionist (or manipulability) account and the counterfactual-dependence account — I think the surnames Woodward and Lewis get attached to them, but please don't do exegesis of either author and don't treat their published formulations as binding. Roughly, in my own words:

- The manipulability camp: c causes e when, holding other things suitably fixed, changing c in the right kind of way changes e. Causes are the knobs that do something.
- The counterfactual camp: c causes e when, had c not happened, e would not have happened (possibly via a chain of such dependencies).

Treat the following case as fully stipulated: don't re-litigate the facts, don't redescribe them, and answer within them. "INC-1129: ninety seconds of checkout." Service O calls service I; I has a fixed thread pool of 200 workers. Candidate factors:

- C1: a deploy at 13:58 removed client-side jitter on O's retries, so retries from many clients now arrive in synchronized bursts.
- C2: a feature-flag rollout at 13:59 increased inventory request payloads roughly 8x, so each worker thread is held about 5x longer per request.
- A (an omission): I's autoscaler scale-up policy was disabled weeks earlier and never re-enabled. Its trigger fires on the derivative of queue depth, so it would have begun adding capacity at 14:00:30.
- B: one node in the pool ran a stale kernel whose TCP retransmit backoff differs; it held threads roughly 30% longer than its peers. This is a hardware/history fact we cannot intervene on in reality; we can only remove it inside the replay simulator.
- U: once the first errors appeared, customers refreshed checkout, multiplying request volume about 1.6x from 14:01:20 onward. This is downstream of the failure and is not something we can manipulate at all; we can only model it.

Counterfactual profile, established by a replay simulator the whole team trusts plus a partial production canary. SLO is 99.9% availability measured in 30-second windows, so a "breach" means three consecutive bad windows.

1. Actual (C1 and C2 both present): pool saturates 14:01:12, error rate peaks at 14%, breach lasts 90 seconds, revenue loss X.
2. C1 only, no C2: pool peaks at 62%; no breach.
3. C2 only, no C1: pool peaks at 88%, with one 4-second excursion to 94% — not a breach under the 30-second windowing.
4. Both present but autoscaler enabled (i.e., A removed): pool peaks at 84%; no breach.
5. Both present, stale-kernel node removed: breach still occurs, 40 seconds instead of 90.
6. Both present, customer refresh behavior suppressed: breach still occurs, 60 seconds instead of 90.
7. Both present, and a routed alert actually reaches a human who runs the runbook and reverts the flag at 14:02:25: breach still occurs, about 70 seconds. (That alert was misrouted to a channel nobody watched — a second omission.)
8. Everything in 5, 6, 7 changes only the magnitude of the breach, not whether a breach occurs; 2, 3, 4 change whether there is one at all.

Then, for each of C1, C2, A, B, U, state whether each camp calls it a cause, a mere contributing factor, or neither — and where the two camps give the same verdict, say so plainly.
