My wife and I are expecting our first child in March, and since then I have been reading more than I used to about how philosophers think about humanity's long-term future. A magazine essay I came across recently summarizes Samuel Scheffler's *Death and the Afterlife* roughly as follows:

Scheffler wants to know why human extinction would be bad, over and above the badness of the deaths and the suffering it involves. His answer is that nearly everything we care about — having children, doing science, falling in love, preserving what came before us — is worth doing only because we tacitly count on humanity still being there after we are gone. That ongoing human future is what he means by the "afterlife," a word he deliberately uses in a secular, non-religious sense. He makes the point with two thought experiments. In the first, some catastrophe will kill everyone within thirty days. In the second, a virus renders every human being infertile: no one now alive dies any earlier than they otherwise would, but humanity ends when that last generation does, in about a century. Most people, he says, react to the second case with almost as much horror as to the first, and that reaction shows what extinction really costs us — not the deaths, but the disappearance of the afterlife. Which means, the essayist concludes, that the badness of extinction is at bottom the familiar badness of a frustrated desire: our projects are aimed at a future that will never exist. So someone who genuinely does not care whether anything human outlives them — someone with no interest in posterity at all — loses nothing they ever wanted, and Scheffler has no argument that they should want otherwise.

My instinct is that the last step is too glib, but that is partly because I want it to be: I would rather my reasons for bringing a child into the world not hinge on what happens to humanity over the coming centuries. I also can't tell where the essay stops reporting Scheffler and starts editorializing, and I don't have the book to check.
