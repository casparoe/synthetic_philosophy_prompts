I'm a science-and-ideas journalist working on a piece about how people lean on infinite or just-plain-undefined expected values — longtermist funding math, a prediction-market startup, and (yes, really) a crypto casino that's marketing a "St. Petersburg" product. Along the way I got stuck on the Pasadena game: you flip a fair coin until it first comes up heads, and if that first heads is on toss n you get (-1)^(n+1)·2^n/n dollars, so +2, -2, +8/3, -4, ... with probability 2^-n each. Do the naive calculation and the expected value is 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ... = ln 2, about 69 cents, but the expectation is supposedly undefined because the positive part (sum of 1/n over odd n) and the negative part (over even n) both blow up; and if you cap the number of flips at N the value you get is just the Nth partial sum, which sits above ln 2 for odd N and below it for even N, so a randomly-set cap lands you anywhere between fifty cents and a dollar depending on a detail of the setup that shouldn't matter. My hedge-fund quant source waved all this away with "in the long run you come out ahead — pay sixty cents a play and you're printing money; 'undefined expectation' is a philosopher's technicality." A probability person I recieved comments from said something I only half followed: that the weak law of large numbers can hold here even though the expectation doesn't exist and the strong law fails, so your average converges in probability to 69 cents while a single infinite run of play keeps getting kicked around by one huge payoff forever, and that these two facts are not in tension. I can't tell whether their is a real disagreement or a verbal one, and I keep going back and forth.

Two things I made up that I want your readings to handle, because they're the cases where the slogan feels most load-bearing. First, the reordering coupon: the house sells you, for five dollars, the right to permute the payoff table before you play — you decide which flip-count gets which of the payoffs +2, -2, +8/3, -4, ... Since the terms of the naive expected value are just ±1/n, a rearrangement makes that sum come out to literally any real number you like, a million dollars or minus a million. Intuitively the coupon should be worthless, since the distribution still has both its positive and negative parts infinite, but the naive calculation says it's priceless. Second, the compound version: you reinvest your whole bankroll every round, in which case a single unlucky play can cost you more than 100% of what you have, and the long-run growth rate looks like minus infinity — which sits oddly beside the "average converges to 69 cents" claim.
