Design a Newcomb-style decision problem in which causal decision theory prescribes one-boxing and evidential decision theory prescribes two-boxing — the opposite of the usual direction — and do it without the machinery that standard Newcomb cases rely on. Keep the scenario itself to a single paragraph; the supporting work can be as long as it needs to be.

Specifications:

1. An ordinary setting — a clinic, a workplace, a courtroom, a family firm. No demons, no perfect or near-perfect predictors, no precognition, no backwards causation, no exotic physics. Anyone in the story may be shrewd, but only humanly so.
2. No predictor at all. The dependence between the act and the large payoff has to be forward-causal: the opaque box is settled by ordinary events after the choice, for instance by what a person or a committee later decides in light of what you did.
3. Both theories must earn their verdicts. The act must genuinely raise the causal chance of the large payoff — otherwise CDT's one-boxing is a cheat — and it must at the same time be genuine evidence of some hidden state that lowers the chance of that same payoff — otherwise EDT's two-boxing is a cheat. Both dependences should follow from the story; I do not want a table of stipulated probabilities doing the work.
4. The agent must be uncertain about the hidden state, since that is what makes her own act informative to her, but for a reason a real person could actually have, not because the story arbitrarily withholds information from her.
5. State the probabilities the story supports and compute both verdicts numerically. Check that the disagreement is not knife-edge: perturbing any single probability by a few points should not flip either verdict.

Then, briefly:

- Name the structural feature that is doing the work, and say which two familiar cases you land on if you delete each of the two ingredients in (3) — I expect one of them to be the smoking-lesion family, and the other to be ordinary Newcomb.
- Give the strongest reply available to a defender of whichever theory comes out looking worse here, and say whether that reply is a principled fix or a patch fitted to this case.
- Say whether the case gives us reason to stop repeating that one-boxing is the evidential answer and two-boxing the causal one, as if that were a fact about the two theories rather than about the standard setup.
- In one line: what would functional decision theory say here, and why?

I am putting together a short seminar note on the claim that the usual Newcomb verdicts are artifacts of the usual setup, so the case has to generate its verdicts from its structure rather than announce them. If some real-world practice contains this pattern under a name of its own — epidemiologists and loan officers both deal with versions of it — point at it, because it would help me show the case is not a contrivance.
