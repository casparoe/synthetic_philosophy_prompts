My father died in March, at eighty-two. For fifty-one years he ran a small investment club — eleven men, later nine, Thursday evenings in a back room — and I have been asked to speak for five minutes at its last meeting, two weeks from now, immediately before the members vote to dissolve it and hand what is left to a scholarship fund at the community college where he taught night classes for a decade. I am the executor. I am also, inconveniently, a financial economist: the club has always treated me as the family skeptic, and two of my colleagues are coming to hear whether I have gone soft on them since he died.

Since March I have been reading his ledgers. His rule, imposed on the club in 1974 and never lifted, was that no one could buy anything without first writing down, in one sentence, what he took the market not to know. There are thousands of these sentences. Reading them in order, I have come to think they fall into two kinds, and the difference between them is doing more work in my head than anything else has since the funeral. Sentences of the first kind claim an informational edge: the market does not know that the drought will break, that the lease will be renewed, that the plant is running at sixty percent. Sentences of the second kind claim only a difference in horizon — the market will not care about this for another five years. Most of the early ones are of the first kind. Almost everything in his last decade is of the second.

That is why this speech is hard. It cannot say that he knew something the market did not: the no-trade result I myself teach — roughly, as I would put it to undergraduates, that with common priors, common knowledge of rationality and voluntary trade, nobody can expect to gain — makes that claim very nearly incoherent, and the two economists in the room would hear it as sentimentality. But it also cannot say that fifty-one years were a wash, or that he was lucky, because the vote on dissolution has to feel like a completion rather than a surrender, and because I do not believe it. And one member, a retired actuary, will certainly raise the Grossman–Stiglitz-style point that a market efficient enough to price information must still leave some return to those who go and gather it — which is not a defence of what my father did, but is not nothing either, and I would rather not be refuted over the sandwiches.

So I want a speech in which the one distinction — knowing more versus waiting longer — carries the whole weight of the praise: what the club did has to be redescribed as something the no-trade argument does not reach, and the dissolution has to follow from that redescription rather than be announced after it. No technical vocabulary at all; if a concept is needed, build it out of one of their own holdings (the small irrigation-pump maker he bought during the drought summer is the obvious one). The room is mostly men in their seventies and eighties who have never taken an economics course.

Please give me:

1. "The speech" — about 700 words, written exactly as I would deliver it, with a few bracketed delivery cues.
2. "Where the argument does the work" — about 250 words: which sentences carry the distinction, and what the speech loses if they are cut or softened.
3. "Lines I refused to say" — about 200 words: the flattering falsehoods and the deflationary ones that were available to me, and why each is unavailable.
4. "Handling the actuary" — about 150 words: the three objections I expect from that room, and the reply the speech already contains.
