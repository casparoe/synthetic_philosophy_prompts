hey — I'm a science-and-ideas journalist working on a piece about whistleblowing and loyalty, and I've been going in circles for about a week. Every piece I read on the Snowden case quietly assumes the individual is the basic unit: a person with a conscience, who then also happens to hold a job with some duties attached. I want to try the opposite starting point and see what happens.

So: you're a specialist in comparative ethics and Chinese philosophy, with a real grip on the Confucian role-ethics reading of the early texts — the version where a person is constituted by there roles (parent, child, minister, friend, teacher), rather than being a presocial chooser who takes roles on and off like a coat. Take your time with this, please, and before you write anything, brainstorm for a bit: which roles Edward Snowden actually occupied in 2013, which of them generate duties at all on this view, and where you suspect the theory is simply going to refuse to give an answer.

The case, so we're not re-litigating the facts: Snowden was a contractor for the NSA, copied and handed to journalists classified material about bulk collection of Americans' phone metadata and the PRISM program, went to Hong Kong, ended up stranded in Russia, and was charged under the Espionage Act. His defenders say he told the public what was being done in its name; his critics say he broke an oath, damaged sources and methods, and handed adversaries a roadmap. I know all that. I dont need the recap, I need the mapping.

Four things I want you to actually work out rather than gesture at.

First, the thick list of roles, not the boring employee-versus-citizen one: custodian of classified information, subordinate inside a chain of command, signatory of a non-disclosure agreement, member of a polity that claims to govern by consent, technician with specialized access, and whatever else you think belongs. Say what each role's duty content actually is on this view, and how conflicts between them get weighed — because role ethics, as I understand it, doesn't have a tidy lexical ordering to fall back on, and I want to know whether that's a bug or the whole point.

Second, and this is the part I can't find anyone addressing: does the view have anything like a doctrine of role corruption — a role whose constitutive end has broken, so the duties hanging off it thin out or even flip sign? The lever I keep coming back to is the rectification of names: if the office whose name is something like protecting the public's security under law has stopped doing what its name says, is the subordinate's loyalty owed to the office as it is, or to the name as it should be? Work that out properly and then apply it, and tell me if you think I'm misusing the idea.

Third, remonstrance. There's a real Confucian tradition of the subordinate's duty to speak up to a superior who has gone wrong, where staying silent is itself a dereliction rather than mere prudence. Does that give Snowden a role-grounded obligation to act once internal channels proved useless — or does remonstrance only ever authorize speech directed upward, to the person who can fix it, which would make going sideways to the public a different animal entirely? That distinction is the crux of my piece, so don't blur it.

Fourth, run it the other direction: what does the case do to the theory? Snowden's defenders and critics talk entirely past each other, one in the vocabulary of oath, loyalty and station, the other in the vocabulary of individual conscience and the public's right to know. Role ethics predicts that incommensurability, which I take to be a point in its favor. But a theory that grounds everything in roles may have nothing to say about someone who has exited every role that could have obligated him — no longer a contractor, arguably no longer a participant in the polity, sitting in Moscow — and that's precisely the moment the case is about. Give me your honest verdict on which of those two readings wins, and say plainly where the theory is silent instead of making it talk.

One more: what would change your mind? If it turned out he'd exhausted every internal channel and been ignored, or alternatively that someone died because of the disclosures, does the analysis move — or is role ethics insensitive to consequences in a way that should embarrass it?

And please, no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables: I read these on my phone and I want continuous prose, the way you'd write it in an email to a colleague. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence or two — does the theory hold up here or does it break — and then work through it. No citations, footnotes or reading lists either; I want your reasoning, not your references. Go.
