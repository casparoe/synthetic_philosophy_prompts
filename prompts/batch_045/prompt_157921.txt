hey — I'm trying to get clear on something before I sink more time into the consciousness literature, and I keep going in circles. I want the spec, not the theories.

Concretely: what does a theory of consciousness have to be able to do to count as adequate — where adequate means good enough to adjudicate actual disputed cases, not to win a metaphysics seminar. The disputed cases I care about: a human fetus around 24 weeks, a crow, the right hemisphere of a split-brain patient, and a language model with a self-monitoring module bolted on. If a theory can only tell me about adult humans reporting on carefully controlled lab stimuli, its not adequate for my purposes.

So give me the list of requirements, each with the case or consideration behind it: the phenomena it has to cover (sleep and anesthesia, blindsight, dreams, the no-report paradigms, the unity of a single visual scene, the specious present), the distinctions it cant collapse (creature vs state consciousness, phenomenal vs access, having an experience vs knowing that you're having one, being conscious of something vs being conscious that you're in such-and-such a state), and the results from neuroscience or psychology it cant contradict. Say why each is a requirement rather than a taste. And flag anything on the usual list that you think is really about something else — attention, report, self-knowledge — that people keep bundling in.

Then the part I actually want:

- Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just the prejudices of one camp. I suspect a few things people demand are commitments smuggled in — e.g. the demand that the theory dissolve the explanatory gap entirely, or the demand that it be straightforwardly testable from the third-person side. If you think either of those (or something else) is a camp prejudice, say so, and say whats left of the list once its dropped.
- Flag any pair of requirements you suspect cant be jointly satisfied. The one I keep hitting: dont over-intellectualize, conscious experience has to be available to creatures with no concepts, including infants and probably most animals — against: explain the tight connection between consciousness and reportability, access, and self-knowledge. If that pair really is a tradeoff then the list itself is a result, and I want it stated that bluntly.

I'll be using the list mainly against higher-order theories, so please include two cases I made up that I think are pressure points for them, and tell me whether theyre fair requirements or whether I've built the answer into the question:

1. silent substitution. An intervention holds the higher-order state fixed — same content, same time-course, something like I am now seeing red — while swapping the first-order target underneath it: first for another colour, then for a state in a different sense modality, then for nothing at all. Does the subject's experience change, stay the same, or become an illusion? Whatever the answer, a theory needs to give one, and needs to say what it predicts about what the subject will say when asked.
2. crossed wires. Two systems are linked so that higher-order states in one of them target first-order states in the other. Whose consciousness is it, and what is it of? This is meant to force the question of whether consciousness travels with the first-order state or with the higher-order one.

Finally: which single requirement on your list does the most work in knocking out candidates — actualist higher-order thought views, the dispositionalist and self-representationalist variants, first-order and global-workspace style views, illusionism — and why? A short scorecard at the end is fine, but keep the weight on the criteria. Dont bother with citations or a reading list, I just want the argument.
