I should say at the outset that I have no academic background in philosophy — I work in logistics and read about this kind of thing in the evenings — so please don't assume a shared technical vocabulary, and please don't point me toward a reading list. I have no library access and I'm not asking for sources; I'm asking for reasoning that I can check against the cases themselves.

What I keep coming back to is how thought experiments work when they work badly. I've seen the label "intuition pump" used for the ones that function by getting you to imagine a scenario and then report a reaction to it, and I'm interested in the specific failure mode where the reaction is strong and stable and yet doesn't travel to the question the case was supposed to settle. The reaction isn't wrong, exactly — it just doesn't reach. Over the past year I've been trying to catalogue the ways this happens. I call them leaks, and my list currently has six:

1. The vanishing stipulation. The setup asks you to imagine something away — consequences, other people, a body, a history, a social context — and then the argument quietly leans on the very thing you were told to remove.
2. The loaded lexicon. The describing words have already taken a side. The case says "the person," or "kills," or "in pain," or "the same ship," and the verdict is packed into the noun before you're asked for it.
3. The orphaned output. What the case hands back is a bare yes or no with no account of why, so the result can be wheeled in behind any theory that happens to agree with it, and carries no weight once it has to be balanced against anything else.
4. The idle wheel. A detail that does no work in the argument but supplies most of the pull, and which you could delete without touching the reasoning at all.
5. The asymmetry trick. The two options aren't described on equal terms — one from the inside and one from the outside, one in close-up and one in the abstract, one with a name and one with a number — so that swapping the styles of description around inverts the intuition.
6. The scale cheat. The intuition is raised on a single isolated instance and then spent on populations, repeated cases, or long-run aggregates, where it either reverses or thins out to nothing.

I'm fairly confident this list is both redundant and incomplete, so there are two things I'd like from you.

First: score each of the six from 0 to 10 on three dimensions — how common it is in the well-known cases that circulate in philosophy books and in arguments online; how hard it is to notice from inside the case, before someone points at it; and how repairable it is, meaning whether the case could be redesigned to pump roughly the same intuition without the leak. A small table would be easiest for me to read. Then, using those numbers, sort the six into tiers ranked by damage, where I mean damage per unit of intuitive force supplied: how much distortion a leak of that kind injects into an argument relative to how much persuasive push it provides. Give me one line per tier on why it sits where it does. For what it's worth, my own guess is that the loaded lexicon is the most damaging and the idle wheel the least, on the grounds that a loaded word contaminates every downstream step while a merely vivid detail can be stripped out in a sentence — but I don't trust that guess, and I'd rather you argued with it than agreed with it.

Second, I want to know whether the scheme is missing something large, so here is a case I built myself to test it. I call it the Delegation Ladder. A judge passes criminal sentences. Over seven years a risk-scoring system is introduced one rung at a time: at rung 1 it only files and sorts the paperwork; at rung 2 it writes the case summary the judge reads; at rung 3 it flags the aggravating factors; at rung 4 it proposes a sentencing range; at rung 5 it proposes a specific sentence; at rung 6 the proposal takes effect unless the judge overrides it within a minute; at rung 7 the proposal takes effect unless the judge overrides it within a minute, and any override requires written reasons and is reviewed by a panel. At every rung the judge can still depart from the proposal, and at every rung everyone involved — including the judge — continues to say that the judge is the one who sentences. The case is built to pose this question: at which rung, if any, does the judge stop being the author of the sentence? Everyone I've tried it on agrees that there is no rung at which authorship clearly lapses, and then does not want to accept the conclusion that the judge at rung 7 is fully the author of the sentence.

Where does the Delegation Ladder sit in my scheme, if it sits anywhere? And does it point to a seventh kind of leak that my six don't cover? If you think it does, name it in the same style as mine, define it tightly enough that I could apply it to a different case on my own, and tell me where it belongs in the tier list and why. If instead you think it's one of my six wearing a disguise, or that two of my six are really the same thing under different names, say that and argue for it — collapsing categories would be as useful to me as a new one.
