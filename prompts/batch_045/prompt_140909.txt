I convene a cross-disciplinary reading group — half philosophy of mind, half vision science — and we are stuck in the shape we always get stuck in: the philosophers argue about what perception is and the scientists argue about which experiments show what, and we cannot tell whether we are having one disagreement or six. So instead of another survey paper I want a classification instrument: a decision tree that sorts a person into a position on whether and how cognition affects perception, on the basis of what they are willing to say about concrete cases rather than on what they call themselves.

Please build the tree for a handout of about four sides, for second-year graduate students who have read some philosophy of perception but no specialist literature. No bibliography, no citations, no reading suggestions: proper names may appear only as short mnemonic labels on leaves, never inside a question.

Constraints on construction:

1. Build from the logical space, not from a list of names. Begin by stating the branch questions and saying how many answer-combinations the space contains; then locate the actual positions inside it, and mark which cells are empty.

2. Use eight to ten branch nodes, each with two or three answers. No node may contain the words penetration, top-down, modular, encapsulated, inferential, or any theorist's name, and no node may ask a person to classify their own view. Phrase every node as a question someone could answer from the cases below without knowing where it leads.

3. Four distinctions must each be represented by at least one node, because these are the ones my group is actually fighting about:
   (a) the dependent variable — whether the thing that has to change is how things look, what the state represents, how well a subject can discriminate, or the processing in early sensory areas;
   (b) the independent variable — whether the causing state must be an occurrent belief or judgment, or whether a standing belief, a desire, an imagining, a task-set, or a subpersonal prior learned over minutes or over years will do;
   (c) the timescale — whether the effect must be contemporaneous with the cognitive state, or whether an effect induced by weeks of practice counts, or whether an effect that persists for a few seconds after the cognitive state has been revoked counts;
   (d) the shape of the change — additive (a novel apparent property that the modality had no way of representing before, as when a shape comes to look menacing or to look like a face), substitutive (the same property with altered intensity, as when a red simply looks more saturated), or occlusive (something that would otherwise have been perceived is not perceived at all). I introduce this trichotomy myself; say if you think it is not orthogonal to the other nodes and why.

4. Every leaf must route all six of the following vignettes, marking each as genuine penetration, not penetration, or not a well-formed case. The vignettes are fixed; do not substitute your own.
   V1. A radiologist with years of training sees a subtle mass in a mammogram that a novice cannot see; she reports that it simply pops out, and she is not thinking about diagnosis at the time.
   V2. A subject stands at the viewing hole of a room built so that its retinal image is that of a rectangular room; she has been shown the construction, firmly believes the far corner is twice as far away, and would bet on it, but the room still looks rectangular.
   V3. A subject is told that some blurry grey patches are faces, and for ten minutes they look like faces; the instruction is then withdrawn, she sincerely judges the patches to be random noise, and yet for about half a minute they still look like faces.
   V4. A thirsty subject's detection sensitivity (d′) for water-related stimuli in a noisy display shifts measurably, with no change in response bias and nothing she can report about how the display looks.
   V5. A bistable figure is preceded by a fifty-millisecond presentation of an unambiguous version of one of its two readings, presented too briefly for the subject to report seeing it; on the critical trial she sees that reading.
   V6. An acoustically fixed, ambiguous syllable between two phonemes is heard as one or the other depending on what the subject takes the surrounding sentence to be about; the effect also appears, in a weaker form, when the subject merely expects a word of a certain kind.

5. Produce ten to fourteen leaves, each carrying: a short conventional label used only as a mnemonic; one sentence on the most awkward commitment the position has; and one sentence on which vignette it is least comfortable with. At least one leaf must be a coherent but, as far as you know, undefended combination that you construct yourself and label as such — a position somebody could hold but, you suspect, nobody does. Positions you should be able to locate somewhere in the space include: impenetrability restricted to early vision; impenetrability unrestricted; a position on which every apparently positive result is really an effect on judgment, memory or response; a position on which a restricted range of cognitive states changes the contents of experience without changing its character; a position on which cognition pervasively shapes perception; a position on which perception just is inference and the cognition/perception boundary marks no joint, so the question dissolves; and a deflationary position on which the whole dispute is verbal. Include a pluralist leaf as well, for someone who thinks the answer differs by modality, timescale, or dependent variable and that there is no unified thesis to have a view about.

6. Name at least four logically available but unoccupied combinations and diagnose each: is nobody there because the combination is incoherent, because it is merely unattractive, or because it is simply unexplored? For each, say what would have to be true for someone to occupy it.

7. Flag at least two pairs of leaves that your question set cannot separate — cases where the tree is honest enough to admit that a certain answer pattern is compatible with two named positions — and for each say what further question, or what kind of empirical result, would pry them apart. Do not paper over these by adding a node that secretly presupposes the answer.

8. Present the whole thing as a plain-text flowchart with numbered nodes and explicit routing, give one worked example in path notation, and close with a routing table (leaves x V1–V6), the empty-cell table, and the cannot-separate section.
