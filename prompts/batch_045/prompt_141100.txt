I'm on the conduct and ethics committee of a mid-sized international health NGO, and we have to decide what to do about our country office in Terembu before our next report to the donor in about three weeks. The country director — local staff, eleven years with us, well liked — has been approving a cluster of things our global policy almost certainly classes as corruption, on roughly the sector-standard definition our policy copies: power entrusted to you, used for private gain. Concretely: monthly transport and sitting allowances paid to government clinic inspectors so they will actually visit the remote clinics they are meant to supervise (their own ministry has not funded fuel in two years); four goats presented to village elders at a clinic opening; hiring the district health officer's younger brother as a driver, who is by all accounts a genuinely good driver and whose hiring the DHO made clear was about the relationship; and a contractor kicking in bags of cement for the clinic fence as a community contribution. None of it appears anywhere as a line item marked bribe, and our programs lead keeps pointing out that the inspections happened, the cold chain got fixed, and the clinic got built.

Our regional director says that reporting this to the donor would be a kind of moral imperialism, that we would be holding a Terembuan office to a Weberian bureacracy standard the surrounding political culture does not share, and that refusing the goats would not have been thrift but an insult that ended the relationship with the elders outright. Our governance lead says the opposite: intent and local meaning are irrelevant, the policy is strict liability, and anything softer is how organizations talk themselves into a scandal. I do not want to write context matters and move on.

To give us something sharper than that, our regional advisor circulated a note with a distinction I half like: facilitation gifting, where the gift buys one specific official act that breaks a rule; maintenance gifting, where it sustains a relationship of obligation and what comes back is discretion and goodwill rather than a specified act; constitutive gifting, where the gift is the relationship, so that refusing dissolves it; and — the one I keep coming back to — something she calls capture by substitution, where impersonal provision gets replaced so thoroughly by gift obligations that officials acquire an interest in keeping their own duties unfunded, since the underfunding is what makes the gifts necessary. That last one seems to me the strongest thing that can be said against tolerating any of this even on its own terms, and I want it in the memo even if we land the other way.

Can you draft the memo I will circulate before the meeting? It should give us a bottom line we can actually vote on — report to the donor or not, sanction the country director or not, and what we say by the deadline; it should say which of the four categories each of the four practices falls into, or argue that the taxonomy is the wrong tool for one of them; it should give us a decision rule for the next case, because there will be one, that is not just ask the regional director; and it should give the losing side honestly — if you recommend reporting, why the moral imperialism charge has real force here, and if you recommend not reporting, what we say both to the governance lead and to the Terembuan staff who have watched expat managers bend these rules for years.
