Our department's reading group devoted a session last month to the so-called gender-equality paradox: the observation, reported across several indices and student cohorts, that the countries scoring highest on measures of gender equality — the Nordic countries above all — also tend to show more gender-skewed educational and occupational sorting, in particular a lower share of women in the math-intensive STEM fields and larger gaps in self-assessed academic strengths. What struck me was how casually the word paradox was doing argumentative work. The discussion slid continuously between three questions I want kept apart: whether the pattern is robust at all, what produces it if it is, and what would follow for justice if the causal story turned out to be partly biological rather than cultural. My own interest is the middle one, and I keep getting stuck on a point I think the group skated over: if the biological contribution to sex differences in interests and aptitudes is roughly constant across human populations — which is the working assumption of any broadly evolutionary or hormonal explanation — then biology cannot by itself explain why the gaps differ between countries. It can only be one term in an interaction: a disposition that egalitarian, affluent, low-stakes environments permit to express itself, or that they decline to counteract. So the live claim is not nature over culture but something narrower, and here is the proposition I want a number on:

P: In the currently most gender-egalitarian wealthy democracies, at least about a third of the observed underrepresentation of women in math-intensive fields of study and occupations is mediated by biologically based sex differences in interests and aptitudes, where the biological contribution is a roughly constant human disposition whose expression is permitted or amplified by egalitarian and affluent conditions, rather than something that itself varies from country to country.

My credence in P is around 0.3; my officemate, who thinks the purely cultural story has become a professional taboo, sits near 0.55; a senior colleague in social epistemology is near 0.15 and holds that the measurement problems swallow the whole question. I don't trust any of the three of us to be reasoning rather than signalling. Sub-questions I have been turning over, which you should feel free to use, reorder, or argue are the wrong cuts: whether the pattern survives disaggregation (life sciences and medicine versus engineering and computer science look very different); whether the predictive variable is really gender-egalitarianism or simply national wealth and welfare-state generosity, with which it is badly collinear; whether the mechanism is choice under abundance — when the material stakes of picking a major are low, people sort by intrinsic interest — which is a fully cultural mechanism that mimics a biological one; whether differential selection explains the direction, since women who enter male-dominated fields in less egalitarian countries are a far more selected subgroup, so cross-national averages may compare unlike populations; whether the cross-sectional, cross-country design can support any individual-level inference at all, given ecological fallacies and country-level confounds such as post-socialist economies with high female STEM shares; what within-country time trends actually show, since changes over decades inside the same institutions are more informative than any snapshot; what a second-generation-migrant or sibling design would be expected to show, as a way of separating inherited culture from host-country institutions; and whether the paradox has an implied vanishing point, i.e. whether extrapolating the trend predicts a residual gap that no achievable policy closes.

Please structure your reply in the following sections, in this order: Headline number (roughly 40 words: a single probability for P, with the reading of P you are assigning it to); Decomposition (roughly 350 words: each sub-question, its own probability, and one line of justification, as a list or table); Combination (roughly 120 words: how the terms compose, and how much the independence assumption is doing); Least confident term (roughly 80 words); Where the three of us disagree (roughly 150 words: which of the 0.3, 0.55 and 0.15 positions you would most expect to be tracking different sub-questions rather than different weightings of the same ones); What would move it most (roughly 120 words: described as a study design or a finding, not as a citation — I have no library access here); Does the number matter (roughly 100 words: what, if anything, your probability for P would entitle anyone to conclude about policy or justice).
