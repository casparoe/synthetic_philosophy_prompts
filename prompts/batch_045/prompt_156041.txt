Here's the question I set:

Ganesha is a statue made by a sculptor out of a single piece of clay, Clay. Consider three episodes.

(a) Clay is squashed into a ball.
(b) The statue is fired in a kiln. Afterwards the materials scientist says that no clay remains — the material is now ceramic — while the museum says the statue has survived firing.
(c) Over thirty years conservators grind away weathered surface clay and patch in fresh clay from the same seam, a little each year. At the end no particle of the original remains, and the museum catalogue still lists the same statue.

1. Set out the argument from modal properties for the conclusion that Ganesha is not identical with Clay. Which of (a)–(c) does the real work for that argument, and why?
2. Explain how each of the following two replies deals with that argument, and give the main cost of each: (i) the constitution view — Ganesha is constituted by Clay, and constitution is asymmetric and is not identity; (ii) the contingent identity view — Ganesha is identical with Clay, but only contingently.
3. Briefly assess the grounding proposal: Ganesha is nothing over and above Clay arranged Ganesha-wise.

Here's one student's answer, typos and all:

The argument from modal properties is that Ganesha and Clay differ in their modal properties, and identical things can't differ in any property (Leibniz's Law), so Ganesha isn't Clay. Clay can survive being squashed — it's the same clay, just reshaped — but Ganesha can't, because a statue essentially has its shape. So Clay has the property of possibly surviving squashing and Ganesha lacks it, so they aren't identical.

Case (a) does the real work: the intuition there is very robust and it's the cleanest of the three. Case (b) helps too, and it's nice because it runs in the other direction: firing destroys the clay (the materials scientist is the expert on that, and clay is a material that stops existing when its chemistry changes) while the statue carries on, so we get a modal difference where the statue is the survivor instead. Case (c) is the weakest: it's just the Ship of Theseus, and people's intuitions go all over the place; also the museum catalogue only records how the institution treats something, which isn't the same as what the thing is. So I won't lean on (c).

On 2(i): the constitution view says Clay constitutes Ganesha. Constitution is asymmetric, and constitution isn't identity, so Ganesha and Clay can come apart modally without Leibniz's Law being violated — squashing destroys the statue but not the clay it was made of. The cost is coincidence: two objects, in the same place at the same time, with all the same parts. Once you've allowed one such doubling it's hard to say why there aren't more — the clay, the statue, the arrangement — so the view looks both profligate and arbitrary.

On 2(ii): the contingent identity view buys ontological economy, one object and no coincidence, but the cost is that it has to deny Leibniz's Law, i.e. accept that two things can be identical and still differ in their properties — in particular in the property of possibly surviving squashing. That's a very big thing to give up, since Leibniz's Law is about as basic as principles get.

On 3: the grounding proposal — Ganesha is nothing over and above Clay arranged Ganesha-wise — is meant to get the economy without giving up the modal difference, but I don't think it can. If Ganesha is nothing over and above its ground, then Ganesha has exactly the persistence conditions of that ground, and the ground here is the clay (or the clay along with its arrangement — on this view that makes no difference, since there's nothing more to the arrangement than the clay). The clay survives squashing. So the proposal predicts that Ganesha survives squashing too, which is absurd; and if the grounding theorist wants to avoid that they have to bring back a second object to carry the statue's modal properties, which just is the constitution view again. So the grounding proposal either collapses into identity or collapses into the constitution view, and it doesn't answer the argument from modal properties at all.
