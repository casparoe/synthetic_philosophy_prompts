I'm in a small graduate seminar on poetic translation, and we've spent the term with Hofstadter's *Le Ton beau de Marot: In Praise of the Music of Language* — the 1997 book that collects dozens of English renderings of Clément Marot's short Renaissance poem known as "Ma mignonne," and whose title puns on *tombeau*, a work made in homage to a dead artist. Our final assignment is to produce a version of the poem that keeps the original's rhyme scheme and roughly constant line length while preserving its situation: a speaker addressing a young woman who is ill. Last week a visiting poet sat in, heard the assignment, and dismissed it with the old Italian proverb "traduttore, traditore." His gloss: every translation betrays its original, so a version that strains to reproduce the form is only a more elaborate betrayal, and the honest options are either to concede that the poem is untranslatable or to write a good English poem loosely after it. Pressed by one of us, he added that since form and content cannot both be carried across, the choice between them is arbitrary, and that we might as well choose whichever version reads better to us now.

I want to push back, but the proverb is doing something slippery, and I'd like it taken apart rather than answered. Please lay out, as a menu I can bring to the next session, the distinct precise claims it could be making, and for each: whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false (or incoherent), and whether it would actually do the work he wanted it to do. I'd like the menu to separate at least these dimensions: how much is said to be lost (something, a great deal, more than is preserved); what the loss is a loss *of* — propositional content, the formal pattern, the fit between the two, the author's intention, the effect on a period reader; whether "betrayal" is a moral notion that requires a prior specification of what was owed, so that the proverb is empty until a constraint-set is declared; and whether any of these readings yields his stronger conclusions, that the poem is untranslatable and that, all renderings being betrayals anyway, the choice among them is arbitrary.

One distinction from my own notes that I'd like you to place in the menu and assess rather than simply accept. Call it constraint-cost fidelity: the thought that what matters in a poem written to a strict form is not just that the form is satisfied but *what it cost* to satisfy it — how much contortion, padding, or sacrificed naturalness the original visibly paid. On that view, a translation can match the original's rhyme scheme and syllable count exactly and still betray it, if it rhymes in a language where that scheme comes cheap, because it has produced a formal achievement the original had to work for; and symmetrically, a prose version is not the neutral baseline but a substitution of a different genre with different conventions. So there may be a reading of "traduttore, traditore" on which betrayal is a failure to preserve the *relation* between form and content even when both are preserved individually. I want to know whether that is a genuine reading of the proverb or a different claim wearing its clothes, where it would sit among the other entries, and whether — if it is right — it cuts for or against my assignment. Please also flag which entries are really conversation-stoppers rather than claims, and say which reading you think someone in his position is most likely to have meant.
