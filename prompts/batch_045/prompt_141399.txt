I work in human factors / patient safety at a regional air-ambulance service — rotor- and fixed-wing, about forty flight crew, a few thousand missions a year. One of my jobs is maintaining the tool we use in post-incident review: for any given fact at issue (the weather at the destination was below minima, the oxygen cylinder was reading low, the receiving hospital hadn't actually accepted the patient), the review has to land on one of three verdicts about the crew member at time t: they knew, they didn't know but should have, or they couldn't have known. That verdict feeds our just-culture matrix, so it has teeth — the first two can trigger remedial training or a competency sign-off, the third usually just changes a procedure or a display. Right now the tool is basically evidence-based invariantism with a checklist: list what was available to them at t, ask whether it was good enough. That's been fine, but our new medical director has been pushing back hard after a case where the same evidence counted as knowing on a low-stakes repositioning flight and apparently stopped counting once there was a deteriorating pediatric patient on board. She thinks — and I now half think — that the bar really should move with what was at stake for that crew member at that moment, i.e. she's asking for something like pragmatic encroachment, and the committee wants to know what we'd actually be signing up for before we write it into the handbook.

So: give me the procedure version of that. I want the ordered questions a reviewer asks off an incident report, runnable in half an hour from what our normal reports contain, written so a flight nurse or a pilot on the panel can run it without any theory background (I did a philosophy minor about fifteen years ago and I'm the most theory-adjacent person in the room). For each step, say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure hard-stops with "did not know," and where it has to hand the decision back to a person because the real issue is a values call about what our org wants to be accountable for, not a factual one. Two problems are where our informal attempts keep falling apart, and I want both handled explicitly. First: stakes the agent didn't appreciate — nobody told them the flight had upgraded to a scene call, or they had no idea the patient was deteriorating, so the stakes were objectively huge but weren't ones they possessed or believed in at the time. Second: stakes that get inflated after the fact — we run a safety briefing or issue a new protocol, and suddenly the same crew with the same evidence didn't know what they plainly did know six months earlier. My current hack for both is a "stakes firewall": the review applies the stakes attached to the role as constituted at t (the mission card, the checklist, the protocol then in force), not the stakes the individual happened to feel, and not the stakes later policy would assign. Tell me whether that hack survives being turned into actual steps, whether it quietly collapses the view back into invariantism with extra bookkeeping, or whether it's doing real work — and if you have a better fix for either problem, use yours instead and say why it beats mine. I'd also like a short version of what the document would look like if we went the other way and took the contextualist line, where what shifts is the standard in the review room rather than the stakes for the agent: my sense is that on that view the deliverable stops being a tool about the crew at all and becomes a tool about how to run the meeting, and I want to see how different those two documents really are before I bring either to the committee.

Then run your procedure on three of our cases. (A) A daytime VFR ferry flight, no patient, where the crew's evidence about deteriorating destination weather was fairly thin but everyone would have said they knew. (B) The pediatric scene call: identical evidence about the same weather, but now there's a kid on board and a twenty-minute window. (C) The one I'm dreading, where I want your procedure to give the answer we won't like: a senior flight paramedic everybody respects, who followed the protocol exactly, who by the procedure's own lights did not know — and where writing that down means his competency sign-off lapses and we have to tell him that to his face. Please end by flagging what the procedure can't do for us: I'm guessing it can settle the knowledge verdict but not whether to sanction, and that there'll be cases where the evidence is fine but the record is too thin to run it at all. Don't give me a survey of the literature or who argued for what — no citations, no reading list; I need something I could drop into an appendix and defend to a room of pilots.
