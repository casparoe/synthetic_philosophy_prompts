Hey — I run a weekend reading group in someone's living room, mostly LW/ACX-adjacent people, usually 12–16 of us, and I've got a two-hour slot coming up. I want to do a session on nuclear deterrence and brinkmanship, but not as a lecture and not as another rehash of the usual Schelling summary plus the would-you-push-the-button discourse. I've read that stuff and I'm not satisfied by it. I can recite "credible threat" and "the rationality of irrationality" and I still don't feel why the paradox is supposed to be hard, and I suspect a lot of the felt force is in the *interactive* part that a blog post can't give you.

So: design me an actual playable thing. Constraints, roughly in order of how much I care:

- No lecture up front. Nobody should hear the words brinkmanship, Schelling, doomsday machine, or rationality-of-irrationality until after we've played. Let the structure do the work.
- Materials: index cards, a d20, a whiteboard, pen. I'm not building an app and I don't want real money changing stakes — points toward something trivial, like who picks the pizza, is fine.
- I'd like people to hit these, but tell me plainly if any of them are a bad idea or won't land:
  1. An escalation ladder with rungs, where each rung has a known, rising probability of an accidental/involuntary catastrophe, rolled in the open. So "leaving something to chance" is a literal die roll that players choose to walk themselves up to.
  2. A way to buy commitment before the round starts — I was imagining a sealed index card committing you not to back down before rung k, and maybe a public "temperament" label you can pay points for (i.e. buying a reputation for being bad at expected value). I want to see whether people actually pay for these and whether they work.
  3. A scaling manipulation: run the same game once with trivial stakes and once with catastrophic ones, where in the catastrophic version the bad outcome hits the whole room, not just the pair playing. I think this is the part I'm most confused about: is the paradox a structural feature of any brinkmanship situation, or does it depend on the stakes being enormous? I want the group to feel whichever is true, and I want the design to actually test that rather than just assert it.
  4. A "glitch": one round where the facilitator forces a challenge regardless of the player's intent, with it being common knowledge from the start that a glitch may happen. My hope is that people install tripwires anyway and then discover that a tripwire good at deterring is bad at surviving a false alarm, and you can't have both. If that's really just the facilitator cheating and won't land, say so.
- Give me: the rules, actual payoff numbers, a timeline for the two hours, what's printed on which card, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the specific ideas (named only after we've played).
- Include an honest list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I especially expect people to treat it as a pure EV calculation, to confuse "irrational" with "aggressive," and to not take the low-stakes block seriously enough for deterrence dynamics to show up. Tell me how to handle each.
- And be frank about what the game can't reproduce and I'll just have to say out loud myself: I'm guessing the real probabilities aren't a known number on a card, real postures shape the probabilities endogenously, and there's a whole domestic-politics / who-actually-controls-the-button dimension a living-room game has no room for. Tell me what else I'm missing there.

Epistemic bit: if the premise is confused, or the task as posed can't be done well — e.g. two hours with fourteen people just isn't enough to produce the intuition I'm after, or my stakes manipulation doesn't test what I think it tests, or the glitch is unfair in a way that breaks the point — say that plainly rather than politely building me the thing anyway. And in the debrief, mark which claims you're confident about versus extrapolating; I'd much rather know where you're guessing than get smooth confidence about something you're making up.
