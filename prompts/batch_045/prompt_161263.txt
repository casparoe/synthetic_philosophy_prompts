Hey — I'm a PhD student working on the conceivability argument, and I keep failing to explain, to my skeptical friends in other fields and honestly to myself, what the two-dimensionalist reply to the standard Kripkean objection is supposed to buy. I get the setup: we can seemingly conceive of Hesperus not being Phosphorus, or of the watery stuff not being H2O, and if those identities hold they hold necessarily, so conceivability can't just entail possibility. Fine. But then the reply is meant to be that the zombie scenario is possible in some weaker, epistemic sense — true at some scenario considered as actual — and that this weaker modal notion is enough to do the work the anti-physicalist needs. I can never reconstruct why it's enough, or why it doesn't just push the bump into the question of whether primary possibility entails secondary possibility in this particular case.

Here's the part I really want, though. Call an alleged a posteriori necessity gap-closable if there's a story about why the gap is epistemic rather than metaphysical: for water, we picked out the stuff by a superficial description and the hidden nature had to be discovered empirically, so of course there's a scenario-considered-as-actual in which the description picks out something else. Intuitivly that kind of story isn't available for the psychophysical link — there's no hidden nature left to find, no reference-fixing description doing the work, the physical story is supposed to be complete. So: what would a gap-closing story for that link even have to look like? Is the difference meant to be that phenomenal concepts are 'epistemically rigid' in a way natural-kind terms aren't, so that there's nothing to discover — and is that coherent? And if no gap-closing story is available, is that itself a seperate argument for dualism that doesn't route through conceivability at all, or just the explanatory gap in modal costume?

I'd rather have gut-level intuition than a careful survey: where exactly does the analogy with the Kripke cases break down, and what's the strongest thing the physicalist can say at that point?
