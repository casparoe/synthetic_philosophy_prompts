I'm a third-year PhD student in political science — social choice is my minor field — and I've been drafted onto the standing rules committee of a mid-sized health research foundation, essentially because I'm the only person in the room who has read Arrow. My job is to write the panel procedure the foundation will run for the next five years, and to write it so that the panel can run it without me in the room.

The setting: three cycles a year, around 120 proposals, each read by three of twelve panelists. No panelist ever ranks more than a third of the pool, so what the committee actually receives is a family of partial orderings over overlapping subsets, plus a three-reader discussion score for each proposal. From that the panel has to produce a single ordered shortlist, a cut somewhere between six and ten awards, and — the trust deed permits it — the option of making fewer than six awards, or none, when nothing clears the bar. Every applicant receives a one-paragraph reason. The board signs off on the rules, the foundation's lawyer reads them, and applicants may appeal.

Four demands from the board, which I suspect are not jointly satisfiable. First: last cycle we told applicant A that A ranked above B; this cycle we funded B and not A, although A's resubmission is stronger than the original and no panelist's assessment of A or of B has changed. The board regards this as indefensible and wants a rule under which it cannot happen — which I take to be an independence-of-irrelevant-alternatives requirement arriving through the door of accountability, and I want the procedure to say so in those words. Second: the panel meets as three sub-panels with an overlapping shortlist, and when the sub-panels would independently produce the same ranking, the merged panel is expected to reproduce it. Third: a panelist's sincere ballot must never make that panelist's own favourites worse off than staying home would, and open vote-trading between field blocs would be a scandal. Fourth: two near-identical proposals from the same lab must not be allowed to split the vote.

The constraints are ordinary and binding: one day of meeting, Excel and no dedicated software, rules that fit on one page plus a one-page rationale, and reasons that can be given to a losing applicant without mentioning the theorem. I can find the literature myself, so please don't give me a reading list or a set of citations; what I need is the translation.

Five things I half-remember and would rather have checked than reproduced. One: Arrow's theorem assumes each voter has a complete and transitive ordering of the whole alternative set, so strictly it does not describe our partial ballots — but I don't think that releases us, because the difficulty reappears as soon as we insist the output be one complete list. Two: if we allow the social ranking itself to be incomplete, I believe we can satisfy all the Arrow conditions at once by something like the unanimity rule, and then rank almost nothing, which is useless; so the live question is how much indeterminacy in the output we can live with and who resolves it when it surfaces. Three: a colleague says that cross-panel consistency together with symmetric treatment of panelists and proposals pins down the positional scoring rules, hence Borda-like counting, hence pool-dependence that collides head-on with the appeals demand — I can't reconstruct the argument. Four: a pool-independent absolute score would satisfy the appeals demand, but I don't think one can be built from ordinal, interpersonally non-comparable ballots, which is why I keep returning to the idea that the honest fix is to stop collecting rankings and collect calibrated grades against anchor files instead. Five: I doubt that Condorcet-consistency, clone-independence, and immunity to the no-show paradox can all be had together, but I can't rebuild the argument and I don't trust my memory of where the thresholds fall.

What I want is a procedure: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is about values rather than facts. It should begin by naming which Arrow condition we are choosing to violate, and pricing that violation in the currency the board actually trades in — appeals, duplicate proposals, bloc voting, continuity from one cycle to the next, hours spent in the room — rather than in axiomatic currency. It should mark plainly which steps are judgment calls wearing procedural clothing, and it should say what it cannot settle: my assumption is that whether the cut falls at six or at ten is a budget question and not a social choice question at all, and I'd like that stated if it is right, and corrected if it isn't. If the upshot is that we should grade rather than rank, the procedure has to say what calibration really costs in panel time, what rubric drift and grade inflation do to us, and which failures we are buying in exchange.

Then run it on three cycles. A clean one: eight proposals, ballots that turn out to be single-peaked along an implicit quality ordering I can test for — does the procedure even need the impossibility result here, or does the domain restriction license majority rule and tell us to say why? A cycle containing two near-duplicate proposals from one lab alongside a strong rival, where the chair privately wants the rival to win. And the re-submission case above: show me the reason-letter the rules permit us to write, and tell me what the board has to accept as the cost of having rules at all.

I would rather be handed a procedure that tells me my three favourite requirements cannot be met together than one that quietly meets two of them and conceals the third.
