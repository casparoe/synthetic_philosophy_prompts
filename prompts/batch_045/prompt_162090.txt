I'm taking a formal epistemology seminar this term and we spent most of last week on updating rules, and I came out of it realising I have no idea what I actually think about learning conditionals. The case we started from was the Judy Benjamin setup: a soldier is parachuted into a square territory split into a Red half and a Blue half, and each half is split into Headquarters and Second Company quarters; she starts out 1/4 on each of the four quadrants, and then gets a radio message from headquarters. I dont remember whether the original version has the odds going the same way in both halves, so let me just fix a version and pile on variants. Here is what my gut says, with quadrant probabilities always listed in the order Red-and-Second-Company, Red-and-Headquarters, Blue-and-Second-Company, Blue-and-Headquarters:

1. Message: "can't tell where you are; if you're in Red the odds are 3:1 that you're in Second Company, and if you're in Blue the odds are also 3:1 that you're in Second Company." My verdict: 3/8, 1/8, 3/8, 1/8 — Second Company goes to 3/4 overall, Red stays at 1/2, because the message says nothing about colour.
2. Same shape, but the odds run opposite ways in the two halves — 3:1 for Second Company within Red, 3:1 for Headquarters within Blue. My verdict: 3/8, 1/8, 1/8, 3/8. Colour margin still 1/2, sector margin still 1/2, but the two are now correlated. Note that every margin I can look at is unchanged and yet something clearly changed; that's the bit we argued about most.
3. A message that constrains only one branch: "if you're in Second Company the odds are 3:1 that you're in Red," with nothing said about Headquarters. My verdict: sector margin stays 1/2, inside Second Company it's 3:1 Red, inside Headquarters it stays 1:1, so Red goes up to 5/8 — i.e. 3/8, 1/4, 1/8, 1/4.
4. The one I flip-flop on: the message says flat out, with certainty, "if you're in Red territory, you're in Second Company." Sometimes I think: delete Red-and-Headquarters, renormalise, 1/3 each, so Red drops to 1/3. Sometimes I think: it's a conditional, it tells me nothing about colour, so Red stays at 1/2 and the Red mass just piles into Second Company — 1/2, 0, 1/4, 1/4.
5. Sequence case: I recieve message 2 and then message 3. Thinking about 2 first, message 3 turns out to be already satisfied and nothing moves. Thinking about 3 first and then 2, I end up at 15/32, 5/32, 3/32, 9/32, which satisfies 2 but violates what 3 told me — Red given Second Company comes out 5/6, not 3/4. Order shouldn't matter here, and I can't tell which of those two commitments to drop.
6. Protocol case: the very same sentence as in 4, except I know the operator sends that sentence only if I'm in Second Company (if I'm in Headquarters she sends the mirror-image sentence). Then obviously I go to Second Company for sure. So the update can't be read off the content of the sentence alone. But in cases 1 through 4 I want to say the answer doesn't depend on unstated facts about the broadcast protocol, and I don't know what the principled default is when none is specified. One friend — a stats PhD student — just says "condition on the total evidence and the whole puzzle evaporates," which sounds too easy but I can't say exactly why.

The rule I've been trying to defend is: a conditional message imposes constraints on conditional probabilities; hold fixed the margin over the antecedent partition, hold fixed the conditional structure inside the branches the message is silent about, and change nothing else. That gets me 1, 2, 3 and the second verdict in 4, but not the first verdict in 4, and it's order-dependent in 5. Someone else in the class suggested instead: minimise relative entropy from your *prior* subject to all the constraints you've learned so far (from the prior, not from your current credences, so that order can't matter). That agrees with me on 1 and 2 and gives 1/3 each on 4, but when I tried it on case 3 it gave me roughly 0.62 for Red and dragged the Second Company margin down to about 0.47, neither of which I expected; and someone pointed out that if you make the conditional in 4 just short of certain — say the odds within Red are 99:1 for Second Company — the entropy rule leaves Red near 1/2, and then jumps to 1/3 the moment the conditional is certain, which feels like a bug somewhere.
