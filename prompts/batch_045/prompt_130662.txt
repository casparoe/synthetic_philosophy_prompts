hey — I'm a TA for an intro logic course and we run the surprise exam paradox as a discussion section every year, so ive ended up with a pile of half-formed views about it that I've never had to write down. Before I inflict them on forty freshmen I'd like to know if they hang together. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) "Surprise" means: on no evening of the week does the class know, going to bed, that the exam is the next morning. Purely epistemic. I reject the "you won't be able to deduce the day from this very announcement" reading as the meaning of surprise — surprise is about what you know, not about what's derivable. (At the same time I think that self-referential version is a genuinely different and much more interesting puzzle. I just don't think it's the one we tell the freshmen.)

(2) The one-day version — she announces on Sunday that there will be exactly one exam, it's Monday, and you won't know tonight which day it is — is flatly unsatisfiable, and I take it as the model for what goes wrong everywhere else.

(3) The ordinary five-day version is true if she picks Wednesday and the class is a normal undergraduate class that never runs the backward induction. And I think the induction is invalid anyway: the class can't help itself to the announcement as a premise in an argument about what the announcement entitles them to conclude.

(4) My go-to move when someone says a surprise exam is impossible: the die version. She makes the usual announcement on Sunday, then rolls a fair five-sided die in front of everybody, looks at the result herself without revealing it, and puts the exam on whichever day came up. I take this to guarantee the announcement is true and to dissolve the whole thing.

(5) A variant I made up for section: the true date is printed in the department newsletter that's been sitting in a pile in the hallway since Sunday, and nobody in the class ever picks it up. I want to say that then it isn't a genuine surprise — the information was right there — so the announcement is false whatever day she picked.

One thing I'm stubborn about: I don't want the truth of what she said on Sunday to depend on who happens to be in the room. If a transfer student who's a perfect logician joins on Tuesday, that shouldn't flip the truth value of her announcement. So if the cheapest fix relativizes everything to the class's reasoning ability, tell me that's what you're doing.

Audit this for me. Give me exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no intro and no summing up at the end: the problems in the set ordered worst first, each with what I'd have to give up. If some of these are only apparent problems or are fine as they stand, put them low rather than leaving them out, and say why they dissolve. Make the last item the single change that would clean up the most.
