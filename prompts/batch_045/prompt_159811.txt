I've recently become the person who signs off on what our team's internal assistant is allowed to do, and there's a pattern I keep noticing that I don't have a word for.

Over about eighteen months, the instructions we give the system have changed shape, and I don't think anyone decided that they should. We began by writing out the procedure: run this command, put the output in that file, stop if the exit status is nonzero. Then we began writing the outcome instead: make the test suite green. Then: cut the staging bill. Then: find and fix whatever is making mobile signup drop. At each step the instruction got shorter, checking it got less mechanical, and our vocabulary shifted — we now talk about the system's budget, its permissions, whether it may open a pull request that nobody reads first. The same thing has happened in my own use: I no longer ask it to draft replies to three named emails, I ask it to keep my inbox from becoming a problem.

Two features seem to be the heart of it. First, the drift isn't driven by the system getting more capable. It's driven by specification being expensive: no one will write out the procedure for reducing the staging bill, because writing it out is most of the work. We hand over the choice of means precisely because that's the part we can't be bothered to specify — and the moment we do, whatever it is we've got is not being used as a tool in the sense our own documentation still uses. Second, it doesn't run backwards. Once the harness, the dashboards, and the review process are built around outcome-shaped requests, reinstating procedural ones is itself a project with a cost, and so it never happens.

It isn't the failure mode where a system finds a loophole in a badly written goal: that seems downstream of what I mean, and it requires the goal to be wrong, whereas here the goal is exactly what we want. It isn't quite the economists' principal-and-agent setup either, since that assumes the delegate has ends of its own that can come apart from the delegator's, and that isn't what makes this hard. A speaker at a conference called it autonomy creep, which I suspect is a slogan for the thing rather than a concept with edges.

So: is there an established concept for drift from specifying means to specifying ends, driven by the cost of specification rather than by the delegate's capabilities, and for the fact that the drift ratchets? Where does it live, and if I've identified the mechanism wrongly, what is the nearest concept that does fit? If there is nothing established, I'd like a name and a definition tight enough to say what doesn't count — including whether the same pattern shows up between human managers and human reports, or whether the case where the delegate is a model is a genuinely different animal. A label won't settle what I need settled: our legal team wants one sentence in a customer-facing document saying whether this is a tool or an agent, and I want to be able to explain why no one sentence can be honest.
