Suppose David Hume could be shown the following arrangement and asked to judge it.

A medium-sized trading country, Meridia, has abolished its central bank. Its unit of account, the merid, is fixed by constitutional provision to a stated quantity of a decentralized token whose total supply is set by its protocol: no government, bank or individual can ever create more of it. A treasury reserve holds token equal to the whole existing stock of merids, and the constitution forbids the treasury to buy or sell that reserve, to lend against it, or to redeem a merid for anything at all. Private banks may lend out merids they already hold, but there is no institution anywhere in the system that can bring a new merid into existence. All wages, debts, contracts and taxes are denominated in merids; foreigners may hold and trade them freely, and there are no restrictions on capital moving in or out. Assume that the token has no use anywhere in the world other than as a means of payment and as something to hold, and that its purchasing power over goods swings violently from year to year, because most of the world's demand for it is a demand to hold it as an asset rather than to spend it. Now suppose that over a decade Meridia's manufacturers become markedly more productive than their foreign rivals, and the country runs large, persistent trade surpluses.

What would Hume say? I want more than the verdict that he would have disliked the scheme, and more than a rehearsal of the price-specie-flow argument. Trace his adjustment mechanism step by step and say where, if anywhere, each step still goes through when the money is a fixed-supply token rather than a metal: the inflow that expands domestic money, the rise in domestic prices and wages, the absorption of the extra money into uses other than money, the response of the anchor's own production, the eventual turnaround of trade. Which steps are doing the real work in his account, and which were incidental features of gold? Given that in his essay on money he held that a gradual increase in the quantity of money does not merely raise prices but animates industry during the interval before prices have fully adjusted, would he treat a completely rigid money stock as a defect of the same kind as a shrinking one, and would he expect Meridia's surpluses to be self-correcting at all? Given his hostility to public credit and to paper money that lets a state spend without feeling it immediately, is there nonetheless an argument he could offer in favour of the arrangement, on the ground that it removes discretion rather than merely constraining quantity? Or does his claim that the value of money rests on nothing but a convention which must settle on some determinate standard push him the other way, toward distrusting a measure whose purchasing power is at the mercy of foreign asset demand? And where, precisely, does his apparatus fall silent: what could he not say about a world in which the dominant cross-border flow is purchases of assets rather than payment for goods?
