I teach a final-year seminar on probability, evidence, and anthropic reasoning. I need a second reading of one exam answer before I finalise marks, and I would rather have an independent assessment than my own. Material from the exam paper is reproduced below, followed by the answer in question.

Background supplied to candidates on the exam paper:

Two principles for handling indexical evidence, stated as they were stated in the handout:

- Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA). An observer should reason as if they were a random sample drawn from the set of all actually existing observers in their reference class. Where e is the observer's total evidence, including its indexical component: P(H | e) ∝ P(H) · n_e(H) / N(H), with n_e(H) the number of observers in the reference class whose evidence is of type e, and N(H) the total number of observers in the reference class, conditional on H.

- Self-Indication Assumption (SIA). An observer should reason as if they were a random sample drawn from the set of all possible observers, weighted by the probability of those observers existing. Equivalently, the fact that one exists is itself evidence favouring hypotheses on which more observers exist: P(H | one exists) ∝ P(H) · N(H).

- Candidates were told that the two principles can be applied in combination, in which case the factors compose: P(H | e) ∝ P(H) · N(H) · n_e(H)/N(H) = P(H) · n_e(H). They were also told to assume throughout that observer numbers are finite and that each hypothesis specifies an exact finite count.

The question set (25 marks: 8 for part (a), 9 for part (b), 8 for part (c)).

Consider the following case, which I will call the Far Side case. Two cosmological hypotheses, H_S and H_B, are assigned equal prior probability and fit all astronomical evidence available to us equally well. H_S says the universe contains 10^11 observers in total, all of them located in the region we can observe, all of them with evidence of the same type as ours (call it e). H_B says the universe contains 10^20 observers in total: exactly 10^11 of them are in our observable region with evidence type e, and the remaining observers are in regions that lie permanently beyond our cosmological horizon, so that we cannot observe them, signal to them, or ever receive evidence distinguishing their situation from ours; they enjoy a different evidence type, call it e′.

(a) State and compute what SSA, SIA, and the combined SSA+SIA treatment each imply about the relative credibility of H_S and H_B in the Far Side case. Show your arithmetic.

(b) Compare the Far Side case with a well-known case in which two cosmological theories, equally supported by all available evidence, differ only in that one predicts vastly more observers than the other, and in which a philosopher tells the physicists that they need not bother building the expensive experiment that would decide between them: the larger theory is to be preferred on anthropic grounds alone, and by a wide margin. (This is the case usually labelled the Presumptuous Philosopher, associated with Bostrom's discussion of the self-indication assumption.) In that case, as in the Far Side case, the disagreement between the hypotheses is invisible to observation, yet a verdict is supposed to follow from armchair reasoning about observer counts. Explain precisely which of the two principles generates that verdict and why, and then say whether the Far Side case is genuinely a further problem or merely the same problem with the numbers rearranged. Your answer should address the role of the reference class in each case.

(c) Assess the significance of the Far Side case. In it, SSA lowers the credibility of H_B by a factor of a billion on account of observers who are in principle unobservable by us, while SIA raises it by the same factor. Does the case tell against one principle more than the other, does it tell symmetrically against both, or does it tell us only something about the reference class? Argue for a conclusion.

The answer I want graded follows, reproduced verbatim.

(a) SSA tells us to treat ourselves as a random draw from the observers in the reference class. Under H_S there are 10^11 observers and all of them have evidence type e, so the chance of finding myself with e is 10^11/10^11 = 1. Under H_B the same 10^11 observers have e, but the total is 10^20, so the chance is 10^11/10^20 = 10^-9. SSA therefore favours H_S by a factor of 10^9, which makes the posterior probability of H_S essentially 1. SIA inverts this: it says that my very existence is evidence weighted by the number of observers a hypothesis predicts, so H_B receives a boost of 10^20 against H_S's 10^11, and SIA favours H_B by that same factor of 10^9. Applying both principles together, the two factors are exactly reciprocal and cancel, so the posterior odds come out equal to the prior odds, 1:1. This is the same mechanism as in the Doomsday argument, where the likelihood of a hypothesis is inversely proportional to the total number of observers it predicts, because in both cases the number of observers with my evidence is held fixed while the total is allowed to vary. The Far Side case is thus the Doomsday argument with a spatial index in place of a temporal one.

(b) The Presumptuous Philosopher is Bostrom's argument that SSA yields an absurd result: given two theories that fit the evidence equally well, SSA instructs us to prefer the one that says the universe contains more observers, so the philosopher may tell the physicists to cancel the experiment. The lesson of the case is that SIA is the correct principle: the number of observers a theory predicts really is evidence for that theory, since a world containing more observers contains more chances for someone like me to exist, and the appearance of paradox comes from SSA's refusal to count merely possible observers. The Far Side case is simply the Presumptuous Philosopher with the numbers rearranged. In both cases the whole dispute is about whether to multiply by N(H), and SSA gets the wrong answer in both, so nothing new is contributed: the observers beyond the horizon play exactly the role played by the surplus observers in Bostrom's example. The reference class does not help here either. SIA quantifies over all possible observers and so has no reference-class problem at all, whereas SSA's reference class is an unconstrained free parameter, which is why SSA can be made to return any verdict one likes.

(c) The conclusion is that SSA should be rejected. In the Far Side case it lowers my credence in a hypothesis by nine orders of magnitude because of observers I can never observe, interact with, or confirm; and unlike SIA's verdict, its verdict is not even stable, since if the reference class is narrowed to observers within our horizon the disfavouring of H_B vanishes altogether. A principle whose output shifts by a factor of a billion depending on an unconstrained stipulation is not doing genuine epistemic work. SIA, by contrast, returns a clean verdict in every variant of the case, and applying the two principles together returns us to the priors, which is just what an observer who has not yet looked beyond the horizon should expect. I therefore conclude that the Far Side case is a decisive argument for SIA and against SSA.
