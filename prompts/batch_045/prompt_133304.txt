I have been placed, I suspect for professional reasons rather than gastronomic ones, on my university's food procurement committee. We relet a contract each year covering some forty line items across halls, cafés, and catered events. Our remit from the administration is to reduce the animal suffering attributable to the university's food purchases. Our constraints are these: two hours per meeting, no budget for original research, no ability to audit suppliers beyond what a procurement officer can request, and an obligation to publish a short written justification for every change, because our decisions are contested in the student press, by the catering contractor, and by the upland farmers whose produce we are under political pressure to buy.

The reason the committee keeps deadlocking is that any single item comparison runs several quite different mechanisms at once, and members keep winning arguments by switching between them. There are the animals slaughtered for the product itself. There are the animals killed in growing its feed — cattle needing far more feed per kilogram of meat than poultry, and feed that is itself crop. There are the animals killed by the plant foods we might substitute instead, since harvesting and tilling kill field animals outright and destroy the habitat that supported them; it is not obvious that the vegetarian option is the one with the smaller animal toll. There are the wild animals living on the land whatever we do with it, whose numbers and whose welfare depend on which system occupies it — rough grazing supports one assemblage, arable monoculture another, abandonment or forestry a third. And there are the animals never bred because we did not buy, which some members count as a benefit and others as nothing at all. On top of this, death and suffering are distinct harms, head-counts are a poor proxy for either, and the ranking flips depending on the denominator: per kilogram, per calorie, per gram of protein, or per tray actually served, where what matters is what displaces what on the plate.

What I want is a decision procedure my committee could actually run, not an essay. It should take as input one proposed substitution — this item for that item, at this volume, from this sourcing region — and return an ordering or a refusal to order. Please build it so that it satisfies the following.

The steps must be ordered, with a statement of what counts as an adequate answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a no, and where it hands the question to a person because it is a question of values rather than fact. It must begin by fixing a counterfactual baseline: what happens to this land, this supply contract, and this volume if we do not make the change — including the point that supply chains contract in fixed batches, so a reduction below some threshold may change no animal's fate at all, and a procedure that ignores this will systematically overstate what small changes accomplish. It must fix the unit of account explicitly and defend the choice; I would propose welfare-adjusted life-days per unit served, computed against that baseline, but I want to know whether that metric can bear the weight. It must say what to do when the credible estimates overlap — where the honest answer is that the two items are not distinguishable at our resolution, and the procedure should be able to return that verdict rather than manufacturing one. It must say how to treat animals of genuinely uncertain sentience, whether by discounting for credence, by refusing to aggregate across the uncertainty, or by some threshold rule, and whichever it chooses it must be stated in terms we could defend to a protester rather than stipulated. And it must be usable by people who have never opened a philosophy book in under an hour per item with the data a procurement officer can actually obtain.

Then run it on three live proposals. First: switching from standard poultry to a slower-growing, higher-welfare breed that costs substantially more and requires noticeably more birds to deliver the same tonnage. Second: on the marginal upland pasture that cannot be cropped, either continuing to buy grass-fed beef from it or replacing it with imported plant protein, where the counterfactual uses of the land are agricultural abandonment or commercial forestry. Third: adding an insect-protein product, where the numbers of individuals are orders of magnitude larger and the sentience uncertainty is at its worst.

Two further requests. Give me at least one verdict that runs against my own prior, which is that these comparisons will generally favour the plant-based option; I would rather be told where I am wrong than have the procedure tuned to ratify me. And tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do — which comparisons it is constitutionally unable to settle, which inputs, if wrong, would reverse a result, and where a real decision would still have to be made by someone voting rather than by anyone applying a rule.
