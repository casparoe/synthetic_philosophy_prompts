I sit on a small committee that is rewriting the eligibility criteria for a home-care program, and the draft rule turns on clauses like "clinically significant deterioration in independent functioning" and "requires substantial daily assistance." We cannot avoid vague predicates: every sharpened substitute we tried either misclassifies cases we already agree about or invites gaming. So before I read any further into the vagueness literature, I want the specification I should be holding theories to: the desiderata a theory of vagueness must meet to count as adequate for my purpose, which is not a purely theoretical one. I need a theory good enough to tell us (a) how to write a rule in vague terms while still being able to defend it to an applicant we turn down, and (b) how to reason about appeals from people who fall just outside a cutoff we ourselves imposed.

Write that list. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp. Please cover at least:

- the forced-march sorites and the tolerance intuition, including why the tolerance premise remains compelling even to someone who has accepted a solution;
- the intuition that there is no sharp cutoff, and the difference between denying that intuition and explaining it away;
- higher-order vagueness: borderline borderline cases, and whether the regress can be stopped without mere stipulation;
- disagreement between equally competent, equally well-informed judges, neither of whom is ignorant or at fault, and what the right account of that residual disagreement is;
- predicates with several incommensurable dimensions that trade off against one another, so that a gain on one dimension can compensate for a shortfall on another;
- the contrast cases I keep tripping over: a vague predicate versus an ambiguous one, versus a context-sensitive gradable one, versus one whose borderline cases are generated by imprecise measurement, versus one whose disputed applications come from the concept being normatively contested rather than vague;
- embedding behaviour: negation, conditionals, the definitely operator, comparative and superlative forms, and multi-premise sorites where several dimensions shift slightly at once;
- the fact that any usable rule must impose a sharp cutoff on a vague predicate, and whether such a cutoff is arbitrary, unjust, or merely conventional;
- two constraints specific to our setting: path-independence (the verdict on a case should not depend on which neighbouring cases were put to the committee first, or on the order in which a docket is worked through) and diachronic stability (decisions on near cases should constrain later ones, so that something like precedent can accumulate). If a theory licenses many admissible sharpenings, it should say what makes one of them the right one to write into a rule.

Then flag any subset of these requirements you think cannot be jointly satisfied: if the list itself turns out to be unsatisfiable, that is the most useful thing I could learn. Tell me which single requirement excludes the largest share of the standard positions, and which requirements are really one camp's commitments dressed up as desiderata. Requirements I have not thought of are welcome, provided they arrive with their motivating case. A short scorecard at the end — one line each for the epistemic view, supervaluationism, subvaluationism, degree-theoretic and fuzzy approaches, contextualism, and the view that there are no genuinely vague predicates — would be useful, but the criteria and the tensions among them are what I actually want.
