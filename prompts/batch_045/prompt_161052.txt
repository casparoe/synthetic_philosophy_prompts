I am writing a paper on whether any artificial system could have what Hofstadter calls a strange loop — the point at which a system's symbol for itself becomes part of what that symbol symbolizes — and I have decided not to read another theory of the matter until I know what I should be demanding of one. So: write me the desiderata.

Fix the purpose first, since I take adequacy to be relative to it, and my purpose is double. First, the account has to work as a test. When someone hands me a candidate system — a language model that says I, a robot with a working body-schema, a coupled pair of systems jointly maintaining a single self-model — the account has to tell me what to look for and what would settle the question, stated in terms I could in principle check from the outside, without smuggling in the verdict. Second, it has to tell me what has broken in the clinical cases: the patient who reports that thoughts occurring in their own stream of consciousness are not theirs; the patient whose hand is theirs and acts, and is not theirs in the relevant sense; the patient for whom the first-person pronoun has, over a year, quietly come to pick out a different person than it did. If no single specification can serve both jobs, say so and say what each job forces you to give up.

Here are three configurations I keep running into, described plainly because I do not want to prejudice the analysis with the vocabulary of any school.

Case A. A system maintains an elaborate and accurate model of itself and uses the first-person pronoun correctly in every test anyone can administer from outside. But the model is updated only when other agents report things to it about itself. Nothing the system does with its own I ever alters what that I picks out. Every third-personally observable difference between this system and the real article is zero.

Case B. A system in which the I does feed back: each time it avows something about itself it becomes slightly more the thing it avowed, and after some years of continuous operation what its I picks out has travelled somewhere no observer would have predicted, although at no point was there a discontinuity anyone could date. Outside observers at the end say the thing we started with is no longer here.

Case C. Two systems, separate bodies, separate memory stores, jointly maintaining one self-model that represents a single agent. Each treats the other's memories as entries to which it has privileged access, and neither can operate the self-model alone. Each says I of the pair.

I want the requirements list: what a theory of the self-symbol has to deliver, with the case or consideration behind each requirement — including the ones above, but do not let them exhaust the list, since I suspect they are the ones I happen to have noticed rather than the ones that matter. Mark each requirement non-negotiable or negotiable given the two jobs I described. Say which requirements are genuine demands and which are the preferences of one research tradition dressed up as constraints. In particular I want you to hold the theory to: no assumption of an inner estimator or central executive that the theory then has to explain in turn; the possibility of degree, so that infants, non-human animals, and machines can be placed on the same scale rather than sorted into a binary; the distinction between something being mine and something being done by me; the asymmetry between how I know my own states and how anyone else knows them; and the demand that having a self-symbol make some difference to something — to planning for one's own future, to the extra weight one's own suffering carries, to what one will do for oneself — rather than sitting inertly on top of a system that would behave identically without it.

Two places where I expect the list to eat itself, and where the answer matters more to me than the rest: the demand for public checkability against the possibility that Case A shows the whole phenomenon to be settled by nothing observable at all; and the demand that the I be immune to getting its own object wrong against the demand that using it reshape what it refers to. If some subset of the desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied, say which, and say what dropping it costs. Tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates, and whether that requirement is defensible on its own terms or is a prejudice of one tradition.

Close with a short scorecard — four columns, no more — against the strange-loop picture, the view that the first-person pronoun is only a rule for referring and not a description of anything, the view that the self is a posit of a model or a narrative the system tells, and the view that what matters is being a subject of experience. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; the scorecard is for orientation only. A table is fine. Skip all bibliographic apparatus — I do not want citations, and I do not want to be told what to read.
