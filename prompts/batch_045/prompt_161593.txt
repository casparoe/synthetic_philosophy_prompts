I'm an undergraduate in a philosophy of physics seminar, and at the end of term I have to defend a short paper in front of an audience that is not my seminar. I've been invited to give a twenty-minute talk to the quantum information group here: roughly six experimentalists who build entangled-photon sources, run loophole-free Bell tests, and use Bell violations to certify randomness and to do device-independent key distribution, plus two theorists whose everyday vocabulary is nonlocal games, the local polytope, the Tsirelson bound, shared randomness, and resource theories. Their working attitude is resolutely operational. They treat "nonlocality" as the name of a resource, indexed by how far a correlation sits outside the local polytope, and none of them believes that a Bell violation implies a causal influence you could exploit to signal; if I tell them that nature is nonlocal, they will hear a claim they already accept, and if I tell them that it isn't, they will suspect I have misunderstood what they mean by the word. They are politely impatient with interpretation talk and will each want to know, in their own terms, what difference my view makes to anything they can compute, certify, or measure. My seminar instructor will be in the room, but she is not the audience I need to survive.

The thesis I am defending is that a Bell violation is evidence against a counterfactual assumption, not against locality, and that "nonlocality" as the quantum information literature uses the term should be reconceived as the failure of a single joint classical model across incompatible measurement contexts rather than as nonlocal causation. I introduce a label for the assumption I think is actually doing the work. Call it cross-context definiteness: in a Bell experiment with wing settings a and b and a variable λ describing the source, it is well-posed to ask, for one and the same run, what each wing would have returned under every combination of settings, so that the four quantities A(a₁), A(a₂), B(b₁), B(b₂) are jointly defined and admit a joint probability distribution. My argument has four steps. First, the CHSH inequality is equivalent to the existence of such a joint distribution over all four quantities, so the violation tells us directly that no joint distribution exists. Second, the constraint that actually encodes relativistic locality is no-signalling — the marginal distribution at one wing does not depend on the distant setting — and quantum mechanics satisfies that constraint exactly, so the locality that matters is not what fails. Third, I propose to treat the hidden variable as defined only relative to a total measurement context, so that cross-context questions are not well-posed rather than answered incorrectly; on that reading the singlet correlations come out without any wing-to-wing influence, and it is not instrumentalism, because it says which questions are legitimate. Fourth, the payoff for this audience: the resource they are exploiting is not nonlocal causation but the impossibility of embedding all measurement contexts in one classical probability space, which is precisely what lets a correlation be certified against any adversary limited to shared randomness.

Please draft my Q&A preparation sheet for the question period that follows the talk. Concretely, I would like:

The eight objections I am most likely to get from this specific group, ordered by how much damage they would do if I handled them badly, weighted by how likely they are to come up. I want them phrased the way these people would actually phrase them — in the idiom of boxes outside the local polytope, no-signalling, shared randomness, device-independent certification, and loophole-free experiments — not the way a philosopher of physics would phrase them, and not the way an internet commenter would.

For each objection, a reply I could actually deliver in about ninety seconds, plus an honest note on where that reply is soft or where I would be bluffing. I would rather know now which of my answers is a deflection.

A judgment about which two or three of these objections have genuine force against my position, as opposed to being verbal or based on a misreading. Please say which of my four steps each serious objection targets, since I suspect most of the damage will land on step three rather than on steps one and two.

What I should concede explicitly, on the first or second slide, before anyone raises it. I would rather give up ground voluntarily than be caught defending it.

The one reply that has to land for the talk to be worth giving: the single point where, if I fumble it, the whole project looks like terminology reform and nothing more.

The question I am hoping nobody asks. My own guess is that it has something to do with the fact that device-independent and self-testing arguments themselves reason counterfactually about what a pair of boxes would have output for settings that were not in fact chosen — so that if I reject cross-context definiteness I may be sawing off the branch that the group's own security proofs sit on. If that is the right guess, tell me what the honest answer is; if there is a worse question lurking, tell me that instead.

Please write the sheet in American English.
