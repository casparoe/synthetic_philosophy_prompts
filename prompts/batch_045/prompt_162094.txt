I teach a final-year seminar that sits between pragmatics and moral philosophy, and I am building one session around the observation that directness and indirectness are not, as they are usually presented, two points on a single scale running from cruel to kind. A colleague from Utrecht defends the notorious directness of her compatriots as a form of respect: you owe people the truth in a shape they can act on, and wrapping it is a way of managing your own discomfort at their expense. A friend who has taught for years in Kyoto defends indirection on exactly the same ground: you owe people the preservation of face and of the relationship, and bluntness is a refusal to pay that. Every example I have been able to find in the material I assign stacks the deck — the blunt speaker is simply being obtuse, or the indirect speaker is simply lying, or one of them is a cultural outsider who misreads the local code. I want a case that lets both of my friends be right about the principle and still converge on the verdict about a particular utterance.

So: construct one scenario, in a single paragraph, realistic and ordinary — a workplace, a clinic, a housing office, a department meeting, nothing exotic — in which the indirect version of what is said is morally worse than the blunt version, with the verdict surviving all of the following. First, the two utterances must carry the same propositional content, or near enough, and neither may assert anything false, so that nothing reduces to a case about lying. Second, both parties must be fluent in the same conventions: no outsider failing to decode a hint, no one who simply does not know the local code. Third, the difference must not come from delivery — no sarcasm, raised voice, curtness, insulting wording, or timing chosen to humiliate; vary the degree of directness and hold fixed the content, the relationship, the stakes, and the speaker's actual goodwill toward the hearer. Fourth, the verdict must be robust across rationales: a defender of directness-as-respect and a defender of indirectness-as-tact should each, reasoning from their own rationale, condemn the indirect utterance, and for different reasons, which you should state explicitly.

The mechanism I have in mind — use it, or replace it with a better one and say why — is that indirectness can be attributability-destroying rather than face-protective. By leaving the speaker a deniable way out, it hands the hearer the interpretive labour of working out that something has been refused, denied, or brought to an end, while leaving them nothing quotable that they could act on, appeal against, or even be certain of having heard. That is harmless, even generous, when the hearer has nothing at stake in the content; it becomes a way of quietly charging them for the speaker's comfort when they do, and worst of all when the speaker is the party with the power.

After the scenario, give me: the two utterances written out as they would actually be said; about 300 words walking through what each of the two rationales delivers, showing that the verdict is generated by the directness variable and not stipulated by the set-up; an isolation check naming the single feature that does the work, with the case re-run with that feature removed to show it collapses back into an ordinary case where bluntness is the cruel option; a variance check that flips one further parameter — make the person the utterance is about an overhearer rather than the addressee, or make the hearer someone with no decision to make on the basis of the content — and say whether the verdict flips; and the strongest attack a defender of indirectness could mount against the case, together with your reply. Commit to a position throughout: tell me plainly that the case works, or that it does not and where it breaks, and argue for one mechanism as the correct explanation rather than listing candidates. Close with one sentence on the load-bearing assumption the case rests on — I would rather have an honest note on where it is fragile than a case that only looks clean.
