I'm coming at this from a slightly odd angle, so let me set it up properly. I'm a final-year PhD student in observational cosmology; my thesis is on Bayesian model comparison for dark-energy equations of state using galaxy clustering and weak-lensing cross-correlations, so the vocabulary I actually think in is the one I use every day: likelihoods and evidences, Bayes factors, priors over parameter space and the Occam factors they carry, the look-elsewhere effect and trials factors, hard versus soft selection functions, posterior predictive checks, model misspecification, and the difference between a likelihood that is uninformative and one that is merely expensive to evaluate. My philosophy of science is about what you'd expect from a working physicist who has read around Popper, Kuhn and Lakatos and absorbed some Bayesian practice: enough to follow an argument, not enough to place it in the literature, and I can't chase references for a few months, so please don't give me any.

What I want translated into that vocabulary is the cluster of arguments used to defend research programmes — string theory and the inflationary multiverse are the usual examples — that do not at present yield any prediction distinguishable from their competitors'. As I understand them, and please correct me if I'm caricaturing, there are three moves plus a fourth that sits slightly apart. First, the no-alternatives argument: a large, well-funded, strongly motivated community has spent decades trying and has not produced a coherent rival, and that failure is itself evidence, on the grounds that if the programme were on the wrong track we should expect the search to have found something by now. Second, the unexpected-interconnections argument: the theory repeatedly turns out to explain what it was not built to explain, the stock example being a framework constructed for quantum gravity turning out to reproduce the black-hole entropy formula. Third, the meta-inductive argument from the field's track record: this style of theorising has previously led, after a long lag, to theories that were eventually confirmed, so present non-empirical success is a leading indicator. Fourth, the anthropic or landscape move, where a theory predicts a distribution over observable constants rather than a value, and the observation is conditioned on our being here to make it.

Please take those four one at a time and restate them as faithfully as you can in my terms, without laundering them into something they are not: I don't want the no-alternatives argument quietly converted into a Bayes factor over hypotheses I have actually written down, or unexpected interconnections dressed up as a posterior predictive check on data I have actually got, unless you tell me that is what the argument amounts to and why. I've also been playing with a distinction of my own that I'd like you to use or shoot down: I call it likelihood starvation, the situation in which a model's likelihood is essentially flat across every experiment I can realistically build, so no accessible data can move it, as against prior pumping, arguments that raise a theory's probability without touching its likelihood at all. If those are bad names for real distinctions, say so and propose better ones. Then, and this is the part I really want, give me the residue report: for each of the four, what maps cleanly onto my machinery, what crosses only with distortion, and what has no counterpart in it at all — and for the residue, say whether it indicts my vocabulary or the original argument. My own suspicion, which I would rather have tested than confirmed, is that the no-alternatives argument is not a likelihood over data but a claim about the generative process of a research community, a model of how good my colleagues are at finding alternatives, and that my toolkit has nowhere to put that; that the meta-inductive argument is a hierarchical prior borrowed from an ill-defined reference class of past theories, the sort of untestable hyperprior I would quietly drop from an analysis; and that the anthropic move differs from an ordinary selection function because my selection functions are fixed by the survey rather than being part of the model, whereas the measure over a landscape looks like it is part of the theory and is itself contested, though I may be over-dramatising what is really just a trials factor. Please write the whole thing in British English. I intend to hand it to my supervisor and to two theorists in the department, one of whom thinks this entire debate is empty and one of whom thinks it is the most interesting problem in the foundations of physics, so it needs to be fair to both and to stand entirely on its own.
