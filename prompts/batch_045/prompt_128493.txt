I'm a novelist, and I need help with an argument I have put in a character's mouth. Early in the book a philanthropist figure corners a room of donors with the shallow pond made famous by Peter Singer: you would wade in and ruin your clothes to save a drowning toddler, so neither distance nor the number of other sufferers can be doing the moral work, so you owe vastly more than you give. My narrator — meant to be the sharper mind — answers with a variant I invented rather than borrowed, and I can no longer tell whether I have given him something acute or something merely sophistical. Call it the crowded pond: a hundred children are in the water, a hundred adults are on the bank, no one acting alone can save anyone, the pond drains only once some threshold number wade in together, nobody can see how many others are about to move, and each wader pays the same cost whether or not the threshold is met. He concludes that his own going in changes nothing about the outcome, that the same is true of a donation, and therefore that the argument which convicted the donors does not reach him and everyone may stay dry.

Please work as a professional moral philosopher would, and before you commit to an answer spend a few sentences getting clear on which features of the original pond are load-bearing — the certainty of the rescue, the single rescuer, the visible and identified victim, the bounded one-off cost, the absence of any coordination problem, the fact that nobody else is already wading — and which are only scenery. Then audit the crowded pond: trace the correspondences one by one, tell me where the mapping breaks, say which features my variant imports that the actual case of charitable giving lacks and which features of that case it cannot represent at all, and judge whether the no-difference premise is doing all the work or can stand on its own. Tell me what happens to the argument if the bystanders can signal to one another before committing, and what happens if the same pond refills every morning and the same hundred people return to the bank each day. Finish by saying whether my narrator's conclusion survives, whether the scene should end with him unanswered or with someone replying to him, and, if the crowded pond is the wrong picture, what image would do the job he needs it to do. Write in continuous prose, as though in a letter to me: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no table, since I want to be able to read it aloud.
