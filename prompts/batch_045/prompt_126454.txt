I sit on a hospital ethics committee, and I've noticed that my verdicts about who is dead don't match any of the standard formulas, so I'd like help extracting the principle I am actually using. My verdicts: locked-in syndrome, fully conscious and almost completely paralyzed — alive, obviously. A patient meeting the ordinary clinical criteria for whole-brain death, still on a ventilator — dead. A patient in a persistent vegetative state, cortex destroyed but brainstem intact, breathing spontaneously with sleep-wake cycles — dead. A newborn delivered without a cerebrum, breathing on its own, with brainstem reflexes and responses to noxious stimulation — I cannot call it dead, even though by the reasoning behind my previous verdict it should be; this is the pair I cannot reconcile. A man with twelve minutes of no circulation after a witnessed arrest who then recovers neurologically intact — never dead, not even during the arrest. A patient declared dead after the usual waiting period following withdrawal of ventilation — dead; but in a protocol I read about, where a machine restores circulation to the body while a balloon blocks the arteries to the head, I still want to say dead and cannot say why, unless the waiting period itself is doing all the work. And a body maintained for months after a brain death diagnosis, healing wounds, mounting inflammatory responses, and in the reported pregnant cases gestating a fetus to viability — dead, but uncomfortably.

The principle I have been trying out is that death is the irreversible loss of the capacity for consciousness, and that everything else the brain does matters only as machinery supporting that capacity — so no function the body can be made to perform artificially, and no merely vegetative integration, counts as keeping someone alive. Test that against my verdicts, repair it or replace it with one or two candidates that fit better, state each clause together with the case that motivates it, and say plainly which of my verdicts each candidate forces me to abandon. Then give me what the best candidate predicts for the two cases where I have no settled view: a living human brain maintained in a laboratory with no body attached, and a human body whose brain has been entirely destroyed but whose circulation, oxygenation, temperature and hormone levels are regulated indefinitely by an external controller, so that it digests, heals and holds its internal state steady. Finally, tell me how confident you are in each part of what you have given me, and single out the one claim you are least sure of.
