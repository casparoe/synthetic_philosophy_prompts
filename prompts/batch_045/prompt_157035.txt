I'm running a one-off seminar next month on incommensurable values and hard choices, and I want to use Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" as the text — but I refuse to spend two hours on the usual "it's a critique of utilitarianism" reading that everybody has already seen a dozen times. So here's the angle I want, and tell me straight if its the wrong one.

People say you can't compare those two things, and it seems to me they mean at least two different failures. Call the first scale-failure: there's no covering value, no common unit, in which both items can even be measured, so the comparison never gets off the ground. Call the second weight-failure: there is a common currency and both items have some magnitude in it, but one of them is the kind of thing no quantity of the other can buy back — it behaves like a lexical priority, or a protected value — so the comparison never closes even though the unit is right there. Question: which one is Omelas actually dramatising? And where the story stays ambiguous, say so, and say where a philosopher would have to pick a side.

The bit I keep tripping over: the child's misery isn't merely balanced against the city's happiness, it's a condition of it — the festival, the prosperity, the whole lovely place is downstream of the suffering in that cellar. Does that constitution relation change the shape of the choice, so that it isn't a trade-off between two independent values at all? Or is that a distraction from the real issue?

Then the ones who walk away. Reading one: they're the living proof of incomparability, they simply won't accept the trade. Reading two: they're not making a comparability claim at all — they're refusing to be beneficiaries, which is a claim about complicity or integrity, not about value. Reading three, from a friend of mine: on one view of hard choices, some alternatives are on a par — genuinely comparable, but neither better, nor worse, nor equally good — so there is nothing there to discover, and what the agent does is put her agency behind one of them and in doing so create a reason that wasn't there before. Do the walkers-away look like agents of that view? Does the story make them look rationally permitted, or just damaged?

Be honest in both directions: where does the story illuminate my distinction, and where does it resist it — where am I dumping a philosopher's puzzle onto a story that's really about complicity, or about how ordinary people live inside gross inequality — and if my distinction is the wrong tool, what would fit better? I'd also like three or four passages to put in front of the group: the cellar, the narrator's insistence that the Omelans aren't simple-minded happy folk, the festival, and the walkers-away going out onto the road. Just describe them, no exact wording needed, I've got the text.

Finish with a last line beginning "Verdict:" and then a single short phrase, under fifteen words, naming the reading you landed on — something like "Verdict: weight-failure, with the walkers-away as integrity not incomparability" — so I can paste it into my notes.
