I convene a fortnightly reading group on interpretability — about a dozen people, mostly ML researchers with no formal philosophy training, plus one or two philosophers of science. We keep talking past each other about whether neural networks really represent anything, and I want to replace the argument with a diagnostic instrument. Please build me a decision tree that takes a person from a shared starting commitment to one of the named positions in the current debate about realism about interpretability.

The starting commitment, which every branch should presuppose: dictionary-learning methods trained on a model's internal activations reliably yield directions that are stable across inputs, that causally influence the model's outputs when intervened on, and that can often be given a one-sentence description in ordinary language ('fires on text about golden retrievers'). Nobody in the tree is allowed to deny that. The disagreement to be mapped is what follows from it.

Requirements for the instrument:

1. At most seven branch questions and at most ten leaves. Every branch node must be phrased as a question a researcher could answer without knowing which position it leads to — no node may amount to 'are you a realist about representations?'. At least five of the nodes must be keyed to one of the cases below rather than to agreement with an abstract claim; describe each case in a sentence or two before asking the question that hangs on it.

2. The cases. Treat each as a hypothetical and assume it; add concrete detail only where needed to make the question answerable.
   - The rebasement case. Apply an invertible random linear map to a model's residual stream and compensate for it exactly in the following layer's weights, so input–output behaviour is unchanged. Every feature direction from the original basis is now scattered across thousands of neurons, and the new basis has directions that look just as clean to a fresh dictionary.
   - The orphaned direction. A linear probe reads property P off the activations at near-perfect accuracy, but ablating or patching that direction changes no output on any input, and no downstream component is sensitive to it.
   - The vestigial feature. During training an auxiliary loss pushed the model to encode P; after training the auxiliary head is discarded and nothing in the network now uses the direction, though it is still perfectly decodable.
   - The fine-tuning chimaera. A model is fine-tuned until the behaviour associated with P disappears entirely while the P-direction survives intact and still drives other behaviour.
   - The granularity case. Training a dictionary of size n yields feature F; training one of size 4n on the same activations yields four finer features, each with its own causal profile, none of which is a superset or subset of another, and no principled criterion picks one granularity over the others.
   - The unglossable feature. A direction is stable across inputs, causally efficacious and read out by several downstream components, but the best available description of its firing pattern is a list of ten thousand tokens with no unifying theme.
   - The probe-resistant case. The model is fine-tuned with a penalty on a probe for P; the probe now reads chance, but the model still tracks P in a direction no probe trained the same way can find.
   - The cross-seed divergence. Two models trained on the same data with different seeds implement the same behaviour, but no direction in one maps to any direction in the other above chance alignment.

3. Leaves. All of the following must appear: robust representational realism; an interventionist or functional-role realism on which content is fixed by what interventions do; a view that fixes content by training history or selected function; an interpreter-relative or stance-dependent view on which representations exist only relative to a chosen decomposition or an interpreter's predictive model; instrumentalist fictionalism about network representations; an error-theoretic view; and eliminativism. Include pluralism about levels of description only if the tree genuinely separates it from the interpreter-relative view; if it does not, say so instead of manufacturing a distinction. Each leaf carries a name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

4. Build from the logical space, then locate the positions. Before naming leaves, set out the branch questions and the combinations of answers they permit; then state which combinations nobody occupies and why — incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored — and which combinations hold more than one named view. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, and say what further case would separate them.

5. Argue only from the cases above and from what the positions themselves claim: no citations, references, reading suggestions or pointers to literature. If you coin a name for a position because the standard one escapes you, mark it as your own coinage.

The purpose behind the instrument: our group's real disagreement is about whether an interpretability result tells us something about the system's own model of the world or only about a decomposition we imposed on it. Optimise the tree for separating views on that question, and let views that agree on it collapse into a single leaf.

Close with a note of roughly 150 words stating how confident you are that the tree partitions the space usefully, and singling out the one branch, leaf or diagnosis you are least confident about, with a brief indication of what would change your mind. Please write in British English, and aim for 1,200–1,600 words in total.
