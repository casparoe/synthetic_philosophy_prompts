The question I set (it is worth 20 marks, weighted roughly 6 for part (a), 6 for part (b), and 8 for part (c)) was as follows:

> **Question 4.** In the brain-in-a-vat argument, is what does the sceptical work the *falsity* of our beliefs, or something else? In your answer you should: (a) set out the closure-based argument from the premise that I do not know that I am not a brain in a vat to a sceptical conclusion; (b) explain the "externalist" or semantic reply — the claim that a brain in a vat could not so much as think, of itself, that it is a brain in a vat, because its words could not refer to brains or vats; (c) consider the following variant, which call *faithful envatment*: you are a brain in a vat, but the computer supplying your sensory inputs is wired to instruments monitoring a real, distant physical environment, so that the great bulk of your ordinary empirical beliefs (that there is a table in front of you, that it is raining outside) are true. Do the closure argument or the externalist reply give us any reason to deny knowledge in the faithful-envatment case? What does your answer to (c) suggest about what the original argument was exploiting?

One of the scripts reads as follows.

> **(a)** Let BIV be the hypothesis that I am, and always have been, a brain in a vat, fed its experiences by a computer. The closure argument has three steps. (i) If I know any ordinary empirical proposition — say, that I have hands — then I know that BIV is false, since my having hands entails that I am not a brain in a vat. (ii) I do not know that BIV is false: my evidence would be exactly the same whether or not BIV were true, and knowledge requires evidence capable of discriminating between the truth and the relevant alternative. (iii) So I do not know that I have hands; and the same reasoning applies to every other empirical belief. Notice that the argument does not require the sceptic to show that BIV is at all probable. It requires only that I cannot rule it out, which is a much weaker and much more plausible claim.
>
> One consequence is worth drawing out, because it is often missed. The argument does not stop at the external world. If BIV is true I have no hands and no body; at most I have experiences as of seeing and touching hands. So the beliefs I would naturally express by saying "it seems to me that there is a hand in front of me" would be false as well; and since I cannot rule BIV out, closure carries the doubt through to my beliefs about my own present experience. Properly understood, then, the closure argument yields a global scepticism and not merely a scepticism about the external world — which is why no merely external-world remedy, such as insisting that perception is generally reliable, can answer it.
>
> **(b)** The externalist reply takes a quite different route. It concedes that BIV is a coherent hypothesis but maintains that we can know it to be false on semantic grounds. Reference to a kind of thing requires the right sort of causal or informational connection with things of that kind. A creature that had always been envatted has had no commerce with brains, vats or computers, so its words "brain", "vat" and "computer" do not refer to brains, vats and computers. The thought it would express by "we are brains in a vat" is therefore not the thought that we are brains in a vat, and the hypothesis cannot be so much as entertained by the creature whose situation it describes. We, who do stand in the relevant connection, can think that thought; and since the two cases differ only in that connection, we are in a position to know that we are not envatted. The reply thus undercuts the sceptic's second premise rather than conceding it.
>
> **(c)** The faithful-envatment case is different again. Here the computer is wired to instruments in a real environment, so my beliefs about that environment are for the most part true, and they are produced by a process that tracks how things are. On any view that ties knowledge to reliable belief-production, I count as knowing that there is a table in front of me. Nor does the closure argument threaten this. Closure does its work when my evidence fails to exclude a possibility in which my beliefs are false; but in the faithful case my beliefs are not false, so there is no failure of evidence to trouble me. And the externalist reply is beside the point here too: it is a thesis about words like "brain" and "vat", whereas the faithful envattee's beliefs — if it has a problem at all — concern tables and rain, which are perfectly ordinary.
>
> I conclude that what the sceptical argument was exploiting all along is the falsity of our beliefs. The ordinary BIV case gets its bite from the supposition that the envatted subject is systematically wrong about the world. Once we stipulate that its beliefs are true, as in faithful envatment, the sceptical worry evaporates and knowledge survives. The interesting question raised by the variant is therefore not whether knowledge is possible under envatment, but how much falsity knowledge can tolerate.
