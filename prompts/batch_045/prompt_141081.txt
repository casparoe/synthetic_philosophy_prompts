I work in multi-agent reinforcement learning — decentralized POMDPs, independent and CTDE learners, self-play and population-based training, emergent communication, and cross-play or zero-shot coordination between separately trained agents. I read no philosophy and have only a working user's grasp of game theory: Nash and correlated equilibria, risk versus payoff dominance, symmetry breaking.

A philosopher friend claims that the standard modern analysis of convention — she called it Lewis's — is roughly what my field keeps rediscovering under names like convention formation in emergent communication. I am suspicious, but I want the claim tested properly rather than dismissed. So: take that analysis as you understand it and restate it clause by clause in the vocabulary I actually use.

The gloss I was given runs roughly like this. A regularity R in the behaviour of a population P in a recurrent situation S is a convention when: (a) almost everyone conforms to R in S; (b) almost everyone expects almost everyone else to conform; (c) almost everyone prefers to conform on condition that the others conform, and there is some alternative regularity in S that would have served about as well, so that what settles the matter is arbitrary rather than forced by the payoffs; and (d) all of (a) through (c), and the fact that they hold, are common knowledge in P. Correct that gloss first if it is wrong, anachronistic, or missing a condition that matters for the cases below — do not translate a strawman.

Rules for the translation:

Map each clause onto something I already have a word for — joint policy, action selection under a policy, opponent model or belief over other agents' policies, equilibrium reached by independent learners, reward function, non-stationarity, centralized training with decentralized execution, exploration, symmetry breaking, risk versus payoff dominance, cross-play score. If the honest mapping is to something my field would call a bug or a nuisance — seeded asymmetry, agent-index leakage, overfitting to training partners — say so rather than reaching for a flattering one.

It is a Nash equilibrium is where I started, not where I want to end. Where the analysis needs more than my equilibrium notion, say exactly what more.

Do not smuggle in my framework's assumptions and present them as translation. In particular, my agents share no parameters, seeds, priors, or training distribution unless I say so, and they have no common knowledge in the strict sense; they have separate belief networks trained against a non-stationary environment. If a clause translates only once I assume weight sharing or centralized training, flag that as a hostage to my machinery rather than a clean mapping.

Two cases to run it on, because generalities will hide the damage.

Case 1, the unlearnable convention. A population trained together converges on a protocol that coordinates it perfectly and is stable indefinitely. Another protocol would have coordinated it just as well. The two are observationally indistinguishable to any agent that has not already adopted one of them: every signal an outsider can observe is compatible with both, and the disambiguating evidence only becomes available once you are already inside, behaving. A new agent cannot bootstrap in from any finite amount of observation; it can only be initialized into the protocol. My field calls this a cross-play failure and tries to eliminate it — other-play, population diversity, penalties on symmetry breaking. Separately: in my setups a convention does switch, but only when some sizeable fraction of the population drifts at once; a single deviating agent is simply crushed by the training signal. Now: does Lewis's analysis, as glossed, count the unlearnable protocol as a convention, and does anything in its conditions require that a convention be acquirable by a newcomer, or that it be switchable by anything short of a coalition? If not, is that a defect in the analysis, or a sign that my field uses the word for something narrower?

Case 2, the sanction-driven regularity. Same coordination problem, but the designer has added a per-step penalty, applied by an external critic, for deviating from R. Agents conform, and expect each other to conform; remove the penalty while leaving expectations untouched and conformity unravels within an episode. There is no punishment by the other agents, no reactive attitude toward deviation, nothing that anyone could be held to — only a shaped reward. Read literally, do conditions (a) through (d) count reward engineering as a convention? If so, the conditions seem to need a no-enforcement rider that is not in them; if not, my notion of a sanction must be mapping onto something finer-grained than the preference clause. Say which, and say what my vocabulary cannot express here. I suspect it is the difference between expecting someone to act and taking them to have a reason they can be held to, but tell me if that is moralizing smuggled in.

Write the translation and the residue report as prose, under about nine hundred words, addressed to me and not to a philosopher. Then append nothing else except a single valid JSON object, in a fenced code block, with exactly this schema:

bottom_line: string, one or two sentences — is my friend right that my field is rediscovering Lewis, and what precisely does working without his notion cost me?
