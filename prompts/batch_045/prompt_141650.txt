My old undergraduate mentor is retiring in May and the department is putting on a one-day workshop in his honour, and I've been asked to give the eight-minute opening remarks before the first panel. I took his two-semester historiography sequence by accident as a sophomore and it basically rerouted me, and I now teach high school history, where my students write exactly the kind of thing he spent forty years attacking: this led to that, which then meant such-and-such was bound to happen. So I'd like the speech to model the thing he taught rather than just describe it. The complication is the room: about thirty historians, several of them his own former students, who will definately catch every reference; his wife and two grown kids, who want warmth and do not want a lecture; the department chair; a dean who will half-listen; and him, in the front row. He has a known allergy here. At a festschrift for a colleague a few years back someone said the colleague's early papers were always leading up to the later books, and my mentor got up and walked out — he said afterwards that it made a career sound like a set of train tracks.

His actual position, as I absorbed it, had two parts he kept apart and that I think people run together. One is the knew-it-all-along effect: once you know how something came out, you can't reconstruct what you'd have said beforehand, so the outcome comes to feel like it was always going to happen. The other, which was more his own hobbyhorse, was about the shape of the path. He used to say that at every step in a historical sequence there were several doors, and that knowing where the sequence ended makes you lose count of them — you only see the door that was actually walked through, so the fragility of something that needed six or seven unlikely things to line up just vanishes, and what's left looks like a corridor. His repeated line was that nothing was inevitable and that most of what happened was one missed coach ride away from not happening. Here's my own worry, and I want the speech to hold it rather than dodge it: outcomes are evidence. If a canal scheme in the English Midlands in the 1790s collapsed, the fact that it collapsed tells you something real about it, and you're licensed to update on it. So "nothing was inevitable" can't mean the outcome tells us nothing, and I don't want to stand up there implying that it does, and I equally don't want to slide into so it had to happen. Meanwhile I can't narrate his career as a trajectory with an endpoint, which is more or less what a retirement speech is made of — so how do you say that someone's body of work added up to something, without implying it was heading somewhere or that it closed? And one more thing: he spent fifteen years on a big book about why that canal scheme failed and never finished it, his family is a little wistful about it, and I want to say something true about that which neither spins it into a lesson about how the not-finishing was secretly the point nor treats it as a failure the speech has to redeem.

So write me the eight minutes. It should land for the historians as a real engagement with the position rather than a pat on the back, but it shouldn't use the word "fallacy" more than once and it must not lose his family; it should praise the work without foreclosing its contingency, and handle the unfinished book honestly. Then, after the speech, give me a short note — a page at most — seperating what's actually well established about hindsight bias from what's contested among people who work on it and on historical explanation, and flagging clearly where the speech leans on his hobbyhorse rather than on anything settled, and where my outcomes-are-evidence worry is itself a contested reading of what he thought rather than something I should assume he'd have accepted. Please don't present any of the contested bits as consensus.
