You are a political philosopher who works on distributive justice. Please think carefully, brainstorm a few different diagnoses before you commit to one, and name the actual step — don't just tell me the literature has an answer to this somewhere.

Me and my friend went at it for two hours last night about equality of what, and I came away stuck with an argument whose conclusion I can't accept and whose steps I can't break. He added one premise I'd never heard before; he calls it the reversibility principle: if two people face needs that are now equally expensive to get rid of, justice has to treat those needs the same whether the need was chosen or was there from the start — because what each of them faces right now, a given handicap in turning resources into a good life, is the same either way. Then:

(1) Inequalities are unjust when they trace to brute luck; people should bear the costs of their own choices (the brute-luck/option-luck cut).

(2) Reversibility principle: what matters for compensation is how costly the need is to shed now, not how it was acquired.

(3) Most expensive tastes, once you've had them a while, are about as hard to reverse as most disabilities — try un-training a palate, or un-learning that you need total quiet to do any work at all.

(4) So justice owes the person with the cultivated taste roughly what it owes someone born with an expensive metabolism.

(5) But then anyone can raise their own entitlement by cultivating an expensive need on everyone else's dime: train yourself to need saffron and the rest of us owe you saffron. Nobody accepts that.

(6) Drop (2) and keep the cut in (1) on its own, and you need to know of each expensive need whether it was chosen. Tastes are mostly shaped by family, culture and peers well before you're old enough to choose much, so in nearly every real case the cut is indeterminate and tells us nothing.

(7) So the cut is either exploitable with (2) or inapplicable without it. And the three standard currencies all seem to inherit it somewhere — resources views hit it with the wheelchair case (same bundle, different conversion), capability views seem to get expensive tastes back through something like a capability to enjoy what you value, and welfare views never escaped it to begin with. So none of them tells us what we're supposed to be equalizing.

I'd of said I want to keep (1) and give up (2), since the cut is the whole reason this stuff appealed to me — but I want to know what that actually costs, and whether (6) is doing something sneaky with how strong no guidance needs to be before a principle is useless. Which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what do I have to swallow if I reject the others instead? Finally: how confident are you in the diagnosis overall, and which single point in it are you least sure of?
