So I somehow ended up on my town's conservation commission (long story, nobody else wanted the seat), and now I have to vote on whether a creek gets protected. The environmental review the developer submitted doesn't use species names at all — it's one of those eDNA things: they filtered creek water, sequenced a barcode gene, and grouped the reads into clusters whenever they differ by more than 3%, then called those clusters "provisional species" in the report. Most of them have no name and no type specimen. The state's list of protected things is all names, so as written the creek looks basically empty. But here's the part that bugs me: the same state wildlife agency will pay to truck in animals from another state to genetically rescue an inbred population of a listed species, and it also traps and removes animals that look like that species but turn out to carry genes from a close relative. So what does this whole setup assume a species actually is? Is it quietly committed to species being genetic clusters with a cutoff, or reproductively isolated lineages, or ecological roles — or does it not really commit to any of those and the DNA clusters are just a convenient stand-in? Is the rescue policy and the culling policy even consistent with each other, or do they need two different answers? I cant tell whether those are one question or seperate ones. And if we stopped treating species as the thing worth protecting — say we protected lineages, or the process of speciation, or just genetic distinctiveness directly — what would we have to change about how the rule is written? I have to explain this to the rest of the commission and I want something better than "the science says so."
