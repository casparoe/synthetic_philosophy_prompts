I lead the model-assurance team at a national epidemiological forecasting centre. Several times a year we audit a simulation model before it is used to set rules affecting millions of people, and most of the risk sits in one place: the model contains dozens of regularities — curves fitted to past data, relationships imported from other countries, structural assumptions about how people and pathogens behave — and they get shipped as if they will keep holding outside the conditions in which they were measured. My analysts have epidemiology or statistics training and no philosophy. What I need is a written procedure one of them can run on each regularity: a short sequence of tests in a fixed order, each with a stated threshold for what counts as passing it, ending in one of a few tags — exportable law, effective or derived regularity, local coincidence, or not decidable on the data we have — plus a rule for what we are permitted to do with a regularity given its tag (extrapolate freely, extrapolate only under stated conditions, refuse to extrapolate, escalate).

The tests should be the ones the philosophy of laws of nature actually supports, not merely statistical ones. As I understand the literature there is a real disagreement here: on one side, laws are just the patterns that survive in the best overall systematisation of what happens — nothing governs, there are no necessary connections, and a generalisation is lawlike in proportion to how much simplicity and strength it buys the total system; on the other, laws are features of the world that produce the patterns, explain why they hold, and ground counterfactuals about what would happen under interventions nobody has performed. I do not care which side is right, but I do care that the procedure does not quietly presuppose only one of them. So give me at least one test that only makes sense on the first picture (does this regularity earn its place in a compact system that also covers other regularities we accept?) and at least one that only makes sense on the second (is there something in the setup that would keep the pattern holding under an intervention, and does the pattern survive when we intervene on it in the model?). Include the grue-style worry about whether the predicates involved are the kind that project, and the distinction between a genuine exceptionless pattern and a coincidence of initial conditions — the difference between a regularity that holds everywhere and one that held in every case we happened to look at. State explicitly what the analyst should do when the two families of tests disagree, because they will.

Constraints: the procedure has to be runnable in under an hour per regularity by someone with no philosophy background, using data already in front of them; the output is a one-page register entry that a senior modeller signs off on; and where a step requires a judgment we cannot specify in advance — how much risk of a false extrapolation we accept, whose values set that threshold — say so and route it to the sign-off rather than pretending the test settles it. Tell me at the end what the procedure cannot do: which ways a model can go wrong that these tests will not catch.

Then run it on three regularities from our current work. (1) In all fourteen regions we model, the epidemic peak follows school reopening by eighteen to twenty-two days, and the model reproduces this with a fitted delay parameter. (2) Transmission rises with indoor occupancy as a power law with a stable exponent across countries and settings, there is a plausible mechanism story about airborne dose, and there are known exceptions. (3) The relation our flagship model is built around: hospital admissions lag reported cases by six days, stable across three years of national data. Assume (3) receives the tag we least want, and write that entry as it would actually appear in the register.
