ok so I've been going down the sortition rabbit hole for the better part of a year — citizens' assemblies, lottocracy, the whole thing — and I think I'm basically converted, but before I write it up for my group blog I want someone to check whether the set of things I believe is actually coherent, because I have that feeling you get when a bunch of individually attractive claims are quietly doing incompatible work.

Where I've landed:

(1) Elections are epistemically bad. They select for wanting power and for being good at aquiring it, which is close to the opposite of what you want in a person with power. I also don't think voting has much intrinsic or expressive value — as a mechanism for the people speaking, any individual vote is so close to causally inert that I don't think the symbolism is worth much.

(2) What matters about a political body is whether it makes good decisions. I'm fully instrumentalist about this; I don't feel any pull from everyone should have a say as such.

(3) The main thing a lottery buys you is descriptive representation — a chamber that actually looks like the population, class and education and geography and all of it, which no elected chamber ever does.

(4) The other main thing it buys you is that it can't be captured: no donors, no party machine, no re-election incentive, no primary electorate to pander to. I also like fast rotation here — nobody serves long enough to build a career or a donor base out of it.

(5) These bodies should deliberate seriously — expert briefings, small groups, real time to change your mind — and what should get implemented is the post-deliberation view, not whatever people walked in with.

(6) Selection should be genuinely random, or stratified only to hit demographic targets. I'm against any competence filter, deliberative-virtue screening, exam, or vetting for good judgement.

(7) The bodies should be binding, not advisory. An assembly that a legislature can just ignore is theater, and I think its recommendations get worse once its members know that.

(8) But I want to keep elected bodies around, for deciding which questions the sortition bodies take up and for broad direction — elected people pick the ends, random people pick the means.

(9) I'm fine with a sortition body deciding something a majority of the public would currently vote against, if the process was a good one. I don't feel bound by existing public opinion.

(10) I lean toward lots of single-issue assemblies rather than one big random parliament.

What I want: go through these and tell me which pairs actually conflict, which only look like they do, and for each real conflict what the cheapest revision is and what it would cost me. Rank them by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and at the end tell me which single change would clean up the most.

Two objections I've already been chewed out about and think fail, so tell me if I'm just rationalizing:

- someone said (6) is inconsistent with (3), that I'll happily rig the composition for demographics but not for competence. I think this is a fake problem: demographic quotas are about the shape of the body rather than a judgement that any individual is better, and half of why they're there is to correct for the fact that the kind of person who accepts the invitation skews educated and leisured, whereas any competence filter reintroduces a judge of persons and something to game.
- someone said (2) and (6) are obviously in tension, because if all you care about is outcomes you should pick the people most likely to get things right, i.e. some kind of epistocracy. I think this mostly dissolves: the argument for randomness is negative — elections actively anti-select for the traits you want, so removing the filter helps — plus there's that thing people cite where a diverse group of mediocre problem-solvers beats a homogeneous group of better ones, on the right kind of task. Not sure how much weight that last bit can actually bear, and I don't know whether political judgement is the right kind of task.

And one patch of my own I want stress-tested: my answer to the worry that elected politicians would end up controlling everything by controlling what questions get asked is that the agenda should also be set by lot — a second, standing random body that picks the questions, so no elected politician ever decides what's on the table. Does that actually rescue the package, or does it just move the thing I was worried about somewhere less visible?
