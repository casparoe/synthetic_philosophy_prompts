Two friends of mine got into it after a pub quiz on Tuesday. We'd been moaning about how every field seems to end up with an arrangement that basically nobody involved likes — credential inflation, journals nobody reads sitting behind paywalls, ad-funded everything, labs racing on capabilities — and by the end Rae and Sam were properly arguing and asked me to referee. I couldn't, so I'm outsourcing it. Please go through the exchange move by move rather than just telling me what you think of the Moloch thing in general, and write the reply in British English.

(1) Rae: the whole 'Moloch'/'multipolar trap' vocabulary is just the prisoner's dilemma and the tragedy of the commons with a demon's name stapled on. It adds no explanatory machinery, and the personification is actively misleading, because it invites you to look for an adversary to defeat instead of an incentive structure to change. (2) Sam: the concept earns its keep by picking out a pattern that recurs across domains that nobody would otherwise think of together — the same shape in arms races, academic publishing, farming, attention economies — and naming a shared structure is what concepts do; 'natural selection' isn't a person either. (3) Rae: fine, but then say which game you mean, because the cases don't share one. In a prisoner's dilemma, defecting is rational whatever the other person does. In most so-called races to the bottom, nearly every participant would rather stop provided everyone else stopped too — that's a coordination problem with a different remedy, and lumping them together means people reach for the wrong fix. (4) Sam: the shared feature isn't the payoff matrix, it's what he called the ratchet: each individual step is locally improving, the steps accumulate, no single actor can reverse the trend by stopping, and unilateral restraint generally just hands your position to someone else and often leaves the people you were trying to protect worse off. That signature is what to look for, and it cuts across different game structures.

(5) Rae: two problems with the ratchet story. First, 'worse for everyone' is measured against an outcome that was never on the table — the nicer equilibrium isn't one anyone could have reached by choosing better inside the game they were in, so calling the result a trap is a category error, and a politically loaded one: it converts a fight about who bears the costs of a fix into a shared plight, and quietly makes a centralised enforcer look like the only way out. Second, in the cases that actually worry people the mechanism isn't anyone's decision at all — it's filtering. The firm that declines the exploitative practice gets outcompeted and disappears, so the practice becomes universal without anyone ever choosing it, and 'tragedy of the commons' misdescribes that, and Moloch misdescribes it worse. (6) Sam: on the first point, the right comparison class isn't the equilibrium set of the current game, it's the set of games we could move to — traps are escaped by changing the game, not by choosing better within it, which is exactly why the framing is useful. On the second, selection is just the mechanism that turns the ratchet, and the fact that there's no one to blame is the point, not a bug. (7) Rae, finally: then the label is unfalsifiable, and some flagship cases aren't traps anyway — where there's a durable first-mover advantage it's a prize, not a trap, and treating everything as a trap discourages the case-by-case work that would actually tell you what to do.

So: rate each of the seven moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justify each rating briefly. Who is right about which specific point, who is more right overall, and what — if anything — are they both missing? In particular, if there is a defensible core notion in the neighbourhood, what should it be, and does the choice-versus-selection distinction change what the remedy would have to look like?
