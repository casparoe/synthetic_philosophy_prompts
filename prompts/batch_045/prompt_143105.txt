I'm 71, spent my career in population ecology — small mammal cycles mostly, vole and lemming time series, a lot of fitting and frankly a lot of arguing about whether what we fitted was real — and since retiring I've been sitting in on a reading group in the history department on cliodynamics. Turchin's secular cycles, structural-demographic theory, that whole business. I know the ecology, I'm shaky on the history, and I'm trying to work out how much of what I'm being told is actually carrying weight.

The guy who runs the group is a retired applied mathematician, and he keeps insisting the mathematical core of the whole thing is "just Lotka–Volterra with a couple of slow variables." Elites are the predators, the commoner population is the prey, the carrying capacity is land and manpower, and the two-or-three-century cycle is the lynx-and-hare cycle writ large, same shape, slower clock, messier data. And he's using that to license something fairly strong: that if we can forecast a vole outbreak or a budworm defoliation a couple of years out, there's no principled reason we can't forecast the disintegrative phase of a state — it's the same mathematics, we just need better data. I want that audited.

So: give me the correspondence table done properly and ruthlessly. What plays the hare, what plays the lynx, what plays the numerical response and the functional response, what plays generation time, what plays spatial coupling and dispersal. Don't explain Lotka–Volterra to me, I taught it. Then take it apart.

Here's where my own instincts are, tell me which of these bite and which are noise. One: the predators are recruited from the prey. Elites come out of the same population they're extracting from, and their recruitment rate depends on how well that population is doing, so it looks less like a two-species consumer–resource system and more like intraguild predation, or cannibalism, or a single structured population with a caste. Does that change what kind of dynamics you should expect, or do you get the same limit cycle either way? Two: the clock. A lynx cycle is roughly ten hare generations; a secular cycle at, say, two centuries is also roughly ten human generations. That made the analogy look much better to me than I expected, but I'm suspicious — is generation time the right clock at all when the state variables are things like fiscal capacity and grain prices rather than bodies? Three: spatial coupling. In my old field cycle synchrony over distance comes from dispersal plus a shared weather signal, and you get travelling waves. In the historical case the coupling is trade, migration, and outright conquest — and conquest doesn't just couple two oscillators, it deletes one and rewires the equations. Is there any room for that in the analogy? Four: reflexivity. Hares don't read about hare cycles. Elites and states read declinist literature, including this literature, and act on it. Does that do the analogue of the Lucas critique to the whole forecasting claim? Five, and this one I feel strongly about: non-stationarity. We spent decades in my field discovering that apparent cycles were artifacts of detrending, or a trend in carrying capacity, or one big shock ringing in a damped system. Agriculture, technology, fossil fuels, the demographic transition — how would you tell a real limit cycle from a trend plus shocks in a record where you get maybe three or four cycles per civilization and the proxies are worse than fur-return records?

Then the part that actually matters to me: tell me what the analogy is smuggling in. It seems to me it quietly imports the picture of the state as an organism-ish unit with a metabolism and a death, and "the population" as homogeneous biomass — does the forecasting conclusion depend on that picture, or is it detachable? And if the predator–prey mapping mostly fails, is there a better source domain in my own back yard — cohort senescence and self-thinning, forest gap dynamics, overshoot-and-collapse like the reindeer introduction where the resource is a standing stock that doesn't grow back, an R*/resource-ratio model — and would a better analogy predict something observably different from the predator–prey one?

Last thing. My own suspicion is that the defensible version of his claim is that the mathematics buys you a *class* of dynamics — overshoot, lag, collapse — and not anything like a timetable or a decade. I want you to say whether that's a retreat from what he's claiming, or whether it's really all the models were ever in a position to say, and whether he'd be entitled to feel he'd won anyway.

Don't bother with references or a reading list — I can't chase them and I don't need them. Just the arguments.
