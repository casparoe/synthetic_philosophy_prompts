Hey — I'm putting together the take-home final for my upper-level normative theory seminar and I want one question on the utility monster that's harder than just "explain Nozick's objection to aggregative views and say whether it works." The version I have in mind swaps the usual setup (a being who converts resources into welfare at a much higher rate than anyone else, so total welfare is maximized by starving everyone else) for two variants I made up. Variant one, the closed-loop monster: its enormous welfare comes entirely from internally generated experiences that use up nothing anybody else could have used, so there's zero opportunity cost to feeding it — call it cheap bliss. Variant two, the swarm: nobody is above average, but there's a big population each of whom is very slightly more efficient at turning resources into welfare, so total welfare is still maximized by giving them everything and holding everyone else at subsistence. The thing I actually want tested is what's doing the work in the original intuition — is it aggregation as such, or is it secretly about scarcity and opportunity cost, or about how concentrated the benefit is in one subject, or something else — and then whatever they conclude, they have to say what it implies for the mirror-image case I'm also throwing in, the dud: a person for whom resources produce vanishingly little welfare, and whom no aggregation-friendly view seems to owe anything at all. Write the question as I'd hand it to students (45 minutes, about a thousand words), then a model answer of roughly 700 words, then a marking scheme out of 20. One formatting thing: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables anywhere, continuous prose only, like an email — so the model answer is one solid block and the marking scheme is a paragraph or two that just says in running text wich moves earn marks and wich ones lose them. In that marking bit, please also flag the two mistakes I see every year: students who treat the swarm as just the monster with extra steps, and students who think "it's a reductio, so the aggregative view is refuted" is an argument.
