I've got a pile of take-home essays for my early modern ethics course to get through and I'd like a second read before I commit to a mark, because this one is very well written and I genuinely can't tell whether the substance is keeping up with the prose. Papers are anonymous, the mark is out of 20. For context, I said in lecture (roughly the standard reading) that for Smith sympathy is fellow-feeling with any passion, not just distress; that the impartial spectator is an internalised figure rather than an actual bystander we happen to be standing next to; and that utility, on Smith's account, enhances our approval of an action rather than being the source of it.

Here's the question I set — one of three, they had a week:

In lecture I drew a distinction between two readings of Smith's impartial spectator. Call the first the AGGREGATIVE reading: the spectator is something like an extrapolation from, or a smoothing-over of, the reactions of the actual onlookers we have encountered, so that the authority of its verdicts is inherited from the authority of real spectators. Call the second the ASPIRATIONAL reading: we build the spectator by stripping away from actual spectators whatever we can recognise as partial or self-serving, so that its verdicts can come apart from — and sit in judgement on — what real onlookers in fact approve. Use those labels to answer the following. Smith also argues that our moral sentiments are systematically corrupted: we admire, and almost worship, the rich and the great, and we neglect or despise the poor, and this warps both our judgments of propriety and our judgments of merit. (a) Does that corruption argument make trouble for one reading and not the other? (b) Can Smith say that a whole society has got things wrong — imagine a society in which everyone fawns on the rich — or does the corruption go all the way down? Pick a reading, defend it, and make sure your answer says what you take sympathy to be doing in all this.

And here is one student's answer, verbatim:

Smith's sympathy is not compassion. To sympathise with someone is to come to share a passion of theirs, whatever it happens to be — their resentment, their joy, even a spite we ourselves would not act on. That is what makes the corruption of our moral sentiments possible in the first place. We do not arrive at admiration of the great by argument; we arrive at it because sympathy with the pleasures of the rich is easy and sympathy with the hardships of the poor is not, and we can see the effects everywhere: the same conduct is called spirited in a great man and insolent in a poor one, and we go along with it, because, as Smith says somewhere, we would rather be looked at in the company of the great than in the company of the wretched. This is also why the deception he describes in his discussion of the poor man's son has the effects it does: what moves us to a life of industry is not the ease we expect to enjoy but the figure we imagine ourselves cutting in front of other people.

The distinction between the love of praise and the love of praise-worthiness belongs here too. Smith clearly thinks being praise-worthy is the better of the two motives, and treats the desire for praise as a useful but easily corrupted stand-in for it — which is why he puts so much weight on the internal spectator rather than on reputation, and why he thinks the wise man cares more about actually deserving approval than about getting it.

On the two readings: the AGGREGATIVE reading is, I think, the only one available to Smith. The ASPIRATIONAL reading sounds better — a spectator that can sit in judgement on actual spectators is what we would like morality to be — but it would require the spectator's verdicts to be answerable to something other than the sentiments of real observers, and for Smith there is nothing else: there is no innate moral faculty and no rational lawgiver of the Kantian sort. So the spectator is assembled out of what we have seen onlookers approve and disapprove, which means it can only ever be as good as they are.

That has consequences for (a) and (b). The spectator is impartial, and impartiality here means what it means anywhere else: that everyone's interests get equal weight, so that what the spectator ultimately approves is whatever conduces to the general happiness of everyone affected. That does give Smith a way of criticising a society that fawns on the rich — the fawning is a miscalculation of the general interest — but it is a utilitarian way, not a Smithian one, and I do not think he would have wanted it. On (b) I want to say something less comfortable. Because the spectator is assembled from the reactions of the onlookers around us, it cannot get outside its own society's sentiments. Where a whole society is corrupt, the spectator is corrupt along with it, and there is no standpoint left from which the corruption can be named. Smith can describe the corruption, and he can hope that the judge inside the breast resists it, but on his own principles he cannot show that the resistance is anything more than the preferences of one part of a society set against another. That is a kind of relativism, and I take it to be the real cost of the AGGREGATIVE reading — the one Smith is stuck with.
