**Exam question (Kant, *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*, Section II)**

(a) State the formula of universal law. Distinguish a maxim that cannot be *conceived* as a universal law from one that can be conceived but not *willed* as one, and say which of Kant's four examples illustrate which failure. Explain why the distinction matters for the kind of duty involved.

(b) A critic objects: the universal-law test is either a disguised appeal to consequences — it merely asks what would happen if everyone behaved that way — or it rests on contingent empirical assumptions, since whether a universalized maxim is coherent seems to depend on how people would in fact react. Assess this objection and give the strongest Kantian reply.

**Student's answer**

Kant's formula of universal law says: act only on a maxim that you could at the same time will to be a universal law — that is, act only on a principle you could will everyone to follow in the relevant circumstances. The test applies to maxims, not to bare actions, which is why the same outward behaviour can be permissible or impermissible depending on the end and the circumstances the agent builds into his maxim.

The test yields two kinds of failure. A maxim fails by contradiction in conception when the universalized version of it is logically incoherent — literally unthinkable — and this is the mark of a perfect duty. In the false-promising case, it is logically impossible for everyone to promise without intending to repay, because if everyone did that the institution of promising could not exist; the same holds for suicide from self-love, where universal adoption would destroy the very feeling of self-love that is supposed to shorten life. A maxim fails by contradiction in will, by contrast, when it can be conceived without contradiction but no rational will could endorse it, and this is the mark of an imperfect duty. That is why the examples of neglecting one's talents and of refusing help to others in need generate only wide obligation: we must adopt some end of self-cultivation and some policy of aid, but we have latitude about how much and when, and on a particular occasion it is not wrong to prefer one's own projects.

On (b): the critic is right about the first horn, and that is not a problem. The test does ask what would happen under universal compliance — that is the point of the law-of-nature formulation — and in Kant's own examples the consequences are exactly what make the maxim fail: universal promise-breaking would destroy credit, universal indifference would leave people worse off. So the formula of universal law is best understood as a restricted form of rule-consequentialism, applied to one maxim at a time. The difference between the two failure modes then reduces to a difference in how bad the consequences are: perfect duties are violated when universal adoption would be catastrophic, imperfect duties when it would merely be worse than the alternative. Finally, because the formula of universal law and the formula of humanity are separate tests, it is worth checking a maxim against both; in practice they return the same verdicts, which is why Kant can present them as formulations of a single principle.
