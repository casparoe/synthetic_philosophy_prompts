I sit on the clinical ethics committee of a teaching hospital, and I have to come to our next meeting with something more useful than a shrug. I would be grateful for your help. Please note at the outset that I do not want citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or the names of authors who have defended or attacked these positions: neither of us can check them, and the standard arguments I already know have not settled this for me. Argue from the cases and distinctions I set out below.

At our last meeting a senior intensivist closed a long discussion with the line that there is no moral difference between killing and letting die. He put it to three separate uses:

(i) withdrawing life-sustaining treatment is morally the same thing as administering a lethal injection, so the difference in our vocabulary is cosmetic;
(ii) since our hospital already permits withdrawal, our rule forbidding active euthanasia is inconsistent, and the rule should go;
(iii) when a family says, after a withdrawal, that we killed their relative, they are, morally speaking, correct.

I do not think the line does the work he wants from it, but I cannot get him to say what it means, and I suspect part of the trouble is that our actual case has a structure nobody on the committee addressed.

Two features of that case seem to me to matter.

First, the withdrawal required an act: the tube was removed, the machine was switched off. Had the team simply declined to start the ventilator when the patient arrived, the patient would have died at very nearly the same time, for the same reason, with the same intentions on the team's part, and everyone would have called that letting die. Whether an agent is interfering or ceasing to interfere therefore looks like something settled partly by an institutional convention about where a course of treatment is taken to begin and end, not by the moral facts alone.

Second, and more important, there is a question I have started thinking of as the baseline problem. Take the following stipulated case. Dr. R is the only physician on call overnight at a small hospital. A patient arrives who will die within two hours unless a procedure is performed that only R can perform. R owes the patient that procedure by every professional and moral standard we accept. R does not perform it; the patient dies. Whether we classify this as a killing or a letting die seems to depend on which of three baselines we compare R's conduct against:

- the Status-Quo Baseline: what happens if R does nothing at all from now on. The patient dies. R's conduct makes the patient no worse off than this, so it is an omission.
- the No-Agent Baseline: what would have happened had R never existed, or never been the one on call. Suppose another physician would have been on call and would have operated. Then the patient lives. Relative to this baseline R's inactivity leaves the patient worse off than they would otherwise have been, and by the only test my colleague seems to be using it is on a par with killing.
- the Role Baseline: the least that morality, or R's role, requires of R here. R owes the operation, so R's conduct is a failure to render what is owed — but this locates the case on a different axis entirely, that of breaching versus discharging a duty, rather than on the axis of killing versus letting die.

Notice that the No-Agent Baseline is corrosive in a way my colleague almost certainly does not intend: if failing to do what someone else would have done is making someone worse off, then a very large class of ordinary failures to rescue, to donate, to show up, become killings, and the slogan turns into a thesis about the stringency of positive duties rather than a thesis about the act/omission distinction.

What I want from you is a disambiguation I can take into the meeting, in three parts.

1. A menu of the distinct precise theses the line could be expressing, ordered from the most trivial to the most radical. I expect at least the following to be separated out rather than run together: a thesis about outcomes being identical; a thesis about there being no property intrinsic to the agent's behaviour that carries moral weight, so that any difference must be derived from other features; a thesis about there being no causal difference, on the ground that omissions are as causally efficacious as actions; a thesis about the unity of the agent's practical orientation, namely that an agent can let die for exactly the same reasons and with exactly the same intention as one kills; a thesis about the victim, namely that whatever it is about killing that grounds the victim's complaint or the violation of the victim's rights is present equally when an agent lets die; a thesis about the comparative stringency of the duty not to kill and the duty to aid; and a thesis about how institutions and law ought to classify the two. Add any further reading you think is doing real work, and do not pad the list with variants that differ only verbally.

2. For each reading: state whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, a substantive and contested claim, or a claim that is false or question-begging as stated; state which of the three baselines it tacitly adopts, or whether it is genuinely baseline-neutral; and state which of (i), (ii) and (iii) it would actually support, if any. In particular I want you to flag explicitly any reading that is not really about the distinction between doing and allowing at all, but about something adjacent that it is routinely confused with — the strength of positive duties, the correct explanation of why killing is wrong, the metaphysics of causation by absence, the design of rules for institutions, or the blameworthiness of the agent.

3. A short closing section addressed to my colleague. If some precisification of his line does get him (ii), say what it is and what he would have to add to make it explicit. If none does, say why not, in a sentence I can read aloud: I expect the central point to be that the choice among baselines is itself a moral question, and one the slogan gives us no purchase on, so the slogan cannot be the premise that settles our policy. Then say, in two or three sentences, what I should say to the relative who said we killed her, given that the withdrawal was carried out by an act and that the team had taken over the patient's care from the team who began it.

Please keep the whole thing under about a thousand words, write in prose rather than bullet fragments, and use no terminology beyond the cases, baselines and distinctions I have stipulated here without explaining it as you go.
