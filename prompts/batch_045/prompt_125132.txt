Two people on my team — call them Priya and Tomas — have been going round and round for months about whether the vision-language model we ship on our warehouse robots is actually "grounded" or just doing extremely fancy statistics over text and pixels, and I want the document they should have written by now instead of the argument they keep having.

Roughly: Priya's position is that grounding is a matter of a system's internal symbols being hooked up causally to the very things they're about, through perception and action, with that hookup doing real work in fixing what the symbols refer to — and that no amount of text can do this, because text is just more symbols. Tomas's position is that this is a romantic add-on: our model gets video, depth and force-torque readings, has to act on them under time pressure, and gets corrected by the world when it's wrong, so if a system can fold a perceptual channel into its inferences, generalize to new setups and take correction, there's nothing left over for "grounding" to be. He also holds that even a text-only model inherits grounding by proxy, since text is a record of embodied people bumping into things, and he rolls his eyes at anything that starts to sound like the Chinese room argument.

Please draft their adversarial-collaboration joint statement. I want: (1) a numbered list of claims both of them can sign — I'd guess at least that no symbol gets its content from syntax alone, that both agree there are systematic things a text-only system won't know about perceptible properties, and that both agree the word "grounded" is doing several different jobs; (2) the disagreements stated exactly, each in a formulation its own side would sign rather than a straw version; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that an answer would move at least one of them; and (4) for each crux, a test or case both would accept in advance as bearing on it, with Priya's prediction and Tomas's prediction written down before anyone runs it.

Please work with and improve on these three candidate tests rather than just restating them:

- The unlabelled channel test. Bolt onto the robot a sensor for some property that no text in the training corpus ever describes in qualitative terms — the corpus only gives you the property's structural/relational role. Can the model fold the new channel into its linguistic inferences zero-shot, and answer questions about it that it was never trained on? Is that the right probe, and what would each of them predict?
- The swapped-channel test. Consistently permute two sensor channels that do have names in the corpus (swap the channels behind "red" and "green" throughout training and deployment), so the system is systematically wrong by our lights but perfectly coherent and successful by its own. Is it still grounded? I designed this one to pry apart "tracks the distal property" from "matches our usage" — tell me if it actually does, and what each side should predict.
- The intervention test: grounded understanding, if there is such a thing, should show up more in answering "what would happen if I did X" about a novel setup than in answering "what is X".

Two more things I care about. First, if some of their fight is verbal — about the word "grounded", or about whether the target property is reference, or understanding, or consciousness — say which part and why, and separate it from the parts that aren't. Second, be honest about whether anything would actually move this: name what each of them should count as evidence against their own view, and say whether there's any result both would agree settles it, or whether one crux is really "does phenomenal content matter for understanding", which neither of them can argue for. Format it as the numbered agreed claims followed by a disagreements table with columns for the crux, the test, Priya's prediction and Tomas's prediction.
