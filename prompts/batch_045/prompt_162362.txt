I have been trying to get clear about the moral difference, if there is one, between killing and letting die, and I keep failing. Rather than read more, I want to take my own verdicts seriously and see what principle they add up to, or which of them I have to give up. Here are the cases and what I say about them.

One preliminary stipulation. I know the standard formulation comes in two versions: a stronger constraint against doing harm than against allowing it, and a claim about an agent's responsibility or blameworthiness for harm she causes versus harm she merely fails to prevent. I want a principle of the first kind, a constraint on what I may do. If my verdicts can only be captured by mixing the two, tell me that, and show me where the mixture is doing the work.

**1.** A runaway trolley will kill five workers on the main track. I can throw a switch sending it onto a spur where one worker is standing. I say: permissible, and more than permissible — I think I would be doing something wrong not to throw it. I feel none of the resistance here that I feel elsewhere.

**2.** The same runaway trolley, the same five, no spur. I can stop it by pushing a large stranger off a footbridge onto the track, where his body will bring it to a halt; he dies, the five live. I say: impermissible. The numbers are identical to (1), the physical effort is less, and my verdict flips.

**3.** A variant on (1): the spur loops back to the main line, so the worker on it will be struck and killed as the very thing that stops the trolley before it can reach the five. I keep changing my mind. Sometimes this is just (1), the same switch and the same exchange of one death for five; sometimes it is just (2), because his death is what does the work. I want the principle to take a stand here rather than reproduce my wobbling.

**4.** Five patients will die without transplants. The only physically suitable donor is a healthy visitor reading a magazine in the waiting room. I say: impermissible, and not close.

**5.** A competent, terminally ill patient asks me, her physician, to end her life. Disconnecting the ventilator kills her within minutes; injecting a drug kills her within minutes. I say: disconnecting is permissible, injecting is not. I have heard the reply that disconnecting is as much a doing as injecting, an action with a death both foreseen and aimed at, and it does not move me. But I want to know whether this pair of verdicts survives once the principle is stated precisely, or whether I am leaning on something that only looks like a distinction.

**6.** (a) I have carelessly left a heavy gate swinging shut on a child. I can pull her clear at no cost to myself. If I do not, she dies. I say: this is not something I get to describe as merely letting her die; it belongs with killing. (b) A stranger's child, the same gate, the same trivial effort, except that saving her ruins my clothes and makes me late. I say: I must pull her clear and I would be despicable not to, but the failure is not the failure in (a) — it is less grave and, I want to say, different in kind.

**7.** A man made violently psychotic by a brain tumour, not culpable and not in control of himself, is about to push five people off a ledge. I can stop him only by shooting him. I say: permissible. Note that his death is not a by-product of my stopping him; it is how I stop him. So whatever separates (1) from (2) cannot be the whole story.

My own candidate, offered to be tested and patched rather than defended: I may redirect an already-running lethal process, or decline to arrest one, even when I foresee that someone who would otherwise have lived will die as a result; but I may bring a new lethal process to bear on a person only where that person is himself the source of the threat to the others, or has culpably made himself such a source. Call it the no-new-threat rule. It gets (1), (2), (4), (5) and (7) where I get them, and it makes (3) permissible, which is one of the two things I am inclined to say about it. What I cannot see is whether it has any room for (6). In neither case does the child threaten anyone or bring anything about, and in (a) the lethal process is mine, yet the rule seems to file both as declining to arrest a process, which yields permissible in (b) as well, and I do not believe that.

So: is there a principle that captures all seven, and what does the closest one cost me? State it precisely, say which case motivates each clause, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Then give me its verdicts on three cases I have not settled. The first: the person I would have to kill is myself — throwing myself onto the track — and whether the principle says anything different about that than about killing someone else. The second: my patient is using the only available ventilator and will recover fully, while five others will die without it. The third: a case in which the choice is between risks rather than deaths, a switch that converts a certain death for one into a small chance of death for each of five — whether this structure survives the move from identified individuals to statistical ones at all.
