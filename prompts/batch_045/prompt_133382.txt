I have just spent two unpleasant hours arguing with a friend about this, and what I want from you is not a verdict on who won but a disambiguation.

The situation: I have signed a contract to deliver a complete English translation of a roughly four-hundred-page Croatian novel to a small press by 1 September, with a penalty clause that only bites if I miss by more than two months. I will be doing it evenings and weekends alongside a full-time job. My own estimate — built up from the one chapter I have already translated, from a words-per-hour figure I tracked across three shorter pieces, and from the fact that this author's syntax is considerably more tractable than that of the last writer I worked on — puts completion in mid-October, comfortably inside the penalty window. My friend works in forecasting. He listened to all of it and answered with one sentence: take the outside view. Pressed, he said it tells him that first-time book-length literary translations undertaken beside a full-time job almost always run many months past schedule; that my chapter-level extrapolation is precisely the bottom-up reasoning the planning-fallacy literature is about; that my inside view is therefore worth next to nothing as evidence; and that I should renegotiate the deadline now or decline the contract.

I do not think that sentence can bear the weight he is putting on it. But I also suspect I spent part of the evening attacking a version of it nobody holds, and I would rather have the menu than win.

So: set out, as separate and precisely stated theses, the different things take the outside view could be claiming — sharply enough that I could hand each one back to him and ask whether that is what he meant, and not as paraphrases of one another. The families of reading I want separated are these, though please add readings I have not thought of.

First: a claim about which evidence to use. That base rates over a reference class bear on my case at all and should be folded in; the stronger claim that they should replace my bottom-up estimate; and the strongest claim that my bottom-up estimate carries no evidential weight once the base rate is in hand — a screening-off claim. For the strongest version, say what would have to be true of my chapter-level extrapolation for it to hold. On the face of it, the fact that other people in this reference class were badly calibrated is a fact about them and not obviously a fact about my measurement, and I want to know precisely what bridges that gap, if anything does.

Second: a claim about which functional of the reference-class distribution is supposed to guide me — the mean completion time, the median, the ninetieth percentile, the probability of a catastrophic overrun, or the expected cost under my actual penalty schedule. These come well apart in a right-skewed distribution, and my sense is that only some of them support renegotiate or decline. Say which, and say whether there is any principled rather than rhetorical way to choose between them.

Third: a claim about how the reference class is to be fixed, given that every narrowing property is one I select after looking at my own case — first-timers, people with my measured throughput, people who have already built a glossary and translated a sample chapter. I want your view in particular on a wrinkle he never addressed: the class of people who are, at the moment of estimating, in the act of taking the outside view may itself have a different outcome distribution, since the disposition to reason that way plausibly correlates with planning competence. If that is right, is the advice self-undermining (the more faithfully I follow it, the less the base rate applies to me), self-fulfilling (following it improves my case, making it advice to intervene rather than to predict), or neither? And does the advice apply to itself — should I take the outside view about the historical track record of people who took the outside view, and if that regress has a stopping point, where?

Fourth: a claim about epistemic modesty rather than about evidence — that I should discount my own judgment because my confidence was produced by a process whose reliability I am not well placed to assess. Keep this separate from the first family: as far as I can tell, someone could fully defer to base rates while being thoroughly immodest about their own judgment, and could be modest while insisting this case really is special. Say which of the two the slogan is normally doing its work as, and whether the modesty reading can be stated in a form that is more than be less confident, which is advice and not a thesis.

For each reading, give me a verdict — platitude; substantive and defensible; substantive but probably false or incoherent; well supported — with a one-line reason, and, which is the part I actually care about, say whether that reading would get him from his premise to renegotiate or decline. My suspicion is that at least two of the readings are true but inert with respect to his conclusion, and that the readings strong enough to support it are the ones I am least willing to grant. If that suspicion is right, say so plainly; if it is wrong, say which reading does the work.

Finally, two epistemic requests. Attach a confidence level to each verdict, however you prefer to express it, and then single out the single point in your reply that you are least sure of, with a note on what would settle it. And flag anywhere my own framing above is muddled or imports a confusion — especially the wrinkle in the third family and my talk of screening off in the first — because I would rather find that out than win the argument.
