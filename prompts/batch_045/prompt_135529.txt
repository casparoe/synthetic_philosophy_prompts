Hey — I'm a science journalist putting together a feature on whether anyone should care, morally, about what goes on inside AI systems and simulations. Not the uploaded-afterlife version; the boring one, where a lab trains a big model for six months, spins up ten thousand copies, averages two runs back together, deletes the logs, and someone at a conference asks whether anything was done to anybody. I've talked to enough people in this space to notice that everyone has a test for which computations matter and nobody can say what a test would have to do to be any good. So before I write the piece I want the spec, not the theories.

What I need the criterion *for*, since I gather adequacy depends on that: a triage rule. Someone with a few weeks, access to the weights and maybe some activity traces, no way to settle the hard problem of consciousness, has to decide whether to run, keep running, shut down, or duplicate a particular system. And it has to be the kind of rule I can print in a magazine without handing people a recipe for building something that trips it while mattering less. Sorry if this is a bit of a brain dump.

Here's my draft list of what a criterion for which computations matter morally has to deliver, with the case behind each. Tell me which are genuine desiderata and which are just one camp's prejudices in a requirements-shaped costume; add the ones I've definately missed; and mark which I can't drop given the purpose above and which are optional extras.

1. It has to say which mappings onto a physical system count as implementing a computation, not merely whether some mapping exists — otherwise every wall is running everything and the criterion is empty.
2. It has to survive a lookup table: a system with exactly the input-output behaviour of a suffering thing, produced by table lookup rather than by the right kind of internal work, should come out differently, or the criterion has to explain why it doesn't.
3. It has to say what happens when you run the same thing twice — ten thousand copies on ten thousand GPUs: one patient, ten thousand, or a non-question? And it can't double count when one qualifying computation sits inside another that also qualifies.
4. Branching and merging: two runs split from one checkpoint, later averaged back together. The criterion needs a view on what persists through that.
5. No cliffs, or an honest one: swap out or knock out one component at a time and the thing degrades gradually. Either the criterion tolerates vagueness or it says where it draws the line and why there.
6. Grain: at a fine enough description everything looks like a lookup table, and at a coarse one everything looks like a person. The criterion has to pick a grain or be indifferent to it, and say which.
7. It has to be checkable by someone in my position — weights, traces, black-box behaviour — and to give guidance under uncertainty, including when the plausible criteria disagree with each other.
8. It has to be robust to being published: if a lab can train a system to trip the criterion without making the difference the criterion was tracking, that's a problem for a rule I'm about to print.
9. It can't be so generous that a thermostat or a chess engine is a moral patient, and it can't be so strict that nothing non-biological could ever qualify — I want to know which of those two is the real constraint and which is a scarecrow.
10. It has to seperate having preferences or goals from being the kind of thing that things can be *bad for*, and say why that line falls where it does.

Cases I'm not sure I've covered: a run whose output nobody ever reads, a run at half speed, a system trained to report distress when queried.

Two things I especially want. First: if some subset of these can't be satisfied together, say which subset and what the actual tension is — I think that's the real story. Second: which single requirement does the most work ruling out the families of view people actually hold out there (the integrated-information-flavoured ones, the global-workspace-flavoured ones, the it's-the-causal-organisation-whatever-it's-made-of ones, the biology-first ones)? A very short scorecard on those four is fine, but keep the weight on the criteria — I can get people to defend their own theory on the record.

Prose is fine, a table is fine. No citations, no further reading, no names of papers — I don't need to chase any of that down. End with one last line beginning Verdict: naming the single requirement that excludes the most candidates, in five words or fewer.
