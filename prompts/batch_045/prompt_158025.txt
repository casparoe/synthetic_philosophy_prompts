I am a philosophy PhD student, and my supervisor has been running a small reading group on diachronic rationality; we have spent the past several weeks on van Fraassen's Reflection principle, and she has been willing to give off-the-cuff verdicts on cases, which I have been recording because I need to predict how she will react before I commit to a chapter on this in my prospectus. Her track record, in my words: (1) you will simply be shown how a coin landed and conditionalize — she defers fully, and says this is the trivial case; (2) tonight you will be given a pill that reliably produces certainty that the moon is made of cheese — she says your current credence should not shift at all, since foreseen irrationality gives you no reason to move; (3) you will be handed a sealed dossier of clinical evidence about whether a drug works, read it, update, and then the dossier will be destroyed and your memory of its contents wiped, leaving only the new credence — she defers fully, and says the fact that you cannot reconstruct the evidence is no objection; (4) a machine reliably prints the number that will be your credence tomorrow and tells you nothing else — she still defers fully, and insists this is the real bite of the principle, that mere foreknowledge of your own future opinion moves you; (5) you will be scanned and two copies created, each told what happened — she says the principle as literally stated does not apply when the future person occupies a different centred world, and that she would defer only to that person's credences in propositions whose truth does not depend on which copy they are; (6) same pill as in (2), but in a low dose whose effect has been independently calibrated to shift everyone's confidence upward by about five points — she says you should move toward your future credence while correcting for the known five-point distortion, so her verdicts are not simply all-or-nothing.
