You are a professional philosopher who works on population ethics and the value of the future. Think carefully before committing yourself: first sketch two or three different diagnoses of where the argument below goes wrong, then defend the one you take to be strongest and say in a line why you dropped the others.

I am in my final year of high school, doing an independent project on whether human extinction is bad in a way that goes beyond the deaths it involves — the "afterlife conjecture," the idea that our confidence in the value of anything at all depends on there being people after us. I tried to extend that idea and reached a conclusion I do not want.

(1) My reason to take up any long project — curing a disease, writing music, making an institution more just — depends partly on there being a future in which the project can be continued, completed, or appreciated.
(2) If my reason to do X depends on condition C, then C's failing to obtain is a real loss to me, proportional to how much my reason depended on it, and is a loss independent of anyone's dying or suffering.
(3) So the end of humanity would be bad for people alive now: it would damage the point of everything they are doing, even in a version where no one dies who would not otherwise have died.
(4) But what (1) points to is not humanity in the abstract; any future that completes my projects is made of particular people — students, readers, colleagues, descendants.
(5) So my reasons depend on there being such people linked to me, not merely on the species continuing.
(6) Then a person with no students, no readers, no children, and no one who will remember them has, to that extent, impaired reasons for acting, and a couple's decision to remain childless damages their own reasons in the way extinction damages mine.
(7) That is absurd; we do not think a childless person's projects are pointless, or that having children is a precondition of a life having a point.
(8) So (1) is false, and nothing I do depends on an afterlife at all.

I would far rather keep (1) and (3) and give up something else, but I cannot say what. Which step do you reject, why did that step look secure, and what does rejecting it cost me — in particular, does it force me to say that the life of a last generation, or of someone no one will remember, is worth less? One case I cannot place: if humanity died out but were succeeded by beings who inherited our projects and cared about the same things, would (1) be satisfied, and must your diagnosis answer that or can it stay neutral?
