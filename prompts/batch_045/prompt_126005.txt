I work on a small internal team that writes the documentation that travels with the models my company ships — the model cards, the evaluation write-ups, and the short "what this system does and does not do" memos that go to the people deciding whether to put it in front of users. Last month we deadlocked over a single sentence in a draft: whether to say that our retrieval-augmented assistant *believes that the retrieved document answers the user's question*, or only that it *behaves as if* it does. Half the team thought the first was loose talk that would lead a reader to trust the system in situations where it will fail; the other half thought the second was evasive, and hid a real and useful regularity that the reader cannot recover any other way. Nobody could say what would settle the disagreement, because nobody had said what a claim of that shape is supposed to do. I have been asked to write the house style guide for these documents by the end of the quarter, so before I read any more of the literature on the intentional stance, I want the specification.

What I need is a list of desiderata that any adequate account of belief attribution to AI systems must meet, with the purpose held fixed: the attribution will appear in prose read by someone with no access to the weights, who will use it to predict what the system will do in novel situations and to decide how much to rely on it, and who will not read it as a claim about credit, blame, or legal responsibility. For each requirement give the case that motivates it, and say plainly whether it is non-negotiable given that purpose or merely a preference of one camp — the pragmatists who treat attribution as a predictive strategy, the interpretivists who tie content to whatever is needed to make a system intelligible as an agent, the people who want attributions cashed out in internal mechanisms, and the people who care whether the system's states play the right tracking or causal-informational role. I also want the distinctions the account must respect rather than collapse: between having a belief and the strength or centrality of it; between representing a content and merely carrying information about it; between an attribution that predicts, one that explains, and one that licenses reliance; and between content fixed by the world and content fixed by the system's training corpus.

The cases it has to handle, at minimum, are these. (1) *The apprentice*: a small model distilled from a larger teacher reproduces the teacher's idiosyncratic error profile, including on inputs neither system received feedback about, so that the content we would naturally ascribe to the student looks like a description of the teacher's dispositions rather than of anything the student itself encodes. (2) *The census-taker*: a model trained almost entirely on the records of a community that uniformly asserts some falsehood asserts the falsehood too; one reading says it believes the false claim, another says its states are better interpreted as tracking what the corpus says, with the content fixed by the community's testimony rather than by the subject matter. (3) *The autobiographer*: a model reliably denies knowing some fact and describes itself as guessing, while its forced-choice behaviour discriminates that very fact in a way no obvious shortcut explains, so that avowal and behaviour pull apart. (4) *The oracle of itself*: a system fine-tuned on its own earlier outputs predicts its own future behaviour better than it predicts the world, so that attributions to it yield excellent predictions of the system and poor predictions of anything else. (5) *The committee*: a scaffold of specialist modules behind a router, where no single component represents that the situation is deteriorating but the routing behaviour as a whole tracks deterioration; whether attribution to the whole requires a part that realizes the content is exactly what I want decided. (6) *The mirror*: a case where belief ascription has real predictive success that we can see derives from an artifact of the prompt template, so that the ascription stops working the moment surface format changes.

Two things beyond the list itself. First, tell me which pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — my own guess is that there is a genuine tension between requiring an attribution to be answerable to what is really going on inside the system and requiring it to be available to someone with only black-box access, but I would rather hear your candidates — and say which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the accounts people actually hold. Second, and briefly: run three contenders against your list, the predictive-strategy pragmatism associated with Dennett, interpretivism of roughly the Davidsonian sort, and the mechanistic read-the-features-off-the-weights view. A short table is fine. Keep the bulk of your answer on the criteria and on the cases behind them, since the scoring is the part I can do myself.
