I'm giving a 25-minute paper next Friday at a small one-day workshop on explanation in cognitive science — last slot before dinner, so I'll get maybe ten minutes of questions, and the organisers have warned me this crowd doesn't hold back. The title is 'Budgets, Not Stances: What the Three Stances Are Actually Doing'. My thesis is that Dennett's three stances (physical, design, intentional) aren't three kinds of description of three kinds of thing — there are really only two moves, pattern-hunting and mechanism-tracing, and what actually separates the stances is a complexity budget I call the compression floor. The idea: a predictor only earns credit for a pattern if it still comes out ahead after you charge it, out of its winnings, for the length of its own program — so a predictor that 'predicts' by carrying a lookup table of the data earns nothing, and so does a predictor with unlimited resources that can always find some scheme. Once the budget is explicit, my claim is that the stances differ in budget and not in subject matter, and the physical stance isn't more fundamental: it's just the setting where you refuse to pay any interpretive overhead at all. The corollary I care most about is grain-incommensurability: in a chess program, the pattern 'it wants to keep its queen safe' and the pattern 'the search tree prunes this branch after depth 9' are both real, both incompressible at their own grain, and neither is a refinement of the other — there's no single budget that displays both, and there's no privileged budget to settle which is the 'real' one. My argument runs in three steps: (1) Dennett's criterion for a real pattern is silent about cost accounting, (2) any cost accounting you add does all the work the stance-talk was doing, (3) the appearance of a hierarchy among the stances is an artifact of pretending the budget is fixed by nature. I've been arguing about this with my supervisor for months and I can definately recite the objections I'd get from her, which isn't the point.

The room is what worries me. There's a senior philosopher who is a thoroughgoing Dennettian — instrumentalist about the intentional stance, uses 'real pattern' as a comfort word, and has spent years attacking greedy reductionism; she'll like anything that protects folk-psychological explanations from elimination but will turn on me the second she thinks I've made pattern-hood speaker-relative. There's a philosopher of physics who works on statistical mechanics and coarse-graining, and who I'm pretty sure will hear 'compression floor' and say this is Boltzmann entropy wearing a philosophy hat. There's a computational neuroscientist who thinks in efficient-coding terms and will ask, not unkindly, for a case with actual spike trains in it rather than chess programs. And there are two ML grad students who think stance-talk is a relic and will say my compression floor is just a description-length prior, plus that deep nets routinely find patterns that pay their way in downstream use without being compressible in my sense. So: I need a Q&A prep sheet for that exact room, not for some generic philosophy audience.

Give me roughly eight objections in the order they'll actually come once people start talking over each other, each attributed to whoever is most likely to raise it and phrased the way that person would phrase it (the physicist should sound like a physicist, the ML students should sound like they're at a lab meeting). For each one: how much damage it does if I handle it badly, a two-minute reply I can say out loud without notes, and an honest note on where my reply is soft or where I'm just changing the subject. Tell me what I should concede in the first two minutes so the Dennettian doesn't spend the whole period thinking I'm doing greedy reductionism, and what the one reply is that has to land for the paper to survive at all. I also have a vague sense that the framing is self-undermining — if pattern-hood is budget-relative, what budget is my own thesis stated at, and why should anyone accept a claim made from a budget? — but I can't put my finger on it, so please fold that in as the question I'm hoping nobody asks, with the least bad thing I could say if someone does.
