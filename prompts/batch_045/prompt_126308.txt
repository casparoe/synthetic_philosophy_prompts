I'm TAing a seminar on the political theory of migration this term and last night I got into a three-hour argument with my flatmate over a cold takeaway about who actually owes admission to a particular group of people. We agreed from the start that they count as refugees in the ordinary sense — the 1951 Convention definition, well-founded fear of persecution on one of the listed grounds, that whole thing is not in dispute — so the question that ate the whole evening was the allocation one: there are about 40,000 people stuck in a camp on a border, realistically only one of three states is going to take them, and someone has to say which state it is that has to. The three principles we kept circling were: (1) trigger liability — the duty falls on the state whose action set the causal chain going, the one that lit the fuse, backward-looking and blame-ish; (2) capacity — the duty falls on the state for whom absorbing 40,000 people costs least once you count everything, forward-looking and basically consequentialist; and (3) a thing I half-invented on the spot and now can't stop thinking about, sustaining-cause: the duty falls on the state whose ongoing and revisable policy is what is currently keeping them from going home, even if that state had nothing to do with why they left, provided it could stop at moderate cost. My flatmate's line was that three is just one in disguise plus geography, and I said no, and then we were both too tired to build a case that would settle it. So: build me that case.

What I need is a realistic scenario — invented countries, no real place names, because the moment I say a real one the seminar starts arguing about the actual history instead of the principle — in which (1) and (2) both deliver the same state, call it X, and (3) delivers a different one, Y. Constraints, and I mean these: the whole scenario in one paragraph, 200 words max. The action by which X triggers the flight has to be something that was reasonable to do ex ante — a genuinely well-motivated intervention, not a cynical war — because if X is just the villain then the blame is doing all the work and the case is worthless. X must genuinely have the spare capacity and no get-out clause like an imminent collapse. Y's sustaining role must not consist in already hosting people or in anything charitable or in mere proximity, because then three collapses into something boring and my flatmate wins by default; it should be an active policy Y could revise at moderate cost. Nobody in the story should intend the displacement. And all three verdicts should look at least somewhat plausible when you first hear them, not one obviously rigged.

After the scenario I want the demonstration: for each of the three principles, what it says and why, laid out from the facts of your own case rather than asserted. Then the honest bits — which single feature of the scenario is doing the work, what happens to the split if you delete that feature (does it collapse back into a plain two-way fight between trigger and capacity, or does something else happen), and how a defender of trigger liability would attack your case, especially whether they can argue that the sustaining condition is itself downstream of the trigger so three secretly inherits its answer from one. Oh — and give me your own read at the end on whether sustaining-cause is a genuinely distinct principle or just a relabelling, since that's the thing I actually care about. I have to hand the seminar plan in on Friday, so the more concrete the better.
