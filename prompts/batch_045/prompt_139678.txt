Hi — I'm a journalist, I mostly cover the economics of platforms and the odd ideas-and-society piece, and right now I'm trying to write something about why the phrase fair price keeps clawing its way back into public argument every time there's a scandal about surge pricing, ticket fees, dynamic supermarket shelf tags, or some app quietly setting what a driver gets paid. Everyone I interview who has any theory at all ends up reinventing a very old wheel, and I keep getting pointed back to Aristotle: the stretch in Book V of the Nicomachean Ethics where a builder and a shoemaker have to be brought to some kind of equality before there can be an exchange between them at all, where the real measure is supposed to be need rather than money, and money is only a conventional stand-in (and, as I understand it, a kind of pledge that future exchange will be possible); and then the early part of the Politics where he grumbles about trade aimed purely at profit and about money apparently breeding more money. I've dipped into the secondary literature and honestly most of it is scholars arguing about what he meant, which is useless for a magazine piece, so please don't give me exegesis, a reading list, or any page citations — I'm writing this on a train with no library access and nothing to look things up on.

What I actually want is a spec, because I've noticed I don't have one. Before I read any more theories of what makes an exchange fair, exploitative, or merely grubby, tell me what such a theory has to deliver: the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it mustn't collapse, and the background facts about actual markets it isn't allowed to contradict — each with the case or the reason behind it, so I can see where the bite is. Please make sure the list covers at least these: straightforward producer-for-producer trade (my builder and shoemaker baseline); exchange of things nobody produced and whose supply is basically fixed — land, attention, personal data, offsets; cases where no human sets the number at all because an algorithm or a platform does, so it's unclear whose will is in the exchange and whether voluntary is a coherent description; markets where money is deliberately banned and allocation runs on need or on matching instead (organ donation, paired kidney exchange), which might vindicate the need-measure reading or might be a totally different principle wearing its clothes; exchange stretched across time, i.e. credit and interest, where I want to know whether the objection to money making money falls out of the framework or is a detachable prejudice of his; unequal-outside-option cases where both parties genuinely consent but one has nowhere else to go; and the nasty one I can't stop thinking about, where the unit of measurement is itself somebody's tradeable asset — platform tokens, airline miles, in-game currency, a stablecoin issuer.

Two things I'd most like from you. First, sort the requirements: mark which are genuinely mandatory for a theory good enough to judge real markets by, and which are just the tastes of one camp dressed up as rigor (I suspect a few of the things people demand — that it never appeal to anything but observed consent, or that it never appeal to anything but a single quantity — are exactly this, but tell me which). Second, commit: if some subset of the requirements can't all be satisfied together, say which pair collides, show me the case that makes them collide, and then actually pick — if you had to drop one, drop it and defend dropping it. I'd also like your bet on which single requirement kills off the most popular contemporary account of fair exchange. A short closing paragraph on how three contenders fare against your list would help me see if it's usable — Aristotle's own need-based one, the consent-plus-competition-is-all-there-is one, and something like a cost-of-production or labor-input one — but the criteria are the part I'm paying for, not the scorecard. And since I'm going to quote someone on this, flag the one place in your list where you're least confident or where you think a decent philosopher would tell me you've oversimplified.
