I am finishing a thesis chapter on indifference principles and the Bertrand-style underdetermination of random geometric probabilities, and over two years of supervisions I have collected my supervisor's verdicts on a series of chord cases. I need to reconstruct the rule she is running, and I need to predict what she will say about a case I am about to put to her.

Her verdicts so far, all for the unit circle and all for the same question — what is the probability that the chord exceeds the square root of three, the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle:

1. Mechanism unspecified, Bertrand's original set-up. She answers one half, on a Jaynes-style invariance argument: the answer cannot depend on where the circle lies in the plane, how it is oriented, or how large it is, and she has called the other two classical answers artifacts of parametrizations with no invariance claim behind them.

2. Mechanism specified as: choose two points independently and uniformly on the circumference and join them. She answers one third without protest — give me the mechanism, she says, and I will give you the number.

3. Mechanism specified as: choose the midpoint of the chord uniformly over the area of the disk. She answers one quarter.

4. Mechanism specified as: choose a point uniformly on a fixed diameter and draw the chord perpendicular to that diameter through it. She answers one half, remarking that here a fully specified mechanism reproduces the invariance answer.

The new case is a two-stage device. Stage one is fully specified: a point P is drawn uniformly from the interior of the disk. Stage two is specified only as follows: a chord is drawn at random through P. The manual says nothing more. The question is unchanged.

I have a candidate answer but no confidence in the rule behind it. On the reading I favour, the gap is filled by conditionalizing the unique rigid-motion-invariant measure on the space of lines on the constraint the problem does specify, which makes the induced density for the line's signed distance p from the centre proportional to the chord length that the line cuts from the disk, and yields one third plus the square root of three over two pi, roughly 0.609 — a number that is none of the three classical ones. A second reading treats a partial specification as no specification at all and returns one half. A third possibility is that her four verdicts license only a refusal: once P is drawn, the symmetries that survive are rotations about the centre and reflection in the line through the centre and P, and I cannot tell whether those leave the conditional law of the chord's angle free — if they do, her answer should be that the case is underdetermined, and I would want to know over what range.

What I would like from you:

Reconstruct the space of rules that fit verdicts 1 to 4 equally well, including ones I have not listed, and say precisely what each predicts about the new case — a number, a range, or a refusal to answer. Check my calculation and my claim about the surviving symmetries rather than taking them over.

Give me your best guess at her actual verdict with the reasons for preferring it, weighted by how well each rule explains not just the numbers she gave but the things she said in giving them.

Then name the single follow-up case I should put to her, given that I have one supervision left before the chapter goes to my committee: the variant whose verdict would separate the rules that are still tied after the new case. I would rather have one case that discriminates than three that merely illustrate.

Finally, tell me where your prediction is most likely to fail and what would move you off it — in particular, which of her possible replies to the new case or to the follow-up would lead you to abandon the rule you have assigned to her, and whether there is any verdict she could give that the rules you have reconstructed could not accommodate at all.
