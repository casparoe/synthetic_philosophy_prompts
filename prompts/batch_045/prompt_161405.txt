Construct a thought experiment that reverses the usual dialectical use of pain asymbolia, built to the following specifications.

The standard clinical picture of asymbolia — a patient who can localize, describe, and intensity-rate a noxious stimulus, yet reports that it does not bother them — is standardly deployed against views that locate pain's badness in the subject's aversive stance, on the ground that here is pain without aversion. Your case must be the mirror image, and must put the pressure on the views that tie pain to what it represents or commands: imperativism (pain is a perceptual experience whose content includes an imperative to protect a body part) and a pure sensory-quality view (pain is a distinctive somatic sensation with location and qualitative character, so that a state lacking these is not pain).

The case: a neurological patient whose state is (1) elicited only by noxious stimulation and graded with intended stimulus intensity, so that it is plainly a state of the pain modality rather than a mood or free-floating distress; (2) devoid of sensory-discriminative content — no location, no describable quality, no ability to distinguish pinprick from heat from deep pressure, no withdrawal reflex, no guarding of any body part; (3) nonetheless thoroughly aversive — high unpleasantness ratings, eager pursuit of analgesia, willingness to incur real costs to avoid the state. Motivate the lesion from the standard lateral/medial division of ascending pain pathways rather than from invented technology, and keep the scenario itself to a single paragraph.

Then do the demonstration. Give the verdict each of three accounts — an attitudinal or desire-based theory, imperativism, and the pure sensory-quality view — renders on whether the patient is in pain and whether the state is bad for them, and show that each verdict follows from that account's own constitutive claims rather than from anything you stipulate. The attitudinal theory must be the one that comfortably counts the state as a genuinely bad pain; the other two must be pushed toward denying that it is pain at all, and you must close their cheapest escape route, an imperative whose content is only something like "something is wrong somewhere." Close by identifying the single feature of the case that does the work, saying what becomes of each verdict if that feature is removed, and stating the strongest reply available to the imperativist — for instance that the patient suffers from something other than pain, or that asymbolia and your case are not true mirror images.

The case is for isolating whether pain's badness can be located in the subject's aversive stance alone. Accordingly, the patient's own judgments about what their state is must do no evidential work, and nothing may be settled by stipulation rather than generated by the theories.
