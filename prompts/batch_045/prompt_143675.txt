Hey, I need a hand with something. I sit on the committee that hands out our hospital's "exceptional needs" fund — its donated money, not insurance money, roughly 180k a quarter, and we get to spend it on things nobody else will pay for. We vote Thursday and I'm the one circulating the memo beforehand, so I need to actually write the thing.

Here's our case. Two packages, money for one:

Package A: one patient, mid-thirties, call him D. A course of therapy that eats essentially the whole pot. Without it he's got maybe eighteen months; with it, plausibly fifteen-plus years of fairly ordinary life. Also, his window is closing — if we don't fund him this quarter he's no longer eligible, so a "maybe next time" is not on the table for him.

Package B: six patients, about 30k each. Each one gets roughly three extra years of decent life. So it's more total life-years, just spread out over six people instead of piled onto one, and every one of the six could wait a quarter without much changing.

Our standing rule is to maximize total health benefit, which we've always cashed out as adding up quality-adjusted life years. On that rule B edges it out. But one of our members has been making an argument I can't shake and also can't quite answer. She says the only way B wins is if you're willing to treat six separate people as one big person with a long life ahead of him, and that we would never do that inside a single patient's care plan — we don't, for instance, harvest organs from one healthy person to save five, even though the arithmetic there is even better than here. Someone else on the committee shot back that this is nothing like that: in B nobody is being operated on against their will or used as a means, they just don't get the money, and failing to help someone isn't the same as hurting them. I think that reply is pretty strong, actually, but I don't think it ends it.

Two more wrinkles I have to deal with. First, D's thing is urgent and the other six are deferrable, and the pot refills in January, so someone's going to propose we fund D now and ring-fence the six for next quarter — and someone else is going to point out that next quarter has its own patients who aren't in the room, so that's just moving the same problem onto people who can't object yet. Second, two of the six are married to each other and each listed the *other* one getting better as a big part of what they'd personally gain, which feels like it should count once rather than twice, but I've no idea how to handle that. Also someone floated a lottery and the chair said that's us giving up, which... maybe?

So: draft me the two-page-ish memo I'll send out Wednesday night. It needs a real bottom line — which package, and on what timeline, with anything we're committing to for future quarters — and it has to engage that member's objection properly, and give the "we're not harming anyone, just not funding them" reply its due even if the recommendation goes against it. Don't just restate the QALY rule at me, everyone's already read it. Two pages, plain English, something the seven-odd clinicians on the committee will actually read.
