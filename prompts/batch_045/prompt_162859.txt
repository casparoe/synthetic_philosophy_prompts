I'm a journalist working on a long feature about sortition — the selection of political officeholders by lot — and I keep watching the same two camps talk past each other. One camp, the lottocrats, holds that elections are defective as an instrument for producing good law: voters are rationally ignorant, the incentives of electoral competition reward pandering and short horizons, the people who end up in legislatures are unrepresentative along axes that matter, and money buys agenda-setting and access. Their remedy is a legislature composed of randomly selected citizens serving staggered terms, working in small facilitated groups with expert testimony, supported by staff, with elections either abolished or confined to a weak agenda-setting role. The other camp, the electoral democrats, grants most of that diagnosis and still thinks elections carry goods that sortition cannot supply: the authorization of a specific person to act in my name, the retrospective sanction of removal, the expression of a collective will at a determinate moment, and a kind of answerability that no rotation of amateurs can replicate. They are happy to defend citizens' assemblies and deliberative polls as advisory complements to the electoral system and hostile to replacing it. I want the document these two should have produced after a year of good-faith argument, and I want to run it as a sidebar: the things they can both sign, and then the disagreement made exact.

Please draft that joint statement. Section 1, numbered, should be the agreed claims — about what elections measurably fail to do, about what the existing record of citizens' assemblies does and does not establish, about what sortition is a claim about (composition, deliberation, independence from donors) and what it is not. Keep them non-vacuous: I want sentences neither camp could dismiss as filler. Section 2 should be a table of the residual disagreements, each row giving the question, then the lottocrat's own preferred formulation and the electoral democrat's, each phrased sharply enough that its own side would sign it without flinching, plus a note on whether the disagreement is genuinely substantive or rests on an unstated difference in what words like representation, accountability, and democracy are being used to mean — and where it is verbal, say exactly which term is doing the work.

For each genuine disagreement I want the crux stated as a question that could in principle be settled, and then the test, case, or evidence both sides would agree bears on it, with each camp's prediction written down in advance and a note on which side would be more embarrassed by being wrong. I have four institutional devices I'd like them to rule on, since a camp's principles sometimes cut against its instincts: (a) the twin-panel test, in which two independently drawn random bodies deliberate the same question in parallel with identical materials, to measure how much of an outcome is attributable to the particular composition rather than to the procedure; (b) the shadow assembly, a random body that is drawn and briefed but convened only if the sitting chamber fails to reach a supermajority, which makes the threat of replacement latent rather than actual; (c) the priced opt-out, under which a selected member may decline by paying a fee scaled to income or by performing an equivalent period of other public service, which puts a number on the burden of service and on who can actually afford to serve; and (d) a staff-rotation rule paired with competing expert briefs, so that no permanent bureaucracy or single expert faction faces a chamber of newcomers alone. For each device, give me which camp should favor it given its own stated principles, and flag any case where a camp's principles pull against what its partisans would actually want. Close the statement with what each side would count as evidence against its own view, and whether there is anything both agree would actually move the debate rather than confirm what each already believed.

Please don't cite papers, name authors, or attribute findings — I'll do that reporting myself, and I don't want anything in the piece that depends on a source I'd have to check. Nothing here should require looking anything up; I want the argumentative structure. Around 1,200 words for the statement.

After the statement, give me a single JSON object I can use to build a structured graphic, with this schema: agreed_claims (array of objects with id, claim, why_both_sign); disagreements (id, question, lottocrat_formulation, electoral_democrat_formulation, genuine_or_verbal, diagnosis); cruxes (id, crux, linked_disagreement_ids, test_or_case, lottocrat_prediction, electoral_democrat_prediction, who_is_more_surprised_if_wrong); institutional_devices (device, lottocrat_verdict, electoral_democrat_verdict, reasoning, where_principles_cut_against_instinct); falsifiers (lottocrat_disconfirmation, electoral_democrat_disconfirmation, shared_movers); verbal_disputes (term, resolution); bottom_line_for_readers (one or two sentences, written for a general audience that has never heard the word sortition).
