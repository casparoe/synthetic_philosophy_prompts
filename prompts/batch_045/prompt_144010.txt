I am the field director of a small excavation that closed its final season yesterday; tomorrow the trenches are backfilled and the mound goes back to the family who farm it. Tonight, at the end-of-dig dinner, I have to speak for about six minutes to roughly sixty people: my students, the twenty-two local men and women we hired (eleven of them for all four seasons), the family and the village elders, and three officers of the foundation that funded us.

The season's finds: a storeroom suite with clay door sealings and several hundred standardized coarse vessels; two rooms whose thresholds had been packed with smashed serving vessels that look deliberate to me but were buried under a floor and never seen again; two burials beneath house floors with modest grave goods. No inscriptions, no images, no foundation deposits — nothing anywhere on the mound that was made to be read by somebody who wasn't there. My deputy's distinction, which I want to lean on: an *addressed* deposit is one produced so as to be read later by an absent party, an inscription or a visible offering; an *unaddressed* deposit is one that merely ended up where we found it, through use, discard, collapse or reuse. Her rule: only addressed deposits license claims about what people believed, and only addressed deposits can separate institutional arrangements that otherwise look identical, since our storeroom assemblage is equally at home in a temple corporation, in redistribution by senior lineages, and in pooling among independent households.

Three parties at that dinner want something this rule won't let me give them. The foundation officers want one sentence they can put in a press release. The elders want me to say openly that the mound is the shrine their oral history names. My students want to be told that the discipline can get from broken pots to minds. All three deserve better than evasion, and the crew deserve thanks that doesn't patronize them. Write the speech: true by my deputy's rule, generous to everyone in the room, and worth hearing for people who have never thought about any of this.

Then a note of no more than 200 words: where the speech takes liberties, what it assumes that is contested, how much weight you think the addressed/unaddressed distinction can really bear — I suspect the burials are where it strains, since grave goods were certainly addressed to someone, just perhaps not to us — and whether any part of what I've asked for is undeliverable in six minutes. If you think the request is misframed, or that declining the elders' claim is itself a claim they will hear, say so first and plainly rather than producing something polite.
