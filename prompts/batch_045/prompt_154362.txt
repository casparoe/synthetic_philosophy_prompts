For an essay in a magazine for general readers on where the line between legend and history falls, I want to use John Ford's The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance (1962) as a genuine test case rather than as window dressing. My working thesis is that the boundary is not truth-conditional — history as the true version of the story that a legend distorts — but institutional: a narrative counts as history to the extent that a community submits it to standing practices of corroboration and correction, whatever its content turns out to be. The film looks like an ideal test because it runs the familiar cases backward. With Troy, Arthur, or the Exodus, the problem is a story arriving with too little evidence and being promoted anyway; in Shinbone, a story with eyewitnesses and a dead body is demoted to legend, and the editor who refuses to print the correction gives his reason in one line — when the legend becomes fact, print the legend.

Give me a careful reading of the film against that distinction: which scenes carry the argument (the schoolroom behind the newspaper office, the shooting, the territorial convention, the editor destroying the reporter's notes, the funeral, the train at the end), and what each shows about what actually certifies a narrative. I also want the honest part. The film is itself an act of correction, since the flashback tells us what really happened, and that may vindicate the truth-conditional view against my own; say where the film resists me. And say plainly whether its case, running in the opposite direction from the ancient ones, shows anything about them at all. Please write in American English, and keep the piece as criticism rather than synopsis.
