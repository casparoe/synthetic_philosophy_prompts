I am teaching a graduate seminar this term on the epistemology of self-knowledge, and we have now spent two full sessions on the margin-for-error arguments against luminosity without reaching anything the whole room will grant. The class has split into two blocs that have begun talking past each other. One holds that knowledge is, at least for a privileged class of grounds, transparent to the knower: if a subject knows that p on the basis of grounds of the kind that make her belief knowledge, she is in a position to know that she knows it, and there are conditions — being in pain, being appeared to thus-and-so, having just this evidence — whose obtaining puts the subject in a position to know that they obtain. The other holds that knowledge is a factive but thoroughly non-luminous external state, that any reliable belief-forming method carries a margin for error, and hence that for every case of knowing there are close cases in which the subject would wrongly take herself to know, so that the second-order belief is unsafe and the iteration principle fails. I would like to convert the stalemate into a document: draft the joint statement these two camps could sign at the end of a good-faith adversarial collaboration — something I can hand out as the reading for our final session.

Concretely, I want a numbered section of agreed claims, where agreed means that a sophisticated member of the other bloc would sign it and would not regard it as a platitude: claims about the operator doing the work (what being in a position to know is, whether it inherits factivity, whether it is closed under known entailment), about what needs explaining (the awkwardness of asserting a proposition while denying that one knows it; the pull of iteration in ordinary epistemic practice; the apparent ease and authority of self-knowledge for occurrent mental states), and about what the familiar no-go arguments actually rule out rather than what each bloc wishes they ruled out. Then I want a disagreements table, one row per crux, with columns for the crux itself, the iterability side's position phrased so that side would accept the wording, the margin-for-error side's position phrased the same way, the case or argument both would accept as bearing on the crux, and each side's prediction about that case written down in advance. Among the cruxes I expect to see treated: whether the anti-iteration argument genuinely requires a sorites premise or only a reliability-plus-margin premise; whether the modal condition on knowledge is safety or sensitivity and whether either admits the sharp-versus-gradual distinction the dispute turns on; whether there is any class of conditions genuinely free of borderline cases for the iterability side to retreat to, and what happens to the doctrine if the retreat is granted; whether the iterability side can hold its principle together with closure under known entailment, the denial of scepticism, and the finite storage capacity of real subjects, and if it cannot, which of the four it surrenders and at what cost to the rest of its epistemology; and how much of the residual dispute survives once both sides replace knows with is in a position to know.

Two further constraints. First, at least one crux must be arbitrated by a case neither bloc has encountered, so that the predictions are registered in advance rather than reconstructed afterwards; please use this one. A reader of medical images works through a large batch of cases in a domain where her pooled accuracy is high and her confidence well calibrated in the aggregate, but where, by design, she receives no case-by-case feedback for a year and cannot tell on any individual case whether she is in the fluent trials she gets right for the reasons she would cite or in the liminal trials where those same reasons are present and she is effectively guessing. On a case of the first kind — call it knowledge, both blocs agree — she is asked, before any feedback, whether she knows that she got it right. The case is designed to separate two candidate explanations of iteration failure, a modal margin around the first-order belief and an evidential deficit at the second order; tell me whether it succeeds in separating them, which bloc's prediction you think it favours, and what each would have to say to make it come out their way. Second, mark explicitly which parts of the dispute you take to be verbal — a fight about how to use knows or is in a position to know — and which are substantive, and say what, if anything, both blocs could agree would actually settle a substantive part. Do not resolve anything by stipulating a technical sense of knows; if a formulation holds only under a stipulation, flag it rather than burying it. And do not appeal to what the literature has said: no citations, no bibliographies, no gesture at what is standard in the debate. Argue from the cases and the principles, since I want my students to be able to check every move themselves. Finally, because I am offering this as a model of how such a document should be written, close with an honest accounting of your own confidence: how confident you are in each proposed formulation, which of the two blocs' positions you think you have stated least faithfully, and which single claim in the document you are least sure a real member of the relevant camp would actually sign. I would rather have a document that marks its exposure than one that looks tidier than the dispute is.
