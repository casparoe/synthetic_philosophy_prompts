I'm a backend engineer who has been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group with a few friends, and I've gotten hung up on something I'd like analyzed properly. My understanding of Bayesian confirmation is the programmer's version: an agent with a prior over hypotheses, a likelihood function, and a stream of observations that get folded in by conditionalization. What I can't make sense of is the problem of old evidence, and the case I keep returning to is Einstein and the perihelion of Mercury. As I remember it (please don't let the analysis hinge on exact figures): by the later 19th century astronomers had an unexplained residual advance of Mercury's perihelion, on the order of tens of arcseconds per century, left over after all known planetary perturbations were subtracted; the live Newtonian-style explanations posited something unseen near the Sun, an inner planet or a cloud of orbiting matter; in 1915 the general theory of relativity appeared to produce roughly the right number from the theory itself with nothing tuned to fit; and a few years later eclipse measurements of starlight deflection gave a dramatic public confirmation. The puzzle as I've seen it stated is that if the Mercury residual was already known with probability one, then conditionalizing on it can't raise the probability of the new theory at all, yet it is universally treated as among the strongest reasons anyone had for taking general relativity seriously.

What I want is a disciplined mapping rather than a restatement of the puzzle. Fix an agent and a hypothesis space and say which you've chosen and why: Einstein himself in 1915, a working astronomer of the period, or us now looking back — and, correspondingly, whether the alternatives on the table are general relativity versus Newtonian gravity plus an inner planet, versus Newtonian gravity plus solar-oblate or dust corrections, or something else. Then say precisely what the evidence proposition is supposed to be, since I think this is where the case is slipperier than it looks: the raw telescopic observations, the residual computed after subtracting perturbations under a particular model of the solar system, or the received summary figure that circulated in textbooks — these seem to have different novelty statuses, and I want to know which one the formalism is meant to take as E and what happens to the argument if you shift between them. Run the standard fixes against this case and tell me where each one breaks: the move where what you actually learn is not E but the fact that the new theory entails E; the move that appeals to whether the evidence was used in constructing the hypothesis rather than to when it was learned; the move that treats the change as a shift in the agent's whole probability assignment rather than as an observation. I'm especially interested in hearing what each of these says about the eclipse result, which looks like a clean new observation but which I suspect was in some ways weaker evidence than the Mercury figure, particularly given that Einstein had earlier published a different and smaller deflection value whose exact size I don't want to rely on.
