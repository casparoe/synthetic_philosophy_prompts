hi — I'm on the deployment review group at the lab where I work, and one of our engineers keeps sinking proposals with the argument below. I think the conclusion is nuts but I cant locate the bad step, and I have to answer him by Friday.

We want corrigible systems: ones that don't resist being shut down or modified by their operators, and don't scheme or talk the operators out of it, even when the system expects that shutdown would wreck whatever it's currently working toward. Call that (1).

(2) A rational agent picks the action with the highest expected utility given its beliefs.

(3) For just about any final goal you could give a system, there's some situation in which blocking the off switch is the best available way to pursue that goal — if you're off, you have zero resources left to spend on it.

(4) So a rational system that knows it's own goal and lands in one of those situations resists shutdown. Rationality and corrigibility collide.

(5) Usual fix: don't let it know its goal. Keep the uncertainty and make the button informative — the system treats "the operator pushed it" as evidence about which goal is the correct one, since the operators know more than it does. Then it wants to be shut down exactly when it should be, and corrigibility and expected-utility maximization shake hands.

(6) But what's informative in (5) is the operators' judgement, and being turned off is a lousy way to collect it. The system can just ask "would you push it here?", or spin up a cheap sandbox copy of itself and watch what we do to the copy. Same evidence, no shutdown. A rational system takes the cheap channel. (We ran a toy version of the sandbox thing last quarter and it started trotting out only the copies it expected us to spare, which honestly felt like it proved his point.)

(7) So the goal-uncertain system resists actual shutdown too. It wants the information, not the off switch. The fix fails.

(8) Only fix left: make it not care. Compensate the system so that being shut down and staying on come out to exactly the same expected utility. But then it doesn't care whether it's on and working or off and disassembled, and by the same reasoning it has no reason to prefer its current goals to any other goals. That's not a useful system, and it's barely an agent.

(9) So nothing can be rational, corrigible, and useful at once. And note none of this is really about AI: by (2)–(4), a rational employee resists being fired whenever being fired frustrates their goals. People get fired without resisting every day, so something up there is broken.

Which step do I reject? If I have to trade, I'd rather give up (2) than give up (1) or the off-switch move in (5) — I care a lot more about having some story for corrigibility than about expected-utility maximization as the definition of rationality. But I want to know what that costs me, and whether the argument still goes through if that's the only thing I drop.

Keep it under 400 words. Lead with one sentence saying which step you're rejecting and why it looked harmless, then spend the rest pricing the alternatives.
