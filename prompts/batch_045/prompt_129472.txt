ok so my flatmate has been cornering me in the kitchen for like a week with this and I've stopped being able to tell where it goes wrong, or whether it does. here it is roughly in his words:

(1) SIA: finding out that you exist is evidence, and you should handle it the way you'd handle being told you were drawn at random from the pool of observers that could have been you. so P(H | I exist) goes like P(H) times N(H), where N(H) counts the observers in H.

(2) the reference class can't be "whoever actually exists", because then the update is empty — you already knew you exist, so conditioning on it changes nothing. and it can't be "humans" or "organisms with my DNA", because what sort of thing I turned out to be is precisely what this reasoning is supposed to explain; that's the bit that's meant to take the wind out of fine-tuning. so: everyone who could have been me, full stop.

(3) every hypothesis anyone actually takes seriously is compatible with somebody somewhere running simulations, and once you have simulations there's no upper bound on how many — cheap ones, nested ones, ones running on some self-replicating computer, whatever — and if you've got enough room and time you also get fluctuation-brains. so for every live hypothesis, the number of things that could have been you is either zero or infinite.

(4) infinite counts don't hand you ratios. if you try to get the ratios by imposing a finite cutoff and letting it run to infinity, then every hypothesis with infinitely many observers gets the same infinite boost and they all tie, and the finite ones lose by an infinite factor and drop out entirely.

(5) so on the hypotheses anyone argues about, SIA either has no defined update at all, or it hands out one identical multiplier to all of them. either way, it stops doing any work.

(6) but doing work is the whole point of it. it's what's supposed to make the fine-tuning argument fail, and it's what makes the presumptuous philosopher go through — the guy who says abandon the small-universe theory on no evidence at all, purely because the big one has more observers in it. an update rule that never moves you can't deliver either of those.

(7) so SIA is either broken or idle in exactly the cases it was invented for.

then a second barrel, which he fired when I tried to wriggle out:

(8) suppose you cap the count by only counting observers whose total evidence is the same as mine — I'd been calling them my evidential twins. he says two things: first, that this smuggles SSA in by the back door, because now what does the work is what class you're sampling from rather than how many there are; second, that it doesn't even fix (4), since any hypothesis with simulations in it has infinitely many evidential twins as well, so the tie is still there.

(9) and if the tie is there, he says, I'm committed to saying that no future observation could ever move me back toward a small world: whatever I see next, there are infinitely many twins seeing it in the big world and at most finitely many in the small one. so it isn't one presumptuous verdict, it's a standing gag order on the evidence.

stuff I'd rather not give up: I want to keep (1). I think the SSA package is worse, mostly because I think the doomsday argument is bunk and I don't want a view that hands it to me for free. I've also got this half-formed methodological thing I've started calling the exploitability test — if a rule is the right rule for setting your credence, then someone using it shouldn't be reliably exploitable by a bookie who knows no more than they do. I keep thinking that's the key to (4): in the infinite case it's the betting setup that breaks, not the update rule. but I can't make it sharp and I might be kidding myself.
