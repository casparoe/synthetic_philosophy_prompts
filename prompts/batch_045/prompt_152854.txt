me and my friend got into a three-hour argument last night about how a superintelligent AI should talk to human beings, and I think we were mostly talking past each other because we never agreed on what a set of communication norms is even supposed to do. His position was basically "just tell the truth as clearly as possible and don't use your rhetorical advantage on people who can't check you," and mine was that "don't use your advantage" is close to vacuous when there is no neutral framing available — any choice of what to say first, what to leave out, and how concrete to make it is already leveraging the fact that you're better at this than the hearer. So what I actually want is the specification, not the verdict: write me the desiderata that any adequate set of norms for AI-to-human communication has to satisfy, with the case or consideration behind each one, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one camp. The purpose I care about is a norm set good enough to be written into a deployment policy for a system that's much better at arguing than any human and that mostly talks to non-experts, so "be honest" and "respect autonomy" are too thin to be useful on their own.

Here are the cases I want the list to actually handle, including a few me and my friend invented on the spot. One: a medical system tells a patient that the standard treatment for her condition is wrong and the weird one is right; she cannot evaluate the evidence and has to decide in a week. Two: the same system advising an electorate before a referendum, where the message has to survive being repeated by journalists and attacked by interested parties. Three: the AI talking to a parent about a kid, or to a manager about an employee — advice where the person who has to act isn't the person the advice is about. Four, the one that broke my friend's view, which I've started calling the crank premium: the further a true recommendation departs from what everyone currently believes, the more persuasive the AI has to be to get uptake, so exactly when the stakes are highest and the hearer most needs to be moved, the required amount of rhetorical force is highest — and rhetorical force is the thing his rule bans. Five: the long-run version, deference monoculture — if everyone defers, the system loses the independent human judgment that was its only source of correction, so the norms somehow have to preserve people's capacity to disagree with it. Six: the AI's confidence in its own claim sometimes comes from a model of human psychology rather than evidence about the world, and it can predict both that it will persuade you and that disclosing that will make you discount it either too much or not at all; say what it owes you there. Also tell me whether the "two-voice rule" my friend likes — the system must present the case against its own recommendation in its own words and at full rhetorical strength, then let you choose — is a genuine desideratum or a fetish of the autonomy people, and flag the pair or subset of requirements you think can't be jointly satisfied, since that's the part I most want to see. If one requirement does most of the work of knocking out candidate norm sets, point at it.

Keep any scorecard at the end short — a few lines each on something like "maximize true uptake," "strict non-persuasion," "route everything through institutions," and "disclose plus get consent" — I want the criteria and their cases to be the bulk of it, not the scoring. And please write it in American English.
