I tutor mathematics part-time and do code review for a living, and over the past couple of years I've started noticing a failure pattern that I can't find a word for — one that doesn't seem to be what people are talking about under the headings I've been pointed to.

Three instances. A student of mine can state and prove that every differentiable function is continuous, and can separately show that the absolute-value function fails to be differentiable at zero; asked whether that function is continuous at zero, he stalls and says he isn't sure. Told that it is, he says yes, obviously — and, pressed on whether he has just learned something, insists he already knew it and simply hadn't put the two facts together. Separately, a colleague signed off on a design document describing a caching scheme that she herself had rejected three weeks earlier in a code review, on the grounds that it creates a stale-read bug; the two descriptions use different vocabulary, and when I placed them side by side she saw the conflict instantly and again reported that she had known it all along. And I seem to see the same shape of thing in language models: state a fact in one phrasing and the model will apply it; restate the identical fact in a paraphrase and ask a question one short step away from it, and it fails — and asking it to spell out its reasoning fixes the long-chain versions of that failure but not this one.

What interests me is that the explanations I've been offered feel like the wrong shape. It isn't that the missing step is long or difficult: in my first two cases it is a single inference, and the person in question has already performed it, in another notation, without effort. It doesn't look like a pure time or effort limit either — my student does not converge on the answer if I give him the rest of the afternoon, but does converge the moment the two facts are written in the same vocabulary. And then there is the retrospective claim, which I am inclined to take seriously rather than write off as a misremembering of a prior state: the subjects treat the derived belief as something that was already theirs, not something newly acquired. A philosopher friend, hearing this, said it sounded like the matter of agents being treated as believing every logical consequence of what they believe, and that the usual name for the problem is something like logical omniscience, with bounded rationality as the standard way of softening it; but as far as I can tell that discussion is about the cost of long derivations, and my cases do not improve when you lower the cost.

So: is there an established concept — in epistemology, in the psychology of reasoning, or in the machine-learning literature on what systems can and cannot do with what they in some sense already have — for a subject's failing to bring a belief to bear exactly where it applies, where the failure is cured by re-encoding rather than by further inference, and where the subject counts the result as something it already believed? If there is, I want the name, but more than the name I want the carving: what the concept is taken to cover, which neighboring notions it is routinely conflated with, and which side of the boundary each of my cases falls on. If there is no such concept, say so plainly and give me one — a term with a definition sharp enough to distinguish it from ordinary ignorance of a consequence, from a failure of memory retrieval, from misremembering one's own earlier uncertainty, and from the resource-limited failure to complete a long deduction, along with a verdict on whether my three instances are really instances of it at all or are two or three different things I have lumped together. Please do not give me a reading list or references; I have no library access where I am and could not follow them up. What I would like instead is for you to attach a confidence level to each of the substantive claims you make, and to single out the one you would be least surprised to turn out wrong.
