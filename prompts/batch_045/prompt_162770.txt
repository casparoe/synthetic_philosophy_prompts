You are a professional philosopher who works on moral responsibility and the ethics of blame. Please think about the following carefully: before you rule on anything, spend a short paragraph laying out the distinctions you think are doing the real work here (what an act of blaming is, what standing to blame might consist in, and what exactly might go wrong when a hypocrite does the blaming). Then apply them move by move.

I am a junior in high school, and two people on my debate team asked me to referee an argument they got stuck in. I genuinely could not, so I am passing it to you. The case is real but anonymized. A classmate, Jonah, was caught looking up answers on his phone during a chemistry midterm. Priya wrote in our class group chat that this was cheating and that he should be referred to the honor board. Someone then pointed out privately that Priya had plagiarized a paragraph in a history paper last spring, never admitted it, and was never caught.

1. Dev: Priya has no standing to blame Jonah. She committed a closely similar violation and is still concealing it, so her blame is hypocritical, and a hypocrite forfeits the standing to hold someone else to a norm she does not hold herself to.
2. Maya: that conflates two questions. Whether Jonah cheated, whether it was wrong, and whether a report is warranted are questions about Jonah. Priya's own record is a separate moral failing, and it cannot make a true and warranted criticism any less true or warranted. She did it too is just tu quoque.
3. Dev: blame is not merely a verdict, it is something you do to a person. You address a demand, you implicitly present the norm as one that binds everyone including you, and you implicitly place yourself above the person you are blaming. A hypocrite cannot do that without misrepresenting her own relation to the norm, so the defect is in the blaming itself, not tacked on beside it.
4. Maya: that proves too much. If any unacknowledged wrongdoing destroys standing, then nobody can ever blame anyone, since everyone has done something. Dev replied that what destroys standing is not the past act but her present posture of exempting herself, and that a hypocrite who owned up and reformed would get her standing back, which shows his view is not the sweeping one Maya described.
5. Maya: Jonah does not know about the history paper. A defect that is invisible to the person blamed and that changes nothing for him cannot be what makes the blame wrong; whatever is defective has to be available to him. Dev: that just shows the problem is not only interpersonal. Blame is a form of enforcement, and selective, self-exempting enforcement is illegitimate whether or not the person caught notices.
6. Maya: even granting all that, plagiarism in a history essay is a different norm violation from looking up answers on a chemistry exam, so Priya's hypocrisy is about a different norm. Standing should be norm-relative rather than global: you lose the standing to blame cheating by cheating, not by littering. Dev: the norms are different but the underlying one is the same, academic honesty, and the whole point of the honor code is that it is one code.
7. Maya, finally: look at the incentives. If hypocrites lose standing to blame, people will conceal their own past wrongdoing precisely so they can keep blaming others, which makes the practice worse. Dev: that is an argument about what the rules of the practice ought to be, not about whether this particular act of blaming is defective.

Please rate each of the seven moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and justify each rating briefly. Then say who is more right overall, and what both of them are missing: for instance, whether standing is all-or-nothing or comes in degrees, whether the blame here is different in kind from the referral to the honor board, and whether a blame can be justified in its content but still unfair to the person blamed.
