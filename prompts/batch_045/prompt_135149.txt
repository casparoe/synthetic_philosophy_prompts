I'm applying for a small internal grant at my institute, basically one course buyout plus a bit of travel for a semester, and the application wants a two-page single-spaced project description. Philosophy / decision theory. Dont bother with citations or references, I'll slot those in myself.

The topic is Good's theorem — the standard result that free information can never have negative expected value for a Bayesian agent, since you can always just ignore what you learn and do whatever you would have done anyway. I'm interested in exactly when that argument breaks.

Here's my half-formed idea. The proof leans on something like: the act you'd have picked without the signal is still available to you after you get the signal. Everyone I know works by relaxing one assumption at a time — strategic settings where other people react to your being informed; belief-dependent utility where the anxiety of knowing is itself a cost; ambiguity or non-expected-utility agents who can't dynamically stick to a plan — and each time you get a "so information can be harmful after all" result. Fine. But I think there's a case nobody treats properly: signals that are about the agent herself, where how reliable the signal is depends on whether you consult it in the first place.

Concretely: a career-counselling test that predicts, very accurately, which of two careers you'd end up happier in — where how happy you end up partly depends on which career you actually go into, because your tastes are partly formed by the life you lead. The test's track record was built on some population; but once you see the prediction, your response changes, and so what the test says about you isn't settled independently of your reading it. There's a fixed-point / self-fulfilling-prophecy structure. I think it's structurally different from the strategic case (no second agent needed) and from the belief-dependent-utility case (nothing hangs on the phenomenology of knowing — the whole problem is that the *content* of the signal is response-dependent).

What I want in the proposal:

- The question stated sharply enough that it has a determinate answer — a yes/no, or a clean classification. My current candidate: is there a nontrivial class of decision problems with these response-dependent (I've been calling them "endogenous") signals in which the expected value of consulting a free signal is provably negative, and if so what feature of the signal or of the agent's response rule does the negativity track?
- A section on why the standard result and the usual escapes from it don't already settle this. Make it honest: someone on the panel will say "isn't this just the known result that information can hurt when other people respond to your being informed, with one player," or "isn't this just preference change," and I want the proposal to have already drawn the distinction rather than promising to draw it later.
- A method section saying what I'd actually do across four months, month by month, including the smallest interesting formal setup — I was thinking two acts, two states, a binary signal, a response function from signals to possibly mixed acts, and the signal's reliability specified as a function of the agent's information policy. Tell me whether that's too thin to be interesting or already too thick to finish, and pick one.
- What a positive result would look like, and — the bit I always write badly — what would count as the project failing. I want at least two failure modes: one boring (the class turns out to collapse into something already known) and one interesting (the value of consulting the signal isn't well-defined at all until you add an equilibrium-selection rule, so the real finding is that the question was ill-posed).
- A short paragraph on why anyone outside the seminar room should care. I want something non-hand-wavy for the panel about recommendation systems and predictive tests that are partly self-fulfilling — risk scores, aptitude feedback, that sort of thing — without overselling it.
