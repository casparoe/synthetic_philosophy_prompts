I am in a graduate seminar on institutional design, and last week we spent a session on Hofstadter's vocabulary from Gödel, Escher, Bach: tangled hierarchies, inviolate levels, and Escher's lithograph of two hands each drawing the other, with the level of the draftsman outside the reach of both. As I am using the terms, a tangled hierarchy is one that, if you climb it far enough, delivers you back where you began, so that levels which look stacked turn out to be mutually constituting rather than ordered; an inviolate level is one that the operations inside the system cannot reach, inspect, or rewrite from below. I want to know whether these are genuine analytic tools or just vivid labels for conflict of interest, so I have been trying them on an institution I cannot get a clear read on: Meta's Oversight Board. A friend in law school says it is accountability theater and that the Hofstadter vocabulary is decoration; my sense is that the structure constrains the company in ways neither the company nor the board fully controls, and I would like the theory to adjudicate between us rather than to dramatize what we already think.

The episode, with the facts as I have them (correct me if a detail is off, but please do not rest the analysis on contesting one): the board was proposed by a Harvard law professor, approved by Zuckerberg in 2018, staffed with outside members in 2020, and began hearing appeals of individual moderation decisions in October 2020; its first batch of rulings, in January 2021, overturned four of the five Facebook decisions it reviewed. Its best-known case is the referral of the suspension of Donald Trump's accounts after the January 6 Capitol attack: the board upheld the suspension but faulted the company for imposing a penalty its own rules did not actually specify, an open-ended suspension rather than a defined sanction, and sent the decision back to be redone properly, after which Meta converted it into a fixed two-year suspension. Structurally: the board can reverse Meta in an individual case and Meta has committed to comply, but on the content rules themselves it can only recommend, and the company publicly accepts or declines those recommendations; the board's jurisdiction is fixed by the company and has been extended by it, most recently to Threads; the board is funded by Meta; and its published decisions operate as precedent, which means Meta's policy staff now draft and revise rules in anticipation of how the board will read them.

What I want is a disciplined mapping rather than an impression. First, lay out the levels you think are in play — users, automated classifiers, human moderators, internal policy, the board, the charter, the funding arrangement, Meta's management and shareholders, external regulators such as those created by the EU's Digital Services Act — and say for each boundary what enforces it, since a level with no enforcement mechanism is not obviously a level. Second, separate the merely nested loops from the genuinely tangled ones, and give me the test you are using; my own candidate is to trace the life cycle of a single norm (a rule is written, applied, appealed, read by the board, rewritten by the company, and then read again) and to ask whether there is a well-formed question at one level that only the level above can settle while the level above owes its authority to the level below. Third, ask whether there is an inviolate level here at all, and distinguish at least three cases that I think the vocabulary runs together: a level the system has no operation for reaching, a level for which operations exist but that no agent has standing or incentive to run, and a level that is simply unviolated so far and would offer no resistance if someone pulled. Say which of these the charter, the money, and the rulemaking power respectively occupy, and name the concrete event that would count as a violation of each. In particular: is the asymmetry between binding the company on individual cases and merely advising on rules the analogue of being able to alter a program's data but not its code, and does that make the level of rules inviolate or merely shielded? Fourth, run the case back against the theory. Does the tangled-hierarchy lens predict anything a plain institutional-conflict-of-interest story does not — about which way the board's influence will drift, about what kind of case the company will comply on and what kind it will maneuver on, about what happens when the board's own precedents start constraining it — or does it only redescribe the arrangement in more flattering language? Be willing to say where the vocabulary goes silent: whether it has any resources for grading degrees of constraint, and whether it can distinguish a fig leaf from a real limit at all. Finally, two extensions: if the same arrangement were created by statute, or given power to sanction non-compliance, or placed under an external regulator that can fine the company, would that detangle the hierarchy or merely add a third hand to the picture; and who, if anyone, occupies the draftsman's level in Escher's print here, given that the board's existence, remit, and budget all trace back to a decision by the body it reviews?
