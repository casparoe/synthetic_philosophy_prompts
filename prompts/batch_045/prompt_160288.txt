I am a senior in high school, and I am the one who has to give the presentation at our philosophy club next month on whether a human being is really one mind. Our faculty advisor lent me a folder of readings about the split-brain cases: patients who had the thick bundle of fibres between the two cerebral hemispheres surgically cut as a last resort against severe epilepsy, and who afterwards seem entirely normal in ordinary life, yet in testing will insist they saw nothing when an image is flashed to the left visual field while their left hand reaches out and picks the matching object; who will button a shirt with one hand while the other hand undoes it; and who, when the speaking hemisphere is asked why they just did something the other hemisphere initiated, will produce a fluent, confident, entirely invented explanation rather than saying they do not know. What I cannot get straight is whether these results show that cutting the brain in half produces two subjects of experience where before there was one, or whether they only show that one subject has lost a channel of internal communication and, along with it, the ability to notice its own disunity. The readings we were given disagree, and the disagreement feels like it is about something prior to the evidence: about what would have to be true for there to be a fact of the matter about how many conscious subjects there are.

So what I would like from you is a dialogue, not an essay. Set it in a lab corridor after a conference talk, late in the afternoon, with two researchers at a whiteboard, one of them drawing hemispheres and arrows. One of them, a neurologist, argues that the surgery genuinely divides the subject: each hemisphere sustains its own goals, acts on information the other lacks, learns tasks the other cannot, and has its own stream of experience, so a split-brain patient houses two centres of consciousness that merely share a body and a name. The other, a philosopher of mind, argues that nothing in the data forces a count of subjects at all: what changed is how much information is globally available to the organism, and the question how many subjects are in there now has no determinate answer beyond whatever convention we adopt for counting persons; she also presses the point that the patients themselves report no sense of being doubled and that we should be reluctant to overrule that report. I want them to actually answer each other. The neurologist has to deal with the fact that the patients feel unified, and with the worry that a subject whose introspective machinery has been halved is exactly the subject who could not be expected to notice its own division, which begins to sound unfalsifiable. The philosopher has to deal with the fact that if the patients' own reports settle the question, then no evidence about neural disconnection could ever show a subject had divided, and with the fact that we do think something very important changes when the fibres are cut. Give each of them one point that clearly lands and that the other concedes outright, in so many words.

Then I want them to take up a case that is not in my folder, because I made it up and I think it exposes something. Suppose that some decades from now surgeons can implant a callosal shunt, a device that copies neural traffic between the hemispheres at any bandwidth you care to dial in, from nothing at all up to complete, and that can be configured to pass some classes of signal but not others: full bandwidth for sensory information and none for motor intentions, say; or full bandwidth but delayed by a third of a second; or a lossy setting that drops half the packets, so that each hemisphere receives a patchy and intermittently garbled picture of what the other is up to. How many subjects of experience are there at half bandwidth, or with a third-of-a-second delay, or when the senses are shared but the intentions are not? Does the unity of a mind come in degrees, and if not, where exactly is the cliff, and what is it about that setting rather than a neighbouring one? And what about running the case in reverse: for a patient who has lived for years at zero bandwidth, opening the shunt gradually over a fortnight — is that two subjects fusing into one, and if it is, is one of them gone, or is it one subject recovering, and if it is one subject recovering, what was it doing during those years? I would like the dialogue to end with each of them naming the single assumption they share with the other — the thing both take for granted about what consciousness would have to be like for the question to have an answer at all — and then saying what observation in the shunt case would actually change her or his mind, or else explaining why no such observation could. Please keep the technical vocabulary light enough that I can follow it, and when one of them uses a distinction such as that between unity of access and unity of experience, have her say plainly what she means by it, since if I cannot follow it I cannot present it. Around eight hundred words is about right.
