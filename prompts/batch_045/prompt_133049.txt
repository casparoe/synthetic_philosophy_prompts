I write political fiction, and the novel I am finishing turns on a body of my own invention: the Board of Charters, which drafts a constitution for each province as it accedes to the Union of Verraine. A good part of the book is the Board arguing with itself in closed session, and I want those arguments to be real ones — the kind a comparative-institutionalist could read with a pencil without laughing. I have read the standard case for fusing legislative and executive power and the standard case for separating them, and I do not need either rehearsed back to me, nor do I want authorities, reading lists, or citations. What I need is this: given the five settlements my Board reached before the story opens, what has it already committed itself to, and what follows for the sixth case, which is the one I am actually writing.

The precedents, as my narrator summarizes them:

**Sable.** Six parties in the assembly, none above a quarter of the seats, no pair able to govern without a third, labels traded between elections, two decades of cabinets lasting under a year. The Board prescribed a parliamentary charter: the assembly elects the premier, and it may unseat a premier only by simultaneously naming a successor (the clerks call this the substitution rule). A proposal for a directly elected governor was rejected without discussion.

**Ondres.** Two disciplined programmatic parties alternating in office, a career civil service, an audit office that had actually prosecuted a former minister, and a public that in the Board's own hearings ranked delivery of legislation above being consulted. The Board prescribed a directly elected governor: four-year fixed term, cabinet drawn from outside the assembly, no ordinance power, a narrow emergency power that lapses unless the assembly affirmatively approves it within thirty days, select committees with subpoena power, and removal only by impeachment on a two-thirds vote for defined offences.

**Kalva.** Two coups in thirty years, weak personalist parties, and a strong popular demand for a directly elected head of province even among those who feared the generals. The Board prescribed a semi-presidential charter: a directly elected president, fixed term, confined to external relations and defence, unable to dissolve the assembly, who appoints a premier holding assembly confidence and answerable for everything domestic. The president is removable by a two-thirds assembly vote followed by a provincial referendum; the premier by ordinary no-confidence under the substitution rule.

**Upper Aule.** Small, homogeneous, a long tradition of initiative and referendum, low trust in parties. The Board rejected a proposal for a directly elected mayor-governor with power to legislate by ordinance and to dissolve the council. It prescribed instead a five-member executive council elected at large, chair rotating annually, no dissolution, any ordinance suspendable by popular initiative within ninety days, and any single member recallable by petition of a fixed share of voters.

**The Verrine Coast.** Three regions, one of them a secessionist minority with about thirty per cent of the population; every party is regional, none provincial. The Board prescribed a parliamentary premier, but required that confidence be withdrawable only by a majority that also carries majorities in each of the three regional delegations, and it refused a directly elected governor on the ground that a province-wide plurality would simply install the largest region's candidate permanently.

The case before the Board in my chapter is the province of **Marche**. Its assembly is elected by proportional representation with a low threshold and currently contains four parties, the largest near thirty-five per cent, internally disciplined but with no stable majority and no tendency to coalesce into two blocs. Administration is clean, the audit office is independent, there is no history of coups and no regional cleavage worth naming, and roughly seven in ten residents tell pollsters they want to elect their governor directly. Uniquely among the provinces, a large share of public money — something like a fifth of the provincial budget — comes not from taxation but from fees collected by a river-basin authority whose board the governor appoints.

The charter proposed to the Board for Marche would create a directly elected governor on a fixed four-year term, without power to dissolve the assembly, with a cabinet drawn from outside it, and would add a device new to the federation: the confidence-locked mandate. Once in any twelve-month period, the assembly may, by a single majority vote that simultaneously names a successor, transfer the remainder of a governor's term to that successor, no election being held. The basin authority's revenues would require annual assembly authorization, though its board would remain the governor's appointment. A clerk's note observes that no precedent pairs direct election with confidence-based removal: in Sable and Kalva the substitution rule attaches to a premier the voters never chose directly, and in Ondres the directly elected governor can be removed only by impeachment.

What I would like from you:

1. Three or four rules the Board might be running that fit all five precedents — stated sharply enough that they come apart on Marche. I can see at least one turning on whether every executive must be removable by somebody other than the electorate, one turning on what the party system has to look like before a single-person direct mandate is safe, and one turning on what powers such an office may hold; if there are others, or if mine are badly drawn, say so.

2. What each rule predicts about the Marche proposal as drafted, and about each of its separable features: direct election itself, removal with a named successor, the once-a-year restriction, the absence of dissolution, and the fee-funded authority.

3. Your best guess at the ruling, the precedent the Board would cite as its closest analog, and the precedent it would be wrong to cite because it is only superficially closest. Give me the reasoning the minute would record and, separately, the dissent — the scene needs both, and the dissent has to be an argument rather than an atmosphere.

4. If the five settlements genuinely underdetermine the sixth, say so plainly, and tell me what principle the Board would be adopting with each option, in the language a clerk would put in the minute rather than in the language of political theory. In particular: is there any amendment — to the removal device, to who may be named as successor, to the basin authority — that would make approval consistent where pure rejection is otherwise called for, and what exactly would the amendment be buying?

5. One altered fact about Marche that would force the competing rules to part company decisively, in case I want the Board to face it in a later chapter.
