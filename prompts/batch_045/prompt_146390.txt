I'm taking an upper-level seminar on Communication and Evolution — philosophy department, cross-listed with biology — and each of us has been asked to design and run one 75-minute session for the rest of the class. There are about twenty of us, mixed backgrounds, and the room has tables, index cards, a deck of cards, and no reliable projector. My session is on costly signaling and the handicap principle, and I want it to be a game rather than a presentation, because the three mistakes I keep hearing in section are all things people could discover for themselves if the incentives were arranged properly. The mistakes are: first, that a signal is honest *because* it is expensive, as though the cost itself bought truthfulness, rather than because the cost falls differently on different types relative to what they have to gain; second, that wastefulness is the essence of the thing, so that any inefficiency in a signal counts in favor of the explanation, when on the reading I find most plausible the waste is a byproduct and what does the work is the differential cost; and third, that costly signaling *guarantees* honesty, when what it does is make honesty one available equilibrium among others — a room full of bluffing signallers is also a possible outcome and I don't want the game to quietly rule it out by construction.

So here is what I want. Design a runnable game with real but harmless stakes — no money, ideally forfeited raffle tickets, tokens, or a tedious five-minute transcription task — in which participants are paired, one draws a hidden type from a deck, and the other decides whether to accept or reject them, with the signaller choosing a signal level beforehand. I'd like it staged so that the first phase is cheap talk and collapses into noise on its own, and the later phases let them find, by playing repeatedly, the signal level at which the types separate, and to notice that the separation point moves when the stakes of being accepted move and when the cost schedule moves. Nobody should hear the phrase "handicap" until the debrief. I also want two wrinkles handled, not just mentioned: a transfer variant, where the tokens a signaller gives up go to their receiver instead of being destroyed, so total surplus is preserved and nothing is wasted — I want participants to feel whether honesty survives that change and what it tells us about whether waste or differential cost was doing the work; and a phase with what I've been calling a "tell," a signal whose intensity is mechanically fixed by the signaller's type and so can't be faked at any price, to see whether they notice that it needs a different kind of explanation entirely.

Give me the full payoff table with actual numbers, the materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the debrief mapping specific moments in the session onto specific parts of the account. Please be concrete about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — students differ enormously in how much they mind doing a boring task, and someone will try to be clever about reputation across rounds — and how I should handle each. Finally, be honest about which feature of the idea the game cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself: my guess is that it's the fact that in the biological case the structure is an equilibrium produced by selection over many generations with nobody reasoning anything out, that real signals are honest only on average rather than perfectly, and that the whole setup assumes common knowledge of the payoff structure that my players will not quite have.
