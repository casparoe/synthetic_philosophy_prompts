Me and my brother-in-law got into it again on sunday. He's big on twin studies and he keeps saying stuff like "personality is about 50% genetic", and I keep pushing back because me and my sister had the same parents, same house, same schools, and turned out nothing alike. He said I don't understand what heritability means, and fair enough — I have zero stats background, I just got curious and started poking around on my own.

So please explain to me, in plain language, what a heritability number actually is. Not "the share of a trait that's caused by genes" — he says that's the wrong picture and I half believe him and half don't.

The bits I specifically can't get straight:

1. I read that if you make everyone's environment identical, heritability goes UP, without anything about anyone's genes changing. That feels like cheating. How can a number that's supposed to be about genes move when all you did was change the environment? Does that mean it's secretly measuring how unequal the environments were?

2. My own made-up idea, which nobody online will answer. Can you have a heritability number for a single individual? Take one kid who's six inches taller than the average kid. I want a number: how much of those six inches is genes? Is that just heritability applied to one person, or is it a completely different question — and if it's a different question, is it even a well-posed one? If you had to make it well-posed, what else would you need to specify before the question had an answer?

3. Everyone says heritability within a group tells you nothing about why two groups differ on average, but nobody ever spells it out. Give me a made-up example with actual numbers where a trait is strongly heritable inside each of two populations, and the gap between the two averages is entirely environmental with nothing genetic behind it. And while you're at it: can something be 100% heritable and still be completely preventable by changing the environment? Like, there's that thing where a baby can't break down some amino acid and you just take it out of their diet and they develop normally — is that the same point as the groups thing, or a different point wearing the same clothes?

4. The reason I got into this at all: I help run a neighbourhood seed-saving group, and we argue every year about wether to save seeds from the best-yielding plants. Our gardens are wildly different — some people water, some don't, some have great soil. So: does a heritability number tell us anything about whether picking the winners in these gardens will actually give us better beans next year? And if the number you'd want depends on how you set the measurement up, say which setup gives the number that answers our question, and what we'd have to actually go out and measure to get it.
