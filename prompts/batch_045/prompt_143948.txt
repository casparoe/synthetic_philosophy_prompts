I'm revising a paper on the unity of the proposition, and I want your help deciding which objection to spend the space I have left answering.

Here is the dialectic as I understand it. Bradley's regress argument is normally taken to show that if a proposition is a complex assembled out of a subject, a predicate, and a relation that is itself a further constituent tying the two together, then that relation is just one more item that must be tied to the others by a yet further relation, and so on without end. Every reply I know of tries to make the regress stop somewhere: treat the relation as active rather than as one more term; treat unity as a brute primitive; give up structure altogether and identify propositions with sets of possible worlds; or identify the proposition with an act of predication rather than with an abstract structured entity.

The view I want to defend, which I'll call the Fixed-Point Proposal, accepts the regress instead of terminating it. Let U be the operation that adds to a collection of items a further item purporting to unify them. Bradley's complaint, on my reading, is that no finite iteration of U ever delivers a genuine unity, because each application of U merely pushes the unifying work up to the next level. But that is just what iteration does; the remedy is to stop iterating and solve the equation instead. Take a proposition to be a structure P such that P = U(P): the unifier is already among the items it unifies, so there is no further level to which the question can migrate. The regress is not cut off by stipulation but closed into a loop, in the way a recursive definition can pin down an infinite object by a finite equation. Two payoffs: no relation needs any special metaphysical status, and the proposition remains finitely specifiable, so nothing is demanded of a finite thinker that a finite thinker cannot be given.

I also want to claim that content comes along for free. A self-unifying structure is one that presents its own terms as related, and that, it seems to me, just is what it is for something to have truth conditions; so the theory of content is not a separate undertaking from the theory of unity.

The objections I can anticipate are these: (i) constituency becomes non-well-founded, since the proposition turns out to be among its own constituents; (ii) the loop explains nothing, since saying that the relation relates itself to the terms says no more than that the terms are related; (iii) if P = U(P), then P is identical with a proposition that would otherwise be distinct, the one to the effect that P is unified, so propositions with different truth conditions collapse into one; (iv) self-application is primitivism with extra machinery bolted on.

Objection (ii) I think I can dispose of quickly, and I would like to know whether you agree: if the whole force of the regress is that unity is always deferred one level up, then a structure with no level above it is simply not a target of the argument, and there is nothing further for the Bradleyan to demand.

So my question is which of (i), (iii) and (iv) is the load-bearing objection, and what the most economical answer to it is, preferably one that tightens the construction rather than conceding a primitive unifier. In particular, can the collapse worry in (iii) be answered by indexing the fixed point to a level of granularity, so that P = U(P) holds while P remains distinguishable from, and can differ in truth conditions from, the proposition that P is unified? And is there a formulation that keeps the free-content result intact?
