Our reading group has spent most of a year going in circles about whether HCH — the recursive construction in which a person answers a hard question by consulting further people who answer sub-questions, each of whom may in turn split their sub-question further, with every node in the tree being an instance of the same kind of thing as the root, so that the whole object is characterized by a self-similarity or fixed-point condition rather than by any bottom layer of mere humans — is a defensible idealization of good deliberation, or only a suggestive picture of one mechanism. Two members have emerged as the poles: the amplificationist, who holds that the fixed point picks out something real, that it is what deliberation aims at when it scales up (courts, laboratories, bureaucracies, prediction markets being rough approximations), and that this makes it the right thing to point a training process at; and the decomposition skeptic, who holds that the fixed-point condition admits wildly many solutions, that which one you land on depends on how questions get split and on how much the parent can check the child, that apparent convergence is mostly shared human priors, and that therefore HCH is at best a machine for producing answers and never a standard by which answers are correct. They have agreed to stop debating and produce a joint statement instead, and I have one ninety-minute session in which to hand them a draft they can argue over. Please act as a professional philosopher of cognition and social epistemology who also works on the theory of machine-learning alignment, and before you write anything, think carefully and privately: brainstorm a long list of candidate agreed claims and candidate cruxes, throw out the ones that are either vacuous or that only one side would ever sign, and only then draft.

The document I want has three parts. First, a numbered section of eight to twelve claims both parties can sign, and I want them genuinely load-bearing rather than boilerplate: claims about what the formalism does and does not by itself entail, about which properties the target is supposed to have and what each property is for, about the root's structural dependence on compressed reports it cannot fully audit, about what would and would not count as an implementation, and about which familiar objections each side now concedes were overstated by its own camp. Second, a table of residual disagreements, with one row per issue and columns for a neutral restatement, the formulation the amplificationist would accept as a fair statement of her own view, the formulation the decomposition skeptic would accept as a fair statement of his, and a verdict on whether the disagreement is substantive, verbal, or verbal in part — and if you call any of it verbal, say precisely which part and what the two sides were each using the contested term to do. Third, a list of the cruxes: the sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one side, ordered by how much else depends on them, with for each crux a concrete case, experiment, or argument both sides would accept as evidence bearing on it, together with what each side predicts in advance and what result would count as a defeat for that side.

Here are the cruxes I would propose, which you should feel free to reject, split, or sharpen rather than dutifully adopt, and to which you should add at least two of your own: (1) decomposition-invariance of the limit — hold the top-level question and the total budget fixed and vary only the shape of the tree (branching factor, depth, whether a node can see its siblings' summaries, whether the root may re-decompose after seeing intermediate results); does the answer stabilize as the budget grows, or does it track the decomposition forever? (2) the no-cognitive-gain question — is the set of questions HCH gets right just the closure of what one unaided person with unbounded time and memory would eventually get right, i.e. is amplification a pure speedup, or does the tree reach questions no single trajectory of thought reaches? (3) whether self-similarity or idempotence is anywhere near strong enough to single out one fixed point, given how many functions satisfy the condition that applying them twice is the same as applying them once, and whether some additional constraint (a monotonicity or non-distortion condition on how nodes aggregate children, say) is doing the real work; (4) the bottleneck-at-the-root worry, that because only the root can decide which decomposition to trust, output quality should track the competence of the root rather than the total budget, which would make the tree a mere force multiplier on one person's judgment; and (5) whether information is conserved or destroyed as each level compresses the level below it, and what that implies about how deep the tree usefully goes.

I want a committed response, not a menu. Take a definite position on which of these cruxes are real and which of the surrounding disputes are about words, commit to one ordering of the cruxes by how much turns on them, put rough odds or confidence numbers on each side's predictions in the ledger, and tell me plainly where your judgments rest on guesses about how actual people behave in these setups rather than on conceptual argument — and say what would move you off the ordering you chose. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; work the problems out from the concepts, and keep the finished document tight enough to be read aloud in about fifteen minutes.
