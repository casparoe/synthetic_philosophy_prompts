For the last two years I have served, as a lay member with a scientific background, on the assisted-reproduction policy committee of a large academic hospital; I am a retired biostatistician, and in retirement I have been reading moral philosophy on my own for about three years. We now have to produce, within five weeks, a recommendation to the hospital board on which embryo culture protocol our IVF laboratory should use, and the case has turned out to be an instance of something I recognize from my reading as the non-identity problem, which I had always assumed was a curiosity confined to thought experiments about fourteen-year-old girls and depletion policy. The facts are these. Our current protocol, call it A, is sold under a supply contract that lapses in five weeks; the manufacturer has quoted a renewal at close to triple the present price. An alternative protocol, B, is substantially cheaper per cycle and, on our own registry, raises the live-birth rate per started cycle by about three percentage points. It is also associated with an increase in the rate of low birth weight among the resulting births, from roughly six percent to roughly nine percent, with the downstream developmental and metabolic consequences that follow from that. Because the laboratory budget is fixed, staying with A at the new price means funding roughly ninety fewer publicly subsidized cycles a year, and on our own conversion rates that means something like twenty-five to thirty children a year who would otherwise have been born will not be born at all, each of them with a life plainly worth living. The complication I keep pressing on my colleagues, and which they find tiresome, is that the two protocols do not simply make the same future children better or worse off: embryo survival and implantation are sensitive to culture conditions and to timing, so switching to B does not merely worsen the prospects of the children who would have been born under A, it changes which embryos implant and hence which particular children come to exist. Whatever we decide, the children born afterward will not be the children who would have been born otherwise.

There are three further wrinkles I want the memo to handle rather than smooth over. First, about eleven hundred embryos created under A are already in storage for some three hundred and forty patients; for that legacy cohort the embryos exist already, so a switch to B cannot be waved through with the observation that different children will result, and the choice for those patients looks like a choice between giving a particular existing embryo a slightly worse start under B, or adhering to A and possibly losing that embryo at thaw and transferring a different one, which raises the separate and to me harder question of whether you can lose anything by letting a particular embryo perish when the alternative is that it becomes a person with a somewhat elevated risk of a bad outcome. Second, a good deal of our evidence is probabilistic: for many cycles we cannot say whether the protocol change would have swapped which embryo implants or only altered the intrauterine environment for the same one, so the no-one-is-made-worse-off defense seems to come in degrees, and I do not know what a committee is supposed to do with a defense that holds at seventy percent confidence. Third, our draft recommendation contained a line to the effect that no child born under B would have refused to be born; I struck it, because the alternative for those children is not a worse life but no life at all, and I would like the memo to say in one sentence why that move is unavailable. For what it is worth, my own instinct is formed by my old field: in epidemiology we were entirely comfortable attributing harm to a population without being able to name the individuals harmed, but we could always say that someone in that population was harmed, and here I cannot say even that, which makes me suspect either that nothing is wrong or that my accounting framework is the wrong one.

What I need from you is the committee's decision memo, addressed to the hospital board and fit to be circulated: a firm recommendation among the options as we have framed them, with the dates and review conditions attached, and an honest statement of the strongest case for the option you reject. Our most persuasive member, a reproductive endocrinologist, argues that every child born under B will have a life well worth living, that no one is thereby made worse off, that the only interests genuinely in play are those of patients who want a child and want it sooner rather than later, and that on that basis B is not merely permissible but obviously right; I would like that argument given its best formulation rather than a caricature, and then answered. The memo should also take a position on two things the board will ask about regardless: whether we should delay and fund an eighteen-month pragmatic randomized comparison of A against B, given that randomizing the protocol is itself the kind of act that determines which children exist and that the control arm would assign identifiable patients to the inferior protocol; and what, if anything, our consent documents should tell patients about the fact that the protocol choice may determine which child they have. At our two patient focus groups several participants reacted sharply to exactly that, saying in effect that they wanted their embryo and not merely some embryo, and I do not know whether that reaction is a consideration we should weigh or a confusion we should design the consent language to avoid. Finally, and this is the part I most want, I would like the memo to end with a decision rule we could apply to the next protocol choice, the next medium, the next incubator setting, stated so that a future committee with different members could actually use it: when the choice before us changes who will exist, what are we to compare, whose standing are we to consider, and what, if anything, do we owe to people who owe their existence to the very decision being reviewed.
