THE QUESTION

Stage game: T = 5 (temptation), R = 3 (reward), P = 1 (penalty), S = 0 (sucker). The game is repeated with continuation probability δ after each round, so a strategy's expected payoff is the sum of its per-round payoffs weighted by δ^t. Players are drawn from a large population and randomly rematched each round; no one observes anyone else's interactions.

(a) Suppose the whole population plays Grim Trigger (cooperate until the partner defects once, then defect forever). Compute the smallest δ at which cooperation is self-enforcing. Do the same for Tit-for-Tat (cooperate in round 0, thereafter copy the partner's previous move). Which rule is more demanding, and why?

(b) It is sometimes said that Tit-for-Tat's victory in Axelrod's computer tournaments shows that reciprocity is what makes cooperation evolve. Assess this claim using three ideas: the folk theorem for repeated games; collective stability, in the sense that no mutant can score more against residents than residents score against each other; and Maynard Smith's ESS condition, whereby resident A resists mutant B iff V(A|A) > V(B|A), or V(A|A) = V(B|A) and V(A|B) > V(B|B). State explicitly which of these stability notions Tit-for-Tat satisfies, and what follows for the claim.

(c) Now suppose each player's intended move is misimplemented with probability ε > 0, and that a player observes only the payoff number it receives, not the partner's move. Explain why Tit-for-Tat and Grim Trigger do badly under these conditions, and describe a rule that does better, saying what it gives up.

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER

(a) If everyone plays Grim Trigger, cooperating forever pays R/(1−δ) = 3/(1−δ). The deviation to consider is defecting now and in every later round, since after one defection Grim Trigger defects forever anyway; that pays T + δP/(1−δ) = 5 + δ/(1−δ). So cooperation holds iff 3/(1−δ) ≥ 5 + δ/(1−δ), i.e. 3 ≥ 5(1−δ) + δ, i.e. δ ≥ 1/2.

Tit-for-Tat has the same threshold. The only deviation worth considering is still defecting in every remaining round: punishment lasts one round, but the bonus from T over R is a one-off, and against a Tit-for-Tat player defecting forever yields exactly the stream it yields against Grim Trigger, T followed by P forever. So both rules need δ ≥ 1/2, and neither is more demanding than the other. The general lesson is that the threshold depends only on T, R, and P, since S drops out.

(b) The folk theorem says that with sufficiently patient players, essentially any feasible payoff profile above the mutual-defection payoff can be sustained as an equilibrium of the repeated game — cooperation included, but unconditional defection included too. So the repeated game does not by itself predict cooperation: it shows cooperation is not irrational, not that it will be selected. Tournament victory is similarly limited evidence, since it ranks strategies by average score against one particular field of entrants and the ranking can change when the field changes; it is not a proof of optimality.

Collective stability is the evolutionary counterpart of the first ESS condition: no mutant scores more against residents than residents score against each other. Tit-for-Tat satisfies this as soon as δ ≥ 1/2, because the only mutant that could gain is an unconditional defector, which collects T once and then P forever. Tit-for-Tat is therefore evolutionarily stable in the sense that matters: no mutant can gain a foothold in a population of Tit-for-Tat players. The remaining gap between this and Maynard Smith's full definition is a technicality about neutral drift, with little evolutionary significance.

(c) With trembles, Tit-for-Tat unravels. One misimplemented defection is answered by a defection, which is answered by another, and the pair locks into an out-of-phase cycle in which each player alternates T and S and averages (T + S)/2 = 2.5 per round, below the 3 of full cooperation. Grim Trigger does worse: a single tremble moves it to permanent mutual defection at P = 1. What is needed is a rule that can absorb a single mistake. Win–stay/lose–shift (Pavlov) does this: repeat your previous move if your payoff was R or T, switch if it was P or S — equivalently, cooperate just in case both players made the same move last round. After a tremble, two Pavlov players are back to mutual cooperation within two rounds, and unlike Tit-for-Tat the rule runs off a player's own payoff alone, so it works even when the partner's move is never observed. What it gives up is deterrence: a rule that forgives this readily can be exploited by an opponent who defects systematically, and how much forgiveness pays depends on how noisy the environment is.
