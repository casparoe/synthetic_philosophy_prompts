Here's what's bugging me. I've been working on a seminar paper that's gone on way too long about what the fight between history-based (etiological) accounts of content and current-causal-role / structural-function accounts is really a fight about, and I keep getting the feeling that nobody has moved in forty years. The moves are all on the table and everybody knows them: the swamp case, where a coincidental physical process assembles a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a creature that has no ancestry at all, meant to show that history is what's doing the work; the learning and novel-content cases, meant to show that history can't reach far enough or can't be fine-grained enough; the vestigial-organ case, where a trait has the right selection history but no longer does the job. Everyone concedes that each of these has force and then goes home still holding their own view. That pattern is what I want explained.

My own hypothesis, which I don't trust: I think it's three arguments wearing one name — a metaphysical question about what makes a state have the content it has, an epistemological question about what could ever entitle anyone to attribute one content rather than another, and a question about which notion of content does real explanatory work in cognitive science — and that the famous intuition cases are secretly epistemological (they manipulate what we're allowed to know about a system's past) while being presented as metaphysical. I also suspect there's no crucial experiment, because in every system we could actually find or build, selection history and current organization are correlated; they only come apart in cases we can stage in the armchair. But here's a case I made up that I think presses on that suspicion. Suppose we implant a neural prosthesis: engineers iterated on the design over many trials to restore one specific tracking function, they intended it to do that, and once it's wired in it does the job stably. History, designer's intention, and current causal role all line up, and it seems to me every account gives the same verdict about what it represents. So what separates the accounts isn't the verdict, it's what they cite as making the verdict true. Is there any case — real, buildable, or merely conceivable — where the accounts give genuinely different verdicts and not just different stories? And if there isn't, is that evidence the dispute is verbal, or evidence that it's really about which feature is explanatory, which is a substantive question that just can't be settled by armchair intuitions at all? Tell me which part of my hypothesis you'd reject and why, and tell me honestly what would count as progress: a distinction I'm missing, a kind of empirical result, a different way of posing the question — or nothing, if you think the whole thing is ill-posed.
