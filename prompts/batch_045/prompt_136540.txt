I convene an upper-level seminar this term on knowledge, prices, and social epistemology, and I am building one session around the socialist calculation debate: Mises's original argument that a commonwealth without markets in the factors of production has no way to make its plans commensurable, Lange's trial-and-error reply in which a Central Planning Board announces parametric prices and revises them by an accounting rule — raise a price when there is a shortage, lower it when there is a glut — until the system settles, and Hayek's later objection that the interesting difficulty is not the arithmetic but the dispersal of local, tacit, continually changing knowledge and the incentives of the people who hold it. A colleague in the economics department has just circulated a manuscript claiming that the debate is essentially over and that the Misesian argument was never more than a complaint about computational capacity, which the last two decades of machine learning have quietly dissolved. Her central move is an analogy, and she offers most of the correspondences herself: the planning board is a learner; the economy is the model; the announced price vector is the parameter vector; the reported stock changes and queues are the training data; excess demand is the loss; the accounting rule that nudges prices up or down is a gradient step; the input-output structure of the economy is the architecture whose weights are being fit; and — this is the part she thinks is decisive — just as a network need not understand the mechanism it models in order to predict well, a planner need not possess any of the dispersed knowledge Hayek describes in order to allocate correctly. She also says, in passing, that Lange was proposing to *simulate* a market, that simulation is exactly what we now know how to do at scale, and that Hayek's argument was written before anyone had serious compute.

I want to put this analogy on the table in seminar and then take it apart, and I would like you to do the audit properly rather than tell me which side is right. Please give me, first, an explicit correspondence table: fill in the slots she leaves empty (what plays the role of the training set versus the test set, of the learning rate, of the optimizer, of the loss's being well defined at all, of overfitting) and say which of her stated mappings are the load-bearing ones. Second, disambiguate: "learning system" is not one thing, and I suspect the audit comes out differently depending on whether she is invoking supervised learning on historical data, on-policy reinforcement learning where the learner's own interventions are the data, or zeroth-order search that never sees a gradient at all — so please say which paradigm her accounting rule actually resembles, whether anything in the planned economy plays the role of the chain rule in credit assignment, and whether the procedure is supposed to converge for the reasons tâtonnement procedures converge in the formal literature. Third, separate the disanalogies that matter from the ones that are merely decorative: does the picture answer Mises and Hayek equally, or is it really only responsive to a complexity objection and silent on what the inputs to the loss function are, on who specifies the objective, on what happens when the planner's own interventions shift the distribution the data is drawn from, and on the fact that the people generating the data have reasons to misreport it? I am especially interested in whether the analogy quietly imports a fixed, exogenous, well-specified objective and a stationary data-generating process, and thus assumes away the very thing in dispute.

Finally: say plainly whether the conclusion she draws — that the calculation debate is settled and the Austrian side was merely computationally premature — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, whether it survives in weakened form (settled against Mises but not against Hayek, say), or whether the analogy was doing all the work; whether any part of the manuscript's case is genuinely new rather than Lange plus a larger machine; and, if there is a better analogy for what a planning board is actually doing, what it is. Assume an audience of advanced undergraduates who have read the primary figures: a table plus roughly six hundred to eight hundred words of prose, no web access needed, and please flag any place where you think the analogy obscures more than it reveals rather than smoothing it over.
