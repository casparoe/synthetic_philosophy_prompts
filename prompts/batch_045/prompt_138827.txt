I'm drafting a position paper on personal identity and mind uploading, and before I build an argument on top of these I want the foundations audited. Here is the package as I currently hold it.

1. The Nightly Protocol. Each night, while I am asleep, two percent of my neural tissue is exchanged for synthetic units that are matched to the units they replace only at the level of their input–output behavior over the range the surrounding network actually uses; the internal mechanism is not replicated. Every morning I wake and pass an exhaustive functional checkup, and I notice nothing. In a variant I will call the Mosaic, the functions of the replaced region are taken over by processes distributed across the whole remaining system, so that at no stage is there a part-for-part mapping between old units and new ones, though the overall functional profile is unchanged at every stage and I still notice nothing. In both versions I say that I survive, all the way to a fully synthetic brain. I also say that there is something it is like to be the finished system: I am a functionalist about consciousness and do not require carbon chemistry or the details of the electrochemical mechanism, but I do not think a hand-programmed system that reproduces only my verbal behavior and my slow, decision-level dispositions would be conscious, even if it fooled everyone who spoke with it.

2. Destructive upload. My brain is scanned at fine resolution, my body is destroyed, and a functionally isomorphic system is started on hardware. I call this death. The system that wakes up is a replica: it has my memories, it is conscious on my own view of consciousness, I have reason to care about what happens to it, and I might well choose the operation for its sake or because what I care about survives in it. But I do not think I would be choosing survival for myself.

3. Reductionism. There is no further fact of identity over time and no Cartesian ego; identity over time consists in relations of psychological continuity and connectedness. I lean toward the psychological version of this view and do not think continuity of the body or of any particular parcel of matter does independent work — it matters only as a carrier of psychology. And identity is not what matters in survival: what matters is the holding of those relations.

4. Fission. Both hemispheres are transplanted into two recipient bodies. I say that neither recipient is me. I also say — and this is the half I am attached to — that I would regard being fissioned as very nearly as bad as plain death: I would pay almost as much to avoid it, I would not accept it as a way of surviving, and I think grief for the person who went in would be appropriate.

5. Step size. Whether a replacement procedure preserves me depends on how much is replaced at a time. The same hundred percent of change spread over fifty nights is survival; compressed into a single operation under anesthesia it is death. I think there is a threshold here and I do not think my verdict is mere squeamishness, though I cannot say exactly where the threshold falls.

6. The Overlap Protocol. My replica is brought up on hardware while I am still alive; for a month the two of us run in parallel on the same sensory stream and converse daily; then I am destroyed painlessly. I find myself more confident here than in case 2 that the survivor is not me — during the month it is obviously a separate person, and it is not clear to me how anything that happens at my death could promote it to being me. I would pay as much to avoid this as to avoid plain death, and I would not count it as survival or even as nearly as good as survival.

7. Method. I think the literature's heavy reliance on case intuitions is compromised by status quo bias, and I try to give no weight to mere revulsion at a procedure; where a verdict of mine rests only on how unsettling the case feels, I take myself to have no verdict at all.

What I want is an audit, not a survey. Go through these and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only look like they are, and for the ones that only look like it, say what creates the appearance. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them as equal. For each, state precisely which commitment would have to give, what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set, and whether there is any cheaper fix that preserves more. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I have included the variants in 1 because I want the audit to run on the cases as I actually imagine them rather than on tidied-up versions. And please do not just report that everything is in tension with everything: I expect several of these to sit together perfectly well, and I would like to know which. I do not know where the trouble is — if I did, I would have fixed it myself — so do not assume I am especially attached to any one item, including item 3.
