I am a novelist, and alongside my fiction I record long-form conversations with philosophers for a literary magazine; my next subject is a philosopher who defends Norman Malcolm's position that dreaming is not an experience — that "I dreamt that such-and-such" is not a report of anything undergone in sleep, that there is no fact of the matter about what a sleeper was conscious of during a given stretch of the night, and hence that a dream report cannot be mistaken in the way a perceptual report can. I need eight or so questions that get past her restating the view. Please include: one on the correlation between REM sleep and dream reports, together with the follow-up to her likely reply that the report is produced on waking and so nothing in sleep need correspond to it — namely, whether her view then makes the correlation an inexplicable coincidence, and whether it ever predicted it; one on Dennett's objection that the position is committed to the entire dream being composed in the instant of awakening, which he treats as an empirical hypothesis with the virtue of being testable, along with the follow-up about what would count as testing it and whether calling it testable concedes that there is something for the sleeper to be wrong about; one on lucid dreamers who signal with deliberate eye movements from within sleep and later describe what they were doing, and on sleep-talkers whose utterances seem to narrate dream events as they occur; one on Malcolm's evident motive — that if dreams are not experiences, the sceptical argument from dreaming collapses — and whether setting that motive aside should alter our assessment of the argument; and one built on a case I want to put to her myself, in which neuroscientists decoding pre-awakening brain activity predict the gist of a sleeper's report, better than chance, several minutes before she is woken. For each question, give the answer she is most likely to make and the single question that should follow it. And please write the whole thing as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as though you were sending me your notes in an email.
