I retired a few years ago from a research career in geophysics and have since been sitting in on a philosophy reading group. Formal epistemology is the part I find most congenial, since it looks like the sort of thing one ought to be able to settle by calculation. I have become stuck on an argument I built myself about updating on uncertain evidence, and I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

The setting, as I understand it: strict conditionalization requires certainty in the evidence, which no perceptual episode supplies; the usual generalization is probability kinematics. Relative to a partition with cells E₁ … Eₙ, rigidity requires the new credence function to satisfy P*(H | Eᵢ) = P(H | Eᵢ) for each cell, from which the kinematic formula P*(H) = Σᵢ P*(Eᵢ)·P(H | Eᵢ) follows. My argument:

(1) No observation leaves me certain of any proposition about the world; at best it redistributes my credence across some partition of possibilities. So the operative rule for empirical learning is probability kinematics, and strict conditionalization is an idealization with no actual instances.

(2) The content of that rule is supposed to lie in rigidity: experience supplies the partition and the new marginals over it, and rigidity then fixes everything else.

(3) Now take any two credence functions P and P* on a finite space of worlds, with P positive everywhere and P* positive only where P is. Define r(w) = P*(w)/P(w), and let the partition be the level sets of r.

(4) On each such cell rigidity holds exactly, since r is constant there; and applying the kinematic formula to that partition and those new marginals returns P* itself.

(5) So for any reassignment of credence whatsoever, however arbitrary or unresponsive to experience, there is a partition on which it counts as a textbook-correct kinematic update, namely the level sets of the ratio of the new function to the old.

(6) Therefore probability kinematics constrains nothing. It is a rule with no content: every possible change of mind is an instance of it.

(7) That is absurd, and the construction plainly fails for strict conditionalization, since conditioning on E forces P*(E) = 1. So either (1) is false and I must find propositions that experience makes certain, or the constraint I thought lived in rigidity lives somewhere else entirely.

I am fairly confident in the algebra and fairly confident that the conclusion is false, so the fault must be in how I am reading the rule. My one instinct is that something is wrong with a partition defined partly in terms of the very posterior it is meant to yield, and that no experience could naturally be described as bearing on such a partition. But I cannot say why that should matter to a rule stated as a constraint over partitions, nor what would have to be added for it to matter.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If it comes to a choice, I would rather give up almost anything than (1), and I would also like to know whether whatever saves the rule from vacuity is a real constraint or only a relabeling of the problem.
