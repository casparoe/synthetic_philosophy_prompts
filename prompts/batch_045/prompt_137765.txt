I am a novelist, and I need a referee rather than a research assistant. My book in progress keeps failing at one scene: an old woman, the last fluent speaker of a highland people's language, is asked by a truth commission whether what happened to her people was genocide, and she refuses the word. No one killed us, she says. We were filed out of existence.

The case I invented for her is this. Over two generations a republic dismantles a pastoral people — call them the Salka — by administrative means alone. The register in which their kinship and grazing rights were recorded is abolished, so membership ceases to be a legal fact. Their language loses standing in courts, land records, and markets. Commons are converted to individual, alienable title and quietly bought. The households that keep the ritual calendar and know the migration routes are offered urban housing first and relocated first; other families are resettled one at a time into distant towns. Nobody is killed, sterilized, or formally transferred to another group. In three generations no one claims the name and the language has no fluent speakers.

Last spring, after a panel on writing atrocity, two of the other panelists — Renata, who spent eleven years prosecuting at an international tribunal, and Yusuf, who works on administrative classification in colonial censuses — argued about my hypothetical for most of an hour. The text they were arguing over reads: In the present Convention, genocide means any of the following acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such: (a) Killing members of the group; (b) Causing serious bodily or mental harm to members of the group; (c) Deliberately inflicting on the group conditions of life calculated to bring about its physical destruction in whole or in part; (d) Imposing measures intended to prevent births within the group; (e) Forcibly transferring children of the group to another group.

(1) Yusuf: by the letter, the Salka are not covered. Only (c) is even a candidate, and (c) requires physical destruction — precisely what this state avoided. But that is a defect, not a verdict. The concept was coined in 1944 for the coordinated destruction of the foundations of a group's life: its institutions, language, religion, economic existence. Killing was one technique among several, and not the most efficient. A definition that catches the slow method only when somebody happens to die is tracking the means rather than the end the category exists to name. (2) Renata: the narrowness is the design, not an oversight. What distinguishes genocide from crimes against humanity is the specific intent, and the deliberate severity of that requirement is what lets a conviction mean something. The 1948 text dropped cultural destruction and political groups; that horse is dead. Every extension is purchased with the currency of the word, and the word has already been inflated by advocacy use until courts and publics discount it. A category that covers forced schooling and extermination in the same breath will not move anyone to act. (3) Yusuf: then test it on my second hypothetical. Another state kills two percent of an identical people, selecting precisely the calendar-keepers and the elders who know the migration routes, and does nothing else. Numerically that is a smaller destruction than killing ten percent at random; structurally it is a far larger one, because what was destroyed was the group's capacity to reproduce itself as a group. If "in part" tracks anything that matters, it should track structure rather than headcount. (4) Renata: she conceded the structural point has force in the killing cases, where there is an act to hang it on, and then denied it can do the work Yusuf wants. A structural test makes the completion of the crime depend on contingencies the accused does not control — whether the dispersed marry out, whether the language is revived in a university department — so liability floats on the victims' later choices. Worse, it obliges a court to rule on who authentically carries the group's continuity. The law would become an authenticity tribunal, reenacting the classificatory violence that made the people legible as a target in the first place.

(5) Yusuf: refusal to name is part of the mechanism. A people can be unmade in a way that no available word registers, and the silence then counts as evidence that nothing much happened. (6) Renata: the debasement runs both ways; states now level the accusation as a cheap instrument against enemies while insulating themselves, and applying it where the legal trigger cannot fire buys condemnation at the cost of making the word useless to prosecutors. (7) Yusuf: you cannot have it both ways. If the term is a narrow jurisdictional trigger, then its application is a matter of fact and law, and consequences are irrelevant to it; if consequences do govern, you have conceded that it is an instrument of advocacy and your complaint about inflation is merely a complaint about other people's politics. (8) Renata: those are two registers, not an inconsistency. The word in an indictment and the word in a headline are homonyms. She is content to keep the legal term narrow and the moral vocabulary broad; it is his demand that one word do both jobs that manufactures the quarrel. (9) Then the wrinkle I added: the republic adopted the program after its own commission concluded that collective tenure and separate courts caused recurring massacres, and the killings did in fact stop. Renata said that settles it — the purpose was to save lives and the group's dissolution was a foreseen side effect, which is not intent to destroy it as such. Yusuf said the purpose/foresight distinction is doing all the work: the state chose this people's dissolution as its instrument, and chose it for this people because of what they were, which is what as such marks. If a benign ultimate end defeats the intent, the exemption swallows the rule, since nearly every assimilationist program in history was justified by security. (10) Finally, my old woman. Yusuf read her refusal as evidence that the extermination paradigm has colonized the concept: she cannot say the word because the camp is its implicit measure, and so her people are unmade twice. Renata read it the other way round: the survivor knows a specific bureaucratic injury when she sees one, and calling it by the name of the camps flattens a distinct wrong into a moral superlative.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move — which moves are right, which are partly right, which are wrong, and where the weight of the argument actually shifts — then say who is more right overall and what both of them are missing. I do not need to be told what my narrator should say; that is my job. And tell me which of the three sub-questions does most of the work: what the 1948 definition correctly covers, what the destruction of a group really consists in, and what follows from calling it by one name rather than another.
