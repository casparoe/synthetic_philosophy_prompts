I am applying for a four-month residential fellowship and need a two-page proposal, and I should begin by saying what I am and what I am not. For a little over thirty years I was an environmental chemist, most of it spent on the assessment of substances released into river systems, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy seriously but from the outside. My professional reflex is to distrust an argument that sounds better than it is, and I would like the proposal to survive a reader who shares that reflex.

The topic is the precautionary principle. I am not interested in another defence of it or another demolition of it. I want to ask whether it has been formulated at the wrong scale.

The standard formulations ask whether a proposed action carries a threat of serious or irreversible harm, and then instruct the decision maker: shift the burden of proof, choose the least hazardous feasible alternative, prohibit until safety is demonstrated. Two complaints are familiar. The first is that the principle, read per action, forbids everything, since inaction and the safer alternative have their own catastrophic possibilities. The second is that it is redundant: once the missing probabilities are supplied and the value of keeping options open is counted, precaution seems to recommend nothing that careful expected-utility reasoning with option value would not already recommend.

My half-formed idea is that seriousness and irreversibility are properties of trajectories through a sequence of decisions rather than of individual acts, and that irreversibility is really two things usually run together: the physical kind, where the system cannot be put back, and the epistemic kind, where what would have been learned by acting cannot be recovered. Applied per act, precaution tends to defeat itself along the second axis: refusing a small, contained, reversible trial preserves physical options while destroying epistemic ones, because staged intervention is often the only way to convert ignorance into risk. In my own field the working version of precaution was never a per-act veto but a design rule — contain it, stage it, monitor it, keep rollback available. I want to know whether reconceiving precaution as a principle about how decisions should be sequenced, and about what shape the remaining set of options should have, answers the paralysis complaint; and whether it can answer that complaint without collapsing into the option-value version of expected utility, which would leave it with no content of its own.

The last point is where I expect the trouble. Option-value reasoning wants a probability distribution over what we might later learn, whereas precaution is supposed to operate exactly where we have none; in my experience the uncertainty that actually mattered was structural, a question of whether we had the right model at all, not of parameter values within a model we trusted. If my reframing secretly needs the probabilities I do not have, I would rather find that out now than in month three.

Please turn this into a proposal with: the question stated so that it has a determinate answer; why the existing ways of setting up the debate leave it open; what I would actually do over four months, and why that route rather than the obvious alternatives, which I take to be a survey of how precaution has in fact been applied in regulation, and a more formal treatment in the manner of decision theory under ambiguity; what the possible conclusions are and which considerations would decide between them; what would count as the project having failed; and an honest estimate of whether four months is too long or, more likely, too short. Do not organize anything around names, titles, or dates — I will add the citations myself, and a proposal that leaned on them would be of no use to me. Describe positions and arguments instead. Where you think I am wrong, argue with me rather than helping me say it more elegantly.
