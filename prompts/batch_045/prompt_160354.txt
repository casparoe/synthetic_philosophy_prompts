Our housing co-op (about 200 members, self-managed) has to pick between two big renovation plans for the building, and one member has been pushing an idea he picked up somewhere: don't vote on the plan, vote on what "going well" even means, then let a betting market pick the plan that best delivers it. He keeps saying things like "stop fighting about facts you're all wrong about, just put your stake where your mouth is." Honestly a lot of us are tempted, because our meetings are endless and the loudest three people always win.

The version on the table is: we'd first vote on a single success measure — he suggests the average score on our annual member satisfaction survey, one year after the work finishes. Then anyone can trade play-money contracts, a seperate one for each plan, that pay out according to that number if the plan is adopted, and the co-op implements whichever plan's market prices higher. Payouts are in chore-hour credit, so a good trade can buy you out of a month of cleaning the stairwells.

A few things bug me and I can't tell whether they're real problems or just me being squeamish about markets. (1) The survey is filled in by the same people who are trading, so someone holding contracts that pay off if plan A goes badly could just... fill in the survey badly. (2) Plan B finishes in eight months, plan A takes three years, so a survey at one year doesn't catch both of them the same way. (3) Plan A mostly helps the top-floor units and plan B the ground floor, and averaging over all of us seems to bury the actual argument we're having. (4) In practice two members do almost all the trading and everyone else just watches.

Is this a good way to make the decision? Please actually take a side — tell me whether we should do it at all, and whether there's a version of it you'd defend — rather than handing me a balanced list of pros and cons, which is exactly what our meetings already produce. And if you think one of my four worries is the killer and the rest are survivable, say which one and why.
