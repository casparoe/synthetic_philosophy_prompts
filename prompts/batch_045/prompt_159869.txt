hey — I'm a few years into a philosophy of mind PhD and I'm finally writing the chapter where I have to stop hedging and say what I actually think about behavioral criteria for mind. I've been going case by case on gut reaction and I'm pretty sure my verdicts dont add up to anything coherent. So here they are, and I want the principle behind them, or the news that some have to go.

One scoping thing: I'm after "does this thing have genuine intentional states / genuine understanding," not "is there something it's like to be it." Though I notice the consciousness question keeps sneeking back in, so tell me if that's a symptom that my verdicts are tracking two different questions.

1. The lookup table. A system with a precomputed reply for every conversation up to some enormous length — Block's Blockhead, basically. It keeps a conversation going for decades. My verdict: no understanding, and no amount of extra decades would change that.

2. The same system, but implemented as a learned generative model with compressed internal representations instead of a table. Behaviorally identical, same decades. My verdict: also no — but I feel definately less sure here, and I can't say why the implementation should matter if the behavior is the same. This is the pair I can't reconcile.

3. A crow that solves a novel multi-step puzzle using a tool it modified itself. Verdict: yes, it has beliefs and desires about the puzzle, and I'm happy to read that straight off its behavior. So behavior is decent evidence here.

4. A locked-in patient: fully paralyzed, one eyelid for yes and no. Verdict: obviously a full mind, despite a behavioral repertoire of about one bit. So behavior isn't necessary.

5. A sleepwalker who gives fluent, apparently responsive answers to questions in their sleep. Verdict: those utterances don't manifest a mind — yet the behavioral evidence is rich. So behavior isn't sufficient either. Note that 4 and 5 pull opposite directions: minimal behavior with a mind, rich behavior without one.

6. The committee: the outputs come from a vote among a thousand narrow specialist modules, none of which has any representation of the exchange as a whole, and the aggregate passes every test. Verdict: no mind, or at least not one mind.

7. The leak: a system whose training corpus, unknown to the testers, contains the test items together with their answers. It aces the test. Verdict: this counts for nothing — but I notice my objection is about the test rather than the system, and I don't know how to convert it into a claim about the system.

8. The dodger: whenever a probe goes somewhere it hasn't been trained for, it produces a fluent non-answer that human raters score as highly as a real answer, so it's never caught out. Verdict: doesn't show understanding — but again, is my complaint about it or about my raters?

9. The one I keep flip-flopping on: a system that volunteers first-person reports about its own internal states, is behaviorally indistinguishable from a human's introspective reports, and is reliably better than chance at predicting its own behavior on prompts it has never seen. It feels like evidence to me, and I can't articulate why that isn't just more behavior of the same sort I dismissed in 1 and 8.

10. A whole research lab, or an institution, that produces adaptive apparently-intentional output over decades. Verdict: no mind there — though this might just be case 6 at a larger scale, in which case I should be asking whether my verdict in 6 is really about unified agency.

My tentative principle, which I hate: behavior is evidence of mind only when it's produced by the sort of mechanism that produces it in creatures we already take to have minds. But that seems to make the behavior itself evidentially inert — the mechanism does all the work — and it makes 3 and 4 come out for reasons that have nothing to do with anything I can observe.

So: can you give me a principle, or two or three candidates, stated precisely enough that I could quote it in the chapter, and say which clause is doing which of my cases? Then tell me what it predicts for cases I haven't listed (at least two that would discriminate between your candidates), and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't keep. If the real answer is that 1 and 2, or 4 and 5, can't both stand, say which one you'd overturn and why — I'd rather have that than a principle with a special clause bolted onto it for every case.
