Two people in my department have been going at each other for about a year now and I want them to come out of it with a document instead of another shouting match after the seminar. One of them is basically a Lange-model market socialist who thinks the Mises/Hayek objection was an artifact of pre-war computing and that modern machine learning finally retires it. The other is a Hayekian who insists the objection was never really about arithmetic, it was about knowledge that doesn't exist yet at planning time and about the stuff a machine operator knows but couldn't state if you asked her nicely.

The proposal they keep fighting about is this, and I want the statement built around it rather than around the 1930s texts. Call it the Ledger: every firm is required to log, in real time and at item level, its stocks, orders, machine states, and the small local adjustments its people actually make — a supplier swapped, a batch re-run, a dial nudged — into one national dataset. The planning board trains a single big differentiable simulator of the economy on that logged data, gets shadow prices by differentiating a welfare objective back through the simulator instead of by trial and error, re-solves nightly, and instructs managers to treat those prices as parametric and set output where price equals marginal cost. Because the Hayekian keeps saying that the real work done by profit and bankruptcy is discovery rather than calculation, the design also includes an in-house red team of paid people whose job is to hunt for holes in the plan and who get bonused for finding them.

Assume generously, by the way: compute is cheap, the simulator is well calibrated, and its forecasts are good. I don't want the Hayekian winning on a cheap your-model-is-wrong point, and I don't want the socialist winning on a cheap look-how-fast-this-runs point.

So: write the joint statement of a good-faith adversarial collaboration between them. Numbered section of agreed claims first — including about what the Lange-model style of argument actually shows, about what the knowledge argument is and isn't a claim about, and about the fact that the Ledger's simulator was trained on data generated under markets. Then a disagreements table. For each disagreement give both the Langean and the Hayekian formulation, worded so that that side would genuinely sign its own row.

Then the cruxes, with for each one a concrete case or test both would accept as bearing on it and each side's prediction written down in advance. The ones I think matter, though add or replace as you see fit: whether a simulator trained on market-generated data can be trusted for the off-support counterfactuals planning actually requires; whether telemetry captures tacit knowledge or only its behavioural residue, and whether the difference does any work; whether a red team that cannot go bankrupt and holds no residual claim is doing entrepreneurship or something thinner; the disclosure dilemma, where publishing the prices lets firms learn and game the update rule while withholding them breaks the parametric-price instruction that the whole scheme runs on; and what would count as solved anyway, given that the welfare objective has to be written down in units the planner has to choose.

Where you think part of their fight is verbal rather than real, say which part and why. And for each side, one sentence on what it would take as evidence against itself. Keep the whole thing under about a thousand words, and plain-English any bit of ML or economics jargon you use, because a couple of non-economists are going to read it. No bibliography, no citations — I only care about the substance.
