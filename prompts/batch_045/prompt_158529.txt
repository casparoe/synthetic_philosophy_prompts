I've been reading rationalist blogs and forum comment sections for like fifteen years, and the "is the world actually getting less violent" argument is my favorite example of a debate that has completly stalled. The optimistic side brings per-capita homicide and battle-death numbers plus the unusually long stretch without a great-power war since 1945; the pessimistic side says you've gerrymandered what counts as violence (state violence, structural violence, domestic violence, whether a famine death caused by policy counts) and that per-capita is the wrong statistic anyway if what you care about is total expected suffering or the risk of one catastrophic century. Then someone always drops the heavy-tails thing: if war casualties really are roughly power-law distributed with a tail exponent at or below 1, the mean isn't estimable from any sample we have, "expected deaths from war per year is falling" isn't a well-posed claim, and a seventy-year quiet stretch is just the kind of thing you'd see by chance — so no trend argument of the usual shape can ever get off the ground.

My hypothesis is that this isn't one disagreement wearing three costumes, it's three stacked ones: a verbal dispute about what 'violence' denotes and which denominator matters; a substantive but nearly untestable empirical question about the shape of the tail; and a deeper split about what the number is for — describing a rate we've observed vs. forecasting a risk we have to act under. Is that the right decomposition, or is the real driver something else (reasoning from trend vs. reasoning from mechanism, how much weight to give a low-probability catastrophic century, whether per-capita vs. absolute vs. "share of all humans who ever lived" is the morally relevant quantity)? Diagnose it concretely, say which layer is actually doing the work, and then — given your diagnosis — tell me what would count as progress: a different statistic, a formal result about heavy tails, agreeing to ask a different question, or honestly nothing, and why.
