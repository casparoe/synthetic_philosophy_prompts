I teach a twelve-week upper-level seminar called The Ethics of Suffering, and I'm now writing the final. The last third of the course was on suffering-focused consequentialism, and I need one essay question on lexical threshold negative utilitarianism — 60 minutes, roughly 1,200 words, 25 marks — that cannot be answered by stating the view and then reciting the greatest-hits objections (the extinction reductio, the trivial-pain-in-a-faraway-place case, the "but we'd have to abolish predation" line). What I actually want to test is whether students have located the real fault line: the tension between saying that pleasure and pain are commensurable magnitudes on one hedonic scale, and saying that suffering above some threshold carries a lexically superior kind of moral weight. My colleague and I have been using the label Parity Constraint for the first commitment — the claim that for any unit of suffering there is some finite, perhaps astronomical, quantity of pleasure that at least matches it in moral weight — and it seems to us that lexical threshold NU has to deny Parity, and that once you deny Parity you owe an account of what sort of scale you are still on.

So please give me three things: the exam question itself; a model answer of 700–900 words; and a marking scheme that separates an A from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain. The question should force at least these moves. (1) Distinguish cardinal commensurability on a single Archimedean scale from genuinely lexical superiority, and say why this is a dispute about the formal structure of the value ordering rather than about the measurability of experience — so that no amount of neuroimaging or experience-sampling could settle it — and what would have to be true of value for a non-Archimedean ordering to be coherent at all. (2) Say what the threshold is a threshold *of*: momentary phenomenal intensity, net valence of an episode, lifetime total, or population aggregate — and show why each choice generates its own problem, using the fact that pleasures and pains can occupy the same moment and interact non-additively (relief that exists only because of prior pain, the enjoyment of a painful stimulus, the bittersweet). If pain is not simply negative pleasure, the single-scale assumption both sides share is already in trouble; if it is, the threshold looks arbitrary. (3) Show how little work the lexical component does: in a world containing many above-threshold sufferings, lexicality settles only cross-threshold comparisons, so the view still owes a complete intrathreshold axiology — aggregation across persons and times, and risk — which is just the hard part of utilitarianism untouched. A top answer should say how much real daylight is left between lexical threshold NU and a merely weighted or prioritarian suffering-focused view. (4) Handle the epistemic squeeze: if the threshold's location is vague or intensity estimates are uncertain, then either minute probabilities of above-threshold suffering swamp every finite consideration, or above-threshold suffering gets a huge but finite expected weight and the lexical structure has quietly collapsed into weighted NU.

Two cases I've used in seminar, which you're welcome to build into the question: the Relief Package, in which a fully informed person prefers a brief above-threshold pain because it is followed by an hour of correspondingly intense relief-pleasure; and the Triage Ward, in which ten thousand people all suffer well above the threshold and resources can either relieve a little for everyone or a great deal for one person. The Relief Package should be made to do the work of forcing students to say whether the view is a theory of personal welfare or an impersonal axiology, and why lexicality sharpens that question rather than answering it. Finally, I'd like the marking scheme to include, alongside the marks, a one-line note on how candidates standardly get each point wrong — in particular the two failure modes I see every year: treating "lexical" as merely a very large finite weight, and sliding from a lexical axiology into an agent-relative deontological constraint on inflicting suffering. A B answer should be able to state and apply the view correctly; an A answer should additionally be able to say what lexical threshold NU would have to give up in order to be coherent, and whether what remains is still recognisably a form of negative utilitarianism.
