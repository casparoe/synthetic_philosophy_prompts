I host a small, slightly scrappy philosophy podcast — half an hour, one guest, no panel. Next week's guest is a philosopher who's just put out a short book on the extended mind thesis, and his angle is that everyone has been fighting over the wrong thing. He thinks the parity principle is basically a red herring, and that what actually matters is what he calls the answerability condition: an external resource counts as part of your mind only if you are the right person to hold answerable for what it gets you to believe — you are the appropriate target of a standing demand for justification, the kind that survives over time and doesn't evaporate when you say you were only reading off the screen.

I don't want nine variations of so what is the extended mind thesis, then. Draft me exactly nine interview questions, numbered, no intro and no sign-off. Each item should be the question plus the follow-up his most likely answer would invite, in no more then two sentences total. Stuff I'd like covered:

- what the answerability condition buys that the parity principle plus the usual reliability, trust and durability clauses doesn't buy;
- the standard coupling-constitution objection — that merely being causally coupled to a resource isn't the same as that resource constituting a cognitive process — and, when he says his condition is normative rather than causal, what settles whether part of the mind is a causal notion or a normative one in the first place;
- bloat: whether the condition lets in satnav, a smartphone, a colleague's memory, an LLM's autocomplete suggestion, and whether he just bites the bullet on the mind turning out to be enormous;
- a case I want to spring on him: a shared resource — a couple's kitchen calendar, a lab notebook two people both write in — where one physical vehicle would seem to be in two minds at once. If answerability is individual, is it in both or neither, and isn't that a reason to doubt the mind is the right unit here;
- one-way couplings: a colour-coded cable, a pill organiser, a signpost. Information flows in, but there is nobody to be answerable to it. Does his condition exclude these, and if so doesn't that show answerability is doing all the work and tracking nothing about constitution;
- the swap test: someone quietly alters the entries in Otto's notebook and he never notices, or he loses the notebook, shrugs, and never reconstructs it. Was it ever part of his mind, and does extended cognition require counterfactual sensitivity to what is in the vehicle;
- whether any of this reaches consciousness or only cognition, and if only cognition whether that is still the radical claim it is advertised as;
- downstream: if a notebook can literally be part of a mind, does compelling its production start to look like compelled self-incrimination, or a phone search like a search of a person? He will probably say the metaphysics is independent of the law, so press on what would have to be true for it not to be;
- and at the end, what would genuinely make him drop the answerability condition, plus the rude version of the so what question: does the thesis tell cognitive scientists or interface designers anything they didn't already know, or is it purely a classificatory dispute.

British English throughout please, spellings and all — and keep it spoken, these are things I'm going to say out loud, definately not read like a journal abstract.
