I have been reading this corner of the internet — the sequences, the comment sections that grew out of them, the long argument threads about anthropics and AI risk — for something close to fifteen years, and in that time I have watched Solomonoff induction drift from a piece of mathematics into a warrant. The move I have in mind goes like this: someone wants to resist the claim that some catastrophic or unprecedented hypothesis deserves a credence of one in a billion; they say the hypothesis can be specified in a few hundred bits, so the simplicity prior cannot be pushing it below two to the minus few hundred, and look, the completeness theorem shows this policy is optimal in the limit, so the numbers are not arbitrary but rigorous. I no longer think the warrant transfers, and I would like you to tell me what would show me that it does.

The position, stated as precisely as I can manage.

First, registers. People invoke Occam's razor in at least three distinct jobs: predicting the next observation, selecting among hypotheses that explain a body of data, and choosing an action on the basis of a model. Solomonoff induction is an optimality result about exactly one of them — cumulative error in next-symbol prediction — and it is silent about the other two, both of which are where the blog-standard arguments actually deploy it. There is nothing in the theorem that says the shortest program consistent with the data is the hypothesis you should believe or act on.

Second, the shape of the guarantee. What the theorem gives is a bound on expected total error over an infinite sequence, proportional to the complexity of the true environment. That is a statement about averages across an endless run of predictions. It entails nothing about the probability assigned to any single proposition at any finite time, and in particular nothing about one-shot credences concerning events that have no repeat instances — the arrival of transformative AI, first contact, the fate of a civilization. Those are the cases the warrant is wanted for.

Third, machine relativity. The dependence on the choice of reference machine is discounted as a constant factor. But the factor is constant only in the asymptotic sense; for any finite body of data and any computable continuation you like, there is a universal machine whose prior favors that continuation, and it differs from your preferred machine by what is in effect a compiled lookup table of fixed size. The asymptotic irrelevance of the choice is therefore cold comfort precisely when what you care about is the next stretch of data rather than the tail.

Fourth, promiscuity in the short run. Before convergence does its work, the prior mass is substantially taken up by hypotheses that are short but from which no prediction can be extracted by any feasible computation, by deceiver and simulation hypotheses whose predictions depend on facts about your own reasoning, and by re-encodings that are short only relative to an arbitrary machine.

Fifth, the positive claim I would replace all this with. When the razor works — in science, in learning a concept from a handful of examples — I think what does the work is a contingent empirical fact about this world rather than the mathematics of the prior: the processes we care about are not merely low in Kolmogorov complexity but low in complexity in a way that is cheap to find, compressible by programs that are short and quick to run, and whose structure is legible at the level of description our own perceptual and conceptual apparatus hands us. Call it the cheap structure thesis. If that is right, then the interesting object is not the Solomonoff prior but something like a prior that penalizes computational cost as well as description length, and the cost of that substitution is that you lose the dominance theorem entirely: a world whose generator is short but slow gets mispredicted forever.

What I want from you is a map of the exposure of this position, not a discussion of whether it is interesting.

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Include the ones I have not thought of. In particular I want the strongest available version of the reply I keep encountering, which is that the choice of reference machine is simply the place where your prior information lives, and that treating it as an objection mistakes a feature for a bug; tell me whether that reply concedes my second and fifth points while saving the practice, or whether it actually answers them. I also want you to deal with the move of taking a mixture over all universal machines, which as far as I can tell just returns you to a choice of machine one level up, and with the suggestion that the correct response to my third point is that uncomputable or adversarial hypotheses cancel out and can be ignored.

Be concrete about what would count against me, in three kinds. Formal: what result, if someone proved it tomorrow, would defeat the cheap structure thesis or make my second and third points practically inert — for instance a theorem showing that across a broad natural class of environments the short-run predictions of the Solomonoff prior and of a runtime-penalizing prior agree within a small factor, or a bound showing that the pathological mass I complain about leaves one-shot credences roughly calibrated anyway. Empirical: what would the world have to look like for me to be wrong — a domain where description-length simplicity systematically beats cost-penalized simplicity, or the reverse — and say in advance which outcome favors which side, so that I cannot read the result either way. Dialectical: which of my five claims carries the most weight, and is the position separable, so that losing one claim leaves the rest intact?

Give me your honest assessment of whether I have qualified the view into unfalsifiability. My own worry is the fifth claim: if I say the razor works because this world happens to be cheaply structured, it is not obvious what could show the razor does not work, and a position that can absorb every outcome is worth much less than one that cannot.
