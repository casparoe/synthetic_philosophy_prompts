I convene a small fortnightly reading group on long-term AI governance, and for our last three sessions we have been stuck on the same question: not whether transformative AI arrives, and not when, but how fast the interval between two milestones would be once the first of them is reached. I want a conditional forecast, explicitly not a date. Stipulate Milestone A: the point at which AI systems can perform the whole loop of frontier AI research — forming hypotheses, designing experiments, writing and debugging training and evaluation code, interpreting results, deciding what to do next, writing it up — at the level of a competent mid-career research engineer, for under a tenth of the all-in cost, with humans supplying only compute, data and high-level direction. Stipulate Milestone B: systems related to the best human AI researchers roughly as those researchers are related to a capable first-year doctoral student, able to compress what a frontier lab does in a year into days, and to do it competently well outside the domains they were trained on. My question is your credence that B arrives within three calendar years of A, and separately within ten.

My own numbers are 0.35 and 0.65; a colleague in the group, who works on hardware supply chains, gives 0.08 and 0.30; and after three sessions I no longer think we disagree about intelligence at all. The framing I have been defending — and which I want you to test rather than accept — is that takeoff speed is barely a function of how capable the systems are, and is instead the elasticity of research progress with respect to *parallelised* effort multiplied by the growth rate of the physical capital base: fabs, power, data-centre build-out and the supply chains behind them. On that view there are four separable questions, and I want a number and a one-line justification for each, plus the combination shown so I can see what my 0.35 is actually made of: first, how much of the research loop parallelises at all once machine researchers are abundant — the serial-depth question, meaning how many irreducible cycles of experiment-to-feedback, each with a duration set by physics (a training run, a fabrication step, a robotics or wet-lab trial), the next advance requires; second, whether the binding constraint by then is ideas, which copy and recombine at machine speed, or atoms, which plausibly grow at something like ten to thirty per cent a year; third, whether gains arrive across cognitive domains at roughly the same time or in a ragged order — call it synchrony rather than speed — since that matters more than the aggregate rate for whether any institution could hand off control gradually; and fourth, whether something outside the model — a moratorium, a war, an economic shock, or a reliability wall that automating research does not itself remove — simply stops the process inside the window. I would also like an honest verdict on whether the three-year figure is even well posed, given that a hundred machine-researcher-years may elapse inside a single calendar year, and if it is not, what the honest re-specification is. I don't need to be walked back through the fast-versus-slow takeoff literature; assume I have read it.

Please lead with your headline number for the three-year claim in the first sentence, then give the decomposition, then the sensitivity analysis: which single sub-question, if I updated on it, would move the headline number most, and where you think my colleague and I genuinely disagree. No preamble, and no restating of the question.
