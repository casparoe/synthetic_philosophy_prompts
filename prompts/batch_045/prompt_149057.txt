I'm in a graduate reading group on the foundations of quantum mechanics, and last week we spent the whole session on the Born rule problem in the many-worlds interpretation. I had thought I understood the basic dialectic: because there is no collapse, every outcome is realized in some branch, so there is a puzzle about what it even means to assign probabilities, and the naive move of counting branches gives the wrong answer when the amplitudes are unequal. But a few of us got stuck on a follow-up point, and I realised that I cannot articulate it cleanly enough to be confident I'm right.

Here is the nub. Take a spin measurement on a particle prepared so that the amplitude for "up" is the square root of 1/3 and the amplitude for "down" is the square root of 2/3. The branch counter assigns credence 1/2 to each, which is wrong; the Born rule assigns 1/3 and 2/3. Now imagine someone proposes replacing the Born rule with a "cubic rule", on which the weight of a branch is the cube of the absolute value of its amplitude, renormalised so that the weights sum to one. I want to claim two things, and I am not sure I can defend either. First, that even if we simply stipulated the cubic rule, we would not have dissolved the Born rule problem, because the problem is not really about which exponent appears but about what talk of probability is doing in a theory in which nothing, apparently, is left to chance. Second, that the cubic rule is not even a coherent candidate, because, unlike the sum of the squared amplitudes, the sum of the cubes is neither preserved under unitary time evolution nor invariant under a change of basis, so it would make an agent's credences depend on which decomposition of the universal wavefunction we choose to call "the branches".

Could you give a careful, advanced-level explanation of whether and why I am right on both counts? In particular, I would like you to (a) work through the sqrt(1/3)/sqrt(2/3) example and then a second measurement, performed only on the "up" branch, that splits it into two equal-amplitude sub-branches, showing exactly how the Born rule and the cubic rule come apart in the weights they assign to the final branches; (b) explain why the failure of the cubic rule to be basis-independent is not a mere technical nuisance but goes to the heart of why the squared-amplitude measure is the only serious candidate for a probability rule here; and (c) say what, if anything, is left of the Born rule problem once we grant that—i.e. what exactly is the residual conceptual difficulty, if the exponent itself is forced by the structure of the theory? I would much rather have a careful conceptual and semi-technical walkthrough than a survey of who argued what, so please do not assume I have read any of the specialist literature on derivations of the Born rule; assume only familiarity with Hilbert spaces, unitary evolution, and basic decision theory.
