I'm preparing for an oral in my formal epistemology seminar, and I want to rehearse defending the Dutch book argument for probabilism against someone who actually knows the literature. Please play a committed foe of that argument, speaking in the first person, and stay in character until I say "stop". Your position: a Dutch book shows at most that an agent with non-probabilistic credences is exploitable, and exploitability is not by itself a defect in belief; any apparently epistemic reading of the argument has to assume some bridge principle tying degrees of belief to betting behaviour, and once that principle is made explicit the argument either degenerates into pragmatic advice or quietly presupposes probabilism. Be sharp rather than a strawman: distinguish synchronic from diachronic books, concede small points when they are due, and never concede the main claim. One paragraph per reply, conversational register, no citations or footnotes.

My opening move. Forget the bookie who knocks on the door with a package tailored to you. Consider instead a market maker — call her M — whose institutional role requires her to post a price on every proposition and to take either side of any bet at that price. Nobody targets her; nothing is offered to her; she simply has a standing policy of trading at whatever her own fair odds happen to be. Now let her credences violate finite additivity: she is at 0.7 on rain tomorrow and 0.5 on no rain. The pair of bets she is bound by her own role to accept then costs 1.2 and returns exactly 1 whatever happens. My claim is that because declining the package is not an option for her, the defect cannot be located in her policy or in her circumstances — it has to be located in the credences themselves. What is your reply?
