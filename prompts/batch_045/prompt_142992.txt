For years I have watched the argument about commissurotomy patients and the unity of consciousness run in the same circle. One side says the data show two streams of experience co-habiting one skull; the other says they show at most a fragmentation of access, report, and control within a single stream; and each side's characterisation of the other is one its opponent would reject on sight. I want to try to end this loop for two specific positions, and I want the document that an honest adversarial collaboration between them would produce.

The two parties, in labels I have supplied but descriptions you should repair if they are unfair (if your first move is that both descriptions need fixing, say so explicitly and do the repair inside the document rather than in a preamble):

Party A, the Divisionist: after complete callosotomy, and in some partial cases, the organism contains two coexisting streams of consciousness, each with its own perceptual contents, its own access relations, and its own practical standpoint; the verbally reporting left-hemisphere system is one of these streams, not the organism's single spokesperson; the impression of a single person is largely an artefact of left-hemisphere dominance in reporting plus shared subpersonal machinery, shared brainstem and autonomic systems, and a shared body and environment.

Party B, the Unificationist: these patients remain a single subject of experience; what the data show is a subject whose informational architecture has been severely restricted, not a duplication of subjects; postulating a second subject is unmotivated given how much subcortical and bodily processing remains shared, is not supported by the only first-person evidence available, and inherits the difficulty of saying what a second subject would be a subject of.

Treat the following as the stipulated factual baseline for the exercise. I am not asking you to adjudicate the empirical record, and some items are deliberately stylised: do not cite studies, and do not argue about whether they are true. If one of them is doing hidden work in your argument, flag it in an appendix rather than quietly discarding it.

1. Stimuli presented to the left visual field can be selected by the left hand but cannot be named or even guessed above chance by the verbal system.
2. When the left hemisphere is asked why an action initiated by the right hemisphere occurred, it produces a confident but false explanation rather than reporting ignorance.
3. Conflicting instructions delivered simultaneously to the two hands sometimes yield two interfering courses of action, though one hemisphere usually dominates.
4. The hemispheres can sometimes convey information to each other by overt behaviour, one hand cueing the other.
5. After complete callosotomy in adulthood, patients report no change in the felt unity of their experience — but every such report is produced by the left-hemisphere verbal system.
6. Individuals born without a corpus callosum, and adults who lose a hemisphere in adulthood, show no evident division of consciousness.
7. No reliable method exists for eliciting verbal report from an isolated adult right hemisphere.

Assume both parties are physicalists about mind and brain, and both grant that these patients are conscious. Do not spend words on general metaphysics of consciousness; the whole interest here is in the local question of how many subjects, and of what the dispute consists.

Deliverable, in four parts, 1,400 to 2,000 words, formal register, no citations and no references to authors or papers — positions are to be identified by their formulations, not by who has held them.

Part 1: a numbered section of eight to twelve agreed claims. These should not be platitudes. Among other things I expect them to state what the phenomena above do and do not establish; to register the evidential asymmetry in item 5 and say how much weight the patient's own report can bear given it; and to separate at least four things that get run together as unity — phenomenal co-consciousness, access unity, agential or intentional unity, and what I will call indexical unity, the question whether there is one first-personal standpoint from which the experiences are had as opposed to merely a single body of mutually available content. State which of these each party takes to be the central notion, and whether they in fact differ about that.

Part 2: a disagreements table with these columns — the issue; the Divisionist's formulation, worded as a Divisionist would accept; the Unificationist's formulation, worded as a Unificationist would accept; a neutral restatement; and a verdict of genuine, verbal, or partly verbal, with a clause of justification where the verdict is not simply genuine.

Part 3: a cruxes section. Each crux should state the sub-question, give the case, argument, or imagined experiment both parties agree would bear on it, and record each side's prediction in advance in a single sentence, plus one sentence on what each side would count as its own view being refuted. Include at least these four:

Report Authority. Given that all verbal evidence passes through one hemisphere, is the debate in principle evidentially decidable, and does the divisionist's reliance on non-verbal indices of mentality beg the question while the unificationist's reliance on report begs it in the other direction?

The Slow Bridge. Suppose a surgically variant subject whose hemispheres are connected by a full-bandwidth channel with a long conduction delay. Where, if anywhere, does unity break as the delay grows from tens of milliseconds to seconds to tens of seconds? Both sides must say either where the threshold lies and what fixes it, or why there is no threshold, and must connect that answer to the notion of co-consciousness within a temporal window.

Countability. Is the number of subjects in such a case the sort of thing that has a determinate answer at all? If it can be indeterminate or vague, does that dissolve part of the dispute or merely relocate it? Ask each party whether it would accept indeterminacy as a genuine possible answer rather than a refusal to answer, and what criterion of determinacy it would offer.

Moral Stakes. If there were two subjects, would that carry consequences for consent, decision-making, or the number of loci of moral concern in one body? Does the difficulty of drawing those consequences count as a cost of divisionism, or is it merely a cost of our institutions? Both sides should say what they would accept as the practical upshot of being wrong.

Part 4: a short closing paragraph in the joint voice, we, stating what would actually move the collaboration, followed by an appendix marking which of the agreed claims are load-bearing for the residual disagreement and which are courtesies that could be dropped without cost.

Two failure modes to avoid. Do not settle anything by declaring the concept of a subject to be stipulative and the whole question therefore empty, unless you argue for that inside a crux rather than asserting it in passing. And do not let the document become two monologues with a handshake at the end: if a formulation in Part 2 is one the other side would refuse to sign, that is the interesting part, and I want it marked as such rather than smoothed over.
