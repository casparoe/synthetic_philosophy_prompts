I keep loosing the thread on the separateness of persons objection, so here's what I'd like: an intuitive explanation of where the real weight of that argument is supposed to sit.

The version I mean is the Rawls-style complaint against the Harsanyi-ish impartial observer argument for utilitarianism — you go behind a veil of ignorance with an equal chance of being anyone, you maximize expected utility, and out drops utilitarianism. The objection, as I understand it, is that this isn't a choice between separate people at all: it's structurally the same as one person making a prudential choice under risk about their own future, and so it helps itself to the very thing in dispute.

Two things I want pinned down:

(1) Why exactly does the analogy with prudence fail? Both cases have a single deliberator, uncertainty, and one utility function being maximized. So what is the feature of a single life over time that is supposed to license trading off the welfare of your earlier stages against your later ones, when the same kind of trade across persons is meant to be illegitimate? And does the answer commit you to something contentious about personal identity — if I imagine someone whose 70-year-old stage is barely more psychologically continuous with their 20-year-old stage than two strangers are, should the objection start applying inside that life?

(2) Am I right that there are really two complaints bolted together — one about aggregation, one about compensation (that a benefit to one person can't make up for a burden to another)? Is either one sufficient on its own, and can the whole objection be re-run in justificatory terms (every person is owed their own justification) so that it doesn't rest on any metaphysics at all?

Give me gut-level intuition rather than the formal argument, and don't just tell me utilitarianism ignores distinctions between persons in a vague way — I want to know which premise is doing the work.
