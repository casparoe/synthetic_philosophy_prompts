I'm a philosopher of science by training, and I've been asked to give a single sixty-minute guest lecture early next term in a methods seminar for psychiatric epidemiology and mental-health-services researchers: about fifteen people, mostly late-stage PhD students and postdocs, comfortable with survival analysis, measurement invariance and latent-variable models, with no philosophy of science beyond whatever they picked up from a critical-appraisal course. They will arrive assuming that a diagnostic category is a measuring instrument that either tracks something or doesn't, and they will suspect — half-rightly — that I've been invited to tell them their categories are socially constructed and therefore not real. I want them to leave able to use a distinction I've found useful in my own work and that I don't think has a settled name: roughly, that a category can loop back on the people classified (their self-understanding, the way they present, what they notice about themselves), it can loop back on the classification itself (criteria, thresholds, the boundary with the neighbouring category, the way a code gets used to secure services or school support), and it can loop sideways, where tightening one category simply relocates the same people into an adjacent one so that the aggregate prevalence barely moves. Call these, if you like, the presentation loop, the criterion loop, and the spillover loop. What I need from you is not an explanation of looping effects — I know that literature — but a plan for making those three distinguishable in one hour to this audience.

So: what has to be established before the word 'loop' is even introduced, and in what order, given that these students will read any feedback from classification to the classified as a threat to validity rather than as the phenomenon? Which single case or category should carry the whole session, and what does a good carrier need to have — I think it needs a prevalence series and a criteria revision visible in the same period, and an adjacent category that could plausibly have absorbed the overflow — and what must it lack: I'd rather avoid the examples everyone reaches for in this literature (the nineteenth-century fugue cases, the multiple-personality epidemic of the 1980s), because with this group they land as period curiosities and the discussion turns into a party trick about suggestible clinicians rather than about classification. Also: which go-to example would you actually pick instead, and what does it buy that the curiosity cases don't?

Then the misconception map, in the order I expect them to arise rather than as a list. My guesses are that at least one person will collapse all three loops into a demand characteristic or an expectancy effect and conclude that the numbers are artifact; that someone will infer that if the category changes the people, the disorder can't be biological; that the statistically minded will want to treat the loop as a nuisance parameter to be adjusted away rather than as part of what a prevalence estimate is of; and that the realism question will ambush the session somewhere in the middle and eat fifteen minutes. For each: where exactly in the sequence do I head it off, and with what move — do I defuse it before it can form, absorb it when it comes, or deliberately let it hang and use it as the bridge to the next step? Finally, give me one question to put to them in the last five minutes that would show the distinction has landed rather than been memorised — ideally something that makes them apply it to a category from their own current project — plus an explicit statement of what I should decline to adjudicate in this slot and say out loud that I'm declining: I'm inclined to refuse the natural-kind question and anything about the politics of the current revision processes, but tell me if I'm wrong about what to cut.
