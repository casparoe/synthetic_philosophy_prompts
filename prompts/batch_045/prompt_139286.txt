I maintain a small file of poems that I use to open each session of a graduate seminar on the philosophy of probability — one poem, read aloud, before the argument starts — and the session on the reference class problem has never had one. Please write it.

The form I want is a sestina: six stanzas of six lines and a three-line envoi, unrhymed, lines of roughly even length so that the rotation is audible. The six end-words should be six classes that a single person belongs to, and I would like them to be exactly these:

mortals, women, sisters, carriers, cyclists, this morning

The person is Vera, fifty-eight, at a kitchen table with a form in front of her. The question — asked once in each stanza, and never settled — is what number to put on her having a stroke within ten years. That number should change from stanza to stanza, keyed to whichever class the rotation has placed in the closing, emphatic position. No stanza should argue for this in prose; the drift of the number is supposed to be the argument.

What the poem needs to get right:

- The six classes must cross-cut rather than nest. Vera is a carrier and a cyclist; her sister had a stroke at sixty-two and Vera never smoked; one membership pushes the number up, another pushes it down. So no class here is simply the most specific one, and the poem should let a reader hear that the instruction to take the narrowest class is not even well defined. The usual refinement — narrowest class for which the statistics are still adequate — is a rule of thumb whose stopping point is fixed by how thin you will let the data get, not by anything in the world; and the other familiar refinement, the causally homogeneous class, quietly presupposes the causal knowledge the frequencies were supposed to deliver.
- Both ends of the ladder must be shown to fail. "Mortals" yields certainty: true, and useless to anyone holding a pen. And the narrowest description available — Vera herself, this morning, this one occasion — yields one, or zero, or nothing at all, depending on how you look. The poem should let the reader notice that the widest class and the narrowest collapse into the same unusable certainty, and that this is the shape of the problem rather than an accident of the example.
- The difficulty must not come across as ignorance that more data would cure. Every frequency can be in hand, every class counted exactly as it should be, and still none of them is the number for this event. The poem should make that plain without turning it into a slogan.
- The envoi should carry all six words, two to a line, and should be the place where the poem stops trying to choose: the six are the whole inventory, and rearranging them will not promote any one of them. It may close on the recognition that the form still has to be filled in this morning, but it must not resolve the tension into either skepticism or a shrug.

Two further constraints. No technical vocabulary on the page — no "reference class", no "frequentism", no "limiting relative frequency", and the word "probability" at most once or twice: the idea has to be carried by the arrangement of the six words and by what the numbers do. And it has to work as a poem for someone who has never heard of the problem: Vera's decision should be a real decision that something hangs on — an operation, a premium, a move to another country — and the tone should be dry rather than consoling. Someone who works on this material should find it accurate; someone who doesn't should find it worth hearing read aloud.
