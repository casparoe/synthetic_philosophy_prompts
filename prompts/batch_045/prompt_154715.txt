I am trying to work out what I actually think about whether the numbers count in rescue cases, and my verdicts on individual cases refuse to line up under any principle I can formulate. I would be grateful for help finding one.

Assume throughout the following stipulations: I am the only person who can help; I can do A or B but not both; there are no third-party effects, no knock-on consequences, and no facts about who deserves what; those involved are strangers to me unless stated otherwise; their prospects if rescued are equally good; and if I do nothing, everyone suffers the loss stated.

1. One stranger drowning in one place, five strangers drowning in another, equal distance, equal effort. I must save the five; failing to would be a serious fault on my part.
2. The same case, except that I toss a coin to decide. I also think this is permissible — not merely excusable — and that I could defend it afterwards to the family of the person who drowned. I cannot reconcile this with 1.
3. My own child, or five children who are strangers to me. I may save my child; no one may fault me for it; and I may equally save the five. Oddly, though, I find the thought of tossing a coin here grotesque in a way I do not find it in case 2, which makes me suspect that whatever licenses the coin in 2 is not really about equalising chances.
4. One person will lose the use of a hand; five people will each lose one finger. I save the hand. The five fingers, it seems to me, do not add up to a hand.
5. One person will lose the use of a hand; fifty people will each lose one finger. I now switch to the fifty. Nothing qualitative has changed, only a number — and I should add that between five fingers and six I feel almost nothing at all, which sits badly with the thought that each extra person makes some difference.
6. Five people will each lose a hand; one person will lose a hand. I save the five. So when the losses are the same size, numbers plainly do count.
7. I have a treatment that saves each recipient independently with probability 0.21, and does nothing otherwise. I can either save one stranger outright or administer it to five. I save the one, though five times 0.21 is more than one. Raise the probability to 0.9 and I reverse: I treat the five. I do not know where the crossover lies for me, and I doubt any smooth function of expected numbers captures the shape of these two verdicts.
8. I hold a post in which I will face the choice in case 1, with different strangers, once a week for a year, under a policy that everyone affected knows in advance. Here I think I must always save the five, and that a policy of randomising would be wrong — even though each week's decision has, as far as I can see, exactly the features of the one-off case in which I am prepared to defend randomising.

My own tentative principle is this: numbers count only as a tie-breaker — where each of the several would suffer a loss at least as great as the loss the one would suffer, save the greater number, and where the losses differ in size, size settles the matter and numbers drop out entirely. I can see that it handles 1, 4 and 6, that it has nothing whatever to say about 3, and that it makes 2 impermissible and 5 and 7 unintelligible.

Please test that principle against the cases and, if you can, repair it; if you think it is beyond repair, say what should replace it. Specifically, I would like:

(i) a principle, or at most two or three candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it unaided to a case you have not seen;
(ii) an indication, clause by clause, of which of my cases motivates it;
(iii) its verdicts on two cases I have not listed: one stranger's death against five strangers each losing a hand; and the case in 1, except that I can tell only that the further raft holds either two people or twenty, with equal probability, and will not learn which until I have committed myself;
(iv) an honest account of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, which one you would overturn, and what we lose by overturning it.

One further thing puzzles me and I would like it addressed if it bears on the answer: in case 4 the losses seem to add up when they fall on one person — five fingers gone from one hand is a worse thing to suffer than one finger gone — and not when they fall on five. If that asymmetry is an illusion, tell me why; if it is real, I would like to know what does the work, since bare appeal to the separateness of persons has never explained anything to me.

I am not asking for references or suggestions for further reading; I want the argument itself, and I would like it in British English.
