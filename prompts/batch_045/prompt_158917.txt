I am a PhD student writing a dissertation chapter on theories of creativity, and I want to test Hofstadter's knob-twiddling picture — creative work as exploration of a space of variations on an established theme, by adjusting parameters rather than by starting from nothing — against a body of practice that has had to decide, repeatedly and in public, how much variation counts as creative.

The practice is the non-obviousness requirement in US patent law. As I understand it (I am working from secondary summaries, so please flag anything you think I have got wrong rather than silently building on it): novelty is not enough — a variation nobody has ever produced can still be unpatentable. The question is whether the invention would have been obvious to a hypothetical person having ordinary skill in the art at the time it was made; the standard gloss asks whether the invention is an adequate distance beyond or above the state of the art. In KSR International Co. v. Teleflex Inc. (2007), Justice Kennedy wrote that a person of ordinary skill is also a person of ordinary creativity, not an automaton, and the Court listed as paradigms of obviousness: combining familiar elements by known methods to yield predictable results, simple substitution of one known element for another, and the obvious-to-try case, choosing from a finite number of identified, predictable solutions with a reasonable expectation of success. Against that, courts treat commercial success, long-felt but unsolved need, the failure of others, and evidence that the prior art taught away from the variation as evidence of non-obviousness, and they limit the prior art that counts to the same field of endeavor or art reasonably pertinent to the problem the inventor faced.

What puzzles me is that patents are routinely granted for new combinations of existing elements, so recombination is not disqualifying, while combining known elements is also the doctrine's stock example of the uncreative. So it cannot be assuming either that novelty suffices or that combination is worthless.

Please reconstruct the implicit theory. Specifically: what does the doctrine take to separate a novel variation from a creative one — a metric on a space of variations, a claim about the psychology of an idealized agent, or merely a proxy for something else? Which theories of creativity does the practice strictly presuppose, which does it merely fit well with, and which does it quietly rule out (a statistical-novelty view, a romantic or ineffable-leap view)? And does it presuppose a theory of creativity at all, or is the whole apparatus reconstructible as an economic device for withholding monopolies where no incentive is needed — in which case, which of its specific tests become unmotivated?

One further distinction I would like pressed, because I suspect the doctrine has no grip on it: Hofstadter's model allows not only movement within a parameter space but changes in the parameters themselves, a re-thematization that makes previously unreachable variations reachable. Does the doctrine's vocabulary — combination, substitution, new use, unexpected results, teaching away — distinguish re-parameterization from a long move within a fixed space, or is that precisely its blind spot? If it is a blind spot, what would have to be added to the tests to recover it?

For each part of your reconstruction, say how confident you are, mark where you are extrapolating from my description rather than from the doctrine you can actually vouch for, and name the one consideration that would most change your mind — in particular, what would convince you that there is no interesting theory of creativity in this practice at all.
