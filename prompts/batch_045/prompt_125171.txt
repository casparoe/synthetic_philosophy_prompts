im in a reading group thats grinding through the mid-century fight about whether history can have laws, and i keep losing arguments to a friend whos a quantitative historian and thinks cliometrics already hands us law-like regularities about the past. i want to practice defending the other side — that there can genuinely be laws of history — against someone who actually knows the anti-historicist case, so:

please play Karl Popper. Sharp, not a strawman. You're allowed to concede small points: you can grant that there are stable social regularities, that trends are real, and that some conditional social generalizations are testable and useful for piecemeal reform. But never concede the main one — that there are laws of history in the sense that would let someone forecast or retrodict the course of history as a whole, or the necessary succession of one social order by another. Stay in character until I say "stop", keep each reply to about a paragraph, and argue in your own words: no page numbers, no "in my essay on…", no bibliography, no quoting yourself; if you need a factual assumption from me, ask it as Popper would. And don't just fire off your most famous one-liner about prediction — I've read the summary, I want the machinery.

Here's my opening. Take my candidate law, the Ninety-Year Pendulum: whenever a regime is overthrown by a movement that publicly defines itself by abolishing a hereditary or sacral status hierarchy, the successor state re-creates a functionally equivalent hierarchy — same effective number of tiers, same insulation from market competition, new names and a new recruitment story — within 80 to 100 years of the rupture. I've checked it against the 23 clearest cases in a dataset I built: different centuries, different technologies, wildly different amounts of scientific knowledge, zero exceptions, and it held even in the handful of cases where people announced the law in advance and organized to prevent it. The mechanism is social, not mysterious: the new state needs a stable administration, and a population that was mobilized against the old titles will only accept a new elite if it doesn't look like the old one, so the elite has to be re-legitimated in a new idiom. Now, your headline move is that the growth of knowledge is unpredictable, so the future course of history can't be forecast. But this law is indifferent to what anyone knows, and it survived explicit attempts to defeat it by prevention. So: if a conditional generalization has a mechanism, no exceptions across huge variation in knowledge, and resists the self-defeating-prophecy trick, what exactly is still missing for it to count as a law of history? Is it just that you reserve the word "law" for something no social generalization could ever possibly be?
