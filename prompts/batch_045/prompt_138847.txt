i'm building a small advice thing and the part that's breaking is dumb: it keeps a running ledger of what there is reason for whom to do, and then it has to say things like "you have a reason to switch meds" or "there's a reason for your landlord to fix the boiler." the bookkeeping is killing me, because a lot of the entries don't seem to belong to anybody. i've started calling the ownerless ones orphan reasons (probably there's a real name for them, doesn't matter). before i commit to a schema i want to know what an account of reason-ownership actually has to deliver.

so: give me the desiderata. the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the constraints it can't violate — with the case or consideration behind each one — and mark which ones you think are non-negotiable and which are just the taste of one camp. please write in American English, and please don't point me at papers or author names or reading lists, i can't look anything up. just the moving parts.

the cases i keep tripping over, in no order:

1. a world with a lot of suffering in it and no agents at all. most people want to say there's still reason for that suffering not to happen. reason for whom?
2. a patient who has no concept of what an ACE inhibitor is, and a fact about their kidneys that makes the drug good for them. does the reason belong to them, or is it sitting there unowned while the doctor holds a different one?
3. an office — night watchman, referee, the person whose job is to unlock the gate. the reason seems to attach to the role. when the role is vacant, is the reason floating, and when someone is appointed do they acquire a new reason or inherit an orphan?
4. a rowing crew keeping time. is there one reason with eight owners, eight reasons, or a reason belonging to the crew that isn't any rower's?
5. someone who died last week. do they still have reasons? can they acquire new ones? do i keep their entries in the ledger or archive them?
6. a reason to have done something in 2010 that you now definately can't do. does having a reason require being able to act on it?
7. a bomb in a sealed room nobody can get into. there's clearly reason to defuse it. addressed to no one.
8. two people, one fact: your being about to be harmed is a reason for me to warn you and (maybe the same?) reason for you to step back. is that one entry or two, and does my entry derive from yours or from the fact?
9. an animal, a baby, a system that doesn't exist yet but might. what capacities does it take to be a reason-holder at all?

what i actually want back is a requirements list, not a tour of positions. more specifically: which of the things people demand here are genuine desiderata and which are one camp's prejudices — e.g. is "a reason has to be the kind of thing that could guide the person who has it" a requirement or a prejudice, and is "the ledger can't explode into infinitely many overlapping entries, and a finite agent has to be able to use it" a requirement or a prejudice? flag any pair of desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied, and be explicit about which case forces the choice — that's the part i most want. also say which single requirement does the most work in narrowing the field, and whether the theory owes me identity conditions for reasons (something that stops the same consideration being counted twice) and a story about how weights compare across different owners, since my thing has to add them up somehow.

a short scorecard at the end is fine — three or four broad families of view, like reasons as facts about the world, reasons as tied to what the agent's evidence supports, reasons as tied to the agent's own motives or capacities, reasons as tied to what a fully rational version of the agent would care about — but a few lines each, and skip it entirely if the criteria section is getting good. the criteria are the deliverable.
