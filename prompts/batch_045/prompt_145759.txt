I joined the grants committee of a small health foundation about six months ago, and I have to run a ninety-minute session for the other members before we vote on our next funding cycle. The reason it matters to me is embarrassingly local: last cycle we expanded a diabetes screening programme by cutting a small hospice transport service, I voted for it on the grounds that the QALY arithmetic was not close, and I have been uncomfortable ever since without being able to say what, exactly, my discomfort amounts to. What I want from you is not an explanation of the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism, but a plan for how I should get eight non-philosophers to feel the force of it well enough to argue about it, and then to vote.

The group is a retired actuary, two GPs, a hospital chaplain, a solicitor, a borough councillor, a former teacher and a twenty-two-year-old patient representative. They arrive believing that cost-effectiveness aggregation is simply what rational allocation is, and I have one structural wrinkle I want the session built around rather than bolted on: our foundation serves a small, stable catchment population on a rolling ten-year strategy, and the sub-groups who gain and lose in any given cycle rotate, so that over the decade roughly the same people are on the losing side of some decision and the winning side of another. That means the case I put to them is not the standard one-off choice between saving a few or saving many. I want you to tell me what the carrying example has to contain and, just as importantly, what it has to leave out — whether the gainers and losers should be identified individuals or statistical ones, whether the losses should be small and numerous or concentrated, whether death should appear in it at all, and what each of those choices does to the objection's grip.

I also want the dependency structure: what has to be in place before the complaint can even sound like a complaint rather than mere squeamishness about trade-offs. The misconceptions I expect are that this is just the trolley problem dressed up, that it is just deontology versus consequentialism by another name, and that it is really only a demand that we weight the worse off, which they will take to be a technical fix to the formula rather than a challenge to it. The one I most want headed off, though, is the thought that our rotating ten-year plan dissolves the objection entirely, on the grounds that if everyone takes a turn at losing then aggregating across persons looks no different from aggregating across the stages of one life; I would like that raised at the right point in the sequence rather than early, and I want to know whether thinking it through should make them more or less sympathetic to the objection. Tell me where in the order each misconception should be defused, give me one question I can put to them at the end that would distinguish someone who has actually grasped the point from someone who has only learned to repeat that you may not add people together, and say plainly what I should openly refuse to settle in ninety minutes.

Please answer in continuous prose, as you would in a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
