I'm a retired field ecologist — thirty-odd years of running 2x2 fertilizer trials on corn, nitrogen crossed with phosphorus, that kind of thing — and since I retired I've been reading my way into political philosophy on my own and it's driving me a little nuts. My constant reaction is that people in this literature are arguing past each other, and nobody ever says so plainly. So I want to borrow my old design habits and force the issue. One housekeeping thing first: please write the answer in American English. I keep getting "organisation" and "whilst" back from these things and it annoys me more than it should.

The topic is what rich countries owe poor ones. I've got three positions I keep tripping over, and I'll describe them doctrinally instead of by name because I dont care who said what:

- the threshold view: the duty is agent-neutral and forward-looking. What matters is whether people are below some sufficiency line and whether we can lift them at acceptable cost. Causal history and relationships are irrelevant.
- the no-harm view: the duty is backward-looking. It bites only where our own institutions or actions are causally implicated in producing the shortfall, and what's owed is repair, not beneficence.
- the shared-scheme view: duties of justice run among people who are co-participants in some institutional scheme we help impose or maintain. It's keyed to the relation, not to the causal history and not to the threshold.

Here's what I want you to build. A 2x2 factorial thought experiment — two binary factors, four cells, one shared setup.

Factor 1: whether the shortfall is traceable to a rule or institution the rich country operates, versus an identical shortfall with a purely natural cause.
Factor 2: whether the affected households, once the loss has happened, are still above the sufficiency line or have fallen below it.

Clamps, and these are the whole point:

- Same households, same country, same relationship to the rich country, same shared scheme (trade arrangement, aid channel, whatever) in all four cells.
- Same size of loss measured the same way, same cost to fix it, same efficacy of the fix. The only live question in every cell is the same yes/no: should the rich country spend that money on that transfer, or not?
- The loss is already done and the rule, where there was one, has since been clarified or repealed. I'm stipulating that so the no-harm view can't duck the question by saying "then just repeal the rule." I want all three views answering the identical question about the identical transfer. If you think that stipulation is itself loaded against one of them, say so.
- Every cell has to be written in flat, non-moral, non-legal causal language: "our rule made X unpayable," not "we wronged them" or "we owe reparations." If the verdict is arriving via the adjectives, the cell is worthless — that's what I'm calling cheating.
- Realistic. No imaginary world court, no magic debt cancellation, no trolleys. My suggestion for the mechanism, though use whatever you like: a documentation or compliance requirement from the rich country's financial regulator that leaves commercial banks unwilling to process payments for importers in the poor country, so a hospital or a farmers' co-op can no longer pay for an input it used to buy cheaply; and in the non-caused cells, a landslide or a storm that wipes out the same stock. Ordinary sums of money, a real decision-maker.
- One short paragraph for the shared setup, four sentences maximum per cell.

What I want back:

1. The setup and the four cells.
2. A 2x2 table with each view's verdict in each cell — yes, no, or indeterminate — plus a line of reasoning per view per cell showing which clause of the view generates the verdict. Derived, not stipulated.
3. State the result the way I'd state a trial result: which factor produces a main effect for which view, and whether there's an interaction.
4. What happens if you remove the feature doing the work — if everyone is above the line in all four cells, or if the causal history is identical in all four, does the design collapse? Which cells become empty?
5. The honest part: tell me which view comes out of your table looking worst, and write the objection its defender would actually make against your design, not a straw version. I expect the no-harm person to go after the counterfactual baseline — the "they'd be no better off but for us" move — but tell me how that objection really lands.
6. And my actual question: does this design show that the three views are rivals about one duty, or that each is tracking a different factor and the apparent disagreement is mostly people looking at different cells? Is there any cell where two views give the same verdict for genuinely different reasons, or does agreement always collapse into agreement about the reason too?

One more thing I suspect and want you to check: I think you can't vary factor 2 without dragging something else in. Given a fixed dollar loss, you have to make the households poorer to begin with, or make them lose a necessity instead of a surplus — and either way something besides the threshold moves. Tell me which way you went and whether your version of factor 2 is confounded.

Audience, since it matters for the wording: this is for me and for a reading group of six retirees, one of whom is an ex-economist. Define any term you use, no jargon I'd have to look up, and no bibliography — I'm after the structure of the thing, not who defended it.
