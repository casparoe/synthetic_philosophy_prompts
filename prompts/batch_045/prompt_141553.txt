I'm auditing a formal epistemology seminar this term — I'm a mathematics graduate student, so I'm comfortable with the possible-worlds apparatus as a piece of mathematics, but much less sure what philosophers take it to be *saying* — and I have to write a short response paper on the problem of logical omniscience. I found what looked like a reliable popular treatment in a book chapter on rationality that a friend lent me, and I've been leaning on it, but the more seminar we do the less sure I am that the chapter and the seminar are describing the same problem. The chapter says, more or less:

"Epistemic logic models what an agent knows by the set of worlds compatible with her evidence, and to know a proposition is for it to be true throughout that set. It follows immediately that knowledge is closed under logical consequence: if P holds throughout the compatible worlds and P entails Q, then Q holds throughout them too, so anyone who knows P also knows Q. That is already absurd. It says that a student who has learned the axioms of arithmetic thereby knows every theorem, including results nobody has ever proved, and in the limit it says that all of us already know every tautology, including ones so long that verifying them would outlast the universe. So the possible-worlds framework grotesquely overattributes knowledge, and the usual verdict is that it is simply the wrong formalism for epistemology: an idealization of the wrong kind, not like a frictionless plane but like a theory of gases that assumes the molecules are immortal. The standard repair is to admit impossible worlds alongside the possible ones, worlds where contradictions and other impossibilities hold; once some of the worlds the agent cannot rule out are impossible ones where P is true and Q is not, the closure result fails in exactly the way we wanted and the problem dissolves. Bayesian epistemology, which replaces outright belief with degrees of confidence, has exactly the same defect and is repaired in exactly the same way."

My own suspicion is that the impossible-worlds paragraph is too triumphant, since the difficulty never seemed to me to be fundamentally about which worlds there are, but I can't articulate why, and I may be wrong about the rest as well.
