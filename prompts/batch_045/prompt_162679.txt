hi — I'm a land use / transactional real estate lawyer, about twelve years in, mostly commercial leases, HOA and condo docs, easement and access disputes, some regulatory takings work. I read philosophy for fun but badly. I've been reading the open borders stuff on my own and I keep having the feeling that everyone in it is arguing about a right that I draft around for a living, and that the lawyers version of that right is both narrower and weirder than theirs.

So: translate the anti-restrictionist side of the open borders debate — the whole thing, strand by strand — into the vocabulary I actually use. The strands as I understand them are: (1) the freedom-of-association point, that a citizen has some claim to hire, rent to, sell to, or marry whoever they want including a foreign national, and the state stops them by force; (2) the coercion point, that immigration enforcement is not just "we decline to admit" but force applied both to the would-be migrant and to the citizen who would transact with them; (3) the territorial-rights point, where even granting that a political community collectively holds the territory, it doesn't follow that it may exclude peaceful entrants, since collective owners still can't do just anything with jointly held stuff and states don't obviously hold whatever authority they claim; (4) the club or private-association reply, that a polity is entitled to choose its members the way a club is; (5) the culture / self-determination argument on the restrictionist side.

My vocabulary, so you know what's available: the bundle of sticks and the standard list of them, Hohfeldian relations (claim-right, privilege, power, immunity), easements and servitudes, covenants running with the land, dedication and public rights of access, nuisance, adverse possession and limitation periods, restraints on alienation, licenses versus property interests, common-interest communities and association rulemaking, standing, police power versus eminent domain, regulatory takings (the physical-occupation line and the balancing test), and dominium versus imperium.

Two bits of apparatus I want you to use, because they're my own attempt at being precise and I want to know whether they hold up:

First, I think "the right to exclude" is not one stick but four things that come apart in practice: the right to set terms of entry, the right to refuse a particular person for a stated reason, the right to refuse for no reason at all, and the power to get the state's enforcement apparatus deployed against someone who enters anyway. It's that last one — the delegated-enforcement power — that seems to be doing all the work in the open borders argument, and I want to know whether the philosophers are treating it as part of the right or as a separate thing the state supplies for free.

Second, my world already contains a doctrine where a property right gets qualified because it has become, functionally, a gate over something people need: duties to serve, public accommodations, the business affected with a public interest. Nobody thinks that's a taking. So I keep wanting to ask whether a citizenship regime is more like a gatekeeper's franchise than like a guy's fence.

So: restate each strand, one at a time, as faithfully as you can, and then give me the residue report — what translated cleanly, what you had to fudge to make it fit, what has no expression in my vocabulary at all. And tell me what to make of the residue. If the coercion point can't even be stated in property vocabulary, is that because property law already presupposes a sovereign with a monopoly on legitimate force and so can't see the question from inside, or is it because the coercion point was never as clean as it sounds? Which parts of the philosophical disagreement look substantive to you after the translation, and which look like they were never about exclusion at all? Also flag places where the legal framing is actively misleading — where my instincts as a lawyer would make me a worse judge of the argument than a layperson. No case names or citations needed, I just want the reasoning.
