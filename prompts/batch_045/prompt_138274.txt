My supervisor and I are co-authoring a paper on when a research team is entitled to extrapolate from the specimens it has examined to the ones it has not. Our running example is a single mineral collection: some four thousand emeralds examined so far, plus a sealed remainder still in a vault. The question in every case is the same — which summaries of the examined specimens the evidence actually supports, as opposed to which merely fit the data. My supervisor has already ruled, in conversation, on eight predicates over this collection. I give her one-line reasons where she gave any. She is away for a month and I have to draft the section that turns on a ninth case, so I need to know where she is going to land.

Her rulings:

1. "green." Every examined specimen is green. Projectible: the evidence supports expecting the remainder to be green.
2. "verdant": green if first examined before 2050, blue if first examined in 2050 or later. Same evidence exactly. Not projectible. Her reason: that one is built out of a date.
3. "vendert": the complement of the previous one — blue if first examined before 2050, green if later. Also not projectible.
4. "primin": green if the specimen's serial number is prime, blue otherwise. Not projectible. Her reason: serial numbers are not the sort of thing that could make a stone green.
5. "therm": green while held below 300 °C, blue at or above it. Coined last year, and confirmed by heating thirty specimens. Projectible, and now supported. Her reason: here the condition does real work.
6. "jade": jadeite or nephrite. Projectible. Her reason: each half is respectable on its own.
7. "verdant-1890": as in (2), with the cutoff moved back to 1890. She says this one is not unprojectible but simply refuted — the examined specimens are green, not blue, so the generalization is false. I take her to be committed to treating (1) and (2) as genuine rivals over the same evidence.
8. "logged": green if the specimen has passed through the cataloguing office, blue if it has not. Not projectible. Her reason: that makes the stone's colour depend on whether we have got round to it.

The ninth case. A mineralogical school in another country has used, for two centuries, a pair of basic colour words — call them *dal* and *vek* — which the field linguists who work with them render as "green if first examined before 2050, blue if first examined in 2050 or later" and its complement. In their language these are unanalysed, ostensively taught colour terms; nothing in their vocabulary corresponds to plain green, and no Ustan sentence mentions a date. Every emerald in their collection has been examined and is classified *dal*. Their standard text asserts that all emeralds are *dal*. They are about to open a vault sealed in 2049 whose emeralds will be seen for the first time in 2051, and on that basis they expect to find them blue. One of my other committee members says the example is empty, because a predicate nobody analyses into parts cannot be accused of being gerrymandered; another says it is the sharpest version of the problem we have. I cannot tell which of them is closer to the truth.

What I need from you:

- Predict her verdict. Do the Ustans' examinations support the prediction that the vault emeralds are blue or not? Is the school making a mistake, or is it rather that *dal* is projectible for them in the way *green* is projectible for us, or is there nothing for her to have a verdict about?
- Reconstruct the rule she is implicitly running, and set out the other rules that fit all eight of her earlier verdicts just as well. I can think of at least three candidates and cannot choose between them; if there are more, say so.
- Say what each candidate predicts about the Ustans, and where exactly they come apart.
- Give me your own best guess, with the reasons that actually move you, and say how confident you are.
- Name one further case — it has to be something I could put to her in an email, and something she has not already ruled on — that would best tell the leading candidates apart, and say what you would expect it to reveal.
- Finally, tell me how to write the section: do I present the Ustans as mistaken, as a case in which projectibility is relative to a practice, or as a case where the question does not arise? Take a definite line and defend it rather than laying the options side by side, and tell me which part of your answer you would abandon first if she comes back and disagrees with it.
