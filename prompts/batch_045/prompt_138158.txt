I am an undergraduate in a philosophy of mind seminar, and we recently discussed Daniel Dennett's paper "Quining Qualia" together with the Chase and Sanborn coffee-taster case. A survey article I read for the course—an introductory chapter in an edited volume on consciousness, not something my professor assigned—summarizes Dennett's argument and the case like this: "Dennett's target is the philosophical notion of qualia: the intrinsic, private, ineffable properties that determine what it is like to have an experience and that are supposed to be directly and infallibly accessible to introspection. His central intuition pump is the case of Chase and Sanborn, two longtime tasters for a coffee company who have always agreed about the taste of the blend. At a certain point the company changes something, and afterward both tasters report a shift. Chase says that the coffee itself now tastes different to him, but he likes the new taste just as much; his qualia have changed while his overall preference has not. Sanborn says that the coffee tastes exactly the same to him, but he has simply gone off that taste; his qualia are unchanged while his preference has changed. Dennett points out that neither taster can appeal to any independent check to settle whether his qualia or merely his reactions have altered, because the only alleged access to qualia is memory, and memory of a quale is exactly the sort of thing that could itself be distorted by a change in the quale or in one's standards of comparison. So there is no way, even in principle, to tell which man is right. From this Dennett concludes that there is no fact of the matter about what another person's—or even one's own past—experiences are like, since any such fact would have to be constituted by qualia, and qualia turn out to be undetectable and idle posits. Therefore the philosophical notion of qualia is not just unknowable but incoherent: it cannot play any role in a scientific or commonsense theory of mind, and in the end Dennett's view is that, strictly speaking, there is no such thing as the way things seem to us at all—there are only our judgments, reactions, and self-interpretations."

Something about this seems unfair, but I do not know enough to say where. In seminar we distinguished the philosopher's technical term "qualia," which is supposed to pick out intrinsic, private, ineffable, and directly or immediately apprehensible properties, from the ordinary everyday phenomenon of there being something it is like to taste coffee or see red. My professor said Dennett is a realist about the latter and only means to eliminate the former, and that his method treats first-person reports as data about how things seem without treating those reports as incorrigible. The summary, by contrast, makes it sound as though Dennett denies that there is any way things seem to us and reduces experience to judgments and reactions. I also noticed that the summary says qualia are supposed to be "infallibly" accessible to introspection; my lecture notes listed direct apprehension but not infallibility, and I am not sure whether that adjective is a harmless gloss or whether it stacks the deck against the view.

Could you give me an accuracy audit suitable for someone taking the course? In particular, I would like to know whether the step from Chase and Sanborn's inability to tell whether their qualia or their preferences changed to the conclusion that there is no fact of the matter about what their experiences are like is one Dennett actually makes, or whether the summary is attributing to him a stronger verificationist premise than the text requires. I am also unsure whether the summary's claim that, for Dennett, there is strictly speaking no way things seem to us—only judgments, reactions, and self-interpretations—follows from anything true in the accurate parts of the summary, or whether it is a non sequitur built on the conflation between the philosopher's qualia and the ordinary "what it is like" that my professor warned us about.
