I spent a little over thirty years in stellar astrophysics, and since retiring I have been sitting in on a metaphysics reading group in the philosophy department. The group's last meeting of the term is on persistence through time, and I have been asked to close it with something short. I would like that something to be a sestina, and I would like it to be about endurantism and perdurantism in substance and not only in name.

As I am the only person in the room without formal training in the subject, let me say what I take the poem's work to be, and what I should like the form to contribute.

The subject is to be a single star, followed from the protostellar clump out of which it condenses, through its long stretch on the main sequence, through the giant branch, to the white dwarf it finally becomes. It must undergo real intrinsic change as it goes — compact and hot, then distended and cool; fusing hydrogen, then helium, then nothing — so that the familiar problem about temporary intrinsics is not gestured at but actually staged: one and the same thing, wholly present at each moment, is F at one time and not-F at another; or else the properties are distributed across temporal parts and the change becomes variation along a four-dimensional career. I would be grateful if the poem avoided the two caricatures that would make my colleagues wince: the perdurantist's object is not a heap of disconnected snapshots, and the endurantist's object is not something frozen and changeless.

The six rotating end-words should be WHOLE, SLICE, NOW, THEN, SAME, and BURN, in the standard sestina rotation, unrhymed, in more or less regular iambic pentameter; the return of the end-words is meant to carry what rhyme would carry in another form. I suggest opening with them in that order, but a better rotation is welcome. It seems to me that the form has something to say on its own account: the same six words coming back in different stations is rather like the persisting thing itself, and I would like the envoi — the three lines in which all six come back together — to be the point at which that analogy is either cashed or broken. The word BURN matters most to me: fusion, pulsation, and rotation are things that take time, and I want the poem to feel the pressure of that fact on a view whose parts are instantaneous.

Then there is a patch I have been defending in the group, which I would like the poem to take up rather than talk around. My suggestion is that a persisting thing's temporal parts be taken as extended rather than instantaneous, and as overlapping, so that the part running from an earlier time to a later one shares a stretch with the part running from an intermediate time to a later one still. This lets the four-dimensionalist say that burning, or rotating, is something a temporal part does outright rather than only relative to a time, since each part has some duration in which to do it. I would like the poem to make this move, or to answer it, but not to ignore it — and if it makes it, to be honest about the worry I cannot shake: if the parts overlap, then parthood among them is not the tidy mereology the view was supposed to inherit, and whatever makes an extended part burn can apparently be asked again of the shorter parts inside it, and again.

Somewhere in the poem, too, I would like the temporal edges of the career raised as a difficulty rather than settled: does it include the clump that has not yet begun to fuse, or the cinder that has stopped?

I do not mind whether the poem comes down on one side, on the other, or visibly declines to choose while showing why the choice is hard; but it must do one of those three things rather than hover. It should be accurate enough that someone who works on persistence can tell which lines belong to which account and would not wince, and it should still be a poem about a star for the colleague who wanders over from the astronomy department knowing none of this. Please write the poem, and any note that follows it, in American English. You may append at most two sentences of prose afterward saying which end-word did which work — no more, since I would rather the poem stood on its own when I read it aloud. My hope is that the philosophers will still be arguing about it on the way to the car park.
