So I started CBT a few months back — panic attacks, mostly about trains — and the whole therapy basically runs on the idea that what I believe causes what I feel and what I do. I notice the thought "I'm going to faint in the tunnel", I argue with it, and I get on the train anyway. My flatmate's a neurology resident and every time I describe this he says the belief isn't doing any causal work: whatever I do is already fully fixed by what's going on in my nervous system, and the belief-talk is just a handy short way of keeping score. We've been going in circles for weeks now, he mentioned something he called the exclusion argument, I looked it up, and I'm in way deeper than I meant to get.

We've agreed to stop shouting and write something we could both actually sign, so: please draft the joint statement our adversarial collaboration should produce. Side one (me): the fact that I believed the tunnel was safe is genuinely part of why I got on the train. Side two (him): the neural story is causally complete, and the mental description isn't adding any work the neural one isn't already doing.

What I want in it:

1. A numbered section of claims we can both sign — the stuff that isn't really in dispute (no extra energy being injected into the brain by beliefs, belief-talk being useful for prediction, and whatever else you think genuinely survives). Please don't make these vacuous; I want claims with some bite.
2. A disagreements table: our positions stated exactly, each phrased the way its own side would accept, not a straw version.
3. The cruxes — the sub-questions where you'd actually expect one of us to move. I suspect at least two: whether a cause is supposed to be proportional to its effect (that the truthful answer to "why did he get on the train" is neither the whole microphysical description nor something as coarse as "because he wanted to"), and whether the mental and neural descriptions are two causes or one cause described twice. If you think part of our arguement has been purely about the word "cause", say which part and why.
4. For each crux, one specific case or argument we'd both accept as bearing on it, with each of us predicting in advance what we expect to happen. Please invent at least one concrete test scenario rather than just describing the crux abstractly — I like the one where you take two people who are identical in every respect my flatmate cares about but whose beliefs came to have different contents, and ask whether anything about them seperates downstream. That kind of thing.
5. Finally, the practical upshot, since this started with me on a platform: if side two is right, is CBT just a roundabout way of changing a brain (fine, keep doing it) or is it a decorative epiphenomenon (not fine)? Write down what each of us predicts about a patient who acquires the belief while the measurable neural story stays put, and whether any of this is supposed to change how we hold people responsible.

Plain language where you can, no citations or reading lists, and keep both of us sounding like people who actually believe what they believe.
