I write a column on the ideas behind policy fights, and I am working on a long piece about the fight over who has a right to the city. Before I file it, I want the document two of my sources should have produced if they had run an adversarial collaboration instead of a two-year public argument. The first is an abundance liberal: the binding constraint on housing is legal permission, supply responds when permission is granted, and new market-rate units take pressure off older ones. The second is a decommodification socialist: housing is a claim against the market rather than a commodity, land rent captures whatever new supply produces, and the right to the city belongs to the people already living there rather than to whoever can pay.

Draft their joint statement. Start with a numbered section of claims both could sign — about what has actually been observed where legalization happened and about how long it takes any supply effect to show up; about what filtering and displacement would each have to mean in order to be measured at all; about whether a right to the city is a right to arrive, a right to stay put, or a right to have a say, and whether those can come apart. Then give a table of the residual disagreements, each row stated twice: once in wording the abundance side would accept for its own view, once in wording the decommodification side would accept for its own. Mark which rows you judge to be about words rather than about the world, and say briefly why.

Then give the cruxes, and for each a test or case both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. For at least one crux, use this probe, which I have invented and neither of them has encountered: a statute doing two things at once. It makes six-unit buildings as-of-right on every residential lot, and it grants sitting tenants — together with a municipal land trust funded by a phased-in tax on land value alone — a right of first refusal at appraised price on any sale. Call it the double-key statute. I want each camp's prediction about net new units at year five, about rents for sitting tenants, about who moves into the new units, and about whether that camp would actually take the deal. Finally, flag any place where the dispute reduces to a single empirical parameter rather than a principle, and name the parameter.
