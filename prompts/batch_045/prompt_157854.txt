I convene a 90-minute session for 18 first-year undergraduates on a course titled Genes, Minds and Society. Most are humanities and social-science students; a handful have done some biology; almost none has ever decomposed a variance. The room has six tables of three, a whiteboard, a long clear stretch of wall, and a laptop for me. Students get no devices. Everything has to fit in a bag I can carry: index cards, small envelopes, dice, a metre rule or printed rulers, sticky notes, markers, and a printed scoring sheet. No money changes hands; stakes can be a leaderboard on the board and a trivial prize such as a bag of apples.

The topic is heritability. I do not want a lecture or a definition. I want an activity in which students generate data themselves with physical materials, compute something from it, make and lose points on predictions, and only in the last twenty minutes discover what the number they computed was, what it licenses, and what it does not.

Please design the activity to meet the following specifications.

Structure. Build it as four short rounds, each of which should produce a specific, nameable confusion in the participants rather than in the facilitator's mouth. Roughly: (1) a round in which the trait is, within each of two separated sub-populations, entirely a function of a value sealed in an envelope, so that the within-group share attributable to the envelopes is very high, while the difference between the two group means is produced entirely by one uniform environmental difference; participants should be led by the setup to predict that the between-group gap is largely inherited, and then be shown the envelopes. (2) A round in which the envelopes are untouched but the range of environments is widened or narrowed, and the share attributable to the envelopes changes accordingly. (3) A round in which a participant is handed a single organism and asked, on the basis of the group-level number, how much of this individual's measured value is due to its envelope, and finds that the question has no answer. (4) A round in which a uniform environmental change shifts the whole distribution even though the envelope share remains high, so that participants who have concluded the trait is fixed by inheritance lose points by predicting that the change will do nothing. Each round needs rules, the actual numbers or card distributions the facilitator must prepare, and what participants record.

Two further ingredients I want worked in, because they are where the interesting difficulty lives. First, include a crossing interaction: arrange the numbers so that one envelope value outperforms another under one set of conditions and loses to it under another, and have a team that must select stock using only measured outcomes do so and then fail when conditions change; there should be no environment-independent ranking to be had, and the activity should make that felt as a loss of points rather than as a claim. Second, include one round in which the environment an organism experiences is partly produced by the organisms themselves under a planting arrangement the facilitator controls — for instance denser planting allowing taller individuals to suppress their neighbours — so that the share attributable to the envelopes rises because of a modifiable arrangement, and the same genotypes under a different spacing yield a much smaller share. The point that should surface is that a high envelope share can be evidence that an environmental rearrangement matters a great deal, which is the opposite of the conclusion participants will have been drifting toward.

Roles and incentives. Give participants something to do with real, if small, stakes: at least one role in which a team wagers points on a claim about the causes of a group difference, and at least one in which a team must spend a fixed, scarce budget of some input under a forced choice between giving everyone the same amount and concentrating it on those at the bottom. Say exactly how points are scored, what is recorded on the wall, and how the reveal works.

Constraints on facilitation. No exposition up front beyond a sentence of framing, and the technical term should not be used until the debrief; beforehand have participants call it something like the sealed-card share or the family component. Use a fictional organism or crop rather than any human trait, and keep transfer to human traits to a clearly bounded final segment.

Debrief. Map specific moments in the activity onto specific features of the concept: why a high within-group share is compatible with a between-group difference that is entirely environmental, what kind of quantity the share is such that it can change when nothing about the organisms changes, and why it does not attach to individuals. Specify what participants should come away able to say. Then be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply in closing: in real behavioural genetics nobody gets to randomize and open the envelopes, so the quantity is estimated rather than measured, from designs that carry assumptions about equal environments, assortative mating, and correlation between genotype and environment; and the activity's clean orthogonality is a luxury that makes the inference participants want to make hard rather than easy.

Finally, tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: the arithmetic stalling the room, the student who has read about the topic and names it in round one, the student who mistakes the envelope for a molecular entity, the group that refuses to wager, and what to do in each case. Include a short facilitator line for handling it if a participant raises human group differences mid-session.
