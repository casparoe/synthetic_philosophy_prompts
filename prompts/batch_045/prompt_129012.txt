I should begin by placing myself, since it will affect what I find satisfactory. I spent a working life in experimental chemical physics, and most of it designing measurements whose entire inferential structure rested on randomization, controls, blinding where we could manage it, and the working assumption that our samples were exchangeable. I have only in retirement begun to read philosophy of physics with any seriousness. So I bring some habits from the bench and a certain impatience with claims that sound deep but pin nothing down.

The occasion is this. In the reading group I attend there are two members who have been arguing for well over a year about what Bell's theorem actually establishes, and who have at last agreed, to everyone's relief, to conduct the dispute as an adversarial collaboration rather than as a sequence of monologues. I have been asked to help draft the document they would sign, and I would be grateful for your help with it.

The two positions, as I would put them myself:

Camp A holds that the theorem, together with settings-independence (the assumption that whatever hidden variables govern the pair are statistically independent of which measurements are chosen — also called the free-variable or no-conspiracy assumption) and the assumption that each run has a single definite outcome, entails that no theory reproducing the observed correlations satisfies Bell's notion of local causality. Since the experiments are in, and since settings-independence is on this view not a substantive physical hypothesis but a precondition on treating anything as an experiment at all, the conclusion is that the world is nonlocal in the specific sense that what happens on one wing depends on the setting chosen on the other. The remaining work is to make that dependence precise and to say how it can coexist with no-signalling and with relativity.

Camp B holds that the theorem is sound but its conclusion is a disjunction, and that the disjunct to surrender is settings-independence: there is, on this view, no positive argument for independence beyond our inability to imagine a mechanism; a common-cause correlation between how pairs are prepared and which measurements get made reproduces the correlations locally; and the cost of the move is not the one Camp A alleges. Camp B has internal variants I want distinguished — a superdeterminist one in which the correlation is laid down in the initial condition or by a global constraint, a retrocausal one in which later settings figure among the causes of earlier outcomes, and any others you think are genuinely distinct rather than relabelled.

What I want from you is the document, and I want it in three parts.

First, a numbered section of claims both could sign. Not platitudes: claims with content, neutrally worded, about what the formalism licenses, what the experiments have established and what they have not, what the no-go results rule out, and what stands in need of explanation. Include in this section a joint statement about the third escape route — giving up the single-outcome assumption, as an Everettian would — which neither of my colleagues takes, with one line each on why. I also want the document to address something that struck me immediately when I read the theorem stated in causal terms: in the directed-graph formulation, settings-independence just is the exogeneity condition, the requirement that there be no latent common cause of the setting variables and the latent variable — the same condition that makes an observational study identifiable, and the same condition I spent my career trying to secure by randomization. I would like the agreed section to say whether the theorem is best read as a claim inside that framework, and, if it is, whether the residual dispute is a dispute about physics or about the status of an identifiability assumption.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row one disagreement, stated twice: once in wording Camp A would accept as a fair statement of its own position, once in wording Camp B would accept for its own. Where you think a row is merely verbal, say so and say why; where you think it is not, say what observable or theoretical difference would show that.

Third, a cruxes table: the sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one side, with, for each, the argument, case, or measurement both would agree bears on it, and each camp's prediction written down in advance.

For the empirical crux I want you to use a protocol I have been turning over and which I do not believe has been run; if you have reason to think it has, say so plainly rather than using it. Take a Bell test that has been operating for a long time with its settings supplied by a physical randomizer of one construction — electronic noise, say — and that has produced a stable violation of the relevant inequality. Hold the source, geometry, and analysis fixed, and substitute a setting generator of an entirely different physical construction: radioactive decay, then a chaotic circuit, then live human key presses, then the original apparatus again. Record the estimated violation parameter across the swaps and, importantly, the joint statistics of the setting choices and whatever source-side variables the apparatus logs anyway — emission times, intensities, the sort of thing that normally goes into a diagnostic file and is then discarded. Now: what does each camp predict? Is there a version of Camp B on which the swap changes the result, because the correlation is a local mechanical coupling between generator and source rather than a global constraint, and is there a version on which the protocol is provably inert? If the latter, mark it as inert rather than pretending the protocol discriminates. And on what grounds does Camp A predict exact invariance — a prediction, or a theorem?

Close with, for each camp, a short clause stating what that camp would count as evidence against its own view, and with your own honest verdict on whether there is anything both would accept as settling the matter, or whether the document should end by recording that the dispute has become a choice between two theoretical bills — locality, or the preconditions of experimental inference — and that nothing on the current menu forces the choice.

Two constraints. State any technical result you rely on in your own words, and mark any gap the document would have to leave rather than papering over it. And I have no use for citations or suggestions for further reading; I can find those myself, and the value here is in the argument.

Format: a numbered agreed-claims section, then the disagreements table, then the cruxes table with predictions.
