I spent last evening arguing with a friend about whether generational cohorts — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z — are real kinds or merely periodizing conveniences that journalism has inflated into something that looks like a kind. The argument went nowhere, and I think I now see why: we were each applying a different unstated test. She kept saying that cohort effects show up in the data and that this settles it; I kept saying that a category whose boundaries are redrawn whenever a polling organization revises them cannot be tracking anything. Neither of us ever said what a cohort would have to be for the dispute to have an answer.

So before I talk to her again I want the specification, not the verdict: the list of requirements a theory of cohort kinds would have to meet. Fix the purpose as follows — the theory is adequate only if it is good enough to settle two decisions: (i) whether a social scientist is licensed to put a cohort variable into a causal model and interpret it as naming a mechanism rather than as a proxy for age and period; (ii) whether the rest of us are licensed to explain an individual's attitudes or behaviour by appeal to their generation in ordinary conversation.

State each requirement as a test, and give the case that earns it a place on the list. The cases I keep returning to, which you should use and add to where I have missed something important:

the birth-year band that is a Millennial on one organization's definition and Gen X on another's, so that the same person changes kind without changing any property;

the fact that cohort effects are not separately identifiable from age and period effects without extra assumptions, since cohort is fixed once age and period are — so a theory must say whether a kind can be real if its distinctive causal contribution cannot be separately estimated;

the Anglophone labels being exported to populations whose formative events — a war, a currency collapse, a draft, a one-child policy — sort birth years into entirely different groupings;

a label attached to people while they are still children, before the experiences said to constitute the cohort have occurred;

the feedback case: coverage of a generation's supposed traits changing how its members act and self-describe, which seems to make the kind causally efficacious and also seems to make it an artifact of the discourse about it;

the contrast between the cohort sense of generation and the lineage sense, where generational position is a relation within a family rather than a property of a birth band;

the grain question — why roughly decade-scale bands rather than three-year or thirty-year ones, and what it would take for a finer or coarser grain to be the right one.

For each requirement I also want whether it is non-negotiable, merely costly to drop, or a preference of one camp, and which other requirement it sits badly with. Where some subset cannot be jointly satisfied, say so explicitly and say which I should give up: that is the part I most want.

Deliver the main list as a table with exactly these columns:

number | desideratum — the test, in one sentence | motivating case — the concrete case that makes it a requirement | what it rules out — the position it kills or forces to qualify | status — non-negotiable / costly to drop / camp preference | conflicts with — row numbers it is in tension with, or none

After the table, four short prose items, in this order and under these headings: (a) Unsatisfiable subsets — which sets of requirements cannot all hold, and which one you would drop and why; (b) The load-bearing requirement — the single requirement that excludes the most candidate theories; (c) What we were each assuming — which requirement my friend's appeal to cohort effects in the data implicitly presupposed, and which one my appeal to shifting boundaries implicitly presupposed, and whether either of us was demanding something illegitimate; (d) Verdict, in one sentence only — whether generations are real kinds, on your own list, with the requirement that did most of the work in getting there.
