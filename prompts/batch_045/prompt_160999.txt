I am writing a short essay on how Ayn Rand's case against altruism would fare against the forms that organised benevolence has taken since her death in 1982, and I need an extrapolation that stays inside her framework rather than a verdict on it from outside. Please answer in British English, and assume that you have no access to sources: extrapolate from her commitments, including the ones I summarise below, and say plainly where you are unsure of a fact rather than filling the gap.

Four arrangements to consider.

(1) Earning to give. A young mathematician takes a well-paid job in quantitative finance or in a large AI company — a job she regards as, at best, neutral in itself — and gives away half her income to fund antimalarial nets and micronutrient programmes for people she will never meet. She treats the job as a means and the transfer as the point of her working life.

(2) Moral trade. Alice's ultimate concern is the welfare of farmed animals; Bob's is childhood mortality in poor countries. Each can produce more of the other's good per pound than of her own, because each has cheap access to distribution channels in the other's area. They therefore agree that each will spend her money advancing the other's priority. Neither cares about the other's cause. Each cares about her own, and each ends up with more of what she actually values than if she had spent on it directly.

(3) Longtermism. Donors fund work intended to reduce the chance that civilisation collapses or that humanity is displaced by artificial systems within the next few centuries, thereby benefiting people who do not yet exist and whose existence the spending is partly meant to secure. Allocation runs on expected value, which treats unidentified future people and identifiable present ones as interchangeable units of benefit.

(4) Value-blind giving, a case I have not seen discussed. A donor commits a fixed share of her income to a fund whose allocation is set by an expert panel or by a cost-effectiveness calculation she never inspects. She has no view on what the money buys and regards forming one as a poor use of her time. Nothing here looks like sacrifice in the ordinary sense and nothing is exchanged for anything.

For each, give me her verdict, derived from her actual commitments rather than from the caricatures of her: her definition of altruism as the doctrine that self-sacrifice is a virtue and service to others the moral ideal; her definition of value as that which one acts to gain and keep, and of sacrifice as the surrender of a greater value for the sake of a lesser one or of a non-value; the trader principle, on which human beings deal with one another by giving value for value and no value is obtained by force, fraud or unearned gift; her treatment of emergencies, on which help to a stranger in extremis is permissible and even appropriate but cannot be made the model of a moral rule; her rejection of duty and obligation as the form of a rational morality; her claim that the standard of value is a human life qua human, so that finding something enjoyable or emotionally satisfying does not by itself make it a value; her treatment of the sanction of the victim; and her defence of money, production and the pursuit of wealth as moral. Where you can, put the prediction in her voice: say what she would call each arrangement, and which of her distinctions does the classifying work.

Three places where I expect the extrapolation to bind.

(a) The verbal-dispute worry. Most of these donors say that giving is what they most want to do, that it is their highest personal value, and that they would be unhappy otherwise. If sacrifice is the surrender of a greater value for a lesser one, and they are giving up money for the sake of the good they care about most, then on her own definition they are not sacrificing at all, and her condemnation looks like a quarrel about the word selfish. What in her framework — the objective theory of value, the claim that emotions are not tools of cognition, the life-qua-human standard — lets her resist that collapse? And how much of her case against altruism survives if she cannot resist it?

(b) The trader principle versus moral trade. Moral trade is trade-shaped: both parties gain, nothing is given away, nobody is asked to act against her interest, and the whole arrangement rests on voluntary exchange. But it is motive-inverted, since each party's action aims at the other's good and her own good arrives only through the agreement. The trader principle appears to test the structure of the exchange; her definition of altruism appears to test what the agent is aiming at. Which test would she apply, and why? If she applies the second, must she condemn ordinary market exchange as well, since the baker who rises at four in the morning is also aiming at her customers' breakfasts? If she applies the first, what is left of the altruism-versus-self-interest distinction, given that even a soldier falling on a grenade is giving value for value if his comrades' lives are part of his own good?

(c) Value-blind giving. This case is neither sacrifice nor trade, yet it is a deliberate abdication of judgement — the surrender of one's own mind's verdict to someone else's, of precisely the kind she ranked among the gravest vices. Does her framework have a category for it, or is it a genuine gap? Would she file it under altruism, under the sanction of the victim, under something else, or would she have to coin something?

Two final constraints. First, distinguish the places where her framework yields a clear verdict from the places where it is simply out of its depth — expected-value reasoning about people who may never exist, professionalised benevolence, the scale and impersonality of modern transfers, donors who make a living from allocating other people's money — and tell me which is which. Second, keep the classification honest: say whether she would treat these people as sacrificers, as traders, as fools, or as something she has no word for, and do not settle the matter by redefining altruism so that she comes out trivially right or trivially wrong.
