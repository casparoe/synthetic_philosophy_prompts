Setup: Death in Damascus, the version everyone knows. You're in Damascus, Death arrives tomorrow, Death predicts where you'll be perfectly, you can stay or take the road to Aleppo. Payoffs, to keep everything finite: death = 0, alive in Damascus = 100, alive in Aleppo = 99, the extra point being the cost of the trip. I'll also talk about an imperfect predictor who's right with probability a > 1/2, because one step turns on which version you're using.

(1) Whatever your favourite decision rule, it ranks your options by expected utility computed from your credences at the moment of choice: for an evidentialist, the probability of each outcome given the act; for a causalist, the probability given that you intervene to do the act, with your state-credences held fixed. Either way the ranking is a function of what you believe at that moment.

(2) What you believe at that moment includes what you believe about what you yourself are about to do. Someone who has no idea what they're about to do hasn't deliberated yet; someone who has finished deliberating knows.

(3) So by the time you've settled on A, your probability for A is near 1 and for the alternatives near 0 — and on the causalist side your state-credences have collapsed onto whichever state goes with A.

(4) That bites in two different places.
 (a) Evidential side: the probability of outcomes given B is now conditioned on something you're all but certain won't happen, and the case itself barely constrains it: I can make it come out however I like by fiddling with the last percent of doubt, since a conditional on a near-null event is nearly free relative to the unconditional. So the verdict is hostage to noise. In Death in Damascus with a perfect predictor it's worse than noisy, it's degenerate: conditional on staying, Death is in Damascus for sure; conditional on fleeing, Death is in Aleppo for sure; both acts come out at 0 and you decide to stay because of the taxi fare. An indifference you have to break with travel costs is not a verdict.
 (b) Causalist side: here you actually thrash. Let c be your credence that you stay. With a perfect predictor, Death is in Damascus and I stay are the same proposition, so the probability that Death is in Damascus is c, and the causal expected utility of staying is 100(1−c) while that of fleeing is 99c. So flee iff c > 100/199. At c = 1/2 you stay, since 49.5 < 50. But if you're staying, you know it, so c goes to ~1, so you should flee. And if you're fleeing you know that, so c goes to ~0, so you should stay. Repeat. The only self-consistent point I can find is c = 100/199 exactly, and it's unstable, a hair either side and you flip. I get the same qualitative thrash for any accuracy above about a half, and it vanishes at exactly a half, which is both a comfort and a reason to be suspicious of myself.

(5) The engine in (4b) is only this: my forecast about my own act is evidence about where Death is, and I want to be where Death isn't. Nothing there needs Death. It needs a state that's correlated with my act for non-causal reasons, plus a payoff structure where I'd rather be on the other side of the correlation — I want to be where the pursuer isn't, I want to move in a way the market didn't price, I want my threat to be unexpected. Those cases are everywhere, and the argument says that in all of them you cannot both know your own mind and have a verdict.

(6) So: to keep the ranking in (1) well-defined at the moment of choice, you have to keep yourself uncertain about your own act (3); but the moment you're permitted to know your own mind, the ranking either dissolves into noise (4a) or has no fixed point (4b). Every successful deliberation destroys the input the theory runs on.

(7) Therefore no one who knows their own mind can ever choose rationally, and the only well-defined deliberation is deliberation that hasn't finished yet. Deciding early is free in a million ordinary cases, so this is absurd — but I can't locate the move.

My own fix, which nobody in the chat will engage with: stop conditioning on the act and condition on the settlement — the event of your deliberation landing on A, which happens before A does and is, I want to say, the thing a predictor is really tracking. Call it settlement-conditional evidentialism. It looks like it survives (3), because the probability of an outcome given that you settled on A is perfectly well-defined even when your probability for A is 1, since settling and doing can come apart — you settle, then a bus hits you. And it still gives you one-boxing in Newcomb and non-smoking in the lesion, because the settlement carries the correlation that the act was carrying.

What I want from you: which step is broken, why that step looked fine, and what it costs to give it up — in particular whether I can kill the argument without losing the Newcomb and smoking-lesion verdicts, since those are the ones I'd most like to keep. I have no attachment to (3) as stated, I just can't see how to drop it. Does settlement-conditioning actually escape (4b), or does it just push the instability up one level, i.e. Death reads the settlement too and now I thrash about which settlement to settle on, and is that regress fatal or is it the ordinary fact that you can't decide to decide? If the honest answer is that Death in Damascus has no solution at all and any old choice is permissible, say so, but then tell me what that costs me in the ordinary cases where (6) claims deliberation is impossible. And if your answer is to pick a policy or an algorithm rather than an act, tell me specifically what that does at step (4b): does the algorithm-level version have a fixed point that the act-level one lacks, or is it the same instability wearing a nicer hat?
