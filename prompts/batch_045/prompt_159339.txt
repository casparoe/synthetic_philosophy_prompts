My friend and I keep going in circles about Hofstadter's thought that the 'I' isn't sealed inside one skull — that the pattern thats me also runs, in thinner form, in other brains, so when someone dies some fraction of them is still around, spread over whoever knew them. I don't want the seminar question about whether I literally survive; I want to use the idea for something, namely deciding how much prudential concern to have — how much to care about a future state of affairs in proportion to how much of me is in it. So give me a spec, not a verdict: what would a theory of degrees of instantation have to deliver to be usable for that, with the case behind each requirement.

Cases I want handled: fission where the two halves aren't equal; one pattern spread over twenty brains at 5% each; a copy made while I'm still alive vs. one made after I'm gone; slow replacement over decades; a structurally identical pattern that arose by accident with no causal link to me. Distinctions it can't collapse: being me vs. merely being correctly described by the same description; how many places vs. how much in each; spread across people at one time vs. survival across time.

The bit I most want: I've been toying with the idea that survival is conserved — there's only so much of a person to go around, so the more brains share a pattern the thinner its spread, and my friend says that's nonsense and its additive. Should conservation be on the requirements list at all, or is it just a hangover from the metaphor we started with? Two or three lines on how a Parfit-style psychological-continuity view and an animalist view do against the list is enough, I care much more about the criteria.
