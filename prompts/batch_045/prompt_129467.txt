I run a small advanced epistemology reading group, and the final session is going to be on higher-order evidence. Rather than end with yet another open-ended discussion, I'd like to close with a scorecard, so I've fixed on one test case and drawn up a list of seven possible responses to it. Four of them are more or less positions people actually hold; three I had to invent because I couldn't find anyone defending exactly these, and part of what I want to know is whether the invented ones are any good or whether they collapse into positions already on the list.

The case (call it the triple-defeat case) is this: after a week spent reviewing forty-odd studies, you come to be about 0.8 confident that a widely used dietary supplement reduces cardiovascular risk. Then, in a single afternoon, three things happen. First, a colleague you regard as your epistemic peer, who has read the same literature, tells you she is 0.8 confident that it does not. Second, you read a well-conducted meta-analysis showing that reviewers who lean as heavily as you did on observational evidence of this kind are wrong about 60% of the time in this domain — and, importantly, wrong symmetrically, in both directions. Third, you learn that the group behind that meta-analysis has a documented track record of overstating exactly this sort of unreliability claim, though nothing lets you tell whether they did so here. Here are the seven responses:

1. **Equal-weight conciliationism.** On learning of peer disagreement you must move to (roughly) the midpoint of the two credences; extended to higher-order evidence generally, you treat the deliverance of an impugned process the way you'd treat an opposed peer's opinion and split the difference.
2. **Total-evidence evidentialism (steadfast variant).** There is no special category of higher-order evidence and no special principle for it; the new information is just evidence, combined by ordinary updating via a bridge premise along the lines of "if the process that produced my 0.8 is unreliable, that lowers the probability that the supplement helps." On this view the correct response is sometimes no change at all.
3. **Level-splitting (permissive akrasia).** Rationality requirements are level-relative: it can be rational to retain your 0.8 in the supplement while simultaneously holding that this very attitude was formed unreliably and is not to be trusted. The two attitudes need not be reconciled into a single one.
4. **Quarantine (managerialism).** Higher-order evidence never bears on what you should believe about the supplement; it bears only on what you should do — decline to co-sign the recommendation, hand the decision to someone else, hold off on publishing, commission a fresh study.
5. **Capped retrograde reweighting** *(my invention)*. Discount the weight of your first-order evidence in proportion to the probability that the process producing it was reliable, but impose a floor at the point of indifference: reweighting can move you toward 0.5 but never across it. Only genuinely new, process-independent evidence can make you confident in the negation.
6. **Diachronic self-repair** *(my invention)*. No synchronic response is available, because any response you make — reweighing, checking, deferring — deploys the very faculty the higher-order evidence impugns. So the rational response is a plan: stop acting and asserting on the attitude, gather independent first-order evidence, and let the higher-order evidence decay as the plan executes, rather than revising the attitude by fiat.
7. **Iterated meta-conciliation** *(my invention)*. Apply conciliationism at every level, including to your own view about how higher-order evidence should be handled, and iterate until you hit a fixed point.

Please give me the scorecard in four parts. (a) Sort the seven into three tiers — top, middle, bottom — with one sentence saying why each lands where it does. (b) Distribute 100 points of your own credence across the seven, so I can see how spread out your confidence is; a lopsided distribution is fine if that's what you think. (c) I want the views measured against four desiderata I've been mulling: **order-invariance** (getting the same answer when the three items arrive in a different sequence); **no indexical privilege** (giving the same treatment to evidence that *you* are unreliable as to evidence that *someone else* is, absent a substantive difference); **stability under self-undermining** (coping when the higher-order evidence also impugns your handling of higher-order evidence); and **non-vacuity** (yielding determinate guidance in the triple-defeat case rather than "it depends"). For each desideratum, name in a clause the view that handles it best and the view that handles it worst. (d) Finally, tell me which of my three invented views — 5, 6, or 7 — is closest to being worth defending in its own right, and what the strongest objection to it would be.

I've read the literature, so please don't give me a reading list or attribute these to named authors: I want your own assessment, reasoned out against the case and the four desiderata.
