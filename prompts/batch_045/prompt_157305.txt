I'm in the lower sixth and writing my EPQ on political trust, and I keep losing an argument with myself. I've read the usual case for the Scandinavian attitude — that a polity which defaults to trusting its officials spends less on enforcement, gets more honest behaviour back because officials are treated as though they were honest, and ends up with institutions that can do things a suspected institution cannot — and the usual case for the American one — that checks and balances, an adversarial press and a right to demand reasons from power are simply taking human nature seriously rather than optimism about it. I want to be pushed on the side I find less intuitive, so please do this: play a committed defender of the high-trust, Scandinavian default as the more reasonable general attitude towards government — someone who thinks suspicion is an expensive habit that mostly purchases the feeling of vigilance rather than vigilance itself. Sharp, not a strawman: you may concede that trust can be exploited, that there are institutional settings where it is unwarranted, and that a country built on checks and balances may have local reasons for suspicion, but you may not concede that suspicion is the better default to bring to politics in general. Please answer in British English, in about two or three paragraphs a turn, argue from mechanisms and small examples rather than by citing studies, statistics or country rankings I have no way to check, give me no reading list, and stay in character until I type 'stop'. (A name I've invented for the part I can't get past, which you should feel free to attack or refine: the paper-trail paradox. In a suspicious system, each new audit, disclosure rule and sign-off is complied with most cheaply by the officials who were going to behave honestly anyway, and most easily gamed by the ones who were not — the honest carry the cost of proving themselves and the dishonest buy a paper trail indistinguishable from theirs. The system therefore spends more on verification and obtains less, and it does so more and more the more suspicious it becomes.)

Here is my opening, spoken in the voice I actually find harder to refute. If the paper-trail paradox is right, then trust is not a pleasant extra bolted onto a system that would run without it — it is the load-bearing part, and it is load-bearing precisely because nobody is checking. But a norm that holds only while no one tests it is a fragile equilibrium: as soon as a few officials take the money that is lying around, everyone else acquires a reason to start auditing, and once you have slid into the low-trust equilibrium there seems to be no way back, since no one can credibly promise to disarm first. Suspicion, by contrast, is self-enforcing: audits and disclosure requirements work whether or not officials are virtuous, so their floor is reasonably high even if their ceiling is lower, and they do not depend on anyone's continuing good will. So even granting that trust is nicer when it is there, isn't suspicion the more reasonable attitude — the one you would choose if you had to pick a default not knowing which country you were going to wake up in? Answer that first move in character before you do anything else, and feel free to end by putting a question back to me.
