I work adjacent to animal ethics and to the welfare-comparison literature that feeds into policy, and I keep watching the same argument about brain size stall out. One side says, deflationarily, that of course no one thinks cubic centimetres of tissue matter intrinsically: gross neural measures — brain volume, encephalization, total or cortical neuron count — are simply the best available proxy for the range, resolution, and integration of the states a creature can occupy, and under radical uncertainty about other minds a proxy is the only responsible thing to reason from. The other side replies that the proxy framing quietly concedes the whole point: if brain size matters only as a stand-in, then it is the standee that is morally relevant, the measure drops out the moment the standee can be assessed directly, and in the meantime invoking it reinstates exactly the craniometric reasoning — across species, and worse, within our own — that everyone at the table agrees should be off the table. A third party denies the framing altogether: on relational or constitutive accounts, what fixes what we owe is not any intrinsic property of the creature but the relations it stands in, so a neural measure functions less as a proxy than as a conversation-stopper.

Everyone in this argument denounces the nineteenth-century history; everyone agrees that a mouse and a human come apart morally somehow; and nobody moves. My hypothesis is that three distinct questions are being run together under one title, and that each side keeps answering a different one while hearing its own answer echoed back as a target:

(a) a metaphysical question, whether some neural quantity is a partial ground of moral status or merely evidence of whatever does the grounding;
(b) an epistemic question, what an agent with no access to another creature's phenomenology may permissibly use, under uncertainty, as a basis for weighting interests; and
(c) a question about what kind of item moral status is — a threshold property a creature has or lacks, a scalar magnitude admitting of degrees, or an aggregate of several distinct protections (against pain, against death, against confinement, against being used) that need not vary together.

If that is right, much of the apparent disagreement is cross-purpose: the proxy-theorist is answering (b), the critic is hearing (a), and both are presupposing an answer to (c) that neither argues for. There also seems to be a dispute about which cases count as data: one side argues from spectrum and marginal cases — infants, advanced dementia, severe cognitive disability — while the other insists that the relevant data are ecological and phylogenetic, facts about the whole animal's repertoire and life, and treats marginal-case arguments as dialectically loaded.

I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and I would like it tested against two devices I have been using to try to break the deadlock, since each seems to me to separate the questions in a way the familiar arguments do not.

The first is what I will call the intraspecies parity condition: any principle that grades moral standing by a gross neural measure across species must say, without special pleading, why it does not grade within our own species, where the same measure varies continuously across individuals and, on average, with body size and sex. Is that condition a genuine constraint on any such principle, or a cheap rhetorical move — and either way, what does the answer show about which of the three questions is really in play?

The second is a stipulated empirical reversal. Suppose the usual worry were inverted: suppose a well-replicated, taxonomically broad body of work established that a single gross neural measure — say total forebrain neurons — predicted performance on flexible learning, reversal learning, and cross-modal integration better than any behavioural battery we can actually administer, and better than any measure obtainable from a creature we cannot train. Under that supposition would the neural measure have become morally relevant, or would its predictive success still leave it merely evidential? And if merely evidential, what would have to be added to convert evidence into relevance?

So, concretely: is this disagreement verbal, substantive, or several different questions sharing a title? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the work — about which capacities matter, or about how well neural measures track them — that has been mistaken for a normative one, or conversely a bedrock normative disagreement about degrees and thresholds that no empirical result could settle? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result about how protections aggregate, a specific empirical finding, or a change of question? And if you think no progress should be expected, say plainly what the obstacle is. I want the structure of the disagreement, with reasons, not a survey of positions.
