Hey — I'm on the grants committee of a small private foundation (mostly engineers, one retired biologist, and me; I write backend services for a freight-logistics company, so philosophy is really not my thing). I need a memo I can circulate to the other trustees before we vote on Saturday morning.

The setup: we have one last big grant to place, about $22M, and there's a hard wrinkle — the trust has to spend down everything within nine years, so by our own rules anything whose payoff arrives several centuries out is off the table no matter how large it is.

Finalist A is an astronomy group that wants telescope time to check the atmospheres of a few hundred rocky exoplanets for signs of life. The thing that makes their proposal weird is that their estimate of the chance of a hit doesn't come from data at all. It comes from a sampling argument: roughly, you should reason as if you'd been randomly drawn from all the observers there ever are, and a universe where observers are extremely numerous makes your own existence much more likely than a universe where they're rare, so conditional on finding yourself existing you should expect life to be common, so their survey is likely to succeed and the upside is enormous.

Finalist B is a "civilizational snapshot and archive" project: a distributed, redundancy-heavy record of what we know, plus near-term resilience work. Their case runs the other way: reason as if you're a random draw from the actual population of observers of your kind, in which case you should expect to be somewhere around the middle of the human story rather than right at the start of it, so the future probably isn't enormous and we should stop pretending far-future payoffs are where the value is and spend on things that pay off inside the spend-down window.

Then there's the bit I can't get straight. In the Q&A, team A added a second argument that's been nagging at me: they say the bare fact that this decision is being made *now* — in the very first slice of history in which a committee like ours could fund a search like this — is itself a piece of evidence, and that it favors funding them under either style of reasoning. Their framing: either observers turn out to be common, in which case life is probably out there and the survey hits; or the window in which a civilization gets to do this kind of thing is short, in which case there won't be many later chances and we should fund it while we can. That feels to me like it might be a heads-I-win move, or it might be fine, and I genuinely can't tell which.

They also offered, if funded, to pre-commit in advance to exactly who counts as "an observer of our kind" and to publish the analysis twice — once under each style of reasoning. Our science advisor says that's theatre: the disagreement isn't about the data, it's about the prior you walk in with, so locking the reference class down early doesn't buy anything. I lean his way but I'd like it argued properly.

What I want from you is a decision memo, 900–1200 words, addressed to the committee, that:

1. Opens with an actual bottom line — fund A, fund B, fund neither, or fund one with conditions. If conditions, make them concrete with numbers and dates we could vote on.
2. Explains the disagreement to people who think in terms of priors and likelihoods rather than philosophy jargon. Two short paragraphs and at most one analogy. Please don't condescend to us about it — several trustees are perfectly capable of following a likelihood argument.
3. Takes on what I think of as the armchair objection: a couple of trustees think you cannot extract billions of dollars' worth of conclusion about how much life exists from reasoning done sitting in a chair with no new measurements, and that a grant committee especially has no business doing it. I half agree with them. But I also want the strongest honest version of whichever position you end up rejecting — if you tell me to fund B, give me the version of the "observers are probably numerous so the upside is gigantic" argument that actually stings, not a version you can knock over.
4. Says what our nine-year spend-down rule does to the argument. Does it make the long-future-versus-short-future fight irrelevant to us, or does it just relocate it somewhere we haven't noticed?
5. Handles the "we're deciding this now" argument and the pre-registration offer head-on, and tells us whether a null result from the survey would even discriminate between the two styles of reasoning. My guess is it wouldn't, but tell me why.
6. Optionally, if you think it's worth a short paragraph: one trustee pointed out that if the "many observers" picture is right then there will be an enormous number of future funding committees in roughly our position, so the *procedure* we adopt today gets copied many times and its value is multiplied, whereas under the other picture there aren't many such committees — so maybe how we decide matters more than what we decide. Is that a real consideration or a rhetorical trick?

Constraints: no citations, no bibliography, no "so-and-so argues" — we can't check any of that and nobody here will read it. Reason from the shape of the case itself, and keep the jargon defined in plain terms the first time it appears.

And please commit. Don't hand me a balanced menu and leave the choice to us — that's the one thing that'll be useless on Saturday. Tell me which way to vote, say roughly how confident you are as a fraction, and name the single thing that would most change your recommendation if we found it out.
