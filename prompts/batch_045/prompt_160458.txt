I'm a second-year PhD student in economics, and I keep running into the Latin tag "de gustibus non est disputandum" being made to do argumentative work I don't think it can do. Most recently: in our behavioral public finance reading group, a discussant was commenting on a paper that proposes a welfare criterion for settings where choice is sensitive to framing and to defaults. A questioner pressed the obvious worry — if the analyst's only evidence is what people pick out of a menu somebody else arranged, then treating choice as the welfare criterion doesn't defer to anyone's tastes, it just ratifies the menu. The discussant's reply was, more or less: at some point you just have to say de gustibus non est disputandum — if they chose the option the planner calls worse, that's their taste, and taste isn't something we get to second-guess. Everyone nodded. I want to know what, exactly, was being asserted.

So: set out the menu of precise theses that sentence could be expressing, from the trivial to the radical, and give a verdict on each (trivial; substantive and defensible; substantive but undefended here; false; or question-begging). The distinctions I haven't been able to settle on my own:

- Is "no disputing" a claim about the truth-aptness of taste statements (no fact of the matter to argue about), about the futility of arguments (there is a fact, but reasoning won't reach it), or about the legitimacy of one person overriding another (the fact is reachable, but it's none of your business)? These come apart in the menu case.
- Does "no disputing" range over disputes between persons only, or also over disputes within one person across time? The cases the paper cares about — automatic enrollment, choices that look present-biased, tastes that form through consumption — are mostly intrapersonal, and the proverb seems silent there.
- In the revealed-preference tradition "taste" is often just whatever utility function represents the observed pattern of choice: utility as a numerical encoding of behavior and nothing more. Is that thin, representational reading compatible with the normative one about deference? My suspicion is that they come apart — the thin one is close to vacuous and licenses nothing, while anything that licenses deference is a substantive claim about welfare. Tell me whether that's right and where it goes wrong.
- I've started calling one reading the "audit" reading (choice is evidence of taste: defeasible, and reliable only under conditions like procedural invariance and real stakes) and another the "armistice" reading (taste claims are not to be adjudicated at all, by anyone, ever). If the armistice reading is the one doing the work in that reply, what is it doing there, and does it survive a case where the very same people would choose differently if the items were reordered?
- Relatedly, "hold tastes fixed and explain behavior by prices, information, and constraints" is a methodological injunction I associate with this slogan in economics. Does it support the discussant's reply, or quietly undercut it?

For each reading, say whether it would actually get him his conclusion — whether it lets him treat a framing-sensitive choice as a taste to be respected rather than as an artifact of the menu he was handed. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence: one sentence on whether any of the readings does the work he needed. Then give the menu, with no preamble and no restatement of my question. A table with columns for the reading, the precise claim it amounts to, your verdict, and a yes/no/qualified on whether it supports the reply would be ideal.
