I'm in a seminar on free will and moral responsibility, and two people in my study group spent most of our last session going in circles over the case we're using as our working example of sphexishness: the digging wasp that drags a cricket to the mouth of its burrow, leaves it on the threshold while it goes inside to inspect the nest, and — if someone nudges the cricket a few inches during the inspection — brings it back to the threshold and goes in to inspect again. We're treating the story as a thought experiment rather than fighting about how faithfully it reports the behaviour of actual wasps, so please keep to the conceptual dispute.

Nadia opened by saying that sphexishness can't be rigidity of movement, since any behaviour is rigid at some level of description. What makes the wasp sphexish, she said, is that the routine is insensitive to what the routine is for: the diagnostic is whether the agent would alter its course when something happens that bears on the goal it is supposedly pursuing. On that test, she added, a human who keeps re-running the same script in the face of clear feedback is sphexish too, however deliberate it looks from the inside.

Owen replied that the test over-generates: much ordinary, competent behaviour is locally rigid precisely because the environment is stable, and the wasp is not inert either — the reset is triggered by the cricket's being out of place, which is a response to circumstances. He also said flexibility has to be built out of fixed parts, so being a loop at one level can't be the mark of unfreedom at another.

Nadia answered that being triggered is not being guided: sensitivity to a cue is not sensitivity to a reason, and the wasp's resets never get it any closer to the nest.

Owen then said the cue/reason distinction is doing all the work and can't be applied without sneaking in a consciousness requirement, that stressed humans do exactly this sort of thing, and that the difference is therefore one of degree — from which he concluded you can't hang responsibility on it. To press the point he offered two invented variants: a wasp that varies its approach slightly on each reset and so eventually succeeds, and one that after a dozen resets switches to an entirely different fixed routine. Both look flexible on any behavioural test, he said, and yet neither retains anything from what its failed attempts showed it; so either Nadia must call them flexible, or her real criterion was never behavioural at all.

Nadia held that both are still sphexish: variation without retention is just noise, and what matters is a standing capacity to notice that one is looping and to re-describe what one is trying to do. Owen said that makes the notion unfalsifiable from the outside and lets you declare any agent sphexish for lacking the right inner item.

Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who is right about what — rating each move rather than just declaring an overall winner — and then say what, if anything, both of them are missing? I'd especially like to know whether the two invented variants really do decide anything between them, and whether they are tacitly using sphexishness for two different purposes.
