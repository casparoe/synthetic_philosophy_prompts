I convene a standing committee that issues one-page joint public statements on behalf of a city-wide coalition: three neighborhood associations, a hospital, a school district, and two labor unions, nine votes in total, each with an effective veto. We meet for two hours a month, our drafts go past a lawyer but not a philosopher, and the finished text is read aloud at press conferences and quoted in local news, so it has to be intelligible to a reader with no training and short enough to fit on a single page. Our recurring failure mode is not that members are unreasonable but that on nearly every topic — a new homeless shelter, a school closure, a police shooting, a chemical plant's emissions permit, a donor's name on a hospital wing — each delegate arrives with a sentence the others experience as a flat falsehood, and we either deadlock, or we paper over the disagreement with vague compromise language that the local paper mocks and that none of the member organizations actually endorses. I have been reading about the Jain doctrine of many-sidedness in a survey course — anekāntavāda, the standpoint-relative theory of nayavāda, and the logical instrument of syādvāda with its sevenfold scheme of conditional predication, where a given predicate can hold of a subject in one respect, fail in another, hold and fail successively under two respects, or be inexpressible when both are pressed into a single assertion, and combinations of these — and I want to know whether it can be turned into something my committee can actually run rather than admire.

What I need is a procedure: the questions to ask, in order, about a contested claim; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure terminates in a flat assertion; and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than of aspects. Please build it around a few concrete mechanisms I have been sketching and tell me if they are the right ones. First, a standpoint ledger: for each disputed sentence, name the respect — whose interests, what time scale, what comparison class, what part or aspect of the subject — under which each delegate's claim is true, and require that a claim be marked unattached when no one can produce a respect under which it holds, since I suspect a good half of our disputes are claims whose speakers have simply never been asked to say in what respect. Second, an explicit sorting step that runs before the ledger, because my worry is that we would apply many-sidedness to disagreements that are not aspectual at all: some of our disputes seem to be straightforwardly empirical and should be settled by asking for a number and a baseline, some are disputes about the sense of a single word (safe, harm, community, consent) that should be settled by disambiguation, and only the remainder are genuine cases where incompatible claims are each correct from a defensible standpoint. Third, a collapse rule: a test for when the qualified, sevenfold treatment must collapse back into a plain, unhedged assertion, including the case where a delegate's claim rests on a factual falsehood that the rest of us can refute, and the harder case where the standpoint a claim is true from is itself one we should refuse to adopt — a claim true only from the perspective of someone who denies another party's standing or personhood. Fourth, a set of drafting rules for what a signed statement may look like when many-sidedness genuinely survives: whether we conjoin qualified claims, whether we attribute each claim to the party who holds it, whether we assert in a respect that is nobody's in particular, how we say in one page that a thing is and is not without looking evasive to a reader who has never heard of the doctrine, and above all how a non-absolutist description gets converted into a recommendation, since our statements almost always end in a request for some action and the reader will not accept a sevenfold answer to the question of whether the shelter should open.

Please also flag, honestly, what this procedure cannot do and where judgment cannot be eliminated: my vice-chair's objection is that any method built on inexpressibility is a license for weasel wording, and my own is that the whole apparatus may just be relativism with better manners, or the journalistic habit of quoting both sides with an Indian vocabulary attached, and I would like the procedure to contain its own guards against those failure modes rather than trusting the goodwill of the drafter. Then run it on three of our pending items. The first: a statement on a drop-in center for homeless residents, where the neighborhood association says the area has become unsafe, the service providers say it has not, and the police have published three years of incident data that neither side has looked at. The second: a statement on whether to remove a benefactor's name from a hospital wing, where one faction's sentence is that he was the greatest friend this city ever had and the other's is that he was a segregationist who enriched himself through discriminatory lending — a case that looks to me like the textbook illustration, two true predicates about one man, except that we still have to vote on taking the name down. The third is the one I expect to dislike: a member organization is pressing us to say in the joint statement that a contested public-health question is genuinely open, when the relevant scientific bodies are not in fact divided, and I suspect the correct output of your procedure is either a statement that names that claim as false or no statement at all, which will cost us a signatory. What I want is the procedure, its failure modes, and those three worked applications, written so that a committee with no philosophical training could follow it in two hours.
