The thesis I'm defending. I want to argue that non-identity is not a puzzle about a strange and circumscribed class of cases but the normal condition of any decision that affects anyone more than about a generation out. To make this precise I introduce a notion I call identity half-life: for a given present choice, the time T at which the expected fraction of the people alive at T whose very existence counterfactually depends on that choice reaches one half. My empirical-ish claim, which I'll call Fragility, is that identity half-life is on the order of a few decades rather than centuries, because who exists is extraordinarily sensitive to tiny differences in timing and circumstance — differences of the sort that any policy that shifts aggregate conditions (energy prices, pandemic timelines, the speed of AI development, school curricula) reliably produces. If Fragility holds, then combine it with the familiar non-identity argument and you get something stronger than people usually conclude: for the far region, there is no one who is made worse off by our choice, so a person-affecting moral theory has literally nothing to say about the long-term future — the region where the longtermists think almost all the expected value lives.

My positive proposal is what I've been calling the Two-Zone View. In the near zone, where identity is stable enough across our options that we can still ask whether identifiable people are better or worse off, we keep a person-affecting constraint as a genuine side constraint: it blocks certain intuitively objectionable trade-offs and it does real work there. In the far zone we drop the person-affecting restriction entirely and evaluate outcomes impersonally, by something like a critical-level total view, with an extra discount factor on welfare for people whose existence was itself counterfactually fragile — my thought being that there is something morally thinner about creating value by determining who exists than by improving the lives of people who would have existed anyway. I also argue, against what I call Temporal Parity, that the longtermist's favorite intuition — that near-term people and far-future people must be treated exactly alike — cannot be had alongside the person-affecting restriction once Fragility is on the table, and that the longtermists should therefore be the last people to want to hold on to it.
