**Where was the cost paid? — notes toward a bookkeeping-relative exorcism of Maxwell's demon**

[P1] Take Szilard's one-molecule engine. A box holds a single molecule at temperature T; a demon inserts a partition at the midpoint, learns which half the molecule occupies, and couples a weight to the appropriate side, extracting kT·ln 2 of work per cycle as the molecule re-expands and the bath resupplies the heat. The standard answer to why this does not violate the second law locates the compensating cost in a discrete step: the demon's memory fills with outcomes, and resetting it to a standard blank state dissipates at least kT·ln 2 per bit. Call the claim that the compensating cost is *located in a memory-erasure step* the erasure-location thesis. My aim is to argue that this thesis is architecture-relative: it holds for one interesting class of demon designs, fails for others that balance the books just as well, and that what is invariant across all of them is a fact about correlations rather than a fact about a step.

[P2] A preliminary constraint. What the demon exploits is not correlation with the gas as such but correlation with the variable its feedback act is conditioned on. A demon that records an irrelevant variable — a fair coin flip, or the exact velocity of a molecule whose *position* is what the partition acts on — gains nothing from the record and pays full price for it. And a demon that records at higher resolution than its protocol can use keeps bits that a reversible compression would have discarded. Call this the parsimony constraint: the thermodynamic asset is mutual information with the *controlled* variable, and any bit of the record that is not part of a minimal sufficient statistic about that variable is pure cost. The naive moral here — that the demon should want to know as much as possible — is backwards; what it should want is to know exactly as much as it can act on.

[P3] Now three designs that locate the cost differently. D0, the long-tape demon, arrives with a tape of L cells all in the blank state and simply writes each outcome into the next cell, never erasing. Over L cycles it extracts L·kT·ln 2 of work and performs no erasure at all. But a blank tape of L cells is a low-entropy resource: preparing it from whatever thermally equilibrated tape was to hand requires exporting L bits of entropy elsewhere, at L·kT·ln 2. The cost has not vanished; it has been front-loaded into a preparation stage that occurs before any molecule is measured. If we describe the demon as the tape-plus-mechanism and the protocol as starting once the tape is in hand, then there is no cost anywhere in the protocol, and the exorcism has to be relocated to the demon's manufacture.

[P4] D1, the scavenger demon, has no prepared tape. Its memory cells start in thermal equilibrium — random, free, no preparation cost. It records by overwriting each cell with the outcome. Overwriting a cell whose prior content is unknown is a logically irreversible many-to-one map on the cell's state space, and by Landauer's principle it carries the same kT·ln 2 price: the cell's prior entropy has to go somewhere. So the cost is paid neither at the end nor at the beginning but inside the measurement-recording step, and the demon's "acquisition of information" rather than its "forgetting" is the dissipative event. Note that D1 is the architecture we implicitly picture when we tell the standard story, minus the standard story's timing assumption that the cost arrives later, at a separate reset.

[P5] D2, the dispersal demon, is the case that makes me think the erasure-location thesis is not merely timing-relative but false as a general claim. Creating a correlation can be done reversibly: a carefully chosen interaction (a controlled-NOT, say) between the molecule's position and an equilibrated ancilla degree of freedom maps independent states to correlated ones while leaving both marginals and the joint entropy unchanged, and so dissipates nothing. Correlation, it turns out, is cheap to create and is freely relocatable between substrates. It is also consumable: a feedback protocol can convert it into work, spending it. But suppose the demon creates the correlation in a degree of freedom it then abandons to the bath. The correlation decays under thermal contact; mutual information is destroyed without having been spent on anything; and the destruction of one bit of correlation without work extraction dumps kT·ln 2 into the bath. Nobody erased anything, nobody prepared anything, no step in the demon's protocol is irreversible, and the books balance. Call this deliquescence: the cost is paid continuously, by the bath, and by no agent in particular.

[P6] The three cases suggest a trilemma about any putative information engine. Either the correlation is created in a substrate the demon can *address*, in which case that substrate is a reusable record and the cost shows up in writing to or resetting it (D1, and in the limit of a prepared tape, D0); or it is created in a substrate the demon cannot address, in which case it cannot be exploited and the cost shows up as dissipation when the correlation decays (D2); or it is stockpiled in fresh substrate cycle after cycle, in which case the demon is not running a cycle at all but consuming a low-entropy stock, and the cost was paid by whoever made the stock (D0 read as an open protocol). What is invariant across all three is the ledger: the work extracted per cycle is bounded by kT times the mutual information between record and controlled variable, and the total entropy exported to non-information-bearing degrees of freedom over a complete cycle is at least that mutual information — irrespective of which stage exports it. Call the resulting position bookkeeping relativism: for non-cyclic protocols there is no fact of the matter about *which stage* paid the cost; only the cycle total is a physical quantity, and the assignment of the cost to a step is an artifact of how we carve the protocol into stages and which system we count as the demon's memory.

[P7] Bookkeeping relativism is not meant to be scepticism about the second law, and it collapses back into the standard story under one condition: impose that the architecture be *cyclic*, that every subsystem — gas, weight, and every cell of memory — be returned to its initial state at the end of a cycle. Under that constraint the record must be reset, D0's tape must be re-zeroed, D2's ancillae must be re-randomized, and the erasure-location thesis is true by construction. So the honest form of the resolution is a dilemma rather than a mechanism. Either the demon is a closed cyclic machine — and then it must erase, and the familiar exorcism is exactly right — or it is not cyclic, and then it is an entropy consumer, an engine running on a low-entropy resource, no more paradoxical than a steam engine running on coal. The paradox feels deep, I want to say, because we silently imagine the demon as both at once: a closed cycle that nevertheless acquires fresh, exploitable correlation from nowhere.

[P8] Three things I am unsure about, in ascending order of how much they worry me. First, whether "information is physical" is better reconstructed along these lines — as a claim about correlations carrying thermodynamic weight, with the kT·ln 2 erasure price as a special case holding only for cyclic, bounded, re-settable memories — or whether that reconstruction quietly throws away the part of Landauer's principle that does real work. Second, whether the trilemma in [P5] is exhaustive, or whether some architecture pays the cost in a way that is neither addressable-writing, nor decay, nor stock consumption. Third, whether bookkeeping relativism is too cheap: someone will say that an interventionist or counterfactual criterion — stage S bears the cost iff, had S been removed and the rest held fixed, the per-cycle work would have exceeded the bound — does pick out a unique location, and I do not know whether that criterion is well defined, since the interventions that remove a stage also change the joint state the bound is computed from.
