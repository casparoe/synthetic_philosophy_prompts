I write literary fiction, and the vocabulary I actually think in is a craft vocabulary: scene against summary, the chapter measured against the whole-book arc, subplot, point of view, dramatic irony, framing, and above all the gap between what a passage does in its place and what it does in the reading a reviewer gives the finished book. I have been told that Simpson's paradox has exactly this shape — every part can say one thing and the whole say the opposite — and I want to know how far the resemblance really goes.

Please restate Simpson's paradox in that vocabulary as faithfully as you can. Take the standard case, in which a treatment improves the rate in every subgroup and worsens it overall, and rebuild it as a novel: the subgroups as chapters or subplots, the reversal as the difference between the chapter-level and the book-level reading, the shifting subgroup mix as something like the distribution of scene length or of point of view across the book. Say what the paradox consists in, and separate it from the nearby errors it is routinely confused with: regression to the mean, base-rate neglect, the ecological fallacy, a merely confounded comparison.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about:

1. What carries over into the vocabulary of craft without loss?
2. What carries over only if you quietly substitute a craft notion for a statistical one — and where, precisely, did you have to fudge?
3. What has no expression in my vocabulary at all? Specifically: do I have anything corresponding to the fact that the paradox turns on ratios and not on counts; to the constraint that no reversal is possible unless the subgroup mix differs between the two arms; or to the point that the paradox is finally settled by deciding whether one wants to condition on a variable or to intervene on it?

My own case, which prompted the question. A reader reports that in every chapter my protagonist grows more sympathetic, and yet the finished novel leaves people colder than the chapters did; when I regroup the very same chapters by point-of-view character, the reversal reverses again. The chapters are wildly unequal in length — some are four hundred words, some are four thousand. Translate this case into the statistical vocabulary and back, tell me which of the two reversals is the real one and why, and say whether craft vocabulary gives any guidance at all about which partition is the right one. If it does not, I want your view on whether that is a defect in how novelists talk about books, or a sign that the choice of partition was never a statistical question to begin with.
