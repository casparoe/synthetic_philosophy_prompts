You're a social epistemologist who designs decision-making workshops for a living — please think carefully and sketch two or three candidate designs in your head before committing to one, and tell me briefly why you dropped the others.

I got handed a "judgement under uncertainty" session at work next month — 14 people from the ops and incident-response teams, one hour, no real money allowed, materials are basically a deck of cards and some index cards. The reason I got handed it is that last quarter we all piled onto one bad call during a major incident review and nobody said anything until it was way too late, so its not an abstract topic for us.

Design an activity where they feel the difference between two things. (a) A situation where it is individually rational to set aside your own private evidence because the public record of what earlier people did already swamps it. (b) A situation that looks identical from the outside but where people pile on for a totally different reason — because going along with the crowd, or going early, is rewarded whether or not the crowd is right. I want them to run into (a) first and feel that following the crowd there isn't cowardice or laziness or deference to the loudest person, and then hit (b) in the same session and feel that it's a different animal with a different fix. Neither gets named until the debrief.

One constraint that matters: the standard urn-and-coloured-balls demonstration is too clean for what I need. Everyone's signal quality is the same, everyone knows it, and accuracy is the only payoff, so it doesn't separate (a) from (b) and nobody actually feels trapped. So build the design around the messier version — participants get private evidence of visibly different quality (some get a much better look than others), everyone can see the full sequence of earlier choices rather than just the running tally, and at least one person somewhere in the sequence ends up holding strong evidence against a unanimous-looking crowd and has to decide what to do with it. Give me the rules, the payoff scheme, a timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the room onto the (a)/(b) distinction.

Also give me: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm guessing someone goes meta and starts reasoning about the fact that the exercise is rigged, and someone else just stays silent the whole time), one thing I can watch for during the session that tells me (a) actually landed rather than (b), and — honestly — which feature of the phenomenon the activity structurally can't reproduce, so I know what I have to say out loud myself at the end.
