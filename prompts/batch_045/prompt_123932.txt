I'm trying to work out what I actually believe about Pascal's mugging, and I'd rather start from my own reactions to individual cases than from a theory. Below are my verdicts. What I want back is a principle: stated precisely, with each clause traced to the case that motivates it. If no single principle fits them all, then give me the two or three best candidates, say which of my verdicts each one forces me to give up, and tell me which candidate you'd adopt and why. Please answer in British English, and please don't cite papers or hand me a reading list; I want the principle in your own terms.

Stipulations. In every case the sum requested is £5, the numbers of lives are finite, and I'm assuming that a life has some fixed finite value and that I'm otherwise happy to reason in expected-value terms. Unless I say otherwise, I have no way of checking the claim and no evidence about the claimant beyond what he says.

1. A long-established, independently audited charity that averts roughly one death per £3,000 of donations. I give the £5, and I'd defend it in expected-value terms.

2. A stranger on a bench tells me he will torture 10^30 people in another dimension unless I give him £5. I give nothing, and I don't really deliberate.

3. The same stranger, same manner, but the number is 10^6. I still give nothing — but the striking thing, which is the reason I'm writing this, is that I feel *less* inclined to pay in case 2 than in case 3. Since I said in case 1 that I scale linearly in the stakes, this is backwards. Either my probability that he's telling the truth is falling faster than 1/N in the number N of lives he names, or I'm not scaling linearly after all, or I'm just confused.

4. A claimant whose power is not in question: this is a world in which a particular being has been publicly and reliably observed, over decades, to do exactly this kind of thing, and the only open question is whether today's threat is genuine. I pay the £5 at once, and I'd mortgage the house if that's what it took. So my refusal in case 2 is not discomfort with enormous numbers as such.

5. The benefactor rather than the mugger: a stranger offers to create 10^30 flourishing lives if I give him £5, and will simply do nothing otherwise. I don't pay. But I'm noticeably *more* comfortable refusing here than in case 2, and I can't say why; if my objection in case 2 is about tiny probabilities of vast stakes, it should apply identically here, and if it's about being exploited, the threat in case 2 seems the more repeatable of the two, not the less.

6. A crowd: a thousand people in the same park each make a 10^30-lives claim, and their claims are mutually inconsistent, so at most one is true. I pay none of them, although the sum of the expected values is astronomical. At the same time, I support public spending on mitigating existential risks, where the case rests on a disjunction of speculative scenarios, no single one of which I'd put much above one percent. I think these two attitudes are structurally the same and I can't see a difference that isn't ad hoc.

7. The escalation: if I paid in case 2, I should expect a claimant citing 10^40 lives tomorrow. I take this as a reason in itself for a standing policy of never paying such claims — but I'm uneasy, because it looks like a reason for ignoring evidence rather than weighing it, and in case 4 I was happy to act on a great deal of evidence.

8. The asymmetry I can't get rid of: I'll pay real money to reduce a one-in-a-million chance of human extinction, where the one-in-a-million comes from a model with some track record. I won't pay for a one-in-a-million chance at an astronomical good, and I won't pay in case 2 to avert an astronomical bad. So I seem to be discriminating on the *source* of the probability rather than its size — and case 6 suggests that the sources I trust are themselves disjunctions of speculative scenarios.

Here is the principle I've been toying with, and which I suspect is broken. Call it the leverage discount: for any claim that N lives hang on my small act, I should discount the stakes by something like 1/N (or faster), on the ground that the space of conceivable setups in which vast stakes hang on my £5 is dominated by setups in which my act is not the real lever — I'm a simulation being run to see what I'd do, a duplicate, a decoy, a test subject, one of countless copies — and the larger N is claimed to be, the more overwhelmingly the decoy setups outnumber the real ones. Apply this discount to any claimant whose power hasn't been independently established, and case 2 and case 3 come out as refusals, while case 4, where the power is established, is untouched, and case 1, where N is small, is barely touched.

What I'd like from you: does that principle survive my own cases, or does it over-generate — does it, for instance, quietly tell me to stop funding existential risk mitigation in case 6, or to stop reducing the one-in-a-million risk in case 8? Repair it if you can, and if you can't, say so and put something better in its place. Set it against the obvious rivals as you see them: a ceiling on how much value any outcome can have, a cutoff below which probabilities are treated as zero, a restriction of my concern to outcomes I could in principle learn about, and anything else you think is a serious candidate. Tell me which of my verdicts no candidate can save, and which you'd overturn.

And tell me what your preferred principle says about three cases I haven't listed. First: a claimant who promises that, whether or not I pay, he will publicly and conclusively demonstrate his power tomorrow. Second: a claimant in a world where I already have strong evidence that I am one of 10^30 copies of myself facing this identical choice, so that my £5 either changes nothing or changes everything 10^30 times over. Third: a claimant who asks for no money at all, only that I talk myself into believing his claim, and says the lives are spared if I succeed.
