I was taking minutes at a meeting of my department's curriculum committee, and two of the members got into an argument that was never resolved. I'd like you to adjudicate it move by move.

The committee has five voting members. Before looking at any evidence they agreed to adopt a new required statistics course for majors if and only if both of the following held: (A) our graduates need quantitative skills for the jobs they actually take, and (B) no currently required course already teaches those skills. The votes then came out as follows: A passed 3–2; B passed 4–1; the proposal itself failed 2–3. The three members who voted against the proposal do not agree with each other — one of them rejects B, the other two reject A — so the members who accept both A and B are only two of the five.

Nadia: The committee has now accepted A by 3–2 and B by 4–1, and we agreed in advance to adopt the requirement just in case both hold. So the committee's own reasoning commits it to adopting. The vote against the package doesn't show that the committee rejects the case for the requirement; it shows that the three of us who voted no did so for different reasons, and no one reason is held by a majority. A majority that disagrees with itself isn't a majority against anything.

Theo: That's backwards. Three members voted against the requirement, and that's the decision we were actually asked to make — it's the only result with any claim to speak for the committee. Your two majorities are different majorities: the three who accept A and the four who accept B overlap in exactly two people. So the position you've constructed is the position of two members out of five. You want to overrule a majority on the real question using a rationale that no majority holds.

Nadia: But your majority is no more coherent than mine. Of the three who voted no, one denies B and two deny A — they don't share a rationale either. So "no majority holds the winning rationale" can't count against my way of doing it without counting against yours too. And there's a positive argument on my side: A and B are narrower, more checkable questions than the package, so a member's vote on each is more likely to be right, and putting them together uses more of what the committee actually knows. Voting only on the package makes each of us do privately the reasoning we convened to do together, and then throws that reasoning away.

Theo: Two problems. First, the reliability argument needs the two premise votes to be independent evidence, and here they aren't: the members most doubtful that graduates need these skills are also the ones most likely to think the existing sequence already covers them. That's one outlook counted twice. Second, your method's answer depends on how the question is sliced. Suppose we had instead asked the single question "do our graduates need a required statistics course that they cannot currently get?" — three of us would have answered no. Same question, asked as one vote instead of two, opposite result. A procedure that flips under redescription isn't tracking the committee's judgment.

Nadia: The slicing worry is real, but it's an argument for fixing the agenda in advance — which is exactly what we did, before anyone saw how the votes fell. And even if the errors on A and B are correlated, premise-level voting at least exposes where the disagreement is, while a package vote conceals it. More importantly, we are not a legislature registering preferences; we are a committee of people with relevant expertise trying to get the curriculum right. When that's the point, the procedure that makes the most of the committee's information should win.

Theo: Getting it right is the right goal, but expertise about what graduates need doesn't settle whether it is legitimate to override a clear 3–2 vote against the proposal. And notice what your procedure delivers: a change to the degree program carried by a conclusion that only two of five members endorse.

Please go through the exchange and say what is right and what is wrong in each move — not just who wins overall. Then say who is more right overall, and whether there is some consideration that neither of them raised that ought to change how the case comes out. For each of your verdicts, state how confident you are in it, and single out the one point you are least sure about.
