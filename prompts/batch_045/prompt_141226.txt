I work on variable-population axiology, and I am trying to understand why the debate about the repugnant conclusion has not moved since Parfit raised it in Reasons and Persons (1984). Totalism gets the repugnant conclusion; average-style views avoid it but imply that adding a person whose life is good but below average makes things worse; critical-level views, which subtract a fixed positive threshold from each life before summing, avoid it at the cost of making the value of an extra life discontinuous at the threshold and of inheriting whatever arbitrariness attaches to the choice of that threshold; person-affecting restrictions avoid it but run into the non-identity problem and require an asymmetry between creating a life that is barely worth living and creating one that is not worth living that many people find unmotivated. Meanwhile almost every published argument has the same shape: display a chain of pairwise case judgments, get the reader to accept each step, and derive either the repugnant conclusion or something else allegedly just as bad, such as a sadistic conclusion or a reverse repugnant conclusion. Nobody in my experience is ever moved by these chains, which is itself part of what I want explained.

My own hypothesis is that the case intuitions are epiphenomenal and that two hidden commitments are doing all the work. The first is a measurement-theoretic one: totalism treats non-existence as a morally significant zero point on the well-being scale, so it requires well-being to be measurable on something like a ratio scale, whereas average-style views are invariant under translating or rescaling everyone's well-being and so need only interval structure. If the two camps are tacitly assuming different scale types for well-being, then their case judgments are not in conflict at all; they are evaluations of formally different objects. The second is a structural one: once you require the betterness relation to be transitive and to respect some dominance condition across disjoint subpopulations, the repugnant conclusion is largely forced, so the real debate is about which axiom to give up, and the A-to-Z chain is only a vivid way of exhibiting a theorem.

There is also a wrinkle I have not seen handled, and I would like your view on whether it is a genuine new front or a distraction. Work in a person-moment framework, where the objects of value are welfare-units indexed to times as well as persons, so that adding a person and extending an existing person's life are formally the same operation. Then the repugnant conclusion has a temporal twin: for a single fixed individual, an extremely long life each of whose days is only marginally worth living beats a short life of great happiness, and this follows from exactly the same additivity that generates the interpersonal version. Everyone I raise this with either bites the bullet in both cases or rejects both, but nobody has offered me a principled reason to treat the interpersonal and the intrapersonal versions differently, and several people have insisted that the temporal twin is obviously fine while the original is obviously not.

So: what kind of stalemate is this? Is it a verbal dispute conducted under a single label, a substantive but unargued difference in starting intuitions about the moral significance of the zero point, a disguised formal disagreement about transitivity, separability and the measurability of well-being, a case where different camps are choosing different objects of evaluation (ordering outcomes versus generating a permissible choice), or a sign that the question is ill-posed? Support the diagnosis with what the views actually commit themselves to rather than with a survey of positions, and say what would count as progress given your answer: settling the scale type of well-being, a new impossibility result, distinguishing or collapsing the interpersonal and intrapersonal versions, changing the question, or nothing at all. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and without restating the question.
