I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting a long feature for a magazine's ideas section on the politics of institutional reform — the recurring enthusiasm for tearing down, defunding, or disrupting inherited institutions, from police departments and university governance to marriage and the tenure system — and the older counter-tradition, running from Burke's suspicion of abstract reform to G. K. Chesterton's parable of the fence, which says that you may not remove an institution until you can say what it was put there to do. My editor wants the piece to have a literary spine rather than being another op-ed, and the obvious candidate is Chinua Achebe's Things Fall Apart, in which the arriving missionaries and the District Commissioner dismantle Igbo institutions — the oracle of the hills, the egwugwu, the exposure of twins in the evil forest — with almost no grasp of what any of them were for.

The problem is that the obvious reading is too easy, and I have been going in circles on a wrinkle I cannot settle by myself. On the standard Chestertonian telling, the missionaries are simply the confident reformers who clear the fence without understanding it, and the novel vindicates the fence. But I do not think the principle survives contact with the scene in which the overzealous convert Enoch tears the mask off one of the egwugwu during the ceremony of the ancestors. The authority of the masked ancestral spirits looks like exactly the sort of thing whose rationale cannot be stated without being destroyed: the institution does its work only as long as no one says, in public, that the ancestors are men in costume, and the reformer who does precisely what Chesterton asks — go away, find out what the fence is for, then come back and tell me — returns with an answer that is itself the demolition. I have started calling these self-concealing fences: institutions whose point is inarticulate because articulating it collapses the awe, deference, or taboo that does the actual work. I would like you to read the novel through that distinction and tell me whether it holds: where do the egwugwu scene and the surrounding crisis confirm that some fences are self-concealing, and where does the novel instead show a fence that was load-bearing in an ordinary, statable way — quarantine, dispute settlement, the provisioning of the clan — so that the reformers' failure was mere ignorance or cruelty rather than a paradox about inspection?

I also want the limits of the reading stated honestly, because I have no interest in conscripting Achebe into a Burkean argument he would have despised, and I would rather my editors hear the objections from me than from a reader. Two worries in particular. First, the novel is plainly not a defense of the status quo: Okonkwo's rigid traditionalism, the killing of Ikemefuna, the treatment of women and of the osu — the fiction indicts at least as much as it mourns, and I need to know where Chesterton's fence stops being a caution and starts being a licence to preserve indefensible practices. Second, I keep suspecting that Obierika, who questions the customs from inside the community and goes away to think rather than smashing anything, is the real Chestertonian figure in the book — the internal reformer as opposed to the external one — and that any defensible version of the principle has to be internalist in that way. Work from the arc of the plot and the major set-pieces rather than from lines: I am not after close verbal analysis and I have no wish to quote the book. What I want is a reading that takes both Burkean conservatism and the actual novel seriously, that says plainly where the novel resists the lens, and that ends by telling me whether Achebe gives us any resources for deciding which fences to keep and which to tear down.
