I'm auditing a criminal law course this term and writing a short paper on the necessity and duress defenses, and I keep circling back to a question the course materials treat as settled background but which looks to me like the actual philosophical puzzle. Here is the shape of the practice as I understand it; please correct me where I have it wrong. In many common-law systems a person who breaks the law to avoid a greater harm can raise necessity, sometimes called the choice-of-evils defense, but only if the harm avoided really was greater, only if there was no reasonable alternative, and — in the versions I have read — not at all as a defense to intentionally taking an innocent life. Duress works similarly: a complete defense to most offences, unavailable for murder, and judged by an objective test about whether a person of ordinary firmness would have resisted, rather than by the defendant's own terror. The famous Victorian lifeboat case fits the pattern exactly: shipwrecked sailors who killed and ate a dying cabin boy were convicted of murder and then had their death sentences commuted to a few months' imprisonment.

What puzzles me is that the institutions around the doctrine seem to say inconsistent things about one and the same act. Criminal law, in the jurisdictions that classify choice-of-evils as a justification rather than an excuse, declares that the defendant did the right thing; yet in tort the same person is often treated as privileged but not immune — the classic illustration being a crew that lashed its ship to a stranger's dock to ride out a storm, was held to have acted reasonably, and was still made to pay for the dock it crushed. And the executive then remits the sentence the jury was obliged to impose. My friend says the objective firmness test settles the matter: the law must be presupposing that apparent dilemmas are never genuine, that there is always a right action available and the defendant either found it or failed to. I read it the other way round: compensation and clemency look like devices for registering a residue — doing the best available thing and still owing something — which is roughly what philosophers mean by a genuine dilemma. Maybe we are both wrong and the practice is simply underdetermined.

So here is what I want: a reconstruction of the implicit theory of this cluster of practices, carefully done. What do they strictly presuppose about the possibility of genuine moral dilemmas, as opposed to what they merely fit comfortably or grew up alongside? Are the criminal, tort, and clemency components consistent with one another, or do they embody different theories of value — note that the proportionality requirement seems to presuppose that the competing harms are commensurable on one scale, while the unavailability of the defense for homicide seems to presuppose that some things cannot be traded off at all, and I want to know whether those two commitments can be held together or whether the doctrine just contains two theories side by side. Where is the practice underdetermined, so that a dilemma-denying view, a genuine-dilemma-with-residue view, and something like a moral-luck or purely expressive view would each license it? And what would a legal system that took genuine dilemmas seriously actually have to change — a third verdict, mandatory compensation, abolition of the objective test, something else? Please mark how confident you are in each part of the reconstruction, tell me which single consideration would most change your reading, and say where your argument is most likely to fail, including whether any of it depends on my possibly garbled description of the doctrine.
