I work in bid support for a company that sells water treatment gear, and part of my job is filling out these gift and hospitality registers every time somebody buys a client lunch or sends a box of dates at New Year, so I end up arguing about where relationship building stops and bribery starts way more than a normal person should. A friend of mine -- an engineer, grew up in a provincial capital, moved here as a teenager -- thinks the whole way my industry talks about corruption is upside down, and we had a long arguement about it last weekend that neither of us won, mostly because I kept falling back on a bribe is a bribe and he kept falling back on it's just cultural, both of which are conversation-enders. So I want to see the argument done properly by two people who actually know what they're talking about.

Here's the setup I want you to use. Picture a made-up country -- call it Talmar -- and a district roads office in a poor northern province. The office keeps two ledgers. The first is the official one: contracts, amounts, who won the bid and why. The second is kept in a drawer that anyone in the district can ask to see, and it records what each household and each small firm has put into the office over the years -- a bag of cement, cash toward a funeral, a share of a harvest, labour on a drainage ditch -- and on the facing page what the office has done for them: whose culvert got fixed first after the floods, who got driven to the regional hospital, who got a letter that unstuck a land dispute. None of it goes home with the engineers; it stays with the office. And in the part of the record people will swear to, contributions don't buy you the contract -- bids really are compared -- they buy you a claim on help when something goes wrong. There is no welfare state worth speaking of in this province, the engineers' salaries arrive late and sometimes don't arrive, and the second ledger is, for a lot of families, the only thing resembling insurance that exists.

What I'd like is a dialogue between two people arguing about whether this arrangement is corruption, and both of them should be good at it -- no lazy lines, no winning by slogan. Call them Nadia and Tomas. Nadia holds roughly the view I was raised on: public office is a trust, the trust is to be exercised impartially, and what makes something corrupt is that official judgement or official help is being traded for advantage, whoever ends up with the money and however openly it's done. Tomas holds that the twin ledger isn't a market in favours at all -- it functions like a tax-and-transfer system or a mutual insurance pool, it's public rather than secret, the obligations it creates run toward the people who are owed them rather than away, and calling it corruption just measures a place with no welfare state against a picture of an impersonal modern bureaucracy that barely exists anywhere. Somewhere in the middle I want them to genuinely disagree about whether the fact that the second ledger is public helps his case or makes it worse. And I want each of them forced to run their own standard over a case that comes out against their own side: Nadia has to deal with a donor-funded programme that pays officials several hundred dollars a day in sitting allowances for attending workshops -- entirely legal, fully disclosed, no rule broken -- and that slowly empties the provincial office of anyone competent; she has to say whether that's corruption or merely bad policy, and if it's merely bad policy, whether her defintion has missed something that Tomas's catches. Tomas has to deal with the family with nothing to put in the ledger, whose culvert is fixed last every single year; he has to say whether his account can call that corrupt or only tragic. They should each concede at least one real point, respond to what the other one actually said rather than to a flattened version of it, and end with the disagreement narrower than where it started but not settled -- I'd like the last line to be a question one of them asks that neither of them can answer yet.

Sections: The setup (about 100 words -- who these two are, where they're talking, and why they're at it), The argument (900 to 1,200 words of actual dialogue), The hinge (about 200 words on what the whole thing really turns on once the noise is stripped away), What I take home (about 120 words -- which piece of this I'd actually use the next time it comes up at work). No footnotes, no citations, no invented statistics, and please don't have either speaker name a real law, a real organisation or a real author, because I want to be able to read the whole thing out loud to my friend without stopping to explain anything.
