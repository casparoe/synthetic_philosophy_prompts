I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, finishing a dissertation chapter on Moore's paradox — specifically on the asymmetry between its omissive form (p and I do not believe that p) and its commissive form (p and I believe that not-p), and on whether the absurdity is best located in the norms governing assertion or in something constitutive about first-personal self-knowledge. I'd like the chapter to open with a short poem instead of a formal statement of the paradox, and I'd be grateful for your help writing it.

The piece I have in mind: five quatrains, loose iambic, unrhymed or very lightly rhymed. The first four quatrains each take a variant of the pattern that is *not* absurd, varying only the grammatical parameters — the third-person present, the first-person past, an embedded or hypothetical variant, and then the commissive case, where a speaker who concedes that the evidence points to not-p finds that she nonetheless believes p, and says so, awkwardly but without absurdity. The fifth quatrain is the omissive form, first person, present tense, and it should end with a single line that is a Moorean sentence. Keep the whole thing under about 24 lines.

Some constraints, which I hope do philosophical work rather than mere decoration:

- No theoretical vocabulary anywhere in the verse: no paradox, absurd, contradiction, Moore, transparency, self-knowledge, assertion, norm. The effect has to come from grammar, person, and tense.
- The speaker must not be explained away. No dream, no drunkenness, no madness, no irony, no self-deception, no meta-fictional wink at the reader. The four safe stanzas should make the fifth fail on its own.
- The final line should be one a reader can see could be *true* — the weather is real, the failure to believe it is real — while being something the speaker cannot say. I want the poem to turn on that gap between the truth of the conjunction and its unavailability to the speaker, not to collapse it into the claim that the state of affairs is impossible.
- The commissive stanza must not do the same work as the last one. It should be genuinely utterable, and the reader should be able to feel that it is a different kind of difficulty, if it is one at all.

After the poem, please add a short prose note (under 200 words): which diagnosis of the absurdity the poem commits you to, and where it takes liberties or leans on a contested reading. And please push back rather than comply politely if I've got something wrong — if the asymmetry as I've described it is not the one the literature supports, if the omissive form isn't the hard case, or if what I've asked for can't be done (say, because a poem cannot make an unsayable line felt except by staging a speaker who does say it). I'd much rather hear that than receive a graceful failure.

Close the note with one final line, beginning "Commitment:", naming in a few words the reading the poem commits to.
