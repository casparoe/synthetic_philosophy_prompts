I should say at the outset how I came to this, because I suspect it biases me. I spent thirty-one years as a comparative physiologist — thermal tolerance in small desert mammals, mostly, which is a roundabout way of saying that I spent my career measuring how far a still-functioning regulatory system can be pushed before its control loops fail — and on retiring I joined a philosophy reading group largely for the arguments. That has turned into a small unpaid appointment: I am now the scientific member of the Condition Review Panel of a regional health system. We meet monthly, eight of us (three clinicians, a health economist, a lawyer, two patient representatives and me), and we work through a docket of eight to twelve petitions asking that some state be entered on the Schedule of Covered Conditions, or struck from it. Listing is not cosmetic: it controls reimbursement, eligibility for workplace accommodation, inclusion in screening programs, and, less tangibly, whether people are entitled to say they are ill. Our enabling regulation tells us to list a state only if it is, and here I quote our own rule, "a departure from normal functioning that a reasonable person would want treated" — a sentence which, as the reading group has convinced me, quietly bundles together the two positions that the philosophy of medicine keeps apart: the naturalist one, which tries to define disease in terms of statistical departure from species-typical functional organization, and the normativist one, which holds that the concept is irreducibly evaluative, together with the various hybrid attempts to split a factual dysfunction component from a value-laden harm component and assign each to a different stage of the analysis.

What I need from you is not more theory but something we can actually run. Please give my panel a decision procedure: an ordered sequence of gates, each with a question, a statement of what would count as a sufficient answer at that gate given the evidence we can realistically assemble, an instruction about where the sequence stops with a no, and — this is the part I care most about — an explicit handoff point where the remaining question is one of values rather than fact and must be recorded and voted on as such, by named people, so that it can be reopened later without pretending we had evidence we did not have. The operating constraints matter and are not negotiable: we receive a one-page dossier per petition, we have about twenty minutes of discussion per item, none of my colleagues has any philosophy, and every decision must be written up in a single paragraph of reasons that a reviewer can audit in three years' time. We already maintain four verdicts, which you may use, replace or subdivide as the theory recommends: disease; risk factor; variation requiring no listing; and a provisional listing with a mandatory sunset review, which we invented ourselves and which I suspect is doing more work than we admit. The docket also contains petitions from two directions, applications to add and applications to remove, and I would like the procedure to treat delisting as a genuinely different exercise rather than as listing run backwards.

Then please run the procedure on three petitions from a recent docket, showing the reasoning at each gate rather than just the verdict. The first is severe sarcopenia in an eighty-five-year-old, which is close to statistically typical for that age and yet is disabling in a way nobody disputes. The second is asymptomatic nocturnal oxygen desaturation, detected only by continuous home monitoring in a forty-thousand-person observational substudy, where most of the people flagged will never have a clinical event but a minority will have a serious one. The third is a condition listed eleven years ago at the urging of a patient organization, defined entirely by a symptom cluster and a cut-point on a questionnaire, with no mechanism ever identified and no biomarker: I will not tell you which way I think it should go, and I would particularly like you to work this one through even if — perhaps especially if — the procedure returns an answer that would be unpopular with the petitioners, with my colleagues, or with me. Finally, two things I would like you to be blunt about. First, flag the places where my own instinct is doing the work: I am strongly drawn to the thought that the biology ought to settle these questions and that the evaluative part is where rigor goes to die, and I would rather be told where that instinct is distorting the procedure than be flattered in it. Second, say what the procedure cannot do — which comparisons it cannot make for us, which costs it cannot price, and which of our recurring disputes it will simply re-describe rather than resolve. I would be grateful for an answer in American English.
