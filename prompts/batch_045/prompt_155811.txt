I'm on the steering committee of a multi-institution data-sharing consortium that is deciding whether to fund an auditing-and-sanctions mechanism. A colleague argued against it with the line "nice guys finish first": in the iterated prisoner's dilemma, he said, reciprocity beats suspicion, so goodwill will do the work that enforcement would. I'd like that slogan disambiguated rather than debunked.

Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, with a verdict on each: trivial, substantive but conditional on background assumptions, or false. I'd like separate treatment of three ambiguities. First, "nice": the technical sense of never being the first to defect, versus the ordinary sense of being forgiving and slow to retaliate, and whether the strategies that do well are nice in both senses or only in the first. Second, "finish first": highest average payoff against one particular field of entrants, largest share of the population in a replicator dynamic, hardest to invade once common, or highest expected payoff for a single agent choosing a strategy against a population that does not change. Third, the scope conditions each reading quietly presupposes: a long enough shadow of the future, low rates of noise or of misread signals, enough reciprocators already present, and enough assortment or memory of past play that cooperators disproportionately meet one another — including whether a given reading is even meant to cover the one-shot case, where defection dominates.

Then say which readings, if any, would support my colleague's conclusion about skipping enforcement, which would instead recommend building the mechanism, and which reading people who repeat the slogan are usually reaching for.
