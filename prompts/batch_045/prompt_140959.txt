I am giving the toast at my parents' fiftieth wedding anniversary, which my father has insisted on holding in the common room of the memory-care unit where my mother now lives. She has advanced Alzheimer's; she will be in the room, will follow almost none of the syntax, and will take in the tone and a handful of words. She spent her career defending a narrativist view of the self — a person is a center of narrative gravity, an abstraction more like a center of mass than a further object — with a twist of her own that she never published: a self, she liked to say, persists the way a footpath persists, by being retraced, and when no one retraces it nothing dramatic happens, it simply stops being there. Her twist now has consequences I have to live with, because in the last year she has begun telling a version of her wedding day that is wrong about nearly everything — a different church, a different dress, a sister who died two years before it — and she tells it with more warmth than she has shown about anything else in years; and my father, who wants to be told that she is the same woman he married, and her former graduate students, who are half the room and will be listening for whether I have got any of this right, are both waiting for me to say something. Write the four-minute toast: it should decide what to do with her false version of that day — repeat it, correct it, or refuse it — and it has to answer, for a room that includes the woman herself and people who disagree about the answer, whether the person being toasted is the person who was married fifty years ago, and if she is not, what exactly the toast is toasting. It may not say that she is still in there, may not say that she is gone, may not lean on a soul or an afterlife, and may not tell this particular audience that the self is a story, since the relatives will hear that as the claim that she is fictional.
