I volunteer as a senior moderator on a large peer-advice forum, where members post practical questions about tenancy, consumer rights, small-claims procedure, benefits and employment, and other ordinary members answer them. No professionals, no verification step. I help train and oversee a team of about forty volunteer arbitrators who between them rule on roughly forty reported answers a week. The rulings are made from the text of a thread, usually on a phone, usually within fifteen minutes, by people with no relevant training. We cannot interview the poster, who frequently never replies, and we can never see inside anyone's head — only what they wrote. Two arbitrators given the same answer should reach the same verdict, or the team loses credibility. The only actions available to us are: leave the answer; require the poster to add a caveat; hide the answer; hide it and put a warning on the account; or escalate to the two paid community managers. People act on these answers, and a wrong ruling either emboldens confident guessers or drives away exactly the careful members we want to keep, so the stakes are real but bounded.

Our rule, inherited from the site's founder, reads: don't state as fact anything you don't know. Nobody has ever defined it, and an argument in the moderators' channel convinced me that the disagreement is a philosophical one that I cannot settle. Reading about it on my own (I have no academic background; I have been going by encyclopedia entries and arguments online) I take the candidates to be: that stating-as-fact is governed by knowledge; or by truth; or by belief, or sincere belief; or by having adequate evidence or justification; or by being reasonable or responsible in the circumstances. Each of these gives different verdicts on the same answer, and I do not know which one our rule should be read as.

So, two things.

First: recommend one. Tell me which of those the rule should be understood as codifying, given what the rule is for — answers that get acted on by people who cannot check them, evidence limited to the text, and a community we want to keep. I do not want a survey of the options with their strengths; I want a recommendation, the reason it beats the others in our setting, and an honest note about what we lose by choosing it. Please do not tell me which philosophers hold which view or point me towards things to read; I cannot chase references and it would not settle anything for us.

Second: turn it into a procedure an arbitrator can actually run, in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, where it stops early with a no violation, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. It has to be runnable in fifteen minutes from a phone, by a volunteer who has never heard any of this, and reproducible. Assume the poster's actual mental state is never available: say which proxies in the text do the work (basis tags, specificity, whether a source is given, whether the claim is about the poster's own case or a general rule, whether it is checkable), and be explicit about how much weight each carries. Tell me which distinctions from the philosophical argument have to survive the simplification and which we can drop without the procedure breaking.

There are four features of our forum that I have not seen discussed and that the procedure has to handle:

1. Answers are almost never edited and stay up for years, and they get re-served to new readers through already-answered links. Must the standard be met continuously, or only at the moment of posting? If a claim was fine in 2016 and is misleading now, is the failure the poster's or ours, and does our action set even fit that kind of failure?

2. Some answers are posted from shared accounts: a small advice charity with three staff on one login, or two family members sharing one. Whose knowledge or evidence has to stand behind the claim? Is there a group version of the rule, and does the verdict change when no individual member of the group qualifies?

3. A large share of posts are written in another language and machine-translated, which flattens hedges into flat declarations and sometimes does the reverse. We often cannot tell whether something was offered as a fact or as a guess. The procedure needs a step zero for whether this is even a claim being stated as fact rather than a suggestion, a guess, a hypothetical or a hedged remark, and it needs to say what to do when the text is genuinely ambiguous, including who bears the cost of that ambiguity.

4. We require a basis tag (personal experience, training, read it somewhere, worked it out), but members leave it blank or tick the wrong one. Say whether the tag does normative work or is merely evidence, and whether a blank tag is itself a violation.

Then apply the procedure to three cases. Take the facts as I state them; I am not asking you to check any law.

Case 1. A member with a blank basis tag answered a question about an old debt with a flat sentence: your landlord has six years to chase this, so don't panic. Nothing else, no source. Assume the six-year figure is correct. Nothing was reported at the time. In an unrelated thread a year later, the same member wrote: I posted that from memory, I wasn't certain, it just sounded right. The old answer has now been reported on the strength of that admission.

Case 2. An answer posted in 2017 states that tribunals award costs in cases like the reader's about nine times in ten, and links a spreadsheet of 180 cases from one region between 2010 and 2014 showing 88 per cent. The basis tag says read it somewhere. The poster has no expertise. Assume the figure was accurate for that period and region and that the poster neither knew nor had any reason to suspect any change. The answer is still live, gets around two hundred views a month through already-answered links, and the relevant procedural rules changed in 2021 so that the true rate is now nearer one in two.

Case 3. A post from a shared account used by two staff at a small advice charity, machine-translated, reads: the landlord is not permitted to do this and you should refuse. A bilingual member later pasted the original-language version into the thread, which reads more like: I don't think they're allowed to do that, but I'm not sure, please check. A reader refused and lost a month's rent. Assume the claim was in fact false.

I expect case 1 to come out as leave — the answer was right and nobody was misled — and I also expect case 2 to come out as leave, because the evidence there is better than in almost anything else I see. If your procedure says either of those is a violation, say so plainly and tell me whether the right response is to accept the verdict or to change the rule instead.

Finally: mark the points at which judgement cannot be eliminated and say why, and be honest about where the procedure will misfire in use — which patterns it will systematically over-penalise or under-penalise, and what mitigation you would build in.

After the procedure and the cases, give me a structured summary as a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

recommended_norm (one sentence, in plain words, as I should word it in the guidance note);
rejected_alternatives (array of objects, each with: alternative — which of the candidate standards it is; decisive_objection — why it fails for our setting specifically);
procedure (array of objects, one per step, each with: step (integer), question (what the arbitrator asks), evidence (what in the text counts towards an answer), if_met (what happens next), if_not_met (what happens next), terminal (true if the procedure stops here with a verdict), maps_to_action (one of: leave, require caveat, hide, hide and warn, escalate, none));
judgement_points (array of objects, each with: step, why_not_proceduralisable, who_decides, what_values_are_in_play);
worked_cases (array of objects, one per case, each with: case (1, 2 or 3), assertion_identified (whether step zero finds a claim stated as fact, and why), step_reached, verdict (one of: leave, require caveat, hide, hide and warn, escalate), confidence (high, medium or low), second_moderator_agreement (high, medium or low), one_line_reason);
verdicts_i_will_dislike (array of objects, each with: case, verdict, expected_verdict, recommendation (accept the verdict or change the rule, with a one-sentence reason));
known_misfires (array of objects, each with: pattern, direction (over-penalises or under-penalises), mitigation);
unresolved (array of strings: anything the procedure cannot settle).

Use British English throughout, and keep the prose tight enough that I can turn it into a one-page guidance note for the team.
