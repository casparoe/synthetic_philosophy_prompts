Hi — I'm the lead on a four-person data team at a regional hospital network. We run the merge layer that stitches patient charts together from four source systems (two legacy EHRs, a lab system, and a hand-entered intake tool) into the single chart clinicians actually read. We can't change the source systems; we only decide what the merged chart says and what gets shown.

Our problem is contradictions. Maybe forty a day survive automated matching: a penicillin allergy in the old system and no known allergies in the new one; a medication that's active and discontinued as of the same date; two dates of birth; a consent flag that's yes in one place and no in another; a patient discharged from one service and admitted to another on the same day. Our rulebook right now is basically: take the more recent entry, unless the older one came from a clinician, in which case escalate — plus a hand-written per-field safety rule for about thirty fields (for allergies we keep the more alarming value, for everything else we take the newest). It's forty pages long and nobody trusts it. On top of that, compliance won't let us destroy anything: every conflicting value has to stay auditable, so "resolve it by dropping the loser" isn't available as a data operation, only as a display decision.

I've been reading about paraconsistent logic and dialetheism and it feels like it's about exactly this — a logic where a contradiction doesn't let you derive everything, and the further claim that some contradictions are actually true — but I can't turn any of it into something my team can run. So: write me a decision procedure we can actually use.

What I need:

- The questions to ask in order, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each. We've got maybe three to five minutes per flag and the team are data stewards, not logicians, so it has to be a one-page thing for the ops wiki plus a laminated card.
- Where the procedure stops with a no and refuses to auto-merge, and where it hands off to a human because the question is one of values rather than facts — our risk tolerance for a missed allergy is not the same as for a false one, and somebody has to own that choice explicitly rather than have it buried in a per-field rule.
- A blast radius rule. When a field is in quarantine, which downstream values have to carry a marker saying they were computed from something contested, and when can we drop the marker? This is the bit I most expect you to get wrong or fudge, and it's the bit the forty-page rulebook is really about.
- It has to work for someone who thinks a contradiction is always a sign of an error somewhere. Our compliance officer thinks the whole idea of accepting both values is mad, and I need something that doesn't depend on settling that. So: don't assume any contradiction is really true, but do say plainly whether there's a case where the dialetheist-sounding thing — both entries correct under two different rulebooks — actually shows up, and if it does, whether it should change what we do differently from the plain "we don't know which" case.
- Two distinctions I think must survive the simplification: the one between an apparent contradiction that dissolves once you fix the time-slice, the granularity or the sense of the predicate (including the case where the two values are really contraries that could both be false rather than contradictories), and a real one; and the one between "we don't know which side is right" and "both sides are right." Tell me which distinctions you're deliberately throwing away, and why.
- Where it's honest about being a judgment call dressed up as a procedure.

Then run it on three real-shaped cases: (1) penicillin allergy documented in a 2011 intake note as a childhood rash, a 2019 allergy clinic test saying there's no penicillin allergy, and no known allergies entered by a resident in 2023; (2) an anticoagulant that's active in the medication list with a refill yesterday but discontinued because of bleeding in a discharge summary three days ago, with no reconciliation note; (3) a consent field where the patient signed a treatment consent and separately declined research use of their data, and the intake tool has a single yes/no box that got filled in twice with different values. I have a feeling your procedure is going to tell me to do something on the second one that my team will absolutely hate — say it anyway, and say what it costs.

Finally: give me four or five one-line test cases I can use to check whether a new hire has actually understood the procedure, and tell me what the procedure can't do — the failures I should expect in six months that won't look like failures at first.
