So my old roommate Dana has been running this thread on a forum for like four years where people mail her variants of the experience machine and she gives a straight yes-or-no verdict plus one line of reasoning. She's not an academic, she's a nurse practitioner, but she's weirdly consistant and the thread has gotten long enough that people now send her cases specifically designed to break her. I finally worked up the nerve to write my own and I want to know what she'll say before I post it, because if I can predict her answer I think I understand what she's actually doing and if I can't then I don't. Here's what I remember of her track record: the original machine — plug in for life, preprogrammed bliss, told about it in advance, memory of choosing wiped — she says no, "nothing that happens in there is anything." Two weeks in a tank while fully knowing it's a simulation, memories kept afterward, back to your real job on Monday: yes, "that's just a vacation, obviously." A year of the same setup where you live as a concert pianist and everyone else inside is a very good NPC with no inner life at all: yes, though she added something like "as long as it isn't the main thing going on in your life." A pill that changes nothing you do — you keep your modest real job, your real family, your real struggles — but makes you feel the deep satisfaction of real achievement from all of it: yes, no hesitation. The ghostwriter case — you can't write, a machine writes brilliant novels under your name, millions of actual readers are genuinely moved, and you know the whole time you didn't do it: no. The Truman case — your life is full of real events, but your friends are actors and your promotions were arranged by a production company, and you never find out: no, and it's the most emphatic no she's ever given, way more emphatic than the original machine. And a slow-degradation case where the simulation imperceptibly gets dumber year by year so you never notice: no.

Here's mine. Call it the Settlement: it's a shared simulation, a whole frontier town. Everyone you actually love has agreed to come in with you and they all know it's a simulation; you can walk out on any day and the outside world keeps going without you and you can send messages back if you want. Inside, your choices genuinely matter to the other inhabitants: some of them are the real human minds who came in with you, and some are natives, artificial inhabitants of the sim whose inner lives are as rich as anyone's and who can really suffer and really flourish depending on what you do. You can genuinely fail at things, get hurt, build something over decades, be loved and betrayed. Nothing you do inside has any effect on the outside world apart from how it makes the people who love you feel. You'd be going in for the rest of your natural life. So: what does Dana say, and what's the rule she's been running that makes her say it? I can think of like four different principles that fit every verdict above — something about deception, something about whether you author your own life, something about achievements and relationships having to be real ones, something about staying in causal contact with the actual world, maybe something about dose — and I'm pretty sure at least two of them come apart on the Settlement, I just can't tell which of them is really hers. Tell me what each candidate predicts here, give me your best guess with the reason for preferring it, and then tell me which single further variant I should send her to settle it, since I can only really post one follow-up without looking like a weirdo. If you think her old verdicts genuinely underdetermine the answer either way, say so and tell me what she'd be committing to with each choice.
