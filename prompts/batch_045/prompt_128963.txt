ok so I've been going back and forth on causation by omission for weeks and I think the only way out is to write down my actual gut verdicts on a pile of cases and see whether anything fits them. Please take the verdicts as given and don't try to talk me out of them until you've found the principle.

1. The greenhouse assistant's one job is to water the seedlings. He doesn't. They die. He caused their death. Totally unhesitating yes.

2. A woman walking past the greenhouse could easily have stepped in, knew the seedlings would die otherwise, doesn't. I want to say she did NOT cause their death — and the counterfactual is exactly the same as in 1, so I don't get to lean on that.

3. I forget to water my own plant, but unknown to me the roots had already rotted and it was dying that week either way. Not a cause.

4. Two people are each independently assigned to water the seedlings. Both forget. Either would have done it alone. I want to say both caused the death, but it feels thinner than 1. And if I scale it to a hundred people with the same assignment, all of whom forget, I suddenly want to say none of them caused it and it was just "the group," which on reflection seems insane. Maybe it isn't.

5. Night guard doesn't lock the back door. A thief walks in and takes the thing. The not-locking caused the theft. Yes.

6. Night guard doesn't leave his post. Because he's there, he sees the thief, and the thief gets caught. I refuse to say his not-leaving caused the thief to be caught. That's just the background. But every reason I can produce for why 5 and 6 differ feels like it assumes the conclusion.

7. The irrigation controller doesn't open the valve because its clock battery died. I'm happy to say the not-opening caused the plants to die. But: the deer get into the garden because there was never a fence. I half want to say the absence of the fence caused it and half don't, and the difference between these two can't just be that one of them has a battery in it.

8. I don't send $500 to a charity that would very reliably have saved a kid's life. I want to say I didn't cause that kid's death. But structurally this is case 1: I could, I knew, nobody else was going to, no real cost to me. This is the pair that actually keeps me up, and I'd honestly rather have a principle that tells me I'm wrong here than one that gerrymanders a distinction between them.

9. I don't call an ambulance because I'm having a seizure. Not a cause, and I don't even want to call it an omission. But if I don't call because I reasonably assumed someone else already had, I lean toward saying it IS a cause, just a mitigated one — and I can't tell whether "reasonably" is doing real work or whether I'm smuggling in blame.

10. A company never builds the review process that would have caught a bad update; the update ships; someone is hurt. I want to say the not-catching caused the harm, but it feels like a different animal from 1: a standing omission with no particular moment, as opposed to a one-off.

Here's the half-baked thing I've been toying with, which I'm calling the closure rule. An omission causes O only if: (a) had the action occurred, O wouldn't have; (b) in the actual world there was a live set-up — a role, plan, or mechanism positioned to produce the contrary event — with a window in which it could still have fired, and the causation happens when that window closes, not at the time of the non-action; (c) the route from the omission to O doesn't run through another absence. I like that it dates omissions to a moment that isn't the moment of not-acting, and I like that (c) kills 6. But I can't tell whether (b) really excludes the passerby in 2 or whether it just redescribes my prejudice, and I think (c) might also kill 7 and 10, which I don't want.

So: repair it or replace it. Give me one or two candidate principles stated precisely enough that I could apply them myself, and for each clause tell me which case forced you to include it. Then give me verdicts on three cases I haven't listed, at least one of which the principle answers in a way I'd find surprising. Be honest about which of my verdicts your preferred principle can't accommodate and say which one you'd overturn and why. And please don't give me citations, a reading list, or "the standard view holds that" as a substitute for an argument — if the honest answer is that causation is just counterfactual dependence and everything else is pragmatics, then say which verdicts I lose and whether the pragmatic story can really carry the weight of the 1-versus-8 pair.
