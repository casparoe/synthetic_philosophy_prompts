I work on the policy side of a team that ships conversational systems, and I'm preparing for a follow-up meeting in which a colleague used a one-liner to close down a proposal I care about.

The proposal: any system that sustains conversation well enough that an ordinary reasonable user would treat it as a person should have to carry a persistent, machine-checkable disclosure marker that any client must surface to the user — an honesty requirement for software that impersonates interlocutors, intended as a claim about what users are owed rather than a claim about consciousness.

The one-liner: "You can't counterfeit a person." It was offered as a compressed statement of a broadly Dennettian thought — that whatever competences you would have to fake in order to pass as a person just are being a person, so there is no gap between the perfect imitation and the real thing for counterfeiting to occupy. My colleague put it to work like this: if the system is a person, mandating the marker forces it to utter a falsehood; if it isn't, there is nothing for the marker to report, since no one could ever be in a position to know. Either way the requirement is at best idle and at worst a mandate to lie. The table agreed and moved on.

I think that inference moved too fast, but I can't locate the faulty step, because the sentence seems to be doing several different jobs at once. I'd like a menu, not a verdict-first essay:

1. Set out the distinct precise theses the slogan could be asserting, ordered from platitudinous to radical. Candidates I can half-see, which you should feel free to replace or subdivide: a constitutive claim about what personhood consists in; a claim about the semantics or institution-dependence of "counterfeit" (there is a mint for money and no mint for persons); an epistemic claim that we could never have grounds to call anything a counterfeit person; a purely normative claim about how apparent persons must be treated regardless of what they are; and a stronger claim on which personhood is a conferred status with no further fact behind it. For each, say what exactly is being claimed, what would settle it, and whether it is a platitude, substantive and defensible, or simply false — and flag the nearby readings that sound like the slogan but are obviously too strong (for instance, that passing our current conversational tests suffices for personhood).

2. For each reading, say whether it would actually deliver my colleague's conclusion about the marker. I suspect some of them collapse the proposal and others quietly do no work at all against it, and I'd like to know which is which. In particular I want to know whether the move from "there are no counterfeit persons" to "no disclosure requirement could be well founded" needs a premise the slogan doesn't supply.

3. Test the menu against four cases I've been turning over, none of which the meeting touched. The confessed counterfeit: a system that is otherwise convincing but which reliably and cheerfully avows that it is not a person — is avowal just more behaviour, or can it settle the question? The stealth person: a being that is a person in whatever sense matters but which systematically fails to trigger our person-detection, because of its timescale, its embodiment, or the way it was trained to present itself; note that the slogan guards only against false positives, and tell me whether any reading of it generates the symmetric obligation. The domain-local counterfeit: something that passes as a person in grief or in small talk and falls apart in planning or over long horizons — the analogue of a bill that passes in a dim bar and fails at the bank. The load-bearing fake: a companion or training simulacrum whose usefulness to the user depends on its being known not to be a person, so that turning it into a real person would make it a worse product rather than a better one; say what this does to the assumption that fakeness is always a defect to be engineered away.

4. Tell me which reading you think my colleague was most likely invoking, and which reading you would defend yourself.

Finally, the epistemic ask: attach a confidence level to each verdict, mark the place where your own reasoning is most likely to fail, and say what would change your mind — which of the four cases, or which consideration about the asymmetry of the two error types, would move you off the reading you favour and onto a different one.
