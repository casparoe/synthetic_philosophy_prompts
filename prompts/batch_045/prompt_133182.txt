In a philosophy-of-biology reading group I convene, one member defends the adaptationist programme with a single analogy, which he insists is methodological rather than metaphysical: an organism is like a machine built to a specification, and a trait is to the organism as a machined part is to the engine. He draws two conclusions from it. First, that for any trait we encounter the productive research question is what it is for. Second, that our inability to produce an adaptive story for a trait is a limit on our ingenuity rather than evidence about the trait. He adds that Gould and Lewontin's 1979 spandrels critique at most shows that some traits are currently idle, which on his view is a finding made from inside the design stance, not a reason to step outside it.

I want to audit the analogy on cases built to be harder than the standard ones (the eye and the camera, the heart and the pump). Consider three traits in one hypothetical lineage of burrowing vertebrates, labelled neutrally as T1, T2 and T3. T1 is a bony flange on the skull that arose as a geometric consequence of a change in the growth allometry of the braincase and now anchors the muscle that does most of the work in a digging stroke, such that knocking it out seriously reduces burrowing efficiency. T2 is a flank pigment patch that currently deters predators, but whose pigment was fixed by selection for heat tolerance, the deterrent effect having been invisible to predators in the ancestral light environment. T3 is a set of cranial ridges whose number and shape are fixed by a constraint of the lineage's developmental programme, which vary across valleys in a pattern that tracks founder effects, and for which no fitness difference has been detected.

Give me the correspondence table for the machine analogy — what plays the designer, the specification, the part, the function, the tolerance, the defect, the prototype — and then where the mapping breaks. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the two conclusions above from those that are merely cosmetic, and say what the analogy quietly imports that the target lacks (foresight, a single coherent design brief, a clean function-versus-accident distinction, an external standard of good design, retention entailing present service). Tell me which of T1, T2 and T3 the analogy can represent at all, whether it can tell them apart or collapses them into one, and whether the claim that it is only a heuristic survives the fact that a heuristic determines which hypotheses get generated and when you stop looking for alternatives. Finally: if there is a better analogy — a retrofitted building, an undocumented legacy codebase, bricolage — sketch what it maps and what it in turn fails to capture about selection.
