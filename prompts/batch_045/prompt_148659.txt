I spent thirty-five years in observational cosmology and instrument design, and since retiring I have been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group at the local university — mostly as the person who is asked whether the physical claims in the paper are true. I have been invited to give one of the group's short talks on why I find a great deal of the realism debate about "interpretations" in quantum mechanics to be ill-posed, and while writing it up I found that I could not state my own position without appearing to contradict myself. A graduate student in the group told me that the combination I had written down is a known paradox — Fitch's paradox of knowability — and showed me the standard trick of substituting, for the arbitrary truth in the knowability principle, a sentence of the form "p and nobody ever knows that p." I have been trying ever since to work out whether my position survives that substitution, and I would rather find out now than in front of thirty people. Here, as carefully as I can state it, is what I hold. (1) A verificationist constraint on determinate truth: a declarative sentence has a determinate truth value only in so far as there could in principle be something that would count as settling whether it is true — I want this because disputes about which interpretation of the formalism is the "true" one look to me like disputes about nothing that could even in principle be adjudicated, and I would rather say they are indeterminate than mysteriously determinate. (2) The knowability principle: for every truth p, it is possible that some physically realizable agent knows that p, where the modality is nomological — same laws, but matter and time may be rearranged without limit. (3) Non-omniscience: plenty of truths will in fact never be known by anyone — how many neutrinos escaped a particular star in a particular year, say, all of whose traces have long since been thermalized — and I do not believe in, and would not like to be committed to, any single cosmic knower. (4) The bare logic of knowledge: it is factive, and knowledge of a conjunction yields knowledge of each conjunct; I am entirely willing to give up positive introspection (the KK principle) if that helps, since I never wanted it. (5) My first escape: read the modal operator in (2) as nomologically weak, refuse S4, and do not require the knowability principle itself to be knowable — my hope being that the collapse needs more modal machinery than I have granted. (6) My second escape, which I like better because it is physical: the finite bound on the entropy — and, on a common reading, the information content — of any bounded region (the Bekenstein bound and its holographic relatives) suggests that only finitely many distinct physically realizable states of a knower exist, whereas there are infinitely many truths; so not every truth can be known, and since the Fitch conclusion I was shown looked like it required one being who knows everything, it seems to be exactly what the finiteness of the world rules out. (7) My third escape, which I suspect is cheating: restrict the knowability principle to propositions whose truth is compatible with their being known, so that sentences like the substituted one simply fall outside its scope. (8) Two case verdicts I do not want to lose: that "the particle went through the left slit" is indeterminate rather than merely unknown in a run where nothing measured the position, and that the early universe had a perfectly determinate microstate at the Planck time, even though no possible observation could recover it.

What I would like from you is an audit rather than a survey. Tell me which of these commitments are genuinely in tension with which, and rank those tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad; tell me which of my three escapes actually works, and if none of them does, say precisely which premise is doing the fatal work and why I cannot simply drop it. Tell me also which of the apparent conflicts are not conflicts at all — I strongly suspect I have misidentified where the danger lies, particularly in (6), but I cannot see how. And for each real tension, say what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost me: I am most reluctant to give up (1), since it is the claim that does the philosophical work I actually care about, but I would rather be told that (1) has to go than be told something comforting.
