I host a podcast for working software engineers — mostly backend and distributed-systems people — where each episode takes one idea from physics or philosophy that gets mangled in tech discourse and tries to state it correctly in terms an engineer can hold onto. Next month's guest is a theoretical physicist who defends a specific package of claims about Bell's theorem, and I want the question list to press on the joints of that package rather than on pop-science talking points.

Her position, as she described it in pre-production, is roughly: Bell's result shows that no locally causal theory reproduces the singlet correlations, so local causality has to go; but what relativity actually requires is only signal locality — that no one can use these correlations to send a message — and that requirement survives untouched; and once you stop demanding a single observer-independent story about which event brought about which outcome, the leftover nonlocality is not the sort of thing that should alarm anyone, so the phrase spooky action at a distance is doing more harm than good.

Please draft eight to ten questions, sequenced so that each one leads naturally into the next. For every question, give three things: the question as I would actually say it aloud to her, one line on the answer she is most likely to give, and a follow-up written specifically against that answer rather than a generic "can you say more." No question should merely ask her to restate the package.

Include at least one question on each of the following.

1. The two senses of locality. My listeners hear nonlocal and immediately think "you could build a faster-than-light channel." I want a question that separates Bell's screening-off condition — roughly, that conditional on a complete specification of the shared past, the remote setting is irrelevant to the local outcome — from the engineer's notion of locality as "no shared memory, only message passing." Ask her whether the first is just a conditional-independence requirement of the kind we already use when reasoning about causal structure, and whether its failure is really best described as influence travelling.

2. Where the violation lands. Press her on the distinction between parameter independence and outcome independence: which one does the formalism give up, and why should that specific one be the one relativity does not care about? She will probably say the split settles the relativity question cleanly; I want the follow-up to ask whether that split is a discovery about the world or an artifact of how we write the theory, given that in a hidden-variable theory with a preferred foliation the violation seems to land on the other side of the divide.

3. My toy model. Here is what I want to put to her: a source emits a pair along with a random 256-bit seed, and Alice's and Bob's setting bits are computed as two different deterministic functions of that seed, so the settings and the hidden state share a cause sitting entirely in the intersection of the two past light cones. To me this breaks the statistical-independence assumption without any conspiracy — the dependence is built into the setup, not fine-tuned. Ask her whether this is what people mean by superdeterminism, or whether it is instead an ordinary common cause of a kind Bell's own formulation already allows, in which case it should fail to reproduce the correlations.

4. Why nonlocality is rationed. The local bound on the CHSH quantity is 2, quantum mechanics reaches about 2.83, and 4 is reachable in principle by correlations that still cannot be used for signalling. If the lesson is simply that the world is nonlocal, why is the nonlocality capped below its algebraic maximum, and is there a principle she would endorse that explains the cap rather than just describing it? Include a follow-up on whether the mere coherence of stronger-than-quantum no-signalling correlations should make us suspicious of treating nonlocality as the fundamental lesson.

5. What would disconfirm it. Close with one question asking what observation, experimental result, or theoretical development would lead her to abandon the package — not merely to add caveats to it — and what she would say to someone who thinks the package was constructed precisely so that no result could do that.

Finally: after each question, add a short host note stating how confident you are, as high, medium, or low, that the question targets a genuine pressure point in the actual debate rather than a confusion on my part, and naming any nontrivial technical claim the question depends on. Then single out the one item you are least confident about, and say what would settle it.
