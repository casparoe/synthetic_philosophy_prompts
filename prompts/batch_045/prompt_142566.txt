I'm retired now — I spent about thirty years as a geochemist, mostly isotope ratios, and somehow I've ended up in a philosophy reading group at the U3A, which is much more argumentative then I expected. Last week we did luck egalitarianism and two of the regulars, Bea and Marcus, went at each other for the better part of forty minutes while the rest of us just sat there. I've turned it over since and keep switching sides, so I want someone to tell me who was actually right about what.

Bea was defending it. Her version: the job of an egalitarian distribution is to cancel out the effects of brute luck while leaving people to bear the costs of the gambles they knowingly took (option luck, she called it), and you can cash out knowingly with a hypothetical insurance scheme — everyone choosing cover from behind a veil about their own endowments — so nobody gets compensated for cultivating a taste for vintage claret, but the person dealt a bad hand of talents does. Marcus came back with the regress: impulsivity, delay discounting, even how much sleep you got last night are heritable or environmental or both, so the bad gambles are downstream of brute luck as well, and the line does no work — either it collapses into straight equalising of outcomes or it just gets drawn wherever you happen to like it. Bea said he was asking the wrong question: not whether the trait was caused by luck (everything is), but whether at the moment of choosing the person had the capacity to weigh that risk and the choice expressed her own judgement about what was worth risking — otherwise you can't treat anyone as an agent and responsibility for choices evaporates entirely. Marcus said that move has two problems: fluid intelligence and stress load at the time of choice are brute luck too, so capacity just relocates the luck one step back; and worse, it commits you to a state that rummages through people's histories to work out whether their misfortune is their own fault before it will help them, which is basically the old objection from the relational/democratic-equality side about demeaning scrutiny and pity rather than respect. Bea said that's an objection to enforcement, not to the principle: run the whole thing through blunt, non-inquisitorial proxies — set tax and transfer at whatever level is optimal given the mix of causes, never adjudicate an individual case — and you keep the theory without the indignity. Marcus said that if you never ask whose fault anything is, you aren't doing luck egalitarianism any more, you're doing something else that borrowed the name; and anyway her hypothetical insurance has no unique premium, so her theory is every bit as indeterminate as the one she accuses him of.

At that point I stupidly put my hand up with something from my old field: we used to separate uncertainty you can price into a model (aleatoric) from uncertainty about whether the model itself is right (epistemic), and it struck me that the brute/option distinction is trying to be that same distinction applied to a life. Bea liked it; Marcus said it actually helps him, because as you push the second kind back you never reach a floor. Then Bea floated a test I haven't been able to let go of — she called it the re-run test: hold the person's traits fixed, re-run the decision a thousand times, and if she'd predictably take the risk again, count the outcome as option luck. Marcus said that test would cheerfully classify a relapsing addict or a parent wrecked with fatigue taking out a payday loan as authentic choice, i.e. it launders systematically distorted choosing into genuine choosing, and that if you were really serious you'd want to insure against the disposition to gamble badly rather than against the bad outcome, which no market could ever price. Then the library was closing and we had to leave. So: can you go through Bea's and Marcus's exchange move by move and tell me which specific points are right, which are mistaken, and what each of them is missing — including whether my aleatoric/epistemic analogy is doing any real work or is just a false friend? I don't need a verdict on who won; I want to know which bits of the argument are worth keeping.
