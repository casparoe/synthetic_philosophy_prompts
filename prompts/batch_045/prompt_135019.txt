ok this is going to sound dumb but it's been bugging me for two days. My flatmate has one of those open-world games with a physics engine, and the other night he spent about forty minutes herding a whole village's NPCs into a river and watching them drown; when I said it was kind of grim he gave me this argument: it's exactly like a novelist writing a sad or violent ending for a character. Nobody involved exists, so nobody is harmed, so there's nothing to feel bad about — the only thing that could even in principle be off is what it says about the person doing it, the way you'd side-eye someone who gets a kick out of writing really graphic torture scenes. And if it's fine for the novelist, he says, it's fine for him, since either way the character is just marks on a page or pixels with no welfare of its own.

I dont think that's it, but I can't say why, and I've got a reason to care beyond winning the argument: I'm slowly building a tiny narrative game in my spare time where the player can either rebuild a companion's trust or quietly break it, and I keep flip-flopping on whether the bad branch should actually hurt. So can you audit the analogy properly? Give me the correspondences explicitly — who plays the author, who the reader, what plays the plot, what plays the "it's already written" part — and then where the mapping stops tracking, and which of the disanalogies matter for the conclusion he's using it for. Some of what I keep tripping over:

- the baseline problem. In a novel there's one text and the fate is settled; in a game there's usually a branch where he just walks away, so the drowning looks like it makes them worse off relative to an alternative that literally exists in the code, which is the ordinary shape of harming someone. To deny that you have to say what the baseline is: the other branch, the branch the developer "intended," the state where he never boots the game at all, or is there just no fact of the matter? I went down the never-booting-it road and it started smelling like the non-identity problem, where the alternative to a miserable person is no person at all, and I don't trust my own reasoning there.
- wronging versus making things worse. Even if there's no subject to be wronged, you can still say the game-world where the village drowns is a worse artifact than the one where it doesn't, and that's a reason not to bring it about that isn't about anyone's welfare at all — closer to aesthetic or axiological than deontic. But then the same seems true of novels, since we do criticise gratuitous suffering in fiction, which would mean his analogy quietly undercuts his conclusion instead of supporting it. Is that right or am I sliding?
- the "what it says about you" escape hatch. He thinks that's the only worry left once the characters drop out. But the rehearsal-versus-release story cuts both ways — cruelty in a safe sandbox could be practice or it could be catharsis — and I want to know what would even settle it, and whether "reveals character" is doing real work or just moving the lump in the carpet.

One more thing I think the analogy hides: responsibility sits differently. The novelist is the only agent in town, whereas in the game there's the developer who built a village with names and grief animations and a physics engine that lets bodies float, and then the player who actually does it. Does the novel picture smuggle in a single-author structure that games just don't have, and does that change what the player's reasons are? And if the analogy can't carry "nothing to feel bad about," is there a better one — people have thrown the tabletop GM at me, and a zoo, and a lab animal, and I don't love any of them.
