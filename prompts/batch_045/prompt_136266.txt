I worked for fifteen years as a fire-safety engineer, and over the past year I have begun practising my religion seriously again. That has turned al-Ghazālī's occasionalism — roughly, that fire does not burn the cotton, that God creates the burning on the occasion of their contact, and that what we call a cause is only a habit in the divine decree — from a curiosity into something close to a personal difficulty: my entire career consisted of arranging matter so that fire would not spread, and on this picture it looks as though what I was really doing was placing myself inside a habit.

Two analogies are carrying the weight in how I have been thinking about it, and I would like both audited. The first is the one my study circle leans on, and the one people use to reassure someone in my position: that God's habit is just Hume's constant conjunction, so the laws of nature are only records of what has always followed what, and science and engineering lose nothing, because they never needed genuine causal powers. The second is one I came up with and distrust: that creation on the occasionalist picture is like a game engine rendering every frame from scratch, where the physics code is God's habit and the burning of cotton on contact with fire is a rule the renderer reliably follows.

For each, set out what maps onto what, then separate the disanalogies that matter for the reassuring conclusion from the ones that do not. Specifically: does the habit analogy quietly import something the occasionalist is not entitled to, given that the atomist backdrop has every atom re-created at each instant, so that there is nothing to endure and do the habit-ing; and does it turn the divine will into something bound by a law from outside, when the whole point was that it is not? Does the rendering analogy smuggle in a second cause in the form of the code that persists between frames? And can the very same constant-conjunction picture genuinely do opposite work in Hume and in al-Ghazālī, deflating causation in one and inflating the creator in the other, or must one of them give something up? Close by saying whether either analogy can actually carry the reassurance, and what a better one would be.
