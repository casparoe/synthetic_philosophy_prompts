I've got a paper from a student in my modality seminar that I keep trying to break and can't, and I'm at the point where I need someone who works on this to tell me I'm not crazy. It's short, it looks valid to me, and I think it proves way too much.

Roughly, in his words:

(1) The standard Fitch derivation — from "every truth is knowable" to "every truth is known" — uses nothing about knowledge except two formal features: factivity (if p is known, then p) and distribution over conjunction (if p & q is known, then p is known and q is known). And these hold of necessity, not just around here; you need that, since the argument applies them inside the scope of a possibility operator, but of course it's true of knowledge.

(2) Those two features are all the derivation needs, so it generalizes. For any operator O that is necessarily factive and distributes over conjunction, the principle that every truth is possibly-O'd — for every p, if p then possibly O(p) — already entails that every truth is O'd. Proof: suppose q is a truth that isn't O'd. Then q & ~O(q) is a truth, so by the principle, possibly O(q & ~O(q)). Distribute inside the possibility: possibly (O(q) & O(~O(q))). Apply factivity to the second conjunct: possibly (O(q) & ~O(q)), which is impossible. So no truth goes un-O'd.

(3) Knowledge isn't special here. Other operators are necessarily factive and distribute too: "someone truly believes that"; "there is conclusive evidence that"; "it is truly recorded somewhere that".

(4) So if the derivation is sound, then the claim that every truth could be truly believed by somebody is false, and so is the claim that every truth could have conclusive evidence for it, and the claim that every truth could be truly written down somewhere. Those aren't verificationist theses — a card-carrying realist about truth should accept them. There are surely truths about how many grains of sand are on some beach right now that nobody will ever believe or have evidence about, even though somebody could have believed them and the evidence could have existed.

(5) So the collapse has nothing to do with truth being epistemic and nothing to do with knowledge in particular. It comes from the modal packaging: quantifying over propositions inside the scope of a possibility operator and then instantiating on a proposition that embeds the operator itself. Call this the Neutrality Requirement — any diagnosis of the paradox has to be statable without mentioning knowledge, or else it isn't a diagnosis, it's a stipulation.

(6) So the anti-realist can keep her principle and just refuse to instantiate it on sentences that embed the operator, which is what the restricted-knowability responses do anyway; and the realist who wants to press the paradox can't, because the Neutrality Requirement says she has no way to say what's special about knowledge.

I want to hold onto two things and on his telling they can't both be true, which is why I think I'm missing something: (a) the platitude in (4) — of course every truth could be truly believed, of course one could have had evidence for it; and (b) that Fitch's result is a genuine problem for the knowability principle and not a piece of modal bookkeeping that would go through for any old factive operator.

Which step do you reject? And — the part I can't do myself — why did that step look fine, and what am I on the hook for if I give it up? The belief case in (3) is the one I can't shake; the other two I suspect I could dismiss. If rejecting something costs me (b), I want to know what the anti-realist gets back, because right now it looks like she gets everything and I'd rather not hand her that in a seminar paper I have to respond to by Friday.
