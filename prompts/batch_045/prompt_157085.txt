Me and a friend have been going back and forth about the Pasadena game for about two years and we're clearly just re-running the same five moves at each other, so I want to try the adversarial collaboration thing on ourselves before we give up and agree to disagree.

Setup first, so nobody has to rely on memory: fair coin, tossed until it comes up heads. If the first heads is on toss n you get (-1)^(n-1) · 2^n / n dollars — so +2, -2, +8/3, -4, +32/5, and so on — and take utility to be linear in money. Weight each payoff by its probability 2^-n and you get 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which adds up to ln 2 in that order and to anything you like in other orders. So the game's expected value is, apparently, undefined.

He's on the bounded-value side: the lesson is upstream, in the theory of value — utilities (or values generally) have to be bounded, or the space of prospects has to be restricted, so that the expectation always exists and there's nothing to be undefined. I'm on the extend-the-rule side: the expectation is simply undefined, that's a coherent thing to say about a gamble, and the thing that has to give is the decision rule — keep unbounded value, rank prospects with something else (partial orderings, dominance principles, comparisons of clamped expectations, whatever survives), and treat Pasadena as a stress test rather than a refutation.

Draft the joint statement we could both put our names to. Numbered agreed-claims section first, and I want claims that are load-bearing rather than 'infinite series are tricky' — including, if you can get us there, agreement about what the game is even a counterexample to. Then a disagreements table: the exact residual disagreement, the wording my side would accept for itself and the wording his side would accept for itself, and for each row a flag saying whether it's a real disagreement or a dispute about which node in the theory we're allowed to adjust.

The cruxes I want handled, each with a case or quantity both of us would accept as bearing on it and each of our predictions written down in advance:

1. The swap test. Moving a payoff from one outcome to another (say swapping what you get on the first-toss sequence with what you get on the second) looks like it shouldn't change how good the gamble is. But chaining such swaps appears to ratchet the Pasadena game up to indifference with an arbitrarily large sure payment. Is swap-indifference a principle either of us should accept, and what does rejecting it cost?
2. The slow family. Let P_s pay (-1)^(n-1) · 2^n / n^s. Stakes are unbounded for every s, but the expectation only converges absolutely when s > 1. Should a view that blames unbouneded stakes treat s = 0.99 and s = 1.01 differently? If yes, is it secretly blaming the convergence and not the stakes?
3. Cap-dominance. Clamp every payoff into [-M, M], take the now perfectly ordinary expectation, and prefer whichever prospect wins for all sufficiently large M. Symmetric caps look like they hand back ln 2; let the two caps drift apart and my guess is the comparison runs off to ±∞. Is cap-dominance a decision rule, a constraint on rules, or an artifact? Does symmetric capping smuggle the answer in? Is it complete on the slow family?
4. Bid–ask. What would I pay to be given the game, and what would I accept to be relieved of it? Does one side predict a single number here and the other a spread, and is a spread a reductio of that side or a feature it should have expected?
5. The hedge. Take the game and a sign-flipped copy driven by the same coin and you have $0 for sure. Drive them by two seperate coins and the package looks undefined again. Which of us, if either, should find that uncomfortable?
6. The many-shot test. n independent copies, look at the average payoff as n grows. Does it settle down, drift, or have no limit? I genuinely don't know the answer, so record both predictions rather than settling it for us.

If you think one of my tests begs the question, say so in the document instead of quietly dropping it. Where you think part of the dispute is verbal, say which part and why. And please don't cite anybody or turn this into a survey of the literature — I don't care who got there first, I care what the two of us can sign.
