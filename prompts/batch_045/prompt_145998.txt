I write fiction, and I am midway through a revision in which two things have collided: my publisher's house style, and a language reform that takes place inside the world of my novel. I would like help reconstructing what the first of these is committed to, because I cannot tell any more whether I am obeying a semantic thesis, a psychological bet, or a moral norm.

The practice in question is the one my copyeditor applies without argument. Generic masculine forms are struck: compounds built on the old generic noun are replaced, and the generic pronoun becomes *they*. Role nouns are marked asymmetrically — a character who departs from the default is introduced as a woman novelist, a Black magistrate, a disabled veteran, while the default occupant is introduced as a novelist, a magistrate, a veteran, and the asymmetry is itself the point. And where a character defends the older usage, the standard move — in the essays I have read on the subject, and in the argument my protagonist makes — is transposition: rewrite the offending sentence along a different axis of social hierarchy and see whether the defender flinches. The best-known instance is the satirical centrepiece of Hofstadter's 1982 column on default assumptions, reprinted in *Metamagical Themas* as the chapter on changes in default words and images: a mock-earnest plea for a single neutral replacement term, driven past the point where it still sounds natural, into compounds where it begins to sound faintly ridiculous, so as to reveal that the putatively inclusive element was never being read as neutral.

My complication is this. The novel is set in a society whose vocabulary marks a caste distinction: most office nouns come in a bare form and a specified form, and the bare form's historical occupants belong to the dominant caste. The reformers in the book abolish the bare form — call it the ordinance of symmetric specification — on the explicit ground that any unmarked noun quietly nominates its historical occupants as the human default. Two things then happen, and I have written both. First, marking rebounds: characters who now hear both forms specified report that they think about caste *more*, not less, and the reform's opponents use this as evidence that symmetric marking entrenches the very category it meant to defuse. Second, my copyeditor has asked for the opposite in the surrounding English: the ruling council is to be rendered with the bare noun, for the sake of what she calls texture and I would call the reader's sense of a lived-in world, while subordinate offices keep their markers. So the practice de-defaults in one place and reinstates a default in another, and I cannot articulate what distinguishes the two cases.

I would like the implicit theory reconstructed. About meaning: in which layer of content is the offending default supposed to live — truth-conditions, something implicature-like, a presupposition, or only a matter of what the word makes salient — and does the practice strictly require the strong claim, or would a weaker one do? About language's power: does de-defaulting presuppose that usage shapes thought and social structure, or only that it expresses and ratifies them, and would the practice survive being reinterpreted as the latter? About fiction in particular: the default changes nothing that is true in my story — it changes what a reader pictures while reading, and often involuntarily. What view of a fiction's content, and of an author's answerability for imaginative uptake rather than for asserted content, is needed to make house-style de-defaulting anything other than an aesthetic preference?

And about the satire: what must be true of an attitude for transposition to count as evidence against it? That such attitudes answer to a consistency norm, that formal or structural similarity is the right kind of similarity to appeal to, and — awkwardly — that the reader already condemns the analogue, so that the device can surface a commitment but cannot manufacture one? If that last condition holds, my invented reformers, who have no such consensus to borrow, are left with no lever at all.

Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and mark the places where it is underdetermined and several rival theories would license exactly these edits. Then tell me what would have to change in the practice — in the copyeditor's rules, in my rendering of the council, and in my protagonist's argument — if each of the presupposed views were replaced by its nearest rival.
