I'm in a small reading group on the history of economic thought — two political theorists, an economic historian, and me; I work in trade policy, which is how I ended up arguing about the eighteenth century at all — and we have just spent three weeks on the long chapter on colonies in Book IV of the Wealth of Nations, published in 1776, the same year as the Declaration of Independence. What I want from you is for that chapter's analysis of empire to be genuinely run on the crisis it was written in the middle of, rather than having two or three famous lines from it gestured at: the gibe about founding an empire merely to raise up a people of customers, the complaint that the whole arrangement serves the producer at the expense of the consumer, and so on. I know those; what I don't know is whether the argument, taken seriously as an account of what empires are for and what they cost, actually illuminates the case it was composed against, or only looks as though it does.

So take the imperial crisis with the thirteen mainland colonies — the Stamp Act and its repeal, the Townshend duties, the Tea Act of 1773 that propped up a politically connected chartered company by letting it sell its surplus tea directly into the American market while keeping the duty and undercutting colonial merchants and smugglers, the destruction of the tea in Boston harbour, the Coercive Acts, the fighting at Lexington and Concord in 1775, and the declaration of independence the following year — and reconstruct it as Smith's framework would have it reconstructed. Begin by setting the framework up as a decision problem: who the agents are, bearing in mind that the account characteristically re-describes them, so that the conflict is not Britain against America but the merchants and manufacturers who capture policy, against the British consumer and taxpayer who fund it, alongside the colonial planter and merchant and the colonial taxpayer; what each is maximising; what the state ought to be maximising on this view, which is the annual produce of land and labour available to the whole population and not the profit of any branch of trade; and what options were genuinely open to London and to the colonial assemblies in, say, 1774 and 1775. I would like three ledgers kept strictly apart — the merchant's, on profits from the colonial trade; the treasury's, on what the army, the navy and the colonial establishment cost against colonial tax revenue; and the nation's, which is the only one the framework treats as decisive and which is not the sum of the other two. Then say what the framework predicts about each option, including the two Smith himself preferred, an incorporating union with American representation and common taxation and, failing that, an amicable separation with free trade, and about the option that was actually taken, coercion and then separation by war. I would like the Tea Act handled in particular, since on its face a legislature relieving a chartered company by granting it a colonial sales monopoly looks like the mechanism of the critique written into a single statute, and I want to know whether the framework does anything with it beyond nodding.

Then press on where it fails, because I have three things I cannot get straight. First, scope: a great deal of the indictment is aimed at exclusive companies and at densely peopled, already cultivated territories governed as revenue farms, Bengal above all, rather than at temperate settlement colonies that prospered because land was cheap and their internal institutions were sound; if the thirteen colonies are of the second kind, how much of the critique reaches them at all, and is there a third kind of possession that the chapter has no name for? Second, the counterfactual: the claim that colonial monopoly raised the general rate of profit and drew capital out of more productive employment at home assumes that capital would indeed have been more productive there, and yet the same book allows that new territory and new branches of trade can themselves raise the rate of profit; I want to know whether both can be true, or whether the case against empire quietly relies on a denial of diminishing returns at home that Smith does not himself accept. Third, the security exception: he concedes that measures damaging to opulence may still be justified by defence, and the mainland colonies furnished naval stores and sheltered the far more prized sugar islands, so I want the fleet put onto the nation's ledger and told how much weight it can bear before it carries the rest of the argument away with it, and what remains of the critique if it can bear a great deal. And then the things I suspect the framework simply cannot register: the colonists' constitutional claim about consent and self-government as something worth having independently of any ledger, treated here, if at all, as an administrative inefficiency rather than as a demand; and the people the ledger omits entirely, the enslaved whose labour produced much of what the colonial trade carried and the Native Americans whose land is what made settlement colonies possible in the first place. Finally, the scoreboard. As I understand it, and you should treat this as a stylised fact rather than something to check, trade between Britain and the former colonies grew after 1783 rather than collapsing, and Britain spent the following century and a half assembling a considerably larger empire; both have been cited in opposite directions as evidence about Smith. Give me an honest verdict on whether the American case confirms his analysis of empire, is an anomaly it has to absorb, or is a case the analysis was never built for, and tell me plainly which features of the episode you think the framework is silent about rather than stretching it to cover them.
