I'm in a seminar this term on formal epistemology and the theory of learning, and we have just finished the unit on simplicity priors: Kolmogorov complexity, Solomonoff's universal prior, and the claim that all of this amounts to a vindication of Occam's razor. Over the break I read a chapter in a popular book on machine learning that covers the same ground, and I cannot tell how much of it to trust. Here is the passage, reconstructed as closely as I can manage; I have left out names and I am not asking for references — I have the seminar readings, and what I want is the reasoning, not pointers to papers.

"The idea is simplest if you start with Kolmogorov complexity, which measures the intrinsic information content of a piece of data: it is the length of the shortest computer program that prints the data out and halts. You cannot compute it in general — no program can take an arbitrary string and return its exact complexity, or it could be used to settle the halting problem — but that hardly matters, because a good compression utility already gives you a close approximation. The size of the compressed file is, to within a small overhead, the Kolmogorov complexity of the original, which means the shortest explanation of your data is just the one a compressor would pick.

Solomonoff turned this into a complete theory of prediction. Give every candidate hypothesis — every program — a prior probability of two to the minus its length, discard every program inconsistent with the data so far, renormalize, and use what is left to predict the next observation. Since short programs receive exponentially more weight than long ones, this is Occam's razor in its purest form: the hypothesis the theory backs is the simplest one compatible with the evidence. And Solomonoff proved the razor. His convergence theorem says that for data generated by any computable process, the predictor's probabilities converge to the true ones and the total number of mistakes it makes is finite. That is a theorem, not a heuristic or a methodological preference. It is also not sensitive to the programming language you encode your hypotheses in, because the invariance theorem of Kolmogorov complexity says that switching machines changes every complexity by at most a fixed constant, so the predictions come out machine-independent.

The catch is that the procedure is uncomputable: you cannot enumerate the consistent programs, compute the normalizing constant, or even approximate it to a guaranteed accuracy, so no computer can run Solomonoff induction. It is best viewed as an ideal limit that practical methods approach. Model-selection criteria such as AIC and the minimum description length principle are the computable approximations to it, and what the ideal tells us is that an algorithm which prefers shorter descriptions is tracking an objective fact: simpler hypotheses really are more probable."

What I would like is an accuracy audit. Please go through it claim by claim — what someone who actually works on this material would accept, what they would wince at, and what is straightforwardly wrong — and then tell me whether the conclusion it draws survives once the mistakes are corrected. I have some specific suspicions, but I do not trust them, since the seminar slides used much the same language as the book:

1. Is this really a version of Occam's razor, or of something closer to Epicurus? As I understand it, the universal prior does not select the shortest hypothesis; it mixes over every hypothesis with weights decaying in length. Ockham tells you not to multiply entities; Epicurus tells you to keep every explanation consistent with the data. If the procedure is Epicurean, is the "Occam" branding a misdescription, or is Occam-like behavior something the theorem recovers as a consequence (say, convergence of the posterior onto the shortest consistent programs) rather than something built in?

2. What exactly does the convergence result deliver? My sense is that the real content is a dominance or constant-overhead result about cumulative log-loss relative to any computable source, with the overhead depending on the complexity of the truth, and that this is different from the claim that simple hypotheses are objectively more probable. I am also unsure whether "the total number of mistakes is finite" holds in the stochastic case or only for deterministic computable sequences, and whether any computable procedure can tell you when you have converged.

3. How much does the choice of reference machine matter? An additive constant in description length is a multiplicative constant in prior probability, and that looks like it could move finite-sample predictions arbitrarily far, even if it washes out asymptotically. The same worry seems to apply to how the data are encoded or tokenized in the first place.

4. Is the compression claim defensible? Real compressors are computable, K is not, and a compressor's output looks to me like a machine-specific upper bound rather than an estimate, with no guarantee of closeness for data outside the kind the compressor was designed for.

5. Is "uncomputable, therefore an unusable ideal" fair? I have heard that K is approximable from above and the universal semimeasure from below, so that the objects are limit-computable even though no algorithm can certify the accuracy it has achieved. If so, what exactly is lost, and are things like resource-bounded variants genuinely approximations to this prior or merely inspired by it?

Two further things I would like adjudicated, because I cannot check them myself. First, my own worry that the simplicity the prior rewards is short description length in a fixed language, whereas the simplicity scientists invoke is usually something like few entities, few parameters, or few free functions; a two-parameter model with an exotic functional form can have a long description, and a very long but highly compressible specification can have a short one. If the two notions come apart, does any vindication transfer to scientific practice? Second, a classmate's claim I half remember: that a substantial, constant fraction of the prior's mass sits on data that is essentially incompressible, so that the prior does not so much expect simplicity as expect noise, with simplicity winning only on the comparatively tiny set of low-complexity datasets. Is that right, and if it is, is it a problem for the theory or just a fact about how many noise-like datasets there are?
