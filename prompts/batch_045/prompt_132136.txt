I'm in the second year of a PhD in evolutionary biology, and this term I'm running the graduate seminar in which every discussion of a trait collapses into the same stalemate: half the room wants to know what the trait is for, the other half wants to know why it is there, and everyone slides between the two without noticing. Before I assign any more of the classic adaptationism debate — the spandrels critique, the exaptation literature, optimality modelling, the comparative method — I want a specification of what an adequate answer to the question "why does this population have this trait?" would actually have to deliver, on either side of the dispute. So please write me the desiderata: what a claim that a trait is an adaptation must establish, and, symmetrically, what a claim that it is a structural byproduct, a developmental constraint, a co-opted relic, or drift must establish, with the case or consideration that motivates each requirement. Include the distinctions a theory in this area is not allowed to collapse — current utility versus historical origin, the trait now having an effect versus having been selected for it, selection maintaining a variant versus selection having built it — and say whether an adequate account may return a mixed verdict or must pick one. I also want a criterion about how traits get individuated in the first place, since in my material it is often unclear whether we are looking at one trait or three. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are really methodological preferences of one camp, tell me which single requirement excludes the most candidate explanations, and flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most want, since I suspect the list is asking for demonstration-grade evidence from every trait while also wanting a programme general enough to be worth running.

To keep this from being an exercise in recall, apply the criteria to a case I've constructed rather than the three textbook examples my students always reach for. A lineage of alpine skinks has a bony supraorbital crest in four of eleven populations; the four are exactly the populations that overlap a visually hunting bird, clay models with the crest are attacked less often than shaved models, and a genome scan shows a recent sweep in the crest region. Against this: five years of mark–recapture finds no survivorship difference between crested and crestless individuals, the crest forms as a mechanical consequence of a shifted jaw-adductor attachment that does improve bite performance, and in the sister genus the same outgrowth serves in burrowing. Each desideratum should say how this case fares under it, and the list should end by naming the single further observation that would do most to settle it. Then, briefly, say whether a trait whose shape matches an engineering optimum to within a few percent but which is invariant across forty species can ever satisfy the list — where the only comparison available is the engineering model itself. Keep any scorecard of the named positions to a few lines; the criteria and their motivating cases are what I need.
