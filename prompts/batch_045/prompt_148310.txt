Build me a scenario where the agent is dutch-bookable — there's a book of bets a bookie can offer her that loses her money no matter what happens — while nothing is epistemically wrong with her: her credences are a probability function, she updates by conditionalization, and she maximizes expected utility. The exploitability has to come entirely from the gap between credence and betting rate, i.e. from her utility for money being concave, so that her single indifference price for a bet of a given stake isn't her credence.

Constraints:
- realistic: an actual person and a sum she could actually be offered — flood insurance on a house, a crop, a client who might not pay. No idealized agents, no infinite wealth, no sci-fi.
- one price per bet: for each bet there is a single price at which she's indifferent between buying and selling, and the bookie trades with her at exactly that price. If the bookie gets to buy at her ask and sell at her bid, the case is worthless.
- the bets offered both add risk for her rather than hedge exposure she already has — a hedge pushes one price above her credence and can hide the gap.
- no transaction costs, counterparty risk, or stake caps; the bookie knows her prices and will pay.

What I want after the scenario (one paragraph, then the numbers): her credences, a concave utility function or indifference prices derived from one, and the book itself, checked outcome by outcome; then confirmation that the same book fails at small stakes, since the price/credence gap vanishes as the stake shrinks. Name the feature doing the work and say what happens if you remove it. Then give me the strongest reply available to someone who wants to keep the dutch book argument — does the usual move of running the book in utils rather than money close the case off completely, or is there a version where a fully coherent agent still looks irrational? And tell me if you think the case cheats; my own line is that it cheats if her credences turn out incoherent or the book needs a spread.
