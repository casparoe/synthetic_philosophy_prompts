hey — I'm on the conduct committee for a mid-sized OSS project (a date/time library, few million downloads a week, and yes it's in some hospital scheduling stacks, which is part of why this is stressful).

One of our two maintainers — call him R — posted a long piece on his own blog last month calling an earlier removal we made "mob punishment," naming the people who organised the petition against the person we removed and saying the committee caved to a campaign. Now there's a petition of about 400 signatures, mostly from people who don't contribute, demanding we strip his commit access. He hasn't broken the code of conduct in any project space; all of it was on his own blog. Two of us think removing him would be punishing someone for criticising us, which is more or less exactly what he's accusing us of. Two think his post did real damage: three contributors left and a corporate sponsor went quiet.

I've been trying to seperate out three things I think we keep collapsing: (1) us declining to keep handing him the platform, (2) us making a public judgement that what he did was wrong, and (3) us signalling to everyone else — sponsors, conference organisers, his employer — that they should withdraw too. My hunch is that (3) is where most of the actual punishing happens, and that we're the only body in the picture that can do (2) with anything attached to it: a named decision, a route to appeal, an end date. But I can't tell whether that's a real distinction or just machinery I built to get the answer I already wanted.

Can you draft the memo I circulate before Friday? It needs a concrete recommendation — do something or do nothing, with a timeframe and what would have to change for it to change — and it has to engage the "we'd be punishing him for criticising us" worry properly instead of defining it away. Someone will also propose that we quietly revoke his commit bit and never announce it, on the theory that this is neutral infrastructure management and not a sanction at all; if you think that's the worst of the options, say so plainly and in terms I can read out to the other three without sounding like I swallowed a philosophy paper. If it matters: the security contact for the package is him, and no one else can currently ship a fix for it quickly, so anything we do to him lands on downstream users too.
