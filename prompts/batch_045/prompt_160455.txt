I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on moral conflict, and I want to replace my usual handout — a list of isms with one objection each — with something that actually sorts students by what they think about cases rather than by which label they find attractive. Please build me a classification instrument for the debate about whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible: a short diagnostic plus the decision tree behind it, which students take before we discuss the literature and use again at the end to see whether they have moved.

The seminar's through-line is professional and role-embedded conflict — clinicians allocating scarce resources, civil servants with dirty hands, journalists bound by confidentiality — so the distinctions I care about are the ones that separate views about what changes when a conflict is generated by a role or a prior commitment, not just by two impersonal duties colliding. Build the instrument around that emphasis, but keep it usable for ordinary cases too.

Please organise the response in these sections, with roughly these lengths.

**1. The hinges (about 350 words).** State the five or six distinctions that do the classifying work, each as a question with the two or three answers it allows. I want these to be structural rather than doctrinal: the behaviour of 'ought' under conjunction (if you ought to A and ought to B and cannot do both, does it follow that you ought to A-and-B, and what follows if that is impossible); the direction of the inference from inability (does 'cannot do both' deliver 'not required to do either', so that the agent has mere options, or 'required to do each, hence wrong whatever she does'); whether there can be wrongdoing without blameworthiness and blameworthiness without wrongdoing; whether the values in the hard cases are comparable but finely balanced or genuinely incomparable, and whether incomparability generates a dilemma or dissolves one by leaving both options permissible; whether the conflict is epistemic (it would dissolve with full information) or survives full information; and whether the residue of the hard case — remorse, apology, reparative action, the appropriateness of others' blame — is evidence of an unfulfilled requirement or merely a fitting response to loss. Add any distinction I have missed that you think separates real positions, and say in one line why each earns its place on the tree.

**2. The logical space (about 250 words).** Take the hinges together and sketch the space of answer combinations, then mark which cells are occupied by a position anyone holds. Present this as the skeleton of the tree: hinge questions as nodes in the order you think a student should meet them, leaves reached by answer patterns. Note the total number of cells and how few are in fact occupied.

**3. The instrument (about 900 words).** Ten items, each a concrete case or a concrete claim about a case, with two or three answer options, and each tagged with the hinge it probes. Constraints:

- No item may ask whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible, and no option may consist in endorsing a theorist or a school. Every option should be something a student with no exposure to the debate could answer from the case: a judgement about what the agent should do, a judgement about what is true of her afterwards, or a claim about what follows from the previous answers.
- Every option must be one that some real position wants to give; no option should be a position nobody would defend.
- The cases must include: a forced choice in which an agent is made to select which of two people dies; a promise that can be kept only by failing to prevent a serious harm to a third party; a rescue case pitting partiality to one's own child against saving a greater number of strangers; a role case in which two patients need one ventilator; a loyalty-versus-public-interest case (whistleblowing or leaking); a dirty-hands case in which an official authorises something she regards as morally impermissible for a public goal; an uncertainty case in which the conflict seems to turn on what the agent does not yet know; and three 'after the action' items asking about the fittingness of remorse, of apology or repair, and of blame or demands for justification from others.
- Mark each item as load-bearing or confirmatory, and say what a student who declines to answer a load-bearing item should be told.
- The whole thing should take about fifteen minutes.

**4. Scoring key (about 450 words).** Map answer patterns onto positions, with these required as leaves: the view that conflict is only apparent and there is always a single thing one ought to do; the view that conflict is only apparent because moral requirements form a consistent system and genuine conflict would be irrational; the view that duties are real but prima facie and always resolvable here by weighing; the tragic view that some conflicts are unresolved by any deliberation and leave the agent wrong whatever she does; the incomparabilist view that the conflict consists in the incomparability of the values rather than in a tie; the view that all apparent dilemmas are epistemic; the particularist or holist view that there are no conflicting general requirements because reasons are context-dependent; the view that dilemmas are a limit of moral theory rather than a feature of morality; and any position that says something distinctive about role-generated conflict, or an explicit note that no standard view does. Give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Crucially, say what the key returns for split patterns — a student who is a dilemmist about promises but not about triage, or who accepts residue but denies unresolved conflict. Does the instrument return a single position, a cluster, a ranked pair, or 'no stable position'? Because the three residual items probe different things, give short separate mini-keys for the deliberative, affective, and accountability dimensions, and say whether a student can legitimately land in different places on each.

**5. Empty cells (about 250 words).** At least four unoccupied combinations, each with a diagnosis: incoherent, merely unattractive, or defensible but unexplored. I am especially interested in one cell that is coherent and attractive and that nobody occupies — the view I suspect is available but unclaimed. Flag it as such and say what would have to be true for it to be held.

**6. What the instrument cannot separate (about 250 words).** At least two pairs of positions that come apart nowhere on these cases. For each, state the further question that would separate them, and explain why that question cannot be posed as a case someone can answer without already knowing the theory — this is the honest limit of the instrument, and I want it stated rather than papered over.

**7. Worked runs (about 200 words).** Run the instrument on two fictional students: one with a clean, consistent pattern, and one whose answers are split or unstable. Show the scoring in each case and what you would tell the second student about her position.

Throughout, name positions by their content rather than by their authors, and do not cite, name-check, or attribute anything to a paper or book — I will supply the reading list myself, and I do not want students (or I) chasing references that I cannot verify. Total about 2,500 words.
