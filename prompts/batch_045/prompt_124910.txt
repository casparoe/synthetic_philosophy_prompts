My flatmate works on AI alignment and has been baiting me for weeks with an argument I cant answer, and I want to know whether its clever or just slippery.

Background so you know what were fighting about: the pitch for low-impact AI is that you give an agent its task objective plus some penalty for changing the world too much, so it fetches the coffee without knocking over the vase, and you get that safety without first having to write down a complete theory of whats valuable. I know the standard complaints (the agent has an incentive to freeze everything, to stop you switching it off, to glue the vase back together and call it even, and that the penalty has to be denominated in whatever matters). He says all of that is downstream of one clean argument that kills the whole programme, and he writes it out like this:

(1) An impact penalty is only doing its job if it seperates the change the principal asked for from the change they didnt ask for. If it cant tell coffee arrived from vase broken, its not a side-effect penalty, its just a tax on acting.

(2) Anything implementable is a function from trajectories (plus the environment) to a real number. Theres nothing else you can put in code.

(3) A real-valued function over trajectories, used to rank the agents options, is a utility function: it induces a total ordering of outcomes by how much impact they involve. So any impact penalty is a ranking of states of the world by disvalue.

(4) A ranking of states of the world by value is precisely a specification of value — the thing everyone agrees we cant write down. So a value-neutral impact measure is a contradiction: its either a complete (and probably badly wrong) theory of value or its not ranking anything at all.

(5) If its a complete theory of value, we dont need it. An agent carrying that around in its objective is just an aligned agent, and the entire selling point of low-impact methods was that they buy safety *without* first solving value specification.

(6) If its not ranking anything — if its genuinely neutral between kinds of change — then to it the point of the task and the side effect are interchangable, and the optimal policy is to do nothing at all, or to prevent any change whatsoever including the ones that would have happened anyway. Paralysis, or something worse than paralysis.

(7) So low-impact AI is either redundant or paralyzing. And note that (2) through (4) never mention AI: any leave-things-as-you-found-them norm — non-maleficence in medical ethics, the precautionary principle, ordinary tidiness — is also a real-valued function over options, so by the same reasoning each of them is either a complete moral theory or empty. He likes that part; he says it shows the research programme is trying to get something for nothing.

The step I keep poking at is (3) to (4). My attempt: an impact measure is a *distance*, and distances are symmetric — d(a,b) = d(b,a) — so it doesnt rank outcomes at all, it just says how far you moved, and moving is not the same as making things worse. His reply is that the moment you stick it in an objective it does rank policies, and that in any case you still have to choose what counts as one unit of distance, and choosing units is choosing what matters. I dont have a comeback to that, which is why Im here.

I also want to throw in one idea of my own to see if it survives. Instead of how much did the world change, penalize how much *work*, measured in the agents own action space, it would take to put the world back — call it undo-cost. Coffee delivered and door left open: cheap to undo, walk it back. Vase smashed: not undoable at any price, so huge. A tree falling in the forest while the agent works: not the agents doing, so it shouldnt register. It feels like undo-cost needs no ranking of outcomes, only a grip on what the agent can do. My flatmate says Ive just moved the value-ladenness into the choice of what counts as restored and into treating irreversibility as bad.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
