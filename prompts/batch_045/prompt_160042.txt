OK so I keep circling back to something about legal burdens of proof and I cant tell whether it is secretly a piece of epistemology or just error-cost bookkeeping in a philosophy costume. So: what does the ladder of legal standards of proof actually presuppose?

The practice as I understand it (US/UK-ish common law, correct me if Ive got it wrong): there isnt one evidential bar, theres a ladder. To stop someone, search them, or get a warrant you need something like reasonable suspicion or probable cause, and some of these bars govern forward-looking guesses (whether evidence will turn up) rather than what happened in the past. To win an ordinary civil suit you need a preponderance, roughly more likely than not. For civil matters that feel quasi-criminal — terminating someones parental rights, committing them to a psychiatric hospital against their will, often punitive damages, sometimes fraud — you need the intermediate bar of clear and convincing evidence. And to convict someone criminally you need proof beyond a reasonable doubt, which courts have traditionally declined to translate into a number and mostly leave undefined, apparently on purpose. Same kind of question every time — did this person do this thing — different bar, and the same body of evidence can clear one bar and not the one above it, which is how you get the well-known spectacle of somebody acquitted of a killing and then held liable for it in a civil suit a year later.

The obvious story is that the ladder just is pragmatic encroachment: how much is at stake changes what counts as knowing, or as justified enough to act on. The obvious deflationary story is that it isnt epistemology at all: fix a credence threshold, note that false conviction is much worse than false acquittal (and that the state should bear the risk), and pick the bar that maximizes expected value. I want to know which of those the practice really needs, and here are the bits that make me think neither textbook version fits:

1. Whose stakes? In the bank cases everyone reaches for, the stakes are the agents own — *my* check bounces if the bank isnt open Saturday, so I need to know. In the legal case the practical situation of the person whose belief is being graded (the jury) doesnt change at all when the penalty goes up; the stakes that move are the defendants, or the states. So either the practice needs a third-personal, institutional version of encroachment where what matters is the interests of whoever is deciding, or it is contextualism about whose context sets the standard, or it is not stakes-sensitive in the philosophers sense at all. Which, and does the choice show up anywhere observable?
2. Magnitude. The bar doesnt scale with the size of whats at risk the way an expected-utility story predicts. A ten-billion-dollar contract dispute is preponderance; a criminal charge carrying a two-hundred-dollar fine is beyond reasonable doubt. It looks like the ladder tracks the *kind* of thing at risk — liberty, family, the states punitive power — rather than how much. Does that mean the practice presupposes a categorical, rights-shaped theory rather than a cardinal one? What would a genuinely utility-theoretic system look like, and would it have one bar and just move damages around?
3. Two knobs. The system can also move *who* has to prove what — burdens, presumptions, affirmative defenses — not just how much. If a fixed threshold plus clever burden allocation could reproduce most of the pattern, what does the existence of a distinct third standard show? That error-cost balancing wasnt enough on its own, or that the thresholds are doing expressive rather than epistemic work?
4. Refusing the number. What does declining to attach a probability to the criminal standard presuppose — that the relevant epistemic status just isnt a credence, that there is such a thing as being settled enough to act that outruns your probability assignment, or only institutional caution about anchoring?
5. Possibly apocryphal: I have a vague memory that English courts said at some point that the civil standard flexes with how serious the allegation is, while also insisting there is only one civil standard. If that is roughly right, is the practice stuck with an inconsistency, or is it two different things (threshold vs weight of evidence) that a decent theory should keep apart? Dont lean on my memory of it — if Ive mangled it, say so and use whichever version is true.

Ive seen the labels contextualism and interest-relative invariantism thrown around on here and I can never keep them straight, so please dont make the answer hang on which is which. Reconstruct the implicit epistemology from what the practice does and only use the labels if theyre load-bearing. And skip the literature review: I want the argument, not the citations.
