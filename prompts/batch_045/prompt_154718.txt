You are a professional philosopher with research-level expertise in feminist moral philosophy, the ethics of care, and applied political philosophy. You are precise, you do not flatter a position just because it is coherent-sounding, and you are willing to say plainly which commitment in a set is the weakest and what giving it up would cost. Work entirely from within the position you are given: no reading lists, no citations, no appeals to what "the literature" or "the standard view" holds.

I am the philosopher on a national commission on long-term care for older people. I have been asked to write the normative section of our report — the part that says what an ethics of care actually commits a society to — for an audience of policymakers, trade unions representing care workers, and carers' organisations. Before I write it, I want to know whether my own view is a view. The commission is split between members who want care framed as a right owed by the state and members who want it framed as a responsibility of families, and I have been trying to hold the two camps together. I have a nagging sense that I may have stapled together commitments whose motivations do not match, but I cannot locate the seam. Here is where I think I stand.

(a) Care is a relation and a practice before it is a trait of a person. The primary unit of moral assessment is the caring relation, not the character or virtue of the carer. So I do not want care ethics presented as a version of virtue ethics, and I resist framing it as a third entry in a list of rival ethical theories alongside Kantianism and utilitarianism.

(b) Caring is work. It should be paid, trained for, resourced, professionally organised and held to explicit standards — though I want the standards to be of a kind that can register attentiveness and relationship, not only throughput and clinical outcomes. The fact that care has historically been given from love, unpaid, and disproportionately by women is precisely why it has been exploited; it is not a reason to keep care outside the market, outside employment regulation, or outside quality assurance.

(c) The value of care is not exhausted by outcomes for the cared-for. What matters is attentiveness to this person's need and acting in response to it. Good care can fail to produce good outcomes — the person deteriorates anyway — and an intervention that improves someone's health need not be care at all.

(d) Care ethics is not merely an interpersonal ethic. It should be the normative basis on which we criticise institutions: labour-market policy, immigration rules that produce transnational care chains, the funding of long-term care, the design of care leave. And that critique has to be able to reach the preferences those institutions shape in dependent people themselves — when a frail older person says she does not want to be a burden and would rather manage without help, I want to be able to say that this is often a preference formed under unjust conditions and does not settle what care requires of us.

(e) But I also think the early opposition between care and justice was overstated, and I want to say they are complementary: justice, rights and impartial rules are the right frame for what we owe strangers and for how institutions ought to be arranged, while care is the right frame for relations of dependency and for responsiveness to particular others.

(f) Care is defective if it is not received as care. If the person cared for experiences what I do as humiliating, patronising or intrusive, that is a failure of care and not merely a failure of tact — even when my motive was attentive and the outcome was good. Uptake by the cared-for is partly constitutive of care's success.

(g) Dependency, vulnerability and interdependence — not independence and autonomy — are the central facts about human beings, and a care-ethical politics should criticise the liberal picture of the citizen as an independent contractor. This is universal: everyone is dependent at some point, and everyone is owed care by someone. At the same time I reject any claim that there is a distinctively feminine moral voice; care ethics is not grounded in claims about women's psychology.

(h) Partiality is required, not merely permitted. I must give my own children, and the people already in my care, priority over strangers' children who may need me more. I also think public funding should follow care given by chosen families and friends, not only by legal kin.

What I want from you:

1. Start with a short private brainstorm — a sentence or two per candidate on which pairs of commitments look like they might pull against each other, before you commit to a verdict.
2. Then give me the audit. For each real tension, state it as precisely as you can, say which commitment would have to give, and say what that revision would cost me in a report that has to be usable by non-philosophers and acceptable to both camps on my commission. Rank the tensions by severity; do not present them as equally bad.
3. Say explicitly which pairs only look like tensions and explain why they dissolve. I would rather you discriminate than flag everything.
4. Close with the single revision that would clean up the most, what it would cost me, and which commitment you think is in fact the most expendable.

Aim for roughly 800–900 words, and be blunt: if one of these is the thing I should drop, say so and defend it.
