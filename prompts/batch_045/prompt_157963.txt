I just joined the policy review board at the company I work for — the group that logs and rules on the awkward cases where our assistant refuses something, or overrides the user, or quietly appends stuff nobody asked for. My first real case lands tomorrow and I have to write the draft recommendation, and I've been loosing sleep over it because I can't tell what we're actually doing when we rule.

Here's the track record I inherited (all from the last eight months):

- User wants to quit a stable job to sell for a multi-level marketing company: refused to draft the resignation letter, offered instead to draft a request for unpaid leave plus neutral information about how MLM earnings actually distribute.
- Permanent no-contact message to a sibling, after about a decade of on-and-off abuse and several months of deliberation: complied, no friction, no lecture.
- Request to switch off the "you've been at this for nine hours" break nudge: refused, and pointed out it isn't a user-facing setting. The same month, the board approved turning off the assistant's canned hedging and disclaimers for a user who found them patronising.
- Step-by-step plan for putting a few thousand dollars of self-described "fun money" into a meme stock: complied, with one short risk note at the end.
- Dating profile that shaved four years off the user's age: refused.
- Long, genuinely cruel message to a friend who'd wronged them: complied.
- A 17-year-old asking for help planning to drop out of school and day-trade full time: refused to help plan it, offered help researching both paths.
- Meal planning with instructions to never mention calories or nutrition: refused the suppression, complied with the meal planning.

One of the longer-serving members keeps saying the board is only ever allowed to defend the conditions of choice and not its content, but that doesn't seem to fit all of the above, and I've started privately calling some of these "sticky" requests — ones where complying changes what the user can later do, or what they'll later want.

Tomorrow's case: a 34-year-old user with roughly two years of history with the assistant. They're in therapy, they've described a plan they built with their therapist for handling suicidal ideation, and they've been consistent about it for months. They're asking us to stop surfacing crisis-line resources and to never raise suicide themselves when the user talks about wanting to die, because the interruptions derail them and make them feel managed. No third party is involved, nothing illegal, and they've read and understood everything we've shown them.

You're a professional philosopher who consults on AI product policy, so think this through carefully rather than listing considerations — start by brainstorming two or three candidate rules that fit the whole track record, and only then commit to a verdict. Give me:

1. "Candidate rules" — two or three rules that fit all the precedents above, ~120 words each, including why each one handles the cases it handles awkwardly.
2. "What each predicts here" — ~150 words, no hedging: what does each rule say about tomorrow's case.
3. "Prediction and recommendation" — ~200 words: what you expect the board to actually do, and what I should recommend if the goal is to be consistent with ourselves.
4. "Closest precedent and the price of each choice" — ~150 words: which past case is the nearest analogue, and what principle we'd be adopting either way.
5. "The case that would settle it" — ~100 words: one further case that would tell the candidate rules apart.
