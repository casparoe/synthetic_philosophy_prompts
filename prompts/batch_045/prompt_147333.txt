Context: I am a journalist writing a feature on what I call *Gödel-mongering* — the habit of invoking incompleteness outside mathematics to establish that something is impossible, inexhaustible, or in need of an outside supplement. My regular source on this beat is a philosopher of logic I have now interviewed six times. She has built up a clear record of rulings, and I want to arrive at our next interview knowing what the live possibilities are. Her rulings, in my paraphrase, with the reasons she gave:

1. The Lucas-style argument that human minds cannot be mechanical, because a mind can always see the truth of the Gödel sentence of any formal system that supposedly models it. Ruled: does not follow. Her reason: the argument needs the extra premise that we can know which formal system models us and that it is consistent, and nothing in the theorems supplies that.
2. The claim that there can be no final theory of everything in physics, on the ground that any finite axiomatic candidate will leave truths undecided. Ruled: overreach — empirical completeness is not the same as deductive completeness, and it is far from clear that the undecidable sentences of a candidate fundamental theory express anything physically real.
3. The claim that mathematical truth outruns provability in any one fixed consistent system, so that mathematics is inexhaustible and new axioms will always be needed — the continuum-hypothesis situation being the emblem. Ruled: legitimate, and indeed the central correct application — with the caveat that this concerns a fixed consistent formal system and does not by itself show that truth in mathematics is indeterminate.
4. The claim that every legal code or constitution must be incomplete, and that judicial discretion is therefore ineliminable. Ruled: loose analogy — nothing in a code of law encodes arithmetic or the kind of self-reference the proof needs, and legal indeterminacy follows perfectly well from vagueness and from conflicts between principles.
5. The claim that incompleteness shows mathematical truth to be relative to a system, so that there is no such thing as truth simpliciter. Ruled: backwards — the theorems separate truth from provability and presuppose a robust notion of truth rather than undermining one.
6. The claim that incompleteness shows no static analyser, antivirus or spam filter can be complete. Ruled: right neighbourhood, wrong theorem — that is undecidability or Rice-style reasoning about programs, a cousin of incompleteness but not an instance of it, and she files such cases separately as *not applications at all*.
