I'm giving a one-off ninety-minute session to a seminar of public-policy graduate students — fluent in cost-benefit analysis and expected utility, no formal value theory — on whether betterness could fail to be transitive, using the style of argument that walks through a long series of neighbouring cases, each a small improvement on the last, and then asks about the first versus the last. Don't explain the issue to me; plan how I should explain it.

First, what has to be in place before the argument type can even be stated: I assume they need the contrast between better than and at least as good as on the board early, since intransitivity in the one is a different thesis from intransitivity in the other, and some way of separating a small improvement from an imperceptible one. Give me the order the components must come in and say what depends on what.

Second, one case to carry the whole session, with the reasons it should have the features it has: I want it built on a good that only counts once it reaches a threshold — having actually finished a friendship, actually understood a proof, actually seen the sea from your window — rather than on a smoothly divisible quantity, because smooth cases let this audience escape by saying the adjacent differences are merely imperceptible.

Third, the misconception map: I expect them to hear intransitive betterness as cyclical preference and to reach for money-pump and exploitable-agent arguments, and to treat roughly as good as as though it were transitive. Tell me which others to expect and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be defused rather than answered.

Close with one question that would show me they can say which premise is under pressure and why, and with what I should openly decline to settle in ninety minutes.
