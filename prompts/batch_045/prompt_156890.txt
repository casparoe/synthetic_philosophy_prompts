I have been reading about the no-free-lunch theorems in machine learning alongside the older philosophical debate about induction, and I keep running into a pattern of argument that I cannot put a name to. I would like to know whether it has one. If it does not, I would like a coinage and a careful definition; if it does, I would like to know exactly how it is bounded, because the nearest things I have found seem to fit only partly, and a bare label would not tell me what I want to know.

Three cases where I think I am seeing the same thing.

First: the standard way of dismissing a no-free-lunch result is to point out that the averaging is over all possible target functions, or all possible problem distributions, whereas the problems we actually face are concentrated on ones that are smooth, or low in complexity, or otherwise structured. When I ask what entitles us to that concentration, the answer always comes back to our experience of which kinds of problem have turned out to be tractable. So a substantive empirical claim about the world, of precisely the kind the skeptical argument was about, is introduced as a technical remark about the measure, and presented as a defect in the theorem rather than as a bet on the world.

Second: the meta-level reply. Instead of defending a bias toward simple hypotheses, one hedges: track whichever forecaster in a pool has been doing best, and rely on results saying that in the long run you will not do much worse than the best member of the pool. I have tried sketching this in a harsher setting, and it is here that I lose my grip. Suppose the choice of problem distribution is made by someone who gets to see, first, which pool you are tracking and how you weight its members. Or suppose a world in which every member of the pool fails at the same rate. In both cases the guarantee I described seems to be fully satisfied and entirely worthless: it is a guarantee against being the worst, not a prospect of being right, and whether anyone in the pool succeeds looks like just the empirical assumption from the first case, now relocated one level up.

Third: the same shape seems to appear outside the formal setting. People recommend restricting attention to hypotheses that are computable, or finitely specifiable, or projectible, and when asked why, they say either that such hypotheses have worked before, or that some restriction is needed in order to get started at all and this one is cheap. Those two answers strike me as different in kind, and I suspect they get treated as interchangeable because both are replies to the same pressure.

My own attempt at a formulation, which I distrust: every available answer to a global tie result of this sort works either by reintroducing as a premise about the space of possible worlds the very empirical claim the tie result made problematic, or by changing the currency from an absolute guarantee to a comparative one that is conditional on someone succeeding. What I want from an answer:

Say whether these three cases really share a structure, or whether I have conflated two or three distinct things; if the latter, tell me which case is the odd one out and why.

Set out the nearest established concepts and, for each, the respect in which the thing I have described does not fit. I have in the back of my mind the old circularity worry about justifying induction by induction, the older pragmatic strategy that argues only that if any method can work then this one will, the puzzle about which predicates may be projected, and the general difficulty of choosing a prior or a reference class; but I do not know which of these, if any, is the right neighborhood, and my description seems to me to differ from each of them in some way I cannot articulate.

If nothing established fits, coin a term, define it, and give me three cases: one that clearly falls under it, one that clearly does not, and one that is a near miss, with the reason for the near miss.

Tell me whether the pattern is an artifact of how these theorems are stated, an artifact of my reading of them, or a real feature of the epistemic situation, and which of those it is.

Finally, under what conditions, if any, does a comparative guarantee of this shape convert into a reason to expect success, and is that conversion itself another instance of the pattern, or a genuine escape from it?

Please write your answer in British English.
