I write backend Go at a mid-sized software company and have no formal philosophy training; whatever philosophy I have picked up came from reading Hofstadter's old essays and from arguing about coordination failures in design reviews. I run a Thursday lunch-and-learn for our engineering org — typically 16 to 18 people, mixed levels, one 75-minute session — and I want to dedicate one session to the Platonia dilemma.

The setup, so we are starting from the same place: an eccentric billionaire gathers twenty people and tells them that if exactly one of them sends a telegram within twenty-four hours, that person receives a billion dollars; if two or more send, or if nobody sends, nobody gets anything; communication and coordination are forbidden. The greedy argument says to send, since abstaining guarantees you nothing. The symmetric-reasoning argument says that everyone who works the problem out will work it out the same way, so you should choose as though you are choosing for the whole group — and the answer that falls out of that is not abstention but randomization: send your telegram with probability one in twenty. I want the room to feel why the second argument has any pull at all, without being told it in advance.

Four things I want them to walk out seeing. First, that "we should all just abstain" is not a solution and not even coherent here: abstainers cannot win, so universal restraint is worth exactly as much as universal greed. Second, that randomized claiming is the real alternative, and that they should construct it themselves rather than have me hand it to them. Third, and most important, that the entire force of the argument comes from how tightly my decision is correlated with everyone else's — not from altruism, not from trust, not from people being similar sorts of people. Fourth, as the payoff for the third point: that at perfect correlation the prize becomes literally unwinnable, because anyone who claims is matched by a second claim and anyone who abstains gets nothing.

Here is the mechanic I want built in, and assume I can write the script for any computation it needs and run it live during the session. Before the session each person answers five or six tiny quantitative questions — how they would split an unexpected windfall, what they would bid in a sealed first-price auction, a couple of probability estimates — and I cluster participants by nearest neighbor in that space, pairing each person with an "echo." In the decisive rounds I tell them that each card has been paired with the card of the person whose answers most resemble theirs, and that with probability c the echo's card is overwritten with a copy of theirs while with probability 1 minus c it is left alone. I want three rounds with c at roughly 0, 0.5, and 1, so that the reason for restraint visibly fades as the correlation dial goes down and collapses entirely at the top of the dial. Tell me if the dial should be ordered differently, and tell me whether a fourth round with an explicit public random draw — one that licenses exactly one named person to claim — would sharpen the point or wreck it.

What I want from you is a runnable design: the rules and materials; a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes; a payoff scheme with real but small stakes (I have about $200 approved for prizes, and I would rather pay out than simulate money, because last year I ran the related lottery structure by email with a fictional jackpot and it fell completely flat); and the exact wording I should read aloud at each stage, since I am not a teacher and I will improvise badly if left to myself. No lecture up front, and nobody should hear the words superrationality, Platonia, or Hofstadter before the debrief. Two of the regulars have read the original column, so assume someone announces the answer in the first five minutes, and tell me how to handle that without letting them take over the session. Also tell me what changes if only nine people show up: the randomization argument is sensitive to the group size and my pairing scheme wants an even number.

Then the debrief, mapped moment by moment: which thing that happened in the room is supposed to carry which claim, and what I say at each of those moments. Include the objection I know is coming — that the other person's card is already sealed, so my choice cannot causally influence theirs, and that any correlation between our cards is just evidence, not leverage — and be honest with me about which part of that dispute the activity genuinely cannot settle and I will have to name myself in so many words.

Finally, an honest list of what will go wrong the first time I run this. I expect spontaneous out-loud coordination, side deals, somebody claiming out of pure contrarianism, somebody trying to bribe the room, somebody announcing that I am running a psychology experiment on them, and somebody accusing me of cheating when the echo mechanic destroys the prize. Tell me which of these are fatal to the exercise, which are actually useful material for the debrief, and how to handle each. And be clear about which feature of the idea the game cannot reproduce at all, so that I do not walk away thinking I have demonstrated something I have only staged.
