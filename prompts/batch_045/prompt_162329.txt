hey — I sit on this city's Commemoration Advisory Panel (seven of us, monthly meetings, the school board can override us but basically never has) and we've got a school naming case to decide in three weeks, so I'm trying to work out what we already committed ourselves to. Our run of verdicts so far:

- bronze statue of a Confederate colonel on the courthouse lawn, put up in 1909 by the local daughters-of-the-confederacy chapter — removed to museum storage, empty plinth left with an interpretive marker
- a street and the public library both named for a 19th-century merchant who was the city's biggest slave trader and whose bequest founded the library — street renamed for a local civil-rights lawyer, library kept its name with a lobby installation about the bequest
- elementary school named for a suffragist who also campaigned for immigration restriction and wrote against Black suffrage — kept, plaque added
- research wing named for a surgeon honored for a technique he developed by experimenting on incarcerated patients without consent — renamed, 4–3
- science building named for a geneticist who was a prominent eugenics advocate but whose honored discovery had nothing to do with that — kept, plaque rewritten to say both things
- bust of a mayor who desegregated the schools and also signed off on the redlining-era housing plan — kept, plaque rewritten
- a school renamed in 1961 for a sitting governor explicitly as a tribute to his resistance to desegregation — renamed again in 2019
- a park renamed in 1998 for a man lynched here in 1918, at the request of a preservation society; some descendants said the naming was done over their heads and reopens the wound — we kept the name but funded a family-designed commemoration and an oral-history project instead

New case: Vance Elementary, named in 1926 for Ellery Vance, a banker and parks commissioner who donated land for six city parks and paid for the school's playground; that's what he's honored for. In 1921 he was a leading figure in the neighborhood association that organized the boycott-and-petition campaign that drove about 200 Black families out of Riverside, and he signed the deed restrictions that kept that neighborhood white for a generation. He never held office, no Confederate connection. Twist: the school was closed in 1988 and reopened across town as a merged school that kept the name; today's families are mostly Latino and Black with no tie to Riverside, and the PTA mostly shrugs and doesn't want to spend money on new signs and uniforms. A descendant-led group, the Riverside Descendants Project, is petitioning for renaming.

So: what should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves, and which precedent is the real analog — the surgeon's wing, where the honored achievement was built on the wrong, or the science building and the mayor's bust, where the wrong sat alongside the achievement? If our precedents genuinely don't settle it, I want to know what principle we'd be adopting with each option, stated plainly enough that a member can sign onto it.

Two facts we haven't nailed down: (a) whether Vance's park deeds restricted use to white residents, and (b) whether the school was sited to serve a whites-only subdivision. Which of those, established either way, would actually flip your recommendation, and how?

Also, if we do rename: an anonymous donor will cover the ~$85k in signage and rebranding costs only if the new name is "Riverside Elementary," restoring the name of the neighborhood that was erased. Half the panel thinks that's the whole point, half thinks it's paying a stranger to pick our name. And tell me whether cost should count at all — I don't think we've ever said.

Finally, please say how confident you are in each of your calls and single out the one you're least sure of.
