I write about science and ideas for a newspaper, and I am finishing a piece on the current fashion for treating markets as knowledge machines: prediction markets on election outcomes, on whether a headline study will replicate, on what a central bank will do next. Almost every source I interview reaches for the same picture — the Hayekian one, from his 1945 essay in the American Economic Review, in which the price system is compared to a communications network that pools dispersed, local, partly contradictory bits of knowledge into a few publicly readable numbers, so that each participant needs to know almost nothing beyond the movement of a few pointers.

My sources use it to license a good deal: that a price is the best available estimate of the truth about the event in question; that a market which has gone quiet has finished its work and settled on an answer; that heavy volume means a great deal of information is being folded in; and that the rest of us — journalists, regulators, expert panels — should defer to the number rather than to our own deliberations.

What bothers me is that the technical literature I have been reading seems to point the other way. The results usually called no-trade theorems say that traders who start from common priors and are commonly known to be rational should not trade with one another at all, and that once you add the thought that a counterparty's willingness to trade is itself evidence about what he knows, the market can seize up entirely. There is also the companion paradox: if prices fully reflected everything worth knowing, no one would have any incentive to pay for the information that makes them reflect it, so a market can only be informative to the extent that it leaves some profitable ignorance in place. On that reading, the analogy's ideal case — everyone informed, everyone agreed — is a network with nothing left to transmit, and real markets trade constantly for reasons (liquidity needs, risk-sharing, hedging, noise) that have nothing to do with what anyone believes.

So I would like an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on markets. Please set out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the senders, the messages, the channel, the code, the noise, the pointers, the engineer reading the dials — and then where the mapping breaks. Separate the breaks that matter for the uses my sources make of it from the ones that are merely decorative. I am especially interested in what the network picture quietly imports that a market lacks: faithful transmission, an engineer who designed it, a shared code, a clean separation between the content of a message and the sender's reason for sending it, a reading that is free and does not itself alter the thing measured, and traffic as a measurable quantity of information.

Then the four conclusions: does each survive once the disanalogies are on the table? In particular, is a silent market evidence of a settled consensus or of a seized-up one — and is that ambiguity even a disanalogy, given that a silent telephone line is likewise ambiguous between nothing to report and a broken cable, or does it become one because the network offers a diagnostic (you can test the line) while the market arguably does not? If there is any observable that would let a reporter tell a settled market from a frozen one — depth, spread, the pattern of quotes rather than of trades — say what it is and what it can and cannot establish.

Two cases I would like run through the audit. First: a political prediction market whose volume collapses in the final week before an election while the price barely moves; is the silence the instrument finishing its computation, or the mechanism jamming? Second: a thin market on whether a phase-three trial will meet its primary endpoint, where a single large order moves the price twenty points; epistemically, what has happened, and what would I be implying if I wrote that the market now puts the odds at X?

Finally, say what the price entitled me to write in a newspaper. When I write that the market thinks there is a seventy per cent chance of some outcome, what am I actually claiming — whose beliefs, under what assumptions about risk and wealth, about a which set of traders — and what would make the sentence misleading even if the number is exact? If a different analogy carries the defensible part better, give it and say what it gives up.
