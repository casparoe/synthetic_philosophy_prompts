I host a small podcast on philosophy of social science, and next weeks guest is a self-described neo-historicist. His claim: now that we have big longitudinal historical datasets we can finally state genuine testable laws of history — his favorite example is a law linking elite overproduction plus fiscal strain to state breakdown about a generation later — and he's rehearsed on Popper. His standard move against the argument from the unpredictability of future knowledge is that it only rules out detailed prophecy: his laws don't care what gets discovered, only that discoveries keep arriving at some rate and diffuse at some rate, and those rates can be trended.

Give me eight or nine interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give — I don't want anything that lets him just restate the view. At least one has to press the trend-versus-law distinction (Popper's point that extrapolating a statistical trend isn't stating a law, and that the forecasts that actually work are conditional on institutions and technology staying put). One on falsification given the one-shot problem: what observation would really count against his laws when history doesn't get rerun — he'll probably reach for cross-societal comparison or natural experiments, so hit that. One on whether his laws are really ceteris paribus laws with the frame smuggled into the antecedents, and whether that's cheating or just how every special science works. One on self-fulfilling and self-defeating forecasts: if the law is published and believed, does it still hold, and is that a bug in the law or a scope condition. And one on the political payoff — if the forecast really is reliable, does Popper's argument for piecemeal engineering still go through, or does reliability itself start licensing large-scale social restructuring?

Keep it to nine questions and under 650 words total, and put first whichever question you'd have me open with.
