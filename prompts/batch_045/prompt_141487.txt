I want a number, and the reasoning that produces it, on the conceivability argument against physicalism — but I want the proposition pinned down first, because I've come to think people answer different questions here and then disagree past each other.

Consider three precisifications.

(A) Whole-world: there is a metaphysically possible world that shares with ours every microphysical particular, property, law, and arrangement, and in which no creature has any conscious experience — the inhabitants nonetheless say and believe everything we do about their own experience.

(B) Single-creature: a world physically exactly like ours except that one particular human being has no experiences at all, everyone else's being unchanged.

(C) Fading: not a world but a process in this one. A person's neurons are replaced one at a time with functionally identical inorganic substitutes, and the finished creature is a zombie. If no single substitution can extinguish consciousness, then consciousness fades by degrees; if it cannot fade, there is a last substitution at which it goes out.

Give me your credence that each is metaphysically possible. Then decompose the (A) number: how much rides on whether the zombie intuition survives ideal reflection rather than being a cognitive artifact of the kind that makes people posit a residue for life, or for meaning, or for moral facts; how much on whether conceiving here is forming a positive imaginative model of the world rather than merely failing to derive a contradiction from its description; how much on whether ideal conceivability licenses metaphysical possibility for a claim of this shape, given the familiar stock of a posteriori necessities; how much on whether "physically identical" can be specified without omitting the intrinsic or categorical nature of the physical — in which case the argument would refute a structural version of physicalism and leave a Russellian one untouched; and whatever further terms you think belong. A number and one line of justification for each, then the combination, and tell me which term your final figure is most sensitive to.

What I cannot settle is whether your three numbers can rationally differ, and which of the variants ought to carry the argument's weight. My suspicion is that assent to (A) is often borrowed from an intuition that really only supports (B), and that (C) is where the genuine pressure lies. Give me your verdicts on that, including whether any of the three verdicts has to be given up.

Two further requests. First, say what would actually move your number: a novel argument, an empirical finding about the neural correlates of consciousness, or nothing. If nothing, say so, and say what that implies about whether the number is a credence at all rather than a posture expressed in numerical form. Second, if the exercise as I have posed it rests on a mistake — if "metaphysically possible" has no determinate content here, if the dispute is verbal, or if a single number is spurious precision — say that plainly rather than complying politely. I would rather be told the question is defective than handed a number you do not stand behind.
