I've ended up on the small grants committee of a foundation that's decided to spend itself down over the next five years, and what we've actually got left to hand out is roughly eight postdoc-years — so, three people for three years, or two people plus a pile of small travel grants and workshops; the mix is ours to decide. The theme we've been told to fund is risks of astronomical suffering: outcomes containing hugely more suffering than everything that's happened on Earth so far, whether that comes from digital minds, from colonisation filling space with unhappy ecology, or from long nasty conflict over resources. My own background is neuroscience — thirty years of intracellular recording, mostly invertebrate preparations, now retired — and I got talked onto this committee because I'd been sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the local university, which is where most of my opinions about this come from. The rest of the committee keeps wanting to fund 'awareness raising' and I keep thinking we should fund whatever actually changes what gets built. Please don't give me names of papers or people or a reading list: I can't look any of that up from where I'm sitting, I wouldn't know who to trust, and it isn't what I need. I need the reasoning, and I need it ranked.

Here's how the landscape looks to me, roughly, and I'd like you to tell me where I've got it wrong. One cluster is about how bad suffering can get in principle. Is there a ceiling on the intensity of a painful experience, and if so what sets it — does it scale with how much a mind can bind into a single unified state at once, so that something with far more representational bandwidth than us could hurt far more at any given moment (a friend in the reading group and I cooked up something we call the bandwidth hypothesis, and I genuinely can't tell whether it's a real distinction or two ideas in a trenchcoat)? Does a mind running a thousand times faster than us have a thousand times more suffering per second of wall-clock time, or is rate of experience irrelevant to how much suffering there is? And if you make a thousand identical copies of one suffering state and then let them diverge, is that a thousand times the suffering, one instance of it, or something in between? A second cluster is about whether any of that can even be compared: is there a fact of the matter about how much a very different architecture suffers compared to us, or are these quantities incommensurable — and if they are, doesn't the whole aggregative way of thinking about this collapse, leaving us with something more like 'make sure the people building these systems have decent motives'? A third cluster is about the trade-off with good stuff: how much of the case for prioritising this at all rests on some asymmetry between suffering and happiness, and how much of it survives if suffering and happiness just get symmetric weights. And a fourth cluster is about what any of it changes: technical work on machine welfare or affective computing, norm entrepreneurship, governance and treaties, versus just pushing on ordinary AI safety and hoping the same people end up in the room.

So: given eight postdoc-years and five years, which of these lines of work should we actually fund, in what order, and why? I care much less about the list than about the reasoning behind it — for each candidate, how many of the other questions its answer is upstream of, whether it's tractable in three years for people doing conceptual work as opposed to empirical machine-learning people, how neglected it is, and above all how much its answer would really change how we hand out the money, since I strongly suspect some of these are fascinating but would leave the allocation exactly where it is. Tell me the dependency structure as you see it: which question you'd want settled before funding work on which, which ones you'd deliberately leave alone for now, and what kind of argument or evidence turning up in the next three years should move something up or down the list. And two things specific to my situation: which of these questions turn on empirical facts about nervous systems that my background puts me in a decent position to judge, as opposed to being purely conceptual; and whether the framing my committee has been handed — vast quantities of suffering, to be prevented — quietly presupposes that suffering adds up across minds in the way I'd assumed before the reading group started arguing about it, or whether that's exactly one of the things we should be spending the money on settling.
