I do data and reporting for a mental health trust — not a clinician, not a philosopher — and I basically build the dashboards the commissioners stare at in their monthly meetings. There's a pattern I keep running into in that work and I was arguing about it with a colleague afterwards without getting anywhere, so I'd like to know whether it has a name.

The pattern is this. A diagnostic label stays in place for years and the number attached to it moves in a nice smooth line, so everybody reads the graph as the same thing, just more of it. But when you look at what is actually going on underneath, the thing being counted at the start of the line and the thing being counted at the end of it are not obviously the same thing at all. Autism is the case I know best: as far as I understand it, the big manual revision a decade or so back folded Asperger's and a leftover catch-all category into a single autism spectrum category and split off a separate communication disorder, so some people who would have been counted before are counted under a different name now and some aren't counted at all; the structured assessment instruments have gone through versions; school and service thresholds move. And the reported rate still climbs steadily across the whole period, and our dashboard draws it as one continuous curve for one quantity. Adult ADHD feels similar from where I sit: a clinician friend told me the age-of-onset requirement in the criteria has been loosened across editions and that most of the recent surge rides on self-report screening questionnaires people fill in before anyone sees them (I haven't checked either claim), and what turns up in clinic now doesn't look much like the kids in the older descriptions — but the label and the trend line don't flinch. And then there's the example that made me think the real tell is the smoothness rather than the rise: IQ tests, where I'm told the tests get re-standardised every so often so the average stays fixed at 100, which means the headline number is stable by construction while raw performance drifts. So sometimes the number barely moves and sometimes it climbs, and in both cases I suspect the stability, or the smoothness, is being manufactured by quietly re-cutting the yardstick rather than by anything being tracked faithfully.

A friend doing a PhD in philosophy of medicine said something about looping when I described this — categories and the people classified feeding into each other — and mentioned concept creep separately as well. Both sounded close, but neither seemed to be quite it: looping, the way I understood her, is about the labelled people changing how they behave or how they understand themselves and the category then being revised in response, whereas what I keep seeing is more like the label and the official definition staying put while the operational criteria and the instruments drift underneath, apparently so that the system can go on producing a usable number. She could well have been describing it too broadly, or I could have misunderstood. So: does this specific thing have an established name, in philosophy of medicine, philosophy of science, psychometrics or wherever — and if not, can you coin and define one for me? I dont just want a label. I want to know what exactly the phenomenon is, what it isn't, and where its edges are: how it differs from the nearest concepts you can think of (including whether looping or concept creep really do cover it and I've got them wrong), and what counts as a genuine instance versus a lookalike — an honest re-validation of an instrument against new evidence, an openly announced change of definition, a category getting wider because advocates pushed for it, people being shunted between two categories so both sets of numbers look better. In the end I have to write about half a page for our commissioners explaining why the prevalence curve on their dashboard isn't measuring what it looks like it's measuring, and I need the distinctions sharp enough to survive a skeptical colleague at the meeting. Please don't just point me at a literature or tell me which author to go read — I can't chase references right now, so make the argument in your own terms.
