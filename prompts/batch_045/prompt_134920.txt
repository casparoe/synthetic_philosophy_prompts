I'm writing a novel set maybe fifteen years out, mostly aboard the barges of a small freight co-op working a river valley in central Europe — the fleet is what the characters call crewed-but-mostly-autonomous: there's always a licensed skipper aboard because the law still demands one, but on a long crossing she mostly sleeps, and the vessel runs itself. The book opens with an accident: one of the co-op's boats, in fully delegated mode at night, cuts across the channel in front of a small passenger vessel and four people die. The detail I've invented, and that I really want the argument to engage with rather than slide off, is that nothing was opaque and nothing malfunctioned. Every subsystem performed exactly to specification, no one was negligent, no training data was bad, no goal was misspecified. What produced the maneuver was an interaction between three separately certified layers — collision avoidance, a scheduling layer that was optimizing for a lock slot, and a low-level thruster arbiter — each of whose timing behavior was individually tested and documented, in a combination nobody had thought to simulate because nobody had thought of the combination. I keep worrying this collapses back into the usual story about inscrutable models, so if it does, say so in the scene.

The scene I keep flubbing: Dita and Malko are stuck for a night on a berth above a closed lock, waiting for the channel to reopen, and they argue about the accident. Dita is the co-op's safety lead, ex-union, quietly furious; Malko is a retired maritime-claims lawyer who now advises the co-op's insurers. They're old friends and neither is a fool. Dita's view is that this is a real gap and not a puzzle about where to point: no one had the kind of control over that outcome that blame requires, and the honest response is to stop hunting for a culprit and build backward-looking practices that don't need one — answerability without blame, compensation out of a common fund, an inquiry whose point is the next accident rather than the last one — because the institution's hunger for somebody to punish is exactly what will keep producing cover-ups and scapegoats. She also thinks there's a second gap people don't name: even when you do find someone to answer, what the public wants from them is harsher than anything that would be fair. Malko's view is that the gap is an artifact of taking retrospective blame for a foreseen outcome as the paradigm of responsibility in the first place: responsibility is a matter of the role you took up and what you owed in virtue of taking it up, so nothing is missing here at all — the party who put that system on the water for their own benefit and can be made to answer for it is perfectly identifiable, and the only live question is how demanding we make that answer. Please write it as a real argument, 900–1200 words, with both of them stating the other's position in a form the other would accept before going at it, and with at least two moments where one of them actually shifts — concedes something, or restates their claim in a weakened form — instead of just reasserting. Don't let me get a Socratic slaughter; I genuinely don't know who should win. Concrete texture is welcome (the night, the wheelhouse, river traffic on the radio) but the atmosphere shouldn't eat the argument.
