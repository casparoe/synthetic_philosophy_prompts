I recently attended a talk on existential risk in which the speaker closed a slide arguing that humanity's odds of a long future are poor with the familiar one-liner "Where is everybody?" The work it was doing there was specific: the apparent emptiness of the sky was being offered as evidence that civilizations which reach roughly our technological stage almost never go on to become visible across interstellar distances, and hence that the hard part of the great filter lies ahead of us rather than behind us. I want to know exactly what that question has to be asserting for that inference to be licensed.

Please set out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims the question could be making. I would like at least the following distinctions drawn, plus any others that turn out to matter:

- what is claimed to be absent — probes or settlements in our own solar system, deliberate messages aimed at us, inadvertent leakage such as broadcast or industrial waste heat, or large-scale astroengineering of the kind that would have shown up incidentally in archival all-sky survey data;
- the temporal scope of the absence — nobody here now, nobody ever within our past light cone, or nothing anywhere at any time;
- what the implicit "should have been here by now" rests on — the observation that crossing the galaxy at even a small fraction of light speed takes millions of years against billions of years of potentially habitable galactic history, versus assumptions about how cheap, attractive and durable expansion actually is for the civilizations doing it.

For each reading, say whether it borders on the trivial, whether it is a substantive and defensible empirical claim, or whether we currently have little or no evidence for it — noting where relevant that much of what gets called silence is really non-detection at sensitivities set by astronomy done for other purposes. Most importantly, say for each reading whether it would actually support the speaker's conclusion that the filter is ahead of us, or whether it is equally well explained by a story that implies nothing about our own future.

Present the menu as a table, with a column for the reading, one for its epistemic status, and one for whether it carries the inference. Lead with your bottom line — which reading the speaker was most likely relying on, and whether it survives — in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of my question.
