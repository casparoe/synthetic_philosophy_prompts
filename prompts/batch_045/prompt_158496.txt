I am drafting a short position paper on sortition for a working group on democratic reform, and before I circulate it I would like an audit of the commitments I have assembled. My worry is not that any single one of them is outlandish — most are standard among people who favor randomly selected deliberative bodies — but that I have bundled together views whose usual motivations pull in opposite directions, or that I have included a verdict about institutional design that my own stated principles cannot actually deliver.

Here is the set, as I have written it down.

1. Diagnosis. The central defect of electoral democracy is that elections select for ambition, money, name recognition and rhetorical skill, so that representatives are systematically unrepresentative, and that in a large electorate any individual's causal influence on the outcome is negligible. Elections are therefore a poor realization of political equality.

2. Equal chance. What makes sortition just, at bottom, is that every citizen has an equal ex ante chance of holding political power. The lottery is the democratic default; elections are the institution that owes a special justification.

3. The mirror. A stratified random sample is authoritative because it mirrors the population — not merely in demographics but in the distribution of the opinions and interests relevant to the issue, so that what the assembly wants tracks what the demos would want under decent conditions.

4. Transformation. The point of a minipublic is that preferences are endogenous to the process. Balanced briefing materials, adversarial expert panels, facilitated small-group discussion and enough time produce a considered judgment that is not the aggregate of the opinions members walked in with. I do not want the assembly merely to register opinion.

5. Experts. Assemblies should hear specialists and then make up their own minds: experts supply causal and technical claims, members supply the value trade-offs. No expert veto.

6. No filtering. No competence tests, no knowledge requirements, no exclusion for ignorance. Service should be compelled like jury duty with lost earnings replaced, and members should be barred for several years afterwards from standing for election or taking paid advocacy work.

7. Openness. Proceedings should be livestreamed and all materials public, because the legitimacy gain comes from citizens watching people like themselves reason about the issue.

8. Procedural authority. Where the draw was fair, the materials balanced and the deliberation genuine, I should treat the output as authoritative for me even in a case where I am fairly confident the recommendation is substantively wrong.

9. Near-term design. Sortition bodies should be advisory. The two models I have in mind are a panel whose only output is a short citizens' statement printed in the official voter pamphlet alongside a ballot measure, and an assembly whose recommendation on a constitutional question is then put to a national referendum. I would add agenda power — the ability to force a vote or place a proposal on the ballot — but not the power to enact.

10. End state. Eventually I want a randomly selected revising chamber with a suspensive veto: it can send legislation back and delay it, but not permanently block it, with continuous partial rotation of members.

What I would like from you:

Go through this and tell me which commitments are genuinely in tension, which pairs only look tense and dissolve once the claims are stated more carefully, and where a verdict of mine is one my own principles cannot underwrite. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and for each say what exactly would have to be given up and what that would cost me — in particular whether dropping a commitment collapses my case for sortition altogether or merely weakens one argument among several.

Two specific things I want answered. First, I hold both that the sample is authoritative because it mirrors the population and that the whole value of the exercise is that deliberation changes what members think; I also hold both that equal chance of office is the fundamental justification and that the sample should be stratified so as to reduce sampling error. Tell me whether these come apart, and if so which claim is doing the real work. Second, I hold that the considered judgment of a deliberating sample is better than raw public opinion, and also that the decision should usually go back to the electorate or to elected representatives. Say whether that combination is stable or whether I have built a deliberative microcosm and then handed the decision to the undeliberated macrocosm whose pathologies motivated the whole proposal.

If I had to guess where the trouble is, I would guess the advisory-versus-binding question and the role of experts, since those are the ones people actually argue about. Please find the problems rather than confirm my guesses, and finish by telling me which single revision would clean up the most.
