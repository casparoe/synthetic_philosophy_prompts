I am a retired geophysicist who, having spent forty years on quite other matters, now sits in on a fortnightly reading group in philosophy at my old university, and I have acquired — to the evident irritation of the regulars — the habit of asking what exactly is being asserted by the short, confident sentences with which people close a discussion off. Last week the subject was the St. Petersburg gamble: a fair coin is tossed until it first lands tails, and if that happens on the k-th toss the house pays you 2^{k−1} pounds, so that every term of the expectation contributes exactly half a pound and the expected payoff diverges. When I said that the plain fact that no sane person would pay more than a few pounds for one go at this had not, so far as I could see, been answered by anything anyone had said, a young postdoc waved the whole difficulty away with the remark that in the long run we are all dead — a line of Keynes's, I believe, from his monetary tract of 1923 — and the group passed on to something else.

I take it the thought was meant to be that the paradox is an artefact: expected monetary value looks compulsory only if one imagines the bet repeated indefinitely, and no one has an indefinite number of bets in front of them. But I can distinguish several quite different claims that sentence might be making, and they look to me to have very different prospects. One that occurred to me on the walk home: the enormous payoffs are precisely the ones requiring an enormous number of tosses, and an enormous number of tosses takes an enormous amount of time, so if you discount the payoff for the delay — or, less delicately, if you are dead before the interesting cases arise — the expected discounted value comes out finite; on my arithmetic, with a constant discount factor δ per toss, it is δ/(2(1−δ)), which is finite but for δ near one is not small, so this defuses the infinity without restoring the intuition that the bet is worth little. Then there is the separate and, I suspect, more powerful point that a house with finite funds cannot pay beyond some ceiling: I make the expectation about sixteen pounds even if the house can lay hands on a billion. And there is a third thing I only half remember having read, to the effect that if one buys a block of n plays the fair price per play grows only logarithmically in n, so that a million plays are worth surprisingly little each.

What I want from you is a disambiguation rather than a solution to the paradox — I have read about diminishing marginal utility and log utility, and I do not need that rehearsed. Please set out exactly five distinct precise claims that the line in the long run we are all dead could be being used to make in this setting, ordered from the most platitudinous to the most substantive, each in no more than two sentences, each tagged in a word or two as trivial, true but beside the point, substantive, or false, and each with an indication of whether it would actually licence the postdoc's dismissal or whether that dismissal rests on some further claim the line itself does not state. Number them one to five and give me nothing else: no preamble, no closing summary, and no reading list.
