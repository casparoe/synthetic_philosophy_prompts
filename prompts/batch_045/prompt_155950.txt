I'm defending my thesis prospectus on Thursday and I want to be ready for the question period, because I think the room is going to be hostile in three different directions at once. Background on how I got here: I had a three-hour argument with my friend who works in market research and does audience segmentation all day, and who says generational cohorts are just astrology for people who own spreadsheets. I found myself defending them much harder than I expected to, and the position I ended up with is the one I'm now stuck with. The thesis: generational cohorts are real, but not as birth-year kinds. More precisely: (1) the standard operationalization — contiguous, non-overlapping birth-year bands with hard cutoffs — doesn't merely approximate something, it reifies; there's no discontinuity in whatever causal process is supposed to make a Boomer a Boomer at the years the sources pick, and the cutoffs differ between sources anyway, so any finding that depends on where the line sits is an artifact of a convention; (2) what makes a cohort real when it is real is shared formative exposure — going through the same historically specific event or institutional regime while inside a plasticity window (I use roughly 15 to 25, leaning on the impressionable-years idea from political socialization plus the usual stuff about habit formation and identity consolidation), so cohorts should be individuated by exposure profiles rather than by dates; (3) it follows that cohorts can overlap, that membership can be fuzzy and non-contiguous, that they come in wildly different sizes, and that a "generation" can be empty in a given country or even a given neighborhood — one person can belong to two, or to none; (4) and on top of all that, the standard age-period-cohort identification problem means a lot of the published literature reporting a "generation effect" from repeated cross-sections never identified one.

My constructive proposal, which is the part I expect to get shredded: drop the birth-year brackets and index people by what I'm calling exposure-anchored cohorting — a vector of which specified events and institutional regimes a person was inside the plasticity window for, with weights for duration and for how old they were at onset — while treating the popular names (Boomer, Millennial, Gen Z) as folk taxonomies that are genuinely useful for coordination, the way "the sixties" or "the postwar settlement" is useful, but not as candidates for scientific kinds.

The committee is three people plus an audience of maybe a dozen grad students, half of whom think generations are obviously real and half of whom think there obviously fake. One committee member is a quantitative sociologist who runs APC models and is allergic to cohort-effect claims, thinks most of the literature is unidentified, and I suspect will say my exposure talk is just cohort with extra steps and more researcher degrees of freedom. One is a life-course psychologist who works on sensitive periods and will have opinions both about my 15–25 window being crude and about whether shared exposure produces shared anything. One is a philosopher of social science who works on social kinds and construction, and who I think is going to press on whether the exposure account is anything more than a homeostatic-property-cluster story in which the homeostasis does no work, or whether it quietly collapses into a conventional grouping whose boundaries I've just made harder to draw.

What I want from you is a prep sheet, not a defense of the view: the objections I'll actually get, roughly in the order they'll come, phrased the way those three would phrase them in their own vocabulary — not the objections I'd get from a metaphysics seminar or from a marketing conference — each with a short reply, a note on what I should concede up front versus what I have to hold or the thesis dies, and a note on where my reply is weakest. The APC identification objection from the quantitative person I can already predict, so tell me what comes after it. Include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks. And be blunt about which objection has enough force that I should be prepared to give up part of the thesis rather than answer it — and tell me what would change your mind about that: which finding or argument would make you think exposure-anchored cohorting isn't actually an improvement on birth-year bands, and where you think your own advice here is most likely to be wrong.
