You're an institutional economist by training who's spent way too long thinking about collective action problems — brainstorm for a bit before you write, and think carefully about where the "Moloch / multipolar trap" framing actually *breaks* rather than just applying it.

Context: I run the secretariat for a voluntary industry standard — about 60 member firms, mostly mid-size consumer web and app companies — covering what they promise users about data retention and deletion. Twice a year, like clockwork, someone tables a motion: "we're in a race to the bottom on X, we need a binding floor in the standard." Sometimes that's right. Sometimes it's just the firms who already paid for compliance pulling the ladder up behind them. Sometimes it's a genuine collective action problem that isn't a race at all. I get one working-group slot (90 minutes, mixed-expertise room, half of them wont have heard the phrase "multipolar trap"), and then I have to write a one-page recommendation: open a drafting process, do nothing, or escalate to a regulator.

So: turn the multipolar-trap / Moloch idea into a procedure I can actually run in that slot. Concretely, I need it to:

- Separate a genuine trap from the lookalikes. Two distinctions I keep tripping over: (a) whether behavior actually changed — firms defecting — or whether the *population* changed, i.e. the compliant ones lost share or quit and what's left just looks worse, which would be an attrition artifact rather than a race; and (b) whether one firm's defection makes other firms' defection more rational independently of anyone's preferences. That cascade property is my rough guess at what makes something Moloch rather than a plain prisoners' dilemma, but I'm not confident, and I want you to tell me plainly if I've got it wrong. Also flag the cases where the "race" is really a principal–agent problem inside member firms, or a values disagreement about what counts as the bottom, or a race to the top that only looks bad from where I'm sitting.
- Then, for the genuine ones, match the trap to what we could actually do: changing the payoff gradient vs. adding a cliff, making defection visible, sequencing moves, shrinking the relevant population, exit or venue change, handing it to a regulator — and be explicit about which of those a voluntary body with no enforcement power has any authority to attempt.
- Mark the steps where the room has to make a values call rather than a factual one — whose welfare counts, whether the good equilibrium is worth the enforcement cost — and stop the procedure there instead of pretending it's a further question of fact.
