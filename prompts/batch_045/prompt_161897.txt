I am a novelist, and my manuscript has stalled on a single scene. The book follows a daughter who left the industrial town where three generations of her family worked, and the mother who stayed. The crisis is a conversation in which the daughter explains why she will not come home to care for her ageing mother, and the mother explains why the question of coming home was never the daughter's to decide by herself. Every draft I have written of this scene has the flatness of a headline: the daughter speaks the language of choice, authorship and self-definition; the mother speaks the language of obligation, belonging and inheritance; and each leaves the room untouched by the other. I want to write the version in which neither woman is a caricature, and I suspect I can only do that once I know, precisely, what the argument between them actually is.

So I would like you to draft the joint statement that two philosophers could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration: one defending a voluntarist individualism, on which a person is the author of her own ends, obligations are generated by consent, by benefits voluntarily accepted, or by what a person has made her own, and communities are associations or instruments that a person may join, use, revise or leave; the other defending a constitutive relationism, on which a person is partly constituted by the roles she occupies within a community, some obligations attach to those roles independently of any act of choice, and the community is not merely a means to her ends but the condition under which she has ends, reasons and an intelligible life at all. Do not cite anyone, name no authorities, and give me no reading list; I want the argument, not its bibliography. Both positions should be given at their strongest, and neither should be allowed to win by defining its own view into the other's premises.

I have two scenes I want the statement indexed to. The first is the refusal to return: an adult child who has built a life elsewhere declines a filial demand, and the demand is made in the vocabulary of role rather than of contract. The second is the bound dissenter: a family firm, or a town meeting, settles something by a decision its members regard as the decision of the group, and one member who argued against it is then treated as bound by it and answerable for it. For both scenes, I want the document to say what each position is committed to saying about the case, and where the two descriptions come apart.

There are several distinctions I have found myself reaching for while drafting, and I would like the collaborators forced to take a position on each of them, and to say which ones are doing real work and which are only redescription. Treat these as stipulations I am supplying, not as positions from the literature:

- the substitutability test: an obligation attaches to a role rather than to a person if a competent stranger placed in that role would owe the same thing, with no residue left over. What does each side predict this test will show about the filial case, and does either accept it as a fair test?
- attribution without authorship: whether a person can be genuinely answerable for an action or omission whose governing end she did not choose, without that answerability being a form of alienation.
- retrospective authorization: the adult's later endorsement, or repudiation, of the formative upbringing she underwent. Is endorsement a validation of what shaped her, or a symptom of it, and is repudiation evidence of freedom or of damage? This matters to me most of all, since my novel's ending depends on whether a change of mind in this direction can be represented as a rational conversion rather than as acculturation or as rebellion.
- exit costs: whether the fact that leaving a community is expensive, shameful or devastating shows that the community has a claim on the person, or that it has power over her, and what the two sides would each predict about a community from which exit is costless.
- irreducibility of "we": whether a complete specification of a person's reasons in the bound-dissenter case can contain an ineliminable first-person-plural element, or whether every such reason can be analysed without residue into facts about her own ends plus expectations about others.
- the two models I keep sketching in my notebook: obligations as entries in a ledger, individually acquired, auditable and dischargeable; versus obligations as grammar, unchosen, constitutive, and the condition of there being anything to say at all. Is there a genuine incompatibility here, or are the two models describing different slices of moral life?
- the double-ascription problem: whether one and the same act can be fully mine and fully ours without the attribution being double counted or the agency split.

What I want at the end of the collaboration is this. First, a numbered section of claims both would sign: about what a person is, about what obligation is, about what the two scenes above uncontroversially contain, and about what an adequate account of either scene would have to explain. Second, a disagreements table, with each row giving the individualist's formulation in wording she would accept for her own view, the relationist's formulation in wording he would accept for his, and a note on whether the disagreement in that row is substantive or merely verbal — and if you conclude that part of the dispute is about words, say which part, and what the underlying substantive question is that the verbal fight was standing in for. Third, a section on cruxes: for each crux, the case, test or argument that both sides agree would bear on it, and each side's prediction about that case written down in advance. Fourth, a short closing section in which each side states what it would count as evidence or argument against itself, and anything both would accept as actually settling the matter.

If you can, give me one further thing: a single sentence, in the voice of each position, that my characters could speak in the last chapter without either of them sounding like a slogan.
