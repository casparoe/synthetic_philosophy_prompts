I am writing the final paper for a seminar on the ethics of artificial intelligence, and I keep running into one move that everyone in the room treats as settling the question of whose values an aligned system should serve. The move is an analogy: an aligned AI should be like a liberal state. It stands above the competing conceptions of the good held by the people it serves, it refuses to rank them, and it resolves conflict among them by fair procedure rather than by taking a substantive stand. The conclusion people draw from it is that we do not need to solve the hard part of value aggregation: we should not pick a value theory and train it in, we should build something like a constitution plus a fair voting rule, a neutral aggregator, and let the values come from the people who are served.

I want that analogy audited rather than praised or dismissed, and I want the audit keyed to the work it is doing in my seminar, which is to license the neutral-aggregator conclusion.

First, the mapping. I want an explicit correspondence table, and I want you to say which of several candidate mappings is the one actually carrying the argument, because I suspect the analogy slides between them. Is the model the state, or is it the constitution, or the civil service, or the referee? Are users citizens, or voters, or subjects, or clients? What plays the role of territory, of coercion, of elections, of constitutional entrenchment, of the veil of ignorance, of public reason? My guess is that the analogy is only intuitive while the model is being read as an impersonal arbiter and stops being intuitive the moment one notices that the same system is also the thing that acts.

Second, the case I think the analogy conflates. In the single-user case, a system serves one person, so the conflict it must resolve is inside one life rather than between lives: incommensurable projects of a single agent, uncertainty about which of them she will endorse in five years, weakness of will, values she holds but does not endorse. In the general-purpose case, by contrast, the system's actions bear on people who never chose it and never will. Liberal neutrality is a doctrine about a bounded demos that authorizes coercion over itself, and neither of my two cases has that structure. Please say whether the analogy survives being split this way, or whether it was only ever plausible for a third case, a many-user system whose users are exactly the people it affects.

Third, the features of the source that the target lacks, and which of them the conclusion leans on. Here are the candidates I have; add the ones I am missing and then separate the ones that matter for the neutral-aggregator conclusion from the ones that are merely colourful. A state has a monopoly over a territory, which is what makes legitimacy urgent in the first place. Exit from a state is expensive enough that voice is the only real option, whereas switching to a different model is cheap and there will be many models, so pluralism of values might be handled by diversity across systems rather than by neutrality inside one. A state is authorized by the people it binds, while a system prompt is written for users by a firm. And a state must choose one policy for everyone, while a system facing a single user could decline to aggregate altogether and instead help her work out what she wants, which is a response the state is not usually taken to have.

Fourth, the reverse direction, which I have not seen anyone raise. Does the analogy quietly import knowledge conditions that fail? Neutrality is attractive partly as a second-best under disagreement and partly as a way of not exploiting information one does not have. But an AI deployed at scale has a vastly more detailed model of each person's psychology than any state has of any citizen, including of what she would repudiate. If the ignorance that motivates neutrality is absent, does that make substantive value commitments more defensible rather than less? I want the answer argued, not gestured at.

Fifth, the verdict. Does build a neutral aggregator and do not pick a value theory survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy doing all the work? If there is a defensible residue, say whether it is better reconstructed as a constraint on the procedure by which values are chosen rather than on which values may be chosen, and whether a better analogy carries it: the civil servant bound by role morality, the lawyer who must pursue a client's aims within professional constraints, the doctor exercising judgment about a patient's good, the referee, the public library. Close with one paragraph stating what you take to be the most that the state analogy can honestly be asked to support.
