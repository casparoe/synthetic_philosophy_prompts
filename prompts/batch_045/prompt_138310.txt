I sit on the oversight board of a mid-sized city housing authority that is about to deploy a risk score to rank tenants for scarce eviction-prevention assistance (legal aid and emergency rental funds). Three features of our setting seem to me different from the COMPAS-style recidivism case the literature standardly discusses, and I want to know how much they matter. First, the outcome we would train on — roughly, would have lost the housing absent help — is observed only for tenants whose cases actually reached court, so the label itself is a product of prior enforcement patterns and of who could be reached by outreach workers. Second, the assistance is a capped good allocated off a ranking, so what matters to a tenant is the probability of being contacted before the money runs out, not the score as such, and the meaning of a given score drifts as one moves down the list and as earlier interventions change later outcomes. Third, our board is under pressure to justify each individual decision to the tenant affected, which pulls toward a calibrated, individual-probability reading of the score, while our community advisory group demands equal rates of being missed across racial groups and our legal counsel wants that same guarantee to hold for every intersectional subgroup she can name.

Please lay out the most important implications for us of the well-known impossibility results in this area — the Kleinberg- and Chouldechova-style theorems showing that calibration and the various equal-error-rate conditions can be jointly satisfied only in degenerate cases, and the related result that satisfying a very large family of subgroup parity constraints is compatible with substantial aggregate disparity. In particular: what these results do and do not settle about which criterion we ought to choose, as opposed to the bare fact that we cannot have them all; whether the selective-label problem and the ranking-with-a-moving-cutoff problem deepen the impossibility, sidestep it, or change which of the criteria is even well defined and auditable in our setting; whether the theorems give us a genuine reason to abandon risk scoring in favour of something like randomization within a broad eligible tier, or whether that response misreads what is being claimed; and where the mathematics is simply silent, so that we do not mistake a formal result for an ethical one. Please write in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
