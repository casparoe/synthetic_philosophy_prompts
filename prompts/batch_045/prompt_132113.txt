I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background beyond the odd podcast, and me and a couple of mates got into this after a few pints and I've been chewing on it since. At work, when we pick between statistical models — churn forecasting, capacity planning, that sort of thing — we use scores that are, roughly, a fit term minus a penalty that grows with the number of parameters (AIC/BIC type things, or just cross-validation with regularisation). So when two models fit the data we already have about equally well, we systematically take the one with fewer knobs. Everyone treats this as obviously sane and I basically agree, but I can't work out what the practice is actually claiming about the world. Does it presuppose that simpler hypotheses are more likely to be true — that nature is itself simple, or at least that simplicity is some sort of truth-conducive property of a theory? Or can the whole thing be justified with no metaphysics at all, just as a trick for not overfitting and getting better predictions on next quarter's data? Those feel like completely different commitments and I genuinely can't tell which one my team is making when we drop a term from a model. One of my mates said it's neither: a model with loads of free parameters can bend itself to fit almost any dataset you threw at it, so the fact that it fits *this* one tells you very little, and the penalty is really a discount for that flexibility rather than a taste for simplicity. That sounded sharp at the time, but now I'm not sure whether it's a genuinely different idea or just the overfitting point said in a cleverer way.

The thing that makes me think there's something real going on here is that the practice seems inconsistent with itself in two places. First, my field now routinely trains enormous models with far more parameters than data points that generalise perfectly well, so we clearly don't believe the simplicity thing all the way down — and yet nobody's torn up the model-selection criteria. Second, I keep running into this idea in philosophy of science that we're entitled to believe whichever hypothesis would best *explain* the evidence, and that explanatory virtues like unification and elegance are reasons for belief in their own right, not just proxies for predictive accuracy. That sounds like what we're doing with the penalty, but with extra commitments bolted on, and I can't tell whether it's the hidden theory behind our practice or a rival to it that happens to recommend the same thing most of the time.

So: what view of evidence and explanation does this penalty-for-complexity practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory for me — the strongest version of it, not the lazy version — and separate out what the practice strictly *needs* in order to make sense from what it merely sits comfortably with. Is it underdetermined, in the sense that several quite different theories of inference would all license exactly the same behaviour, and if so where exactly? Tell me whether the two apparent inconsistencies I mentioned (the huge models, the explanatory-power talk) actually threaten it or can be absorbed. And tell me concretely what would have to change in how we do model selection if we swapped the presupposed theory for a rival — say a flat "shut up and predict" view where only out-of-sample accuracy matters, or a Bayesian view where the penalty is just a prior over models rather than a claim about simplicity being special. Please don't send me to any papers or name-drop a reading list; I want the argument, and I'd rather you explained any jargon you use than assume I know it.
