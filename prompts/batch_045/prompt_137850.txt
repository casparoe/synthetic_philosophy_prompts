I work in HR at a mid-sized manufacturing firm, and over the past year I've been helping a colleague — she's lived in this country for about eleven years, arrived on a visitor visa at twenty-two, overstayed, and has since worked, paid taxes, and raised two children here — try to get her situation regularized. Reading through the rules with her, I was struck by how internally odd the whole structure seems. Officially the position appears to be that unlawful presence creates no claim at all: the fact that you managed to stay is not supposed to count in your favour, and her lawyer told us it even matters how you got here, with entry without inspection treated differently from entering legally and overstaying. Yet the same body of law contains long-residence or 'rooting' routes, where a lease, a work history, school records and enough years can convert unlawful presence into a lawful permit. The lawyer also said that some kinds of time don't count, or count less — time in immigration detention, or a run of short renewable 'tolerated' statuses where you may stay and work but can't travel or bring your family — while time spent in an ordinary job clearly does. So the law says that mere presence is worthless, but it also seems to be keeping a very specific kind of score: not presence on the territory, but participation in ordinary social life.

A friend I was discussing this with said it's really just adverse possession, or squatters' rights: the state slept on its claim and after long enough the claim lapses. That fit badly to me — the state never meant to give anything up, it enforces constantly, and the doctrine that unlawful presence confers nothing is supposed to block exactly this result. But then I can't see what the practice does presuppose. If the state really has the kind of right over entry and membership that a private association has over its members, or an owner over her house, then what is a long-residence amnesty doing other than conceding an exception to the one right that was supposed to be the whole point? And if instead the relevant thing is that the state's authority is over a population it actually governs, so that what matters is who is subject to its laws and woven into its social life, then why does crossing a line without permission carry the weight it evidently does, and why is that weight apparently never fully erased by twenty years of residence, work and children?

I'd like a reconstruction rather than a verdict. What view of the right to exclude, of territorial rights, or of political membership does the package — the no-claim-from-unlawful-presence rule, the long-residence route, and the fact that some sorts of time count and others don't — actually presuppose, as opposed to merely sit well with? Where does the practice rest on a real distinction, and where is it an administrative or political compromise that several different theories could equally license? Is the adverse-possession framing a genuinely alternative theory or just a bad metaphor for the same one, and what would it predict differently? If we took the social-membership story seriously as the actual ground of the practice, what would have to change — would length of residence stop mattering, would tourists or posted temporary workers start accruing claims, would citizens who have lived their whole lives abroad lose theirs? Finally, please keep separate the things that people who actually work on these questions would agree on from the things that are live disputes or your own reconstruction of a rationale, and say which is which; I've read confident claims in both directions on the internet and I have no way to tell them apart.
