For my metaphysics seminar I have to hand in a one-page "where I stand on truth" statement and then get grilled on it by the rest of the group, and I'd rather find the holes now then in front of twelve people, half of whom have read much more about this than I have. Here's the package I've ended up with, roughly in the order I'd argue for it: (1) truth has no hidden nature — there is no substantive property of being true waiting to be analysed, and the whole content of the concept is given by the equivalence schema, the proposition that snow is white is true iff snow is white and likewise for every other instance. (2) That doesn't make 'true' redundant, because the word does work I couldn't do without it: infinite generalisations like 'everything the finance minister said in the hearing was true', and blind endorsement, where I want to sign on to what someone said without being able to repeat it. (3) Truth is the constitutive norm of belief — it's a real normative fact, not just a platitude, that you ought to believe only what's true, and it's this rather than any semantic machinery that makes the concept worth having around. (4) Truth also does explanatory work: when I explain why the travellers who believe there's water over that hill make it and the ones who don't die of thirst, the explanation runs through the fact that their belief is true, and I don't think that's just a façon de parler I could paraphrase away instance by instance. (5) A semantic theory for a language should take the form of a truth theory — stating truth conditions is the right way to capture what a speaker knows when they understand the language, and I want that to be a substantive project with something at stake in it, not bookkeeping. (6) On the liar, I want a gappy or paracomplete treatment rather than a hierarchy of languages, and I want to insist that this doesn't smuggle a substantive account of truth back in: the gaps are a technical device for pathological sentences, not a discovery about what truth is.

So: is this a stable set, or am I helping myself to something somewhere? I don't have a favourite among the six, I'll drop whichever is actually costing me the most, so don't tell me which one to keep — tell me what keeping each one commits me to. Concretely: go through the pairs and say which are genuinely in tension and how badly, and rank those by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad; for each real tension say what the least costly revision would be and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the set; and flag the pairs that only *look* like they clash, with the reason they don't, because I'd rather not defend against objections I don't actually face. One more thing: if any pair is in tension only given some extra premise I haven't stated, name that premise explicitly, since I suspect at least one of these clashes is inherited from something I'm assuming without noticing. If there's a single revision that cleans up more than one of the tensions at once, say what it is and what it would end up committing me to.
