I'm externing this term for Judge Whitlock on our state's intermediate appellate court, and I've been asked to draft the "which way does she vote" memo on a case that gets argued in two weeks. She's the judge everyone here calls the statutory-interpretation judge, but nobody in chambers agrees on what she's actually doing. Dan says she's a plain-meaning person who just lets old words pick up new referents as the world changes. Priya says she's a closet purposivist who stops at the edge of what the words can bear. I have a third theory I can't shake: she reads statutory words as naming a mechanism or an arrangement, and she treats a new technology as covered only when it runs on that same mechanism — a text that arrives on your residential phone line is a telephone solicitation to her, but an email is not first-class mail, even though it does the same job better. She never says any of this in so many words, so I want to test it against her record.

Her opinions that seem relevant: (1) Under the Unsolicited Solicitation Act of 1974, which bars "an unsolicited telephone solicitation to a residential subscriber," a company blasted promotional SMS messages to mobile handsets on residential family plans. She held the Act reached it, saying something to the effect that a reading which lets the same intrusion in through a different door would leave the Act a dead letter. (2) Under the Residential Tenancies Act of 1961, a rent-increase notice must be "mailed by first-class mail to the premises"; the landlord emailed a PDF to the tenant's personal address and the tenant answered "got it." She held the notice invalid and the increase void — actual receipt, she said, is a windfall for the landlord, not a reading of the statute. (3) Under the Wage Claim Act, a claim must be "a written complaint signed by the employee"; a worker filed through the agency's online portal by typing his name into a signature field after a password and a one-time code sent to his phone. She held that signed: a signature is whatever mark a person adopts as his own, and the portal tied the mark to him. Priya counts this as her purposivist case; I think the word "signed" just isn't specific enough to do any work. (4) A 1996 city ordinance bars any person from operating "a motorized vehicle on a residential street" between 10 p.m. and 6 a.m.; a company ran remote-supervised delivery robots — electric, about forty pounds, on the sidewalk, crossing at crosswalks — through a residential neighborhood at 2 a.m. She held the ordinance didn't reach them, conceding that a speaker today might well call a robot a motorized vehicle but saying the ordinance was written about trucks and cars in the roadway. (5) Our wills statute requires that a will be signed "in the physical presence of two witnesses"; during 2020 a testator signed on camera while the two witnesses watched a live video call and then signed the paper later that day. Not valid, she held, and her opinion more or less rested on the word "physical" being there to be read.

The new case: our Home Solicitation Sales Act of 1969. Section 2 defines a home solicitation sale as "any sale of consumer goods or services for a price of twenty-five dollars or more made at the buyer's residence, at which the seller or the seller's representative is present at the time the buyer signs," and gives the buyer until midnight of the third business day to cancel. There is no purpose clause or findings section anywhere in the Act. Facts: Ms. Alarcon, 71, alone at her kitchen table, clicked an ad on her tablet for a home security package — cameras plus monitoring, $4,180 — and within a minute a salesman appeared by two-way video on her 55-inch smart display. Ninety-minute pitch, five hundred dollars off if she signed before the call ended, she e-signed on the tablet at 6:40 p.m., and she emailed a cancellation two business days later. The salesman was in a call center two states away and no human being from the company was ever within forty miles of the house. The trial court assumed the written cancellation notice buried on page nine of the e-sign packet was adequate, so the only question on appeal is whether this was a home solicitation sale at all. One more thing from discovery that I can't figure out how much to weight: in roughly a third of these calls the thing on the screen isn't a person but the company's synthetic video agent, and nobody has established which kind of call Ms. Alarcon got.

So: how do you think she votes, and how confident are you? Which rule is she actually running, which of her five opinions is the closest analog, and which single further case would you put to her to tell Dan's, Priya's and my theories apart — I was thinking of one where the seller's "presence" is a telepresence robot wheeled into the living room with a remote employee's face on it, but I'm not sure that's the most diagnostic one, and I'm also not sure whether the human-versus-synthetic-agent question is the real dividing line or a distraction. If you think her record genuinely doesn't decide this, say that plainly, and tell me what principle she'd be committing herself to with each answer.
