Write a pantoum about anatta, the Buddhist denial of a permanent self. (A pantoum is a series of quatrains in which the second and fourth lines of each stanza become the first and third lines of the next, and in which the final stanza returns the poem's opening line as its closing line.) The repetitions should do the philosophical work: a line comes back, but in a new context, altered by what has intervened, so that continuity is visible on the page while identity is not. Work with the five aggregates — form, feeling, perception, volitional formations, consciousness — rather than with a soul or a hidden substratum, and do not let a persistent subject sneak back in: 'I' may appear, but never as the name of something that stays the same from stanza to stanza.

Write it as if for a student who has just objected that if there is no self, then no one acts, no one is answerable for what they did, and no one is benefited by liberation. Let the closing stanza be where the poem answers: the turn should make continuity enough — enough for memory, for regret, for consequence — without letting it collapse back into identity, and without sliding into the opposite error of saying that no one is there at all. Five quatrains, rhymed ABAB if you can manage it without padding. Someone who knows the doctrine should find it right; someone who does not should still find it a poem.
