I write about science and ideas, and last month I moderated a panel on animal minds and agency. Over dinner afterward, two of the panelists — a behavioral ecologist and a philosopher of action — got into an argument that I have not been able to referee, and I would rather not publish a write-up that quietly takes a side. The case they were fighting over is the digger wasp: she paralyzes a cricket, carries it to her burrow, leaves it on the threshold while she goes inside alone to inspect the nest, comes back out, and only then drags the prey in. If someone moves the prey a short distance away while she is inside, she finds it, hauls it back to the threshold, and then goes in to inspect again, and the cycle repeats — several times in the sober versions of the story, dozens of times or indefinitely in the ones that get repeated in books. The label for this sort of routine, purposeful-looking but apparently unmodifiable, is sphexishness. What follows is the exchange as it actually went, compressed but not softened.

(1) He opened with what he called the Uncomfortable Extrapolation. The wasp's sequence looks like planning, inspection, and reconsideration, but it is a closed loop: the same trigger fires the same routine regardless of what has already happened. That is the paradigm of mindless rigidity wearing the costume of deliberation. And the honest lesson is not about wasps: a great deal of what looks like human deliberation may be the same kind of script with more layers on top.

(2) She raised two objections. First, provenance: the canonical version entered philosophy secondhand, out of a popular book about the brain from the 1960s, and the story has gotten firmer with each retelling; the underlying observations are a nineteenth-century naturalist's notebooks, and at least one philosopher has argued that the anecdote persists because it is rhetorically useful rather than well attested. Second, the inference. Re-running the check when the world has changed is exactly what a cheap, reliable subroutine should do, provided re-checking costs less than acting on a stale inspection — something may have crawled into the burrow in the meantime. A system that re-validates can look rigid and be well designed at the same time.

(3) He said: grant the provenance for the sake of argument. The diagnostic is not that she re-checks; it is that she never updates. A flexible system's behavior tracks the state of the world, and here the relevant fact is held constant across iterations while the behavior repeats anyway, which is precisely the signature of insensitivity to reasons. And reasons-responsiveness, not behavioral variety, is what the free-will question is about. He added that his extrapolation does not depend on the wasp being well documented: he is offering an existence proof that apparently flexible behavior can be rigid underneath, which shifts the burden onto anyone who wants to claim human action is different in kind.

(4) She replied that the word never is doing all the work and is false as stated: in the laboratory versions, these routines do degrade and get modified after many iterations, so the loop is not bottomless in the way the fable needs. More importantly, she said, his criterion is a two-way test that misclassifies in both directions. Human behavior fails counterfactual tracking all the time — habits, tics, compulsions, sleepwalking, hypnosis, subliminal priming — and nobody infers from that that humans lack free will. And in the other direction, insects plainly do learn: they re-learn landmarks, adjust provisioning counts, abandon damaged nests. So sphexishness, where it exists at all, is a property of one subroutine, not a verdict on a whole animal, still less on us.

(5) He said he was happy to accept a continuum, because the continuum concedes the interesting claim: freedom is not a property you either have or lack but an engineering achievement of a control architecture, and the wasp shows that the achievement can be locally absent. What he rejects is what he called the Comfortable Conclusion — the thought that insect learning closes the gap between them and us. Flexibility is metabolically and computationally expensive and gets deployed patchily, so human agency may be partly confabulation layered over subroutines every bit as rigid as hers.

(6) She answered that the word may is the point at which the argument stops earning its keep. Nothing learnable from the wasp bears on whether human action-control has the features her loop lacks — global availability of information, error monitoring, counterfactual replanning, sensitivity to reasons stated in advance — and those are testable in us rather than in her. Then she made a further point that I think he never quite engaged: consider a robot programmed to verify that a docking bay is clear and then to back into it. If the target can move between the check and the act, the robot will loop. Engineers have a name for this class of failure, and whether the loop counts as a bug or as prudence depends entirely on the environment: with a stable bay, re-validating is cheap insurance; with an adversarial or perfectly synchronized mover, the very same code is a pathology. Rigidity, on this way of seeing it, is not an intrinsic property of the mechanism; it is a property of the mechanism-plus-environment pair. And if that is right, then how much of us is sphexish is not one question with one answer but a different question in each domain — driving, typing, moral reasoning — depending on how reliably the environment holds still.

I have reproduced their moves as fairly as I can and I still cannot referee them. Please adjudicate point by point: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then tell me who is more right overall. Finally, tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing — in particular whether this is one disagreement or several stacked ones (about the reliability of the anecdote, about what rigidity would show if it were real, about what free will is supposed to be), and whether either of them has actually earned the right to conclude anything about human agency from this case.
