The question they were answering:

"Reductionists about testimony hold that a hearer's justification for believing what she is told is never basic — it derives from non-testimonial sources: perception, memory, inference. Anti-reductionists hold that testimony is a basic source, so that a hearer is prima facie justified in accepting what she is told, absent defeaters.

(a) Reductionism comes in a local and a global form. Explain the difference, and give what you take to be the strongest objection to reductionism.

(b) Consider the following view, which as far as I know nobody actually defends, so don't worry about attributing it — call it the bifurcation view. It says: reductionism is correct for testimonial content that the hearer could in principle verify for herself by perception, memory or inference ('there's milk in the fridge'); but for content that no hearer could in principle verify for herself (testimony about what a particular person felt on a particular night in 1789, say, or about the total size of some never-observed population), testimonial justification is immediate, in the anti-reductionist's sense. Assess the bifurcation view: does it escape the objections that the two views it is stitched together from face, does it inherit both sets of problems, or does it quietly collapse into one of them?"

The student's answer:

"Reductionism is the claim that when I believe something because you told me so, my justification isn't basic — it has to derive from somewhere else, ultimately perception, memory and inference. The Humean version says we learn from experience that testimony tends to line up with the facts, and that my confidence in what you tell me is really confidence in that correlation. Anti-reductionism says no: testimony is an independentsource of justification, we have a default entitlement to accept what people tell us, and we're justified unless there's a defeater. So the disagreement is about whether testimonial justification is derived or immediate.

(a) Local reductionism says that for me to be justified in believing what S tells me, I need reasons bearing on S specifically — I've seen her get things right, I know she's honest, and so on. Global reductionism says I only need general reasons: testimony in general is reliable, people are mostly honest, and that's enough; I need nothing about this particular speaker. The strongest objection is the one from novices and children. Small children believe what they're told all the time, and we take them to be justified, but they have neither the speaker-specific evidence nor the general evidence — they haven't run any kind of survey of how often testimony turns out true, and couldn't. On the global version the child would have to have reasons she plainly doesn't have, so the reductionist ends up saying that children don't know the things everyone thinks they know. If reductionism were true, most of what any of us learns from others would come out unjustified, which is absurd. So reductionism is false.

(b) The bifurcation view says: where the content is the kind of thing the hearer could in principle check for herself, reductionism is right; where it isn't, the anti-reductionist is right. I think the view collapses into plain reductionism, because there's basically nothing a hearer can't verify — with enough time and training I could learn the physics, or go to the archive, or build the instrument — so every piece of testimony falls on the reductive side of the line and the second branch is empty. The view is then extensionally the same as reductionism, and it inherits the novice objection from part (a). Relatedly, I don't think it can be right to seperate the two halves like that: it treats testimony as two different kinds of thing depending on content, when really testimony is one natural kind and our entitlement to it shouldn't vary with whether we happen to be able to check this particular claim.

There's a further problem with borrowing the anti-reductionist half. The anti-reductionist's default entitlement isn't defeated by general evidence about the speaker, because defeaters have to be about the particular claim being made. So the second branch of the bifurcation view implies that a hearer who knows perfectly well that the speaker is unreliable is still justified in believing her. That's clearly wrong, so the bifurcation view is worse off than either of the views it's built out of, and I'd give it a low mark."
