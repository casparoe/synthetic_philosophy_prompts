ok so i fell into a rabbit hole about consent a while back and now im not even sure the law has *one* idea of what consent is. i'd like you to tell me what each bit of the practice presupposes.

here's what i think i know (correct me if i've got any of it wrong). if a surgeon is about to operate, they have to tell you the things a reasonable patient would want to know — the alternatives, the risks, even fairly unlikely ones — and if they skip a risk and that risk then happens, the touching can count as battery, i.e. as if you never said yes at all. this is true even if the operation itself went perfectly and even if you'd probably of agreed anyway. so medicine: a duty to volunteer information, and the penalty is "your yes didn't count."

but with sex, at least as i understand it, the criminal law is weirdly relaxed. lie about your job, your money, whether you're single, whether you love them, and apparently the consent still stands: rotten thing to do, but not the kind of lie that touches validity. lie about what the act actually is, or about who you are (pretending to be someone's partner), or secretly take the condom off, and suddenly it does. and i've read that places disagree with each other about lying over STI status, which is the one case that looks exactly like the medical case — its a lie about a *risk* rather than about the act.

my friend has a name for what she thinks medicine is doing. she calls it the swap test: rerun the whole scene with the true facts swapped in, and if the person wouldn't have said yes, the consent is void. i sort of like it, but it seems to blow up. the person lied to about their partner's marital status would obviously have refused, so the swap test says that consent is void too, which makes the legal line look arbitrary. and it blows up the other way as well: there are undisclosed risks that wouldn't have changed anyone's mind, and medicine still cares about those, so the medical rule isn't really the swap test either.

so, the things i actually want:

1. what does each practice presuppose consent *is*? a mental state, a speech act, a permission with a scope set by the description it was given under, an autonomous choice, a waiver of a claim? and does the sex rule secretly presuppose a view about how finely you individuate the act — like, when i consent to "sex with you," is the thing i consented to the bare physical event or the event-under-a-description?

2. how much of this is strictly presupposed versus just a good fit? could the medical rule really be a duty-of-care rule wearing a consent costume? could the narrowness of the sex rule come from evidential worries about trials — that any wider test is unprovable — rather than from a theory of consent at all?

3. if you plugged the medical theory into the sex cases, or the sex theory into the doctor's office, what would concretely change in the rules, and would anything that seems obviously right break?

4. is the pattern just underdetermined — could three quite different theories all generate it, so that there's no fact of the matter about what the practice "presupposes"?

and one last bit that makes it weirder for me: the same systems that say a 15-year-old can't consent to sex with a 21-year-old will happily treat a 15-year-old as competent to consent to medical treatment, or to refuse it. so competence looks like a property of the person-plus-the-situation rather than of the person. does anything in your answer explain that, or is "consent" doing completely different work in the two places?
