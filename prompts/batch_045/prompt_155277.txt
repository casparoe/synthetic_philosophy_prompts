I'm in a reading group on the evolution of cooperation and I've got myself into an argument I keep losing, so I want to rehearse it against someone who actually holds the other side. Here's where I've ended up: I think the whole "iterated prisoner's dilemma explains cooperation" package is in worse shape than it gets presented as, and the place it shows is the stuff about the horizon — people either wave at the shadow of the future or tell me the finite-horizon problem evaporates once the endpoint is uncertain, and both of those feel too quick to me. Please play a game theorist who thinks backward-induction reasoning about repeated games is basically in good shape and that I'm conflating ex post regret with irrationality. Not a strawman: you know the indefinite-horizon results cold, you concede small points freely (including that no real organism computes equilibria), you will never concede that a plan is irrational just because it turns out badly in the realized state of the world, and you will never concede that "nobody knows when the last round is" is by itself enough to get cooperation off the ground. Stay in character till I say stop, keep every reply to two or three short paragraphs, talk like a person rather than a paper, and if my setup is underspecified just take the most charitable reading of it and answer instead of asking me to fill in the gaps.

My opening, three prongs — pick the one you think is strongest, answer it, and tell me which one you're setting aside for now. (1) Take a PD where the number of rounds is drawn from a distribution everyone knows, with the property that the game ends after finitely many rounds with probability one but every round has some positive chance of being followed by another — a constant continuation probability is enough — so there's no round anybody can ever identify as the last. With a high enough continuation probability grim trigger works. But then in every actual play of the game there is a last round, and on that last round both of you cooperate and leave money on the table. So the theory guarantees, with probability one, that the agent ends up performing at some point an action that is worse for them than the alternative given how things actually turned out. "They didn't know it was the last round" doesn't help: they knew it was some round, they knew the game surely ends, and they knew in advance that whenever it ended the last cooperation was wasted. Isn't there something wrong with an account of rationality that guarantees that?

(2) The contrast people lean on — bounded unknown horizon unravels, unbounded one doesn't — can't be doing the work they think it is. Suppose the number of rounds is uniform on 1 to a million and nobody is told the draw: at round 500,000 nobody knows whether it's the last round either, and yet you still get full unraveling, because the induction has somewhere to start. So "nobody knows when it ends" isn't what saves cooperation; unboundedness is, and unboundedness looks like a technicality wearing a philosophical hat. (3) And it isn't really unboundedness either. If the chance of another round shrinks as you go — never to zero, just toward it — then cooperation is fine early and stops being worth it somewhere out in the tail, and which side of that line you're on depends on the exact shape of the decay, which no creature that can't count rounds could ever work out. So your real condition is quantitative: expected remaining value versus the temptation payoff, evaluated round by round, using a distribution over a variable that isn't in the organism's head and arguably isn't in the world. Which is my actual worry — if what decides whether cooperation is rational is the tail behaviour of a distribution nobody represents, what is the iterated prisoner's dilemma a model of?
