I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on a seminar paper about the argumentative structure of Darwin's account of the moral sense in the Descent of Man (1871), Chapter IV — the section headed "The moral sense," which opens by quoting Kant's rhapsody on duty and its closing question ("whence thy original?") and then offers the famous proposition that "any animal whatever, endowed with well-marked social instincts, the parental and filial affections being here included, would inevitably acquire a moral sense or conscience, as soon as its intellectual powers had become as well, or nearly as well developed, as in man." I keep stubbing my toe on the passage a few paragraphs in, where Darwin hedges that claim and then takes it to an extreme:

"In the same manner as various animals have some sense of beauty, though they admire widely different objects, so they might have a sense of right and wrong, though led by it to follow widely different lines of conduct. If, for instance, to take an extreme case, men were reared under precisely the same conditions as hive-bees, there can hardly be a doubt that our unmarried females would, like the worker-bees, think it a sacred duty to kill their brothers, and mothers would strive to kill their fertile daughters; and no one would think of interfering."

My supervisor's view is that this is a piece of Victorian shock-rhetoric with no load-bearing role: a vivid aside, not an argument. I think it is doing precise work, but I can't get agreement — or even get clear myself — about which of three jobs it is doing. Call them: (A) the genealogical-debunking reading, on which the point is that the content of our moral judgments is fixed by contingent facts about our social history, so a naturalized moral sense carries no authority and Darwin is quietly undercutting the very "ought" he opens the chapter by celebrating; (B) the form-only reading, on which he is claiming universality only for the mechanism — the inward monitor, the struggle between enduring and transient impulses, the role of community opinion in supplying content — so that the passage shows the phenomenology of obligation can be fully intact while its content varies, without saying anything about the truth of any particular duty; and (C) the anti-theological reading, on which the passage is an answer specifically to the "whence thy original?" question he has just quoted, designed to show that no divine faculty is needed to explain conscience and that the theist's own account is worse off, since it must either bless the hive-world duties or explain why God's law tracks human social instincts so neatly.

I'd be grateful for a close-reading answer rather than a general discussion of Darwin on morality. Concretely: which of the three readings, or what fourth one, best fits what the sentence is actually constructed to do? What in particular is the phrase "sacred duty" doing — is it there to stress that our counterparts' obligation-experience would be as compelling as ours, and thereby to pry apart the phenomenology of obligation from any claim to correctness, or is it merely Darwin's ironic way of marking content he takes to be obviously abhorrent? Relatedly, does "no one would think of interfering" mean no one would judge interference right, no one would be motivated to interfere, or both — and does that clause matter for reading (B)? I'm also stuck on a narrower, more technical point: Darwin says men "were reared under" the bees' conditions, not that they descended from bee-like ancestors or were selected under those conditions, yet his own explanation of the moral sense in the preceding paragraphs works through inherited social instincts and habit. So is this counterfactual a claim about developmental plasticity plus the malleability of community opinion (the third of his subordinate propositions), or does it secretly require a change in the instincts themselves, i.e., a selection history — and whichever way it goes, what does that imply about how strong the "inevitably acquire" claim in the opening proposition really is? Finally, one worry I can't settle: given Darwin's own settled moral convictions elsewhere in his writing, can he afford reading (A), or does the passage sit in tension with them in a way that actually tells us something about which reading he intended?

Please reason from the passage and its immediate context in the chapter — the "sense of beauty" analogy, the "inevitably acquire" proposition, the list of subordinate propositions (sympathy, memory of past actions, community opinion, habit), and the way he closes the discussion — rather than from general summaries of Darwin's ethics, and tell me explicitly which nearby sentence or clause you think is the strongest evidence for and against your reading. Also say what kind of textual evidence, if it turned up, would most change your mind. Please finish with a single sentence giving your bottom-line interpretation, followed by a final, machine-readable line of exactly this form — \answer{[your label, e.g. A, B, C, or a short label of your own if you favor a fourth reading]} — with nothing after it.
