I'm applying for a year of research leave to write a short monograph — about 35,000 words, the length that a few series commission for compact, single-argument books — and I need help turning a half-formed idea into a proposal of roughly two pages. The idea is the residue of a long argument I had with a friend last night, and I would rather hand you the mess than a tidied version of it.

Here is where we got stuck. My friend is broadly sympathetic to deflationism about truth, and she defended it with what I have started calling the *inheritance* strategy: whenever someone produces a normative fact that seems to require a substantive property of truth, the deflationist tries to show that it is already forthcoming from the norms governing the sentences embedded under the truth predicate, together with the equivalence schema (or, in the inferentialist variant, the introduction and elimination rules). The norm of assertion, the aim of belief, the prohibition on asserting what you do not believe, the point of deferring to others — for each of these, the deflationist offers a derivation of the form: the norm attaches to what is said, and the schema transmits it to the claim that what is said is true. I pressed her on the value of true belief and on the aim of inquiry, she produced derivations, and we went around this loop several times without either of us gaining ground. It felt like a stalemate of the kind that comes from a shared assumption rather than from a genuine disagreement.

My suspicion is that the shared assumption is this: that the interesting cases are all *categorical*. A categorical norm tells you whether to do something at all — assert, believe, inquire, defer — and its content can be given by embedding a sentence. But many of the places where we appeal to truth are *gradational*: we rank a map, a forecast, a model, a verdict, or a body of testimony by how close to the truth it is; we trade accuracy against completeness, or against simplicity, or against how much of a question gets answered; we say which of two mistaken inquirers is nearer the mark. There seems to be no sentence p such that the norm about p transmits, via the schema, to a norm about being closer to the truth than some alternative, because the schema pairs truth with a proposition wholesale and says nothing that could order the propositions that fail to be true. Call this the metric gap. If I am right, the inheritance strategy is not merely hard to apply to these cases; it is structurally unavailable, and the deflationist owes something different in kind rather than one more derivation.

That is the half-formed thing I want made into a proposal. The question I want stated precisely enough to have a determinate answer is roughly: can any version of deflationism about truth accommodate genuine gradational accuracy facts without either taking closeness-to-truth as a substantive primitive alongside a thin notion of truth, or trying to eliminate gradational talk in favour of something non-truth-theoretic such as expected predictive success? And if neither option is available, does the failure tell against deflationism, or does it simply show that deflationism was always a local thesis about one concept that requires substantive supplementation elsewhere — in which case I want to know what the supplemented package would have to look like, and whether it is still recognisably deflationary.

What I would like the proposal to contain:

1. The question stated in a form that could actually be answered, not just explored. I would rather it be broken into component theses — something like the no-nature claim, the no-explanation claim, and the claim that the schema exhausts the concept's role — and tested against the gradational case one at a time, so that the project still has content even if the word 'deflationism' turns out to be contested.

2. Why the existing positions leave it open. Not a literature review — I will add the references myself, and I do not want any citations — but an argument for why the standard dialectic does not settle this: why the critics' favourite counterexamples are all categorical, why the deflationist's replies are all inheritance replies, and why the case study literature on verisimilitude and approximate truth in science has developed largely independently of the truth-theoretical debate, on the assumption that the two are unrelated.

3. The method, and why this method rather than the obvious alternatives. The obvious alternatives I can see are: to run the whole argument through the liar paradox and non-classical logic; to run it through the debate about why a deflated truth predicate is expressively useful at all; or to write a book about truthlikeness in general and only gesture at deflationism. Say why my approach is better than each of these for a book of this length, and be honest if one of them is actually better. I would like a chapter-by-chapter structure (I am imagining five chapters) and a realistic work plan across the year, including where I expect to get stuck.

4. The possible conclusions, and which considerations would decide between them. I can see at least three: a division-of-labour view on which truth is thin and truthlikeness is a separate, substantive posit — with the awkward question of why the discontinuity should fall exactly there; an eliminationist view on which gradational talk is merely instrumental, with the cost that a great deal of epistemology and philosophy of science then rests on nothing; and a constructionist view on which closeness-to-truth is built out of deflationarily admissible materials, which requires saying what admissibility consists in and why a measure over propositions does or does not count. I would also want the proposal to consider a fourth: that the right moral is about how deflationism should be stated — as a claim about the function of the truth predicate rather than about the property — and that the gradational case is what forces this reconstrual.

5. What would count as the project failing. Two failure modes worry me in particular: that the constructionist option turns out to be trivially available, so that there was never a real problem; and that the whole question dissolves into a verbal dispute about how 'deflationism' is to be defined. I want the proposal to say how the work would be designed so that either outcome would still be a discoverable result rather than a collapse, and whether 35,000 words is the right size — whether the question is too narrow for a book, or too large for one written in a single year.

Use concrete, non-technical examples wherever a case is needed — a cartographer deciding between a complete map with large errors and an incomplete one with small ones, a forecaster assigning probabilities, a biologist working with a deliberately idealised model, a jury returning a partial verdict — rather than anything that requires knowing the literature.
