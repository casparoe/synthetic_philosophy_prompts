I do a small hobby newsletter on the history of science and I'm working on a short piece about Kepler and astrology. I've got a chapter from a popular book in front of me and I want to know how much of it I can repeat in my own words without embarrassing myself. It goes more or less like this:

"Kepler was in most respects the first modern astronomer — ellipses, the Rudolphine Tables, the whole apparatus — but he had one embarrassing blind spot: he believed in astrology. He took money for casting horoscopes for aristocrats and generals, Wallenstein among them, and he thought the configurations of the planets actually brought about what happens down here — weather, plagues, moods, the rise and fall of states. What he wanted was to clean the subject up. He threw out the magical furniture — the twelve signs, the houses, the dignities — and tried to rebuild it as physics: two planets at a given angle to each other produce a real effect on the earth, much as the moon's position produces the tides. His reformed astrology was supposed to stand to the old one as Copernican astronomy stood to Ptolemaic. He was also, unusually for the period, willing to test it — he kept records, checked forecasts against what actually happened, and found the hits were no better than chance, which is why he kept telling astrologers to stop making specific predictions. So he is the man who invented the empirical testing of astrology and then quietly disproved it himself, and by the time he wrote the Harmony of the World in 1619 the astrology has more or less dropped out and it is pure science."

I cant tell where the author is being fair and where they're tidying things up, and I've got two suspicions I can't cash in. First, the "one embarrassing blind spot" framing feels like it's written backwards from the line we now draw between science and pseudoscience, but maybe that's fine as a way of talking to a modern reader. Second, the ending is suspiciously neat — he tested it, it flopped, he quietly moved on — and its the version I'd like to be true because it makes a good story, so I don't trust my own reaction.

There's also a charge of my own I want checked. Reading this, it sounds like he wanted it both ways: take the fee for the horoscope while building a version of astrology that no failed forecast could ever touch. Is that a fair thing to say about him, or is it the kind of thing you could say about anybody in a period when these subjects hadn't separated yet? And one question I couldn't answer from the chapter: if someone had asked him what observation would make him abandon the whole thing, what would he have said? I don't want to imply he never thought about it if he did.

If the accurate version is less flattering to Kepler than the author's, I'd rather print that.
