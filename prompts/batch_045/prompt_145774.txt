My half-formed idea, which I can't yet make sharp: everyone working on the definition of life seems to treat the question as if there's one concept with a hidden necessary-and-sufficient condition, and then the discussion just trades borderline cases at each other — viruses, sterile mules, dormant seeds, prions, fire, crystal growth, self-replicating robots, computer viruses in a sandbox — until someone declares the concept a cluster concept or a family-resemblance term and everybody goes home. I think that's the wrong diagnosis. My suspicion is that 'alive' is doing three or four seperable jobs that have got collapsed together: (a) a functional/causal job — picking out systems that actively maintain themselves far from equilibrium and resist dissolution; (b) a genealogical job — picking out things that belong to a lineage, i.e. are descended from reproducers and sit inside an evolving population; (c) an evidential job — telling a probe what to measure when you'll never get to dissect the thing; and maybe (d) a moral/legal job — deciding what gets protected, funded, or barred from being patented. My hunch is that every stalemate case in the literature is a case where two of these jobs come apart, and that if you index the question to a job, most of the apparent disagreements evaporate and what's left is a real but much smaller disagreement.
