So I've somehow ended up running the thursday night discussion group at my church — dont ask, our rector twisted my arm — and the session I've got coming up is on whether the universe needs a cause. The problem is everyone in the room keeps treating the kalam version and the contingency version as the same argument in different clothes, and I want one evening where they can actually see the seam between the two. So I need a case where they come apart.

What I'm after: a single scenario where the kalam-style argument (the one that leans on everything that begins to exist having a cause, plus the universe beginning to exist) goes through and gets you a first cause, while the contingency-style argument (the one that starts from the existence of contingent things and pushes toward a necessary being) stalls out and doesn't hand you a necessary being at all. The other direction is the easy one — just make the universe past-eternal and the kalam dies — so that's not what I want.

Constraints, sorry there are a bunch:

- One paragraph of scenario, plain english, no vocabulary my group will trip over. They're smart but they're engineers and a retired GP, nobody there has read any of this.
- No speculative cosmology: no multiverse, no quantum fluctuations popping into being, no Boltzmann brains, no time travel, no inflaton fields. The setup can be artificial and metaphysical — I don't mind that at all — but no exotic physics, no sci-fi furniture.
- The divergence can't come from just declaring a premise false or from building the answer into the setup. Every premise should be one a defender of that argument would grant as true-in-your-case, and the verdicts should fall out of how the arguments actually work. If a premise ends up unviolated only because the case never triggers it, say so and own it.
- The kalam side has to reach something that is genuinely a cause of the whole order, not a bit of the order causing itself.

The half-baked idea I've been playing with, which you can use or throw out: what if it's necessary that some contingent thing or other exists? Every individual thing in the case is genuinely contingent, there's no world where nothing at all exists, but there are worlds with different things in them. Then the contingency argument's big why-is-there-anything-at-all question gets answered by a necessary truth rather than by a necessary being, while the kalam, which only cares about beginnings, sails straight through. I can kind of see the shape of it but I cant turn it into a clean case, and I can't tell whether it's cheating. If it doesn't work, use some other device, but tell me why mine fails.

Then, after the scenario:

1. Run both arguments on it. List the premises you're using for each in your own words (we have no books there), mark each as true in the case / not triggered / false, and say what verdict each argument ends up with.
2. Name the one feature of the case that's doing the work, then delete just that feature and show me the two arguments collapsing back into agreement.
3. Give me the best objection someone who really likes the contingency argument would make — strongest version, not a strawman — and how you'd reply.
4. Be straight with me: if you don't think the spec can be met without quietly cheating somewhere, say that, and give me the closest honest version you can instead.
