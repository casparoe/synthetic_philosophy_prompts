Hi — I write novels for a living (my last one, Lowtide, is set almost all of it inside a persistent online world), and I've somehow let myself be talked into giving the closing keynote at a game studies conference next month. I'm going to argue two things: first, that virtual objects are genuinely real objects of a peculiar kind — my line is that a virtual sword is a real sword-shaped knot of permissions and expectations, held in place by a server — and second, that harms done to people in virtual worlds are real harms, just thinner ones, whose size is set by how much agency and care the person has invested rather than by what the thing is made of.

The room will be mostly game developers and engine programmers, a couple of them openly hostile to theory (one of them definitely has a slide somewhere that says it's just rows in a database), plus maybe four or five philosophers who work on social ontology and fiction, and one or two legal types who care about whether taking someone's in-game gear is actually theft. Nobody there does decision theory or consciousness stuff. They'll be thinking about my examples rather than my arguments, they'll want everything short, and I can't deliver a lecture on institutional facts — I'm a novelist, so give me things I can actually say out loud.
