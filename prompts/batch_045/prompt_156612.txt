I have no training in philosophy or physics. I came to this through a popular book on Zeno's paradoxes and have been turning it over by myself for months, so please forgive the homemade vocabulary; if there is a standard term for something I am reaching for, name it, but answer in your own words and do not send me to a reading list — I cannot look anything up from where I am asking, and I want the reasoning rather than the references.

The question I am stuck on is what is true of a system at the moment a supertask finishes: a machine that performs infinitely many operations in a finite stretch of time and then stops. Everything below happens between twelve o'clock and one minute past; one minute past is the moment the machine is done. Here are my verdicts, given on instinct rather than on argument.

1. A man walks across a room, and we describe the walk as: first half the distance, then half of what remains, then half of what remains, and so on — infinitely many stages, all completed by one minute past. Verdict: at one minute past he is at the far wall, obviously. The infinite description is just a peculiar way of carving up one ordinary walk; there is nothing to work out.

2. A hose pours half a litre into a bucket in the first half-minute, a quarter of a litre in the next quarter-minute, and so on. Verdict: at one minute past the bucket contains exactly one litre. Determinate, and I can say how much.

3. A lamp on a dimmer. At each stage the brightness is cut to half of what it was: full, then half, then a quarter, and so on. Verdict: at one minute past it is dark, that is, off. Determinate, and I hold this one very confidently.

4. A lamp with an ordinary on-off switch, thrown at the half-minute, then a quarter-minute later, then an eighth of a minute later, and so on, always flipping it. Verdict: at one minute past there is no fact of the matter whether it is on or off; the question has no answer. I have since seen this called something like Thomson's lamp, which suggests other people have landed where I did, but two things pull against me: at every instant before one minute past the lamp is determinately one or the other, and it is a real lamp in a real room, so it feels as though it must be doing something.

5. Two lamps wired so that they are always in opposite states, switched on the schedule of case 4. Verdict: that exactly one of them is on at one minute past strikes me as determinately true, even though the left one is on and the right one is on both strike me as indeterminate. I do not know how to say that in the same breath as case 4.

6. The same lamp, but instead of a toggle switch it has a dial: at each stage the dial is turned half a turn in the same direction, and the lamp is on while the pointer is in the top half of the circle and off while it is in the bottom half, starting in the middle of the top half. The turns add up to exactly one full turn, so the pointer comes back to where it began. Verdict: I go back and forth and cannot settle. Some days: on, determinately, because the pointer's motion converges and the wiring then settles the lamp. Other days: no fact of the matter, because this is case 4 with extra machinery, and the fact that the numbers sum to one full turn is a fact about a series, not about where a physical pointer is at one minute past.

7. The case 4 lamp is sealed in a light-proof box before twelve, nobody touches anything after one minute past, and at five past I open the box and see that it is dark. Verdict: at five past it is off — but I cannot tell whether looking made it so or merely revealed that it had been off all along, and both answers seem wrong to me: the first makes the lamp's recent past depend on my opening a box, the second says case 4 was never really indeterminate, only unknown.

8. The case 4 switch is thrown by a long rigid rod, and to keep the schedule the rod must be shoved faster and faster, so that before the minute is out it would have to move faster than light. Verdict: here I do not want to say there is no fact of the matter; I want to say the arrangement cannot exist, so there is nothing for a fact to be about. That feels like a different kind of no answer from case 4, and I want my principle to keep the two apart.

The rough rule I have been working with: if the quantity that matters settles down to a single value as you approach the stopping moment, then there is a fact at the stopping moment and it is that value; if it never settles down because it keeps flip-flopping between two values, there is no fact. That sorts cases 1 through 4 the way I have them and leans towards on in case 6, but it says nothing obvious about the disjunction in case 5, nothing about case 7, where the quantity never settles and yet I end up knowing the answer, and nothing about why case 8 is a different sort of failure.

What I want is that rule sharpened into something I can actually apply, or replaced: state the principle precisely, tell me which clause is bought by which case, and repair it where it breaks. Then give me its verdicts on cases I have not thought of — I would especially like to know what it says about the lamp's state five minutes later if nothing touches it, about a machine that counts its own operations as it goes, and about a machine that has always been running, with no first step at all. Then tell me plainly which of my eight verdicts the best principle cannot keep, which you would overturn, and why the simpler principle that overturns one is better than the baroque one that saves them all. Finally, separate what is settled among people who actually work on this from what is still a live fight: I would much rather be told that the experts split, and how, than be handed one side of a dispute as the received view, and I would like you to mark which of the verdicts above are your own rather than theirs.
