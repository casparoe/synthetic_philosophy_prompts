Two of my roommates argued about this for like an hour last night, and I got volunteered to settle it, which I really cant do.

Dev keeps telling everyone that the new record by a band he likes is a masterpiece. He hasn't heard it. His grounds are just that a critic whose taste has matched his on about forty previous records called it a masterpiece. Marta says he isn't entitled to say that, and that what he's got isn't an aesthetic judgment at all.

(1) Dev: deferring to someone with a track record of agreement is ordinary testimony. If my mechanic says the brake pads are worn, I know the pads are worn. Why is "my critic says it's great" different in kind?

(2) Marta: because "great" isn't a free-standing property the way worn pads are. What the critic can hand you is a well-grounded prediction about how the record will strike you. A prediction isn't a verdict about the record's value, and it can't do the work of one — you haven't heard the thing.

(3) Dev: I don't see the gap. I know with high confidence that when I hear it I'll judge it great, and that that later judgment will be properly grounded. So my claim now borrows the authority of the judgment I'm confidently going to make. Also: you'd happily take a friend's word that a restaurant's tasting menu is sublime before you've eaten there, and that's just as experiential.

(4) Marta: the restaurant case actually proves my point — there you're going to eat the food, so the testimony is a note you'll cash in. Where you'll never have the experience, the note never gets cashed and the claim stays hollow. And borrowing authority from a judgment you haven't made is a bet dressed up as a verdict.

(5) Dev: then what about records nobody can hear? Say a band presses fifty copies, plays it once for a room of critics, then destroys the masters. In 2050 someone reads those reviews. On your view they can't justifiably think it was great — can't even regret the loss properly. That's absurd.

(6) Marta: they can justifiably believe it was the sort of thing people responded to in those ways, and that its loss was a real loss. That isn't the same belief as "it was great," and I'm fine saying the second one just isn't available to them.

The thing they never got to, and the reason I think they were both missing something: a lot of what a good review does is tell you what to listen *for* — that the bassline in track four is doing something strange, say — and then you hear it, where otherwise you'd have walked right past it. That looks like testimony doing work that isn't merely predicting your reaction and isn't a stand-in for hearing it either.

Can you go through the six moves and say who's right about what at each step, whether the restaurant analogy and the destroyed-record case really do help Dev, and how the listen-for-it point bears on the disagreement — does it vindicate one of them, or does it show they carved up the options wrong?
