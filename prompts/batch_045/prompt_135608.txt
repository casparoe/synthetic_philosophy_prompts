Right — I'm rebuilding the first session of my MA seminar on human enhancement, and I've decided I don't want to open with the usual therapy-vs-enhancement tableau (growth hormone for the short kid, Ritalin for the kid who can't sit still, cochlear implants, steroids, the lot), because every year the students just sort the cases by gut and then reverse-engineer a principle, and every year the principle falls apart on the fourth example. So this year I want the spec first: what a theory of the therapy/enhancement distinction would have to be able to do in order to be *good enough for a specific job*.

Fix the job as this: the theory has to be usable by a reimbursement committee in a tax-funded single-payer system — people who get real claim forms, have to say yes or no (or maybe yes-but-tiered), and cannot appeal to a contested conception of the good life in their published reasons. Secondary job, and I want you to tell me whether it can be the same theory: it should also say something about what the state may discourage (or quietly encourage) in people who pay out of pocket, and about what employers may require as a condition of a role.

So: write me the desiderata. For each one give the case or consideration that motivates it — I want the case, not just the criterion — and mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp. Use these to get you going, kill any you think are bogus, and add the ones I've missed:

- it must not classify as enhancement a bunch of things we already fund without controversy: vaccines, contraception and fertility treatment, orthodontics, prenatal vitamins, reconstructive surgery after injury or mastectomy, prevention generally. (A theory that implies statins are enhancement has failed, not revealed a surprising truth.)
- it must cope with the fact that the very same intervention — same drug, same mechanism, same dose — is therapy in one recipient and enhancement in another, and that intentions of the prescriber don't obviously settle it either.
- it must handle non-disease cases where the welfare gain is real and large but the "abnormal" bit is social: idiopathic short stature, or the kid born with a cleft palate in a culture that treats it as a curse.
- here's the one I keep chewing on, and I'd like you to give it a proper name and say whether it's a genuine requirement or just egalitarian prejudice: the ratchet case. Some interventions are positional — they help you only because others don't have them, and once uptake spreads the benefit evaporates while the intervention becomes, in practice, compulsory. Height, cosmetic orthodontics, exam-focus drugs in a competitive school system, sleep-reduction drugs in a profession with 30-hour shifts. So the theory must be able to say no to something that everyone involved will experience as a need, without thereby having to call every positional good a disease. I suspect the requirement "only facts about the patient's body count" and "handles the ratchet" cannot both be had, because the ratchet is constitutively comparative.
- it must handle the degraded-baseline cases, where the environment is what's malfunctioning: the person is functioning normally for their environment and abnormally for the species, or vice versa. Immune boosters where a pathogen is endemic, weight-loss drugs in an obesogenic food environment, air filtration, hearing protection, cognitive prosthetics for people whose jobs wreck their sleep. Can the theory admit that the environment is what's sick without sliding into "and therefore everything is therapy"?
- it must handle trade-offs, not just improvements: interventions that raise one capacity and lower another (less sleep at the cost of emotional memory consolidation, say, or focus bought at the price of divergent thinking), so it can't just define enhancement as all-things-considered improvement.
- it must handle cases with no prior baseline to restore — germline editing, prenatal intervention, anything where there is no "before" the person was healthy in.
- it must handle the cases where the subject denies there's anything to fix (the Deaf-culture objection to cochlear implants is the easy version; the deep-brain-stimulation patient whose family says he's no longer himself is the hard one), and say what to do when "the patient reports no need" and "the patient's adaptive preferences have been lowered by a lifetime of exclusion" pull in opposite directions.
- it must handle third-party benefit: the intervention that mostly helps other people (steady hands for a surgeon, alertness for a pilot, a vaccine) — is benefit-to-others a therapy-making feature, and if so does that let any occupationally useful trait become a medical need by the back door?
- it must respect a distinction I think is load-bearing but can't state cleanly: between whether something is therapy at all, and how much we should spend on it given that it is. A theory that collapses those two will misclassify expensive-but-genuine therapies as enhancements, and cheap-but-optional ones as therapies.

Then, and this is the part I care about most: tell me which of these are real desiderata and which are just the reflexes of one camp — the medical-naturalist wanting the account to be value-free, the luck-egalitarian wanting it to track what justice requires, the disability-studies side wanting it to be socially constructed all the way down, the administrator wanting binary verdicts. And tell me which subset is jointly unsatisfiable: I have a suspicion that "descriptive rather than evaluative" plus "determinate enough for a real committee to apply" plus "gets the ratchet right" is a set of three where you can have any two, and I want you to argue me out of it or into it. Finally, the single requirement that does the most exclusionary work — which one would knock out the most of the usual candidates?

At the end, three lines each on how the main contenders do — wellbeing/welfare-based accounts, normal-function or biostatistical accounts, and fair-equality-of-opportunity style accounts, plus whichever of the "it's all a continuum, we should just do cost-benefit" views you think is strongest. Three lines, not a table, and don't pad it: the criteria are the assignment, the scoring is a footnote. And skip the literature, skip the reading list, skip defining the basic distinction — I do that bit in the first ten minutes and I've got it covered.
