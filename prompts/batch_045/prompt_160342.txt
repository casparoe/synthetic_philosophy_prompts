so i'm on this new Behavioral Design Review Committee at my hospital system — we're the board that has to sign off on any "choice architecture" change to the patient portal before IT ships it. first real case is in front of us thursday and i need something i can circulate tonight.

the proposal: for patients who are overdue for colorectal cancer screening, the portal sends a text saying a screening slot has been reserved in their name at a specific date and time, and they can text back CANCEL to release it. no slot is actually being held — it gets released automatically if they never confirm. the vendor's pilot roughly doubled uptake compared with what we send now, which just tells people they're due and gives them a link to book.

here's the wrinkle that's split us. the vendor also ran a transparency arm: same message plus one line saying the text is designed to make scheduling easier and that most patients their age get screened. pooled across everyone, the disclosed version did slightly better. but when we broke it down, the disclosure helped among patients who'd rated us high on the last trust survey and actually hurt among patients who rated us low — the average was hiding that. one member says this settles it: transparency doesn't remove the influence, it relocates it, and we'd be spending trust we earned in exam rooms to move people with a text message. another says that's backwards — disclosed influence is exactly the kind a person can respond to, and the fact that it travels through trust is a feature, since trust is a real reason and not a glitch.

second wrinkle: our own staff audit found that the reserved slots are all weekday mornings, and for our shift-work patients and the ones who need a family member to drive them, cancelling and rebooking costs way more than one word — a couple of people in the pilot never got rebooked at all. so the "you can opt out with one text" defense isn't equally true for everybody, and the mirror-image problem is that the current message has its own friction that also isn't equally distributed.

what i want: a memo with an actual recommendation — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — plus the reasoning, and i specifically need the strongest version of the case against whatever you recommend, because whoever loses is going to make it at the meeting and i'd rather it be in the memo than sprung on me. the argument i keep circling back to is that we don't get to have no default: if we do nothing, the message stays as is, and "you're due, here's a link" is itself a bit of choice architecture that pushes people toward not getting screened, and it pushes the same shift workers hardest. so neutrality isn't on the menu — but i'm not convinced that by itself gets us to approval, and i want the memo to say whether it does.

format: exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no intro and no closing summary. item one is the bottom line — the decision and, if there are conditions, who has to do what by when. and please don't cite studies or suggest further reading, nobody on this committee will follow up on it.
