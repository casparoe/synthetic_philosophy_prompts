I took early retirement four years ago from a research career in chemical physics and have since been sitting in on a graduate reading group in philosophy of mind and cognitive science. I can follow an argument and tell when one is doing no work, but I have none of the background in this literature, and I would like help separating what is genuinely right in a characterisation I have just read from what is merely well phrased.

A friend passed me a well-reviewed trade book on machine learning. Its chapter on large language models summarises the sceptical position — the one the chapter calls the "stochastic parrot" view — in the passage I copied out below:

"These systems are trained only on the form of language — sequences of symbols with no contact with the things those symbols are about — and they work by recombining patterns extracted from the text they were trained on, which is why their output is fluent but empty. The argument from the much-discussed octopus case makes the point in principle: an observer that sees only the marks exchanged by two agents, and never their surroundings or their purposes, cannot in principle recover what those marks mean, however much data it is given; a language model is just such an observer, and so no amount of further training will make it understand either. What it can acquire is a map of which words go with which — statistically adequate, semantically inert. And the evidence bears this out: the models fail on benchmarks that require real-world grounding, and they hallucinate, which is what one would expect of a machine that has mistaken the shadow of language for its substance. Interpretability results that find something like a representation of a game board inside a network trained only on lists of moves are therefore beside the point; they show only that the network has learned correlations, and correlations are not understanding."

Later in the same chapter the author adds that the dispute cannot be settled empirically at all, since no behavioural test could ever distinguish a system that understands from one that merely simulates understanding — understanding not being a behavioural property. The chapter closes with a practical upshot: because the models do not understand, their apparent competence is an illusion we project onto them, and we ought to treat every output as if it had been produced by something with no idea of what it is talking about.

Two things made me uneasy, though I cannot locate the trouble precisely. First, in forty years of spectroscopy I never once worried whether an instrument understood what a sulfate ion was; we happily said that it detected, resolved, discriminated, and sometimes that it "knew" where a peak was. So I suspect the chapter is using "understand" as an all-or-nothing, consciousness-adjacent achievement term while quietly trading on its everyday, graded sense — but I cannot tell which sentences depend on that slide. Second, and more to the point as I see it: the whole of a model's training signal consists of sentences written by people who did mean something by them. If the model's internal states are shaped to track the structure of those sentences, it is not obvious to me why intentionality stops at the corpus. A printed page inherits its meaning from its author; why is a model not at least a candidate for the same courtesy? My guess is that the argument in the passage needs a step that blocks that transmission, and I would like that step stated explicitly and then assessed on its merits rather than asserted.

What I am asking for, then, is an accuracy audit I can act on. Please take the passage claim by claim and tell me: what a careful defender of the sceptical position would accept as a fair statement of it; what is a defensible popular simplification; what is a genuine misrepresentation — in particular, whether the passage conflates two claims the position keeps apart, or overstates a modest thesis; and whether the practical upshot follows from whatever survives correction. I would also value your judgement on three specific things: (i) whether the octopus argument, as the passage renders it, generalises further than its defender should welcome — for instance to a human who learned a language only from texts and never saw the world those texts describe; (ii) whether the chapter's use of benchmark failures and hallucination as evidence is consistent with its later claim that no behavioural test could settle the question; and (iii) whether the dismissal of the interpretability results is fair, or whether it quietly assumes the very point at issue. Finally, tell me which single sentence a defender of the sceptical view would most want the author to rewrite, and why.

Please do not give me a bibliography, citations, or suggestions for further reading: I have no journal access and would rather you argue from the shape of the claims themselves. Where you think the passage is right, give me the strongest version of the point, not merely the verdict that it is right.

I would find a short table most useful — one row per claim, marked accurate, simplification, or material distortion, with a one-line correction — followed by prose on the three specific questions and on the practical upshot, and a count of the material distortions at the foot of the table. Finish with a single line, with nothing after it, of the form \answer{...} containing exactly one of these labels: broadly fair / fair with minor infelicities only / fair in outline but materially misleading / misleading in outline.
