I am writing a novel, and I would be grateful for help with a piece of its machinery.

The novel is set over roughly eighteen months in Ashfield, a city of about ninety thousand in the industrial Midwest, and each chapter is the monthly hearing of the city's Naming and Memorials Commission: five volunteer commissioners — a retired high-school history teacher, a city attorney who advises but does not vote, a hospital chaplain, a twenty-year-old appointed by the mayor, and a small-business owner — who hear petitions about street names, statues, plaques, murals, and the names on schools and public buildings. The Commission is advisory. It must produce written findings within a week of each hearing; those findings go to the city council, which votes and may ignore them. Hearings run ninety minutes, are televised on the local cable channel and written up in a weekly paper, and the only research available is a one-page memo from the city archivist. The Commission also receives petitions it cannot research at all: the record is thin, or contested, or everyone who remembers is dead.

I have read enough in the ethics of commemoration to know the vocabulary — the difference between honoring and merely remembering, expressive and speech-act accounts of what a monument does, whether the city is the same agent as the one that erected the thing, whether removal is a second injury or a repair, whether "it's history" is an answer — and I need neither a survey of the literature nor any citations. What I need is something the Commission can be shown actually to run.

So: write me the procedure. Ordered questions, phrased so that a volunteer with no philosophy can ask them and recognize an answer; for each question, what counts as a sufficient answer and what counts as no answer; the point at which the procedure returns a disposition; the points at which it stops with a no; and the points at which it has to hand the question to a human being as a question of values rather than of fact. Keep the distinctions that carry the weight, and say explicitly which ones you are dropping and what is lost by dropping them.

Three notions I have invented for the book should be built into it, and I define them here so that you can use them rather than guess at them:

- The second audience. Every commemoration has the audience it was addressed to and the audience that inherits it: people who never chose the symbol, cannot easily exit from it, and are required to use it in the course of ordinary life — a street name they must give a 911 dispatcher, a school name on a diploma. Decide what standing the second audience has, and how it is weighed against the claims of descendants and of the original donors' intent.
- The legibility condition. A commemoration must be able to carry its own meaning to a reasonably attentive passerby from what is on the site plus what any citizen of Ashfield can be presumed to know; anything whose meaning requires specialist knowledge not available on site is doing something other than commemorating. Then face the corollary that haunts one of my chapters: a plaque added to satisfy the condition may change what the object is.
- Captured meaning. An object can acquire a second use that overtakes its original one — a rally point, a threat, a badge — and once it has, retaining it may not be the neutral act that "we didn't put it up" pretends. Say how the Commission should establish that capture has happened and what it should count as evidence, given the archivist's one page.

Take a position, too, on whether the procedure treats installing and removing as mirror-image acts. My instinct is that they are not, and that the asymmetry does a great deal of work in the arguments I want my characters to have; but I would rather the procedure commit than be diplomatic.

Then run it on three petitions, treating the facts I give you as sufficient or saying where they are not:

1. Alden Street, named in 1904 for Josiah Alden (1831–1901), a textile mill owner whose unrestricted bequest built the public library and still funds its book budget, and who was also the author of a widely circulated nativist tract arguing that Irish immigrants were a degraded race, and whose mill by policy did not hire them. The street runs through the neighborhood where his workers lived and is now home to families descended from the people he would not employ. The petition asks that it be renamed for a local suffragist.
2. "The Pioneer Mother," a 1926 bronze of an unnamed woman and child on the courthouse lawn, given by a women's organization that no longer exists. From the 1930s into the 1950s the lawn was the assembly point for an annual "better families" rally, and photographs from those years show the rally gathered at the statue's base. The statue has stood unremarked since. The petition asks for removal on the ground of what it came to mean; the counter-petition says you cannot remove a generic statue for what a third party did with it seventy years ago.
3. A new pedestrian bridge is to be named. One proposal: a living developer offers a large gift to the parks fund and asks that the bridge carry his mother's name. Another: that it be named for a labor organizer who died eleven months ago, whose funeral the narrator attends in the first chapter. The Commission must decide whether either is eligible, on what standard, and whether there should be a waiting period before the recently dead are commemorated at all.

I should declare my sympathies, since I want the procedure to be able to overrule me: I want Alden Street renamed, I want the Pioneer Mother to stay, and I want the bridge named for the labor organizer. If the procedure delivers any of those, say so; if it refuses one, tell me plainly which step does the work and what a commissioner who agreed with me would have to argue.

Finally: tell me which two or three steps are the ones a reasonable commissioner would fight about, and give both sides of each fight in language a character could say out loud. And tell me what the procedure cannot do — which petitions it should decline to decide at all, and what it will get wrong when it is forced to decide them.

End with a single line, with nothing after it: \answer{Alden Street: <disposition>; Pioneer Mother: <disposition>; Bridge: <disposition>}, where each disposition is one of the labels your procedure defines — for example, retain, retain with alteration, relocate, remove, or defer.
