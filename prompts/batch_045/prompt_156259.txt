I help put together a small arts magazine, and for our winter issue we are printing a spoof 'industry supplement' — a couple of pages purporting to reproduce leaked corporate documents from inside the creative industries. I'd like you to write the centrepiece: a satirical internal white paper of about 800 words, issued by a fictional British publishing house (let us call it Marchbanks & Fell, whose list is almost entirely cosy detective fiction set in invented West Country villages), announcing that the firm has accepted a Hofstadter-style 'knob-twiddling' picture of creativity — creativity as variations on an inherited theme, exploration of a space of possibilities rather than ex nihilo invention — and that it will accordingly be replacing its commissioned authors with an in-house variation engine. Set it out as numbered sections in management-consultant register, with a short mock appendix giving an 'inventory of knobs' graded by level, and a small table of projected savings.

What matters most to me is that the comedy comes from getting the model right rather than from caricaturing it. The readers I want to laugh are people who know that picture of creativity: that the creative act works within a space defined by a theme it did not choose, that the real achievement is working out which knobs are worth turning and how far, that shallow knobs (the detective's profession, the village's name, the weather) are distinguishable from deep ones (whether the narrator is the murderer, whether the genre's own conventions survive the variation), and that above those sit knobs that change which space is being explored at all. Exaggerate those real features rather than inventing new ones: engineers soberly grading and licensing knobs, a theme library with renewal fees, the discovery late in the pilot that the engine has quietly twiddled the knob marked 'whether anyone wants this'. Without ever stating them explicitly, the piece should leave a reader feeling the genuinely awkward upshots — that on this picture there seems to be no principled way to distinguish a master from a hack turning the same knobs badly, that the model explains exploration inside a space but says nothing about where themes and spaces come from, and that the regress of knobs governing knobs has to end somewhere with a shrug.

Keep it deadpan from first line to last: no winking, no punchline announcing that all this is absurd, no moralising final paragraph, and no mention of Hofstadter by name inside the document itself. It should still be legible and funny to a reader who has never encountered the model. Invented technical vocabulary, invented proper names and invented in-house jargon are very welcome. Please write it in British English.
