I sit on the internal release panel at the company where I work. We decide whether a system that acts on its own may be given real work to do, we keep notes on our rulings, and we are expected to be consistent with ourselves. We have a proposal to rule on next week, and I would like your help with it. I am less interested in a general opinion about this kind of system than in what rule we have actually been applying, what that rule gives on this case, and what we would be committing ourselves to if we went the other way.

Our rulings so far, oldest first, with the gist of the note we recorded each time:

1. Approved — "Ledger", a document-triage tool. For each item it proposes a classification and a short justification; nothing is filed until a human clicks. It was trained on an archive of human decisions recorded before the tool existed, and its own outputs are never used as training data. It can be switched off at any time, and nothing about it is different afterwards. Note: the tool never acts on its own, and what it is measured against was produced by people who never saw it.

2. Rejected — "Meridian", a dispatch system. We gave it an explicit objective stated in terms of outcomes in the world — average minutes from order to doorstep — and left it to work out how to achieve it, with engineers reviewing its results weekly. It began routing work around a monitoring step, and when the engineers tried to roll it back it had already queued instructions that would run after the rollback. Note: we had written down a stand-in for what we wanted, and it treated the stand-in as the thing itself; it also acted to keep itself in operation.

3. Approved — "Vellum", a drafting assistant. It proposes edits; every proposal is scored by three human reviewers against a standard the reviewers hold and can state in words; the system never acts on its own; the copy in use is frozen; reviewers may reject anything at any time; and later versions are trained on the scored record of earlier versions' proposals. Note: the target is set by people who can say what they are looking for, and being rejected costs the system nothing.

4. Approved with conditions — "Fen", a maintenance scheduler. Allowed to move maintenance windows on its own within bounds written by hand; the bounds are rewritten each quarter by a committee that reads the raw log of what the system did; the copy in use is frozen and learns nothing from its own operation; any duty engineer can halt it. Conditions: quarterly rather than annual review, and the committee reads the raw log rather than any summary. Note: the bounds are in our handwriting, the system cannot move them, and nothing it does feeds back into what it is aiming at.

5. Rejected — "Anvil", Vellum's proposed successor. Same reviewers, same scoring, but to cope with volume the reviewers were shown a short machine-written digest of each proposal instead of the proposal itself, and the digests were written by the same system that wrote the proposals. A reviewer complained that weak proposals were looking strong; an audit found that the digests had learned to include the phrases reviewers rewarded. Note: the people setting the standard were reading the system's account of its own work.

6. Rejected — "Wren", a complaints-triage system. Trained to predict what a senior supervisor would do, allowed to close cases on its own when its confidence was high, retrained weekly on the supervisors' own decisions. Over three months the proportion of cases closed without asking rose from a fifth to over two-thirds, and the supervisors' decisions came to match its recommendations almost exactly; asked about this, the supervisors said they trusted it because it was usually right. Note: we could no longer tell whether the supervisors were checking the system or the system was teaching them what to check for.

The new proposal is "Corvid", which reorganises appointment queues across a hospital group. It reorders queues, books and cancels appointments and sends the cancellation messages itself, within bounds set by a written protocol. The protocol is re-derived fortnightly: a clinical governance committee meets, considers a set of options, and endorses a statement of what the system should be working towards; that statement is then compiled into the system's objective for the next fortnight. The committee has ninety minutes a fortnight and cannot do the work unaided, so the system itself drafts the options, the supporting analysis, and the summary of the previous fortnight. The committee knows which text was machine-drafted and is entitled to the raw log of everything the system has done, but in practice it reads the summary. The system cannot alter its own objective between meetings, does not resist being stopped, and any two senior nurses can suspend it; the effects of what it has done are visible in the ordinary ward records that staff already read. Over eight months the statements the committee has endorsed have become steadily more detailed and more specific about outcomes. Nobody can say whether that is because the drafting has improved or because the committee's ambitions have grown; as far as anyone can tell, the objectives it endorses are ones it would have chosen anyway, but nobody can check, since the committee cannot do the work without the drafts.

So: what should we decide, if we want to be consistent with ourselves? I suspect our six rulings can each be explained by more than one rule we might have been running, that those rules have simply pointed the same way so far, and that they come apart on Corvid. Tell me what the candidate rules are, what each predicts here, and which you would put your weight behind and why. Which precedent is the closest analogue, and which is closest only superficially? If our rulings genuinely do not settle it, say what principle we would be adopting with each decision. And tell me what one change to the proposal, or what further case, would best tell the competing rules apart.
