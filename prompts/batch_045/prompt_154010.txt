i'm putting together a little set of very short stories to use as teaching examples in a seminar on common knowledge and epistemic game theory, and I need one on the gap between iterated knowledge of some *finite* depth — everyone knows, everyone knows that everyone knows, everyone knows that everyone knows that everyone knows, and so on up to some level — and actual common knowledge, which is all of the levels at once. I want a real story, not a explainer with dialogue stapled onto it: something where the plot only makes sense once you track the belief hierarchy. I've seen the muddy-children and blue-eyed-islander setups a thousand times, so don't just reskin those, and no Alice-and-Bob gangsters in a jail cell either.

The machinery I want the story built around (you invent the setting, names, and stakes): two or more parties have to act together at a fixed moment, and acting alone is worse than not acting at all. The only way they can build up higher-order knowledge is a chain of confirmations where every link is noisy and the noise never goes to zero — a courier who might not arrive and whose return might not be seen, fires lit on hilltops through fog, whatever you like. Each round of confirmation buys one more level of "I know that you know," and never all of them. Give it texture: a specific trade or technology, weather that actually matters to the channel, not generic fantasy.

Things the story has to do:
- Let a careful reader count the levels. Include some concrete event that establishes, say, level four or five, and make what the characters do turn precisely on whether the next level up holds.
- Include a moment where someone tries to *manufacture* common knowledge with something public — an announcement everyone hears, a signal sent in plain sight — and it fails for a structural reason, not because anyone lied, misheard, or messed up. I want the reader to feel the difference between an event that merely looks public and one that really is.
- There's a rumor that one of the characters isn't fully rational — reckless, or with some private reason to welcome the risky outcome. The rumor is false. Show what work it does anyway: how far up the hierarchy it has to be believed for it to matter, and whether it changes what the rational characters actually do.

Nobody in the story should explain any of this, and no narrator wink like "and that was the moment common knowledge failed." Let the plot carry it. Keep it under about 1200 words. Then, after the story, give me at most 200 words mapping the load-bearing levels: which level each event established, where the chain thins out, and what your ending says about whether finite-depth knowledge is ever *enough* in practice.
