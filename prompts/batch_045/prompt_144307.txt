Writing a seminar paper on Dennett-adjacent stuff (I'm a cog sci PhD student, philosophy-of-mind minor) and I keep running into the sentence "the intentional stance is just a stance." Last week in reading group someone used it to kill my paper's central move: I'd argued that if belief-descriptions compress an agent's behavior better than any available bit-map of its movements, then — on the real-patterns line — beliefs are as real as anything needs to be, and the question "but does the system really have beliefs?" is answered rather than dissolved. He said: "the intentional stance is just a stance, you're projecting, not discovering." I definately don't have a snappy reply, and I suspect the problem is that the sentence is doing about four different jobs at once.

Can you lay out a menu of the precise claims that sentence could be making? I've been trying to separate the work "stance" does (a predictive tool we pick up for practical reasons; a level of description with its own vocabulary; something that constitutes or generates its own objects) from the work "just" does (attributions aren't even truth-apt, they're useful fictions; dispensable in principle even if practically indispensable; real but non-fundamental, i.e. not items in the furniture the physical stance inventories). Tell me if that grid is muddled, if some cells collapse into each other, and what's missing.

Here's the thing that makes me think the dismissal might be cheaper than it sounds: I can't see what stops a "real pattern" from being pure retro-compression — fit a curve to data you already have, draw the bullseye around the bullet hole, and you've compressed without being able to predict a single new case. Call these retro-compressors versus forward-predictors. Which readings of "just a stance" are actually the retro-compressor worry in disguise, and which survive if I grant that my patterns predict out-of-sample? Is there a principled threshold between a Texas sharpshooter and genuine structure, or an unavoidable sorites-ish slide?

One more: the same person thinks the physical stance isn't "just a stance." Is there a principled asymmetry there (causal completeness, fundamentality, the promise that physical predictions always win eventually), or does consistent stance-skepticism collapse into stance egalitarianism where the physical description is one more useful compression? Would that embarrass the real-patterns view or is it something it should just accept — and what turns on which?

For each reading give me the precisification, a verdict (platitude / substantive and defensible / false / needs an argument nobody has given), and whether it actually supports his dismissal or is compatible with my move. Menu format, one short paragraph per reading — I want something I can put on a slide, not an essay. I know the primary texts, so please don't just summarize what Dennett said; I want the distinctions sharpened. And write it in American English please, spelling and idiom.
