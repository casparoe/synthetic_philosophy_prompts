I'm a PhD student in political theory, I TA the graduate formal-theory sequence, and I'm on the committee rewriting my department's hiring rules after a search that ended badly. My colleagues will grant that no voting rule is perfect, but they treat Arrow's theorem as a technicality: they think the pathologies only appear in contrived profiles and that a sensible, widely used rule like the Borda count is safe in practice. I want a case small enough to check by hand, realistic enough to put in front of a faculty committee, and sharp enough that the failure cannot be waved off as a curiosity. It is for two things: persuading that committee, and a short section of a paper I'm drafting in which I want to introduce a condition strictly weaker than Arrow's independence of irrelevant alternatives — call it *independence of withdrawn losers* (IWL): if the alternative that is last in the social ordering is removed, the social ordering of the remaining alternatives is unchanged. I want to know how much that weakening actually buys.

Approach this as a formal social choice theorist who has also sat on hiring committees, and build me the following object. A five-member search committee has three finalists, A, B and C; every member has a strict ranking, no ties anywhere in your construction, and the committee aggregates by Borda count (2–1–0 over three candidates). The profile must satisfy all of: (i) A is the Borda winner and C is the Borda loser; (ii) C is nevertheless some member's first choice, so C is not Pareto-dominated and cannot be dismissed as an obviously irrelevant alternative; (iii) B defeats A by a strict pairwise majority, 3–2, in the original profile; (iv) C then withdraws before the vote — she accepted a job elsewhere, which really happens — the committee re-runs the identical rule on A and B alone, and B now wins, so the committee reverses its ranking of A against B even though no member changed her view about A versus B and even though B had a 3–2 majority over A all along. Then the demonstration: show that the majority relation over the survivors is unchanged by the withdrawal, so that no rule satisfying Arrow's IIA could produce this flip, and verify on your profile that Borda satisfies unrestricted domain, Pareto, non-dictatorship, and yields a complete and transitive social ordering — so that IIA is the only one of the five conditions that fails. The flip must fall out of the rule's arithmetic on the profile; stipulating it is not acceptable. Identify the numerical feature doing the work (my suspicion: the asymmetry between how many members rank C above A and how many rank C above B) and prove it by deleting that asymmetry — put all five members on the same side of C with respect to A and B — and showing the flip disappears with everything else intact. Then give the strongest reply available to a Borda defender, roughly that IIA is not a requirement of rationality once positional or intensity information is allowed to count, and say honestly what that reply costs elsewhere in light of Arrow. Finally, two honest notes: report whether your profile also contains a Condorcet cycle and whether the cycle is doing any of the work, and tell me what happens if I add the requirement that the profile have a Condorcet winner.

For the paper: is IWL genuinely weaker than IIA, and is there any rule — described concretely, not proved to exist by citation — that satisfies Pareto, non-dictatorship, completeness, transitivity and IWL together? Check the near-misses rather than asserting: Copeland, plurality, sequential pairwise voting on a fixed agenda. If plurality is your candidate, check Pareto on it; if you think no such rule exists, say where exactly the obstruction bites and where you get stuck. Before you write the finished answer, do the scratch work: try profiles, tally the Borda scores and the pairwise scores, discard the failures, and show me a trace of that search. Then present the finished answer in at most 600 words, under short headings — Profile, Verdicts, What does the work, The defence, IWL. I will check every number.
