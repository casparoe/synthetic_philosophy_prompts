I am a high school senior working on an extended essay for a school history-of-ideas competition, and the section I am stuck on is a deliberately speculative one, so I need help reasoning it through rather than a list of things to read: I have to do the footnotes myself anyway, and my school blocks most journal sites.

The essay follows one thread: how people have argued that you ought to size a bet or an investment. My current understanding is this. In 1738 Daniel Bernoulli proposed, in his paper on the St Petersburg gamble, that what matters is not the money you stand to gain but the usefulness of that gain to you, and that an extra dollar matters less the more money you already have; written down as an equation, that gives you a logarithmic curve. Roughly two centuries later, Kelly, working at Bell Labs, derived a formula for what fraction of your money to risk on a favourable bet by asking what makes your capital grow fastest over many rounds. On paper the two rules look like the same rule, since both end up telling you to care about the logarithm of your wealth. So my working thesis for the essay is that Bernoulli had already found the right answer in 1738, that Kelly merely rediscovered it, and that nothing important happened in between.

Here is where I want to put Bernoulli in front of something he never saw. Suppose we hand him the description of a modern daily-rebalanced triple-leveraged index fund, together with this small calculation: let the index go from 100 to 90 on the first day and back to 100 on the second. The index has gone nowhere, but the 3x fund goes 100 to 70 to about 93.3, so it has lost roughly 6.7 percent. Extend that over a year of choppy markets and you get funds that can finish below where they started across periods in which the underlying index finished higher. Would Bernoulli say this is his point all over again, merely dressed up in modern finance? Or would he say it is a different phenomenon from the one he was writing about, on the grounds that he was worried about a single choice and the diminishing usefulness of money, not about what repeated multiplication does to you over time?

The second thing I want his reaction to is a simulation I ran on my own laptop. Take a bet that pays back twice what you staked when you win, with a 60 percent chance of winning, offered to you 100 times in a row. Compare two policies: risk 20 percent of your current money every round against risking everything every round. In nearly every individual run, the 20 percent policy ends up far richer. But if I average across all the runs instead, risking everything wins by an absurd margin, because of the vanishingly small handful of runs in which it never loses once. My teacher says this shows that two different questions are in play and that Bernoulli only ever asked one of them. I am not sure I follow her, and I am not sure which of the two numbers Bernoulli would have considered the relevant one.

So, putting it together: given what Bernoulli actually argued and the reasons he gave for it, how do you think he would have reacted to the leveraged-fund example and to my simulation? Would he have told the hypothetical investor to use the Kelly fraction, to stay out of the bet altogether, or to do something else, and on what grounds? And would he have accepted the growth-rate argument as a genuine argument distinct from his own, or would he have taken it to be his own utility curve restated?
