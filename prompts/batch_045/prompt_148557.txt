I write about science and ideas for a weekly magazine, and I'm working on a piece about generative music systems. I want to put Schopenhauer in the room: he is the one major nineteenth-century thinker for whom music is metaphysically central rather than decorative, and I suspect he is also the one least impressed by the obvious complaint about machine-made music. So what I need is a disciplined speculation, not a summary of his aesthetics.

Consider three artefacts: a model trained on the complete Mozart that emits further Mozart-consistent symphonies; a model composing in real time against a listener's heart rate and skin conductance, reliably driving them toward a target emotional state; and a system producing endlessly varying, never-repeating ambient music inside a game world that no one scores.

Where would he come down on each, and on what grounds? I care about which strand of the account does the real work — music as immediate objectification of the will rather than a copy of the Ideas, the primacy of melody over harmony and over words, or his views on genius, imitation and mere virtuosity — and about which of the three cases would force him to revise something he actually says. State the counterintuitive part plainly too: on his metaphysics, does the complaint that no one is behind the music carry any weight at all?

Two constraints. Attribute to him only commitments he really held, and mark clearly where you are extrapolating beyond them; and tell me the one place where my framing misleads.
