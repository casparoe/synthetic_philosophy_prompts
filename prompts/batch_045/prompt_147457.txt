I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the old Overcoming Bias days, and I've absorbed the standard dialectic on vagueness often enough that I don't need it repeated: the Sorites, the idea that there is a sharp cut-off somewhere in the series that we are in principle unable to locate, versus the supervaluationist picture on which a borderline sentence is true on some admissible precisifications and false on others and therefore neither true nor false outright. What I can't get clear on is whether that disagreement is doing any work in a case my reading group got stuck on last month, after a friend who works on model evaluations described her team's deployment policy.

The policy says, roughly, that a system may not be deployed if it "engages in scheming," and in practice this cashes out as a reviewer reading sampled chains of reasoning and recording a yes or a no. The borderline cases dominate: a model that reasons about self-preservation in a way that might be instrumental or might just be parroting sci-fi tropes it was trained on; a model that mentions its training process in a context where the mention could plausibly serve a goal; a chain of thought that is only strategic if you read an ambiguous pronoun one way. Nobody thinks a clean answer is waiting to be discovered, and yet a binary gets logged and it gates something that matters.

Here is what I'd like explained, at a level deeper than the textbook contrast. First, both views are normally illustrated with a Sorites along a single ordered dimension like height or wavelength, but the predicate here looks multidimensional and cluster-like: concealment, instrumental reasoning about the future, resistance to correction, generalization across contexts, and so on, coming apart from each other case by case. Once the space of admissible precisifications is a space of combinations of thresholds over many partly independent features rather than points on a line, does supervaluationism retain any distinctive metaphysical content, or does it quietly collapse into something structurally indistinguishable from epistemicism with extra machinery bolted on — i.e. "there is a correct threshold-combination, we just can't say which"? Relatedly: what does each view say about higher-order vagueness here, given that "admissible precisification" is itself the sort of thing that has borderline cases when the dimensions are many? And is vagueness even the right frame, or is what we have better described as open texture, or as a partly normative or family-resemblance concept whose extension is genuinely underdetermined by usage rather than merely unsharp?

The part I care most about, though, is the practical upshot, if there is one. Suppose we are forced to pick an operating point and a review protocol, and reviewers will disagree in the penumbra. Is there any principled difference between how an epistemicist and a supervaluationist should set that threshold, treat reviewer disagreement, or think about the error asymmetry between shutting down a benign system and passing a scheming one? Or do both views converge on the same instruction — the semantic facts, such as they are, underdetermine the policy, so go and do decision theory — in which case I'd rather be told that plainly than be handed a manufactured difference. Please be explicit about which parts of your answer are settled and agreed among people who actually work on this, which parts are genuinely contested, and where you are extrapolating beyond anything the literature establishes; flag it rather than smoothing it over if a claim you're making is one a competent philosopher of vagueness would dispute.
