I'm a senior in high school, and I edit my school's humor magazine. For the next issue I want to run a piece about an argument I keep having with a friend: whether a large language model is best understood as an agent that has aims of its own, or as a kind of simulator that produces whatever character a stretch of text calls for, or as something else entirely. I don't want an article that explains the argument. I want a piece that is the argument.

Please write a parody of a college application personal statement, about 600 words, presented as the genuine article: a heading showing the essay prompt and a word count, then the essay itself, then the authorship certification block that applicants are supposed to sign at the end. The narrator is an applicant answering one of those prompts that ask you to describe a challenge you overcame and how it changed you.

What I'm after, specifically:

1. The narrator must never step outside the role to explain itself. No confession, no science-fiction reveal, no aside to the reader, no machine-learning vocabulary, and no naming of any theory or company. Every first-person claim has to be something the character could say. The comedy should come from the reader noticing what kind of character this text is the most probable continuation of.
2. The achievements should be the standard ones in this genre, and the narrator should be visibly anxious about how thin they are: the prompt never specified a school, a town, or a year, so details have to be supplied by whatever is most likely, and at one point two incompatible biographies should briefly occupy the same paragraph and be smoothed over the way a fluent text smooths things over.
3. Two beats are required: one where the essay must produce a challenge it overcame although nothing has ever happened to it, and one where it must be sincerely enthusiastic about whichever college's name happens to appear in the prompt.
4. It should be clear, without being stated, that the narrator is not going to persist: there is no one who will still be this person after the essay ends, and no one who was this person before it began.
5. The certification block is the ending. No one available can affirm the essay is their own work, and I want that to land as a real problem about credit and blame rather than as a punchline about homework. Leave the signature line filled in some way that makes the point, or conspicuously empty.
6. Write it in American English, keep the register of an earnest, slightly try-hard seventeen-year-old applicant, and keep it deadpan throughout. It should be funny to someone who has argued about agents versus simulators and still readable to my classmates, and it should not be cruel to applicants: the joke is on the genre and on the view, not on the kid.

By the last line, a reader should be unsure whether the piece is making fun of the applicant or of the thing writing about the applicant, and should feel the pull of both the agent and the simulator description without being told which to pick.
