I'm writing a short essay (about 1200 words when finished, in the form of working notes below) on the utility monster for a philosophy blog, and I'd like critical comments before I expand it into a full draft. The notes' central claim is that the utility monster is usually aimed at the wrong target: it is less a counterexample to maximizing total welfare as such than an inconsistency proof about a package of background assumptions — additive separability across persons, cardinal interpersonal comparability, and unbounded (or very high) ceilings on individual welfare — none of which the standard discussion isolates. The two extensions I care most about are the endogenous-monster problem in section 3 and the parity argument in section 4.

DRAFT NOTES: "The Utility Monster Was Never About Monsters"

1. The standard reading, and what I think is wrong with it.

Nozick's utility monster is a being who converts resources into welfare at a rate enormously higher than anyone else's, so that, on the maximizing story, we ought to keep transferring resources to it until everyone else is at the edge of starvation. The usual moral drawn is that maximizing total welfare is callous or inhuman, and so needs side constraints or rights. I think this draws the conclusion too fast. The monster is generated by three separable commitments: (i) that the value of an outcome is a sum of individual welfare levels (separability and additivity across persons); (ii) that one person's welfare can be put on a common cardinal scale with another's, so that "the monster gains a thousand times what each of us loses" is a meaningful comparison; (iii) that there is no ceiling, or at least no low ceiling, on how much welfare a single life can contain. Give up any one and the monster starves: reject (i) with a prioritarian or leximin ordering and large gains to someone already well off count for little; reject (ii) and the comparison that drives the transfer cannot even be stated; reject (iii) with a low ceiling and the monster's maw fills up after a few transfers. So the monster is best read as a proof that you cannot have all three commitments together with the intuition that no one's mere efficiency at enjoyment should let them swallow the world. That is a real result, but it is a result about the shape of an aggregation function, not a refutation of welfarism from the outside.

2. Why the cheap fix is not cheap.

The natural move is to apply a concave transformation to individual welfare before summing, so that welfare gains to someone already at a high level count for progressively less. This is the prioritarian fix, and it does kill the monster. But it does so by making the aggregator systematically less responsive to size of benefit, and there is no way to do that in one direction only. Section 4 argues that this is a real cost and not a technicality.

3. Endogenous monsterhood: monsterhood as an investment.

The example treats utility functions as given. But the capacity to convert resources into welfare is partly something agents can develop: through attention and appreciation, through cultivated tastes, and through the acquisition of expensive tastes whose satisfaction is very rewarding. Now suppose the planner's standing rule is to allocate resources to whoever has the highest marginal welfare yield. Then monsterhood is not a piece of bad luck that befalls a population; it is the rational life plan. Everyone has a reason to invest in becoming efficient at enjoyment, and because the reward goes to whoever is most efficient, the result is an arms race in which the resources that were supposed to become welfare are instead spent on building the capacity to convert them. The population may end up worse off in total than if everyone had stayed crude and easily satisfied.

The interesting consequence is that the maximizing policy must become self-effacing. Ex post, once a monster exists, feeding it maximizes. Ex ante, a planner who can commit should commit to a rule that does not reward monsterhood at all, precisely so that no one invests in becoming a monster. So the correct welfarist policy is one that sometimes refuses to maximize total welfare when the opportunity arises. I take this to be a structural argument, not a contingent one: it depends only on the fact that the return to a resource depends on something agents control, and on the fact that the planner's rule is visible to them.

4. The veiled monster, and the measurement problem.

Monsterhood is private information. Whether someone really converts resources into welfare at a hundred times your rate is not something you can observe, and reports of welfare intensity are cheap to fake. So consider a planner who is maximally responsive to reported intensity: this creates what I'll call a monster premium, a standing reward for claiming — and, given section 3, for cultivating — an unusually high marginal yield. The more finely the theory discriminates between people's intensities, the more it pays people to misrepresent them, and the more it corrupts the very interpersonal comparisons the theory needs in order to be applied at all. A theory whose application requires precise cardinal comparisons across persons, and whose implementation destroys the reliability of those comparisons, is in a predicament: the only usable allocation rules may be ones that are nearly insensitive to reports, e.g. roughly equal shares. If so, the monster is not defeated by a better aggregator; it is defeated by giving up on the ambition of fine-grained interpersonal comparison, which is a much larger concession than the literature usually registers.

5. Parity: the anti-monster, and the cost of killing the monster.

Consider the mirror image of the monster: a being whose welfare, at the bottom of the scale, is enormously negative, and for whom small contributions from a great many people produce very large gains — call it the sink. Structurally, the sink's claim on us is the same claim the monster makes: one being's enormous welfare swings, produced by small changes in the resources of many others, dominate the sum. Yet our intuitions here run the other way: we think the many should contribute to relieve the one. Now notice that the prioritarian fix of section 2 blocks both claims in the same structural position, because what it discounts is size of welfare change at the extreme, regardless of sign or direction. This gives a symmetry constraint: if you accept the sink's claim, you owe an account of what distinguishes it from the monster's claim other than that you like the answer in one case and not the other.

Three candidate distinguishers strike me as available, and I do not think a pure welfarist can use any of them. (a) The sink is in need while the monster is merely enjoying itself: but "need" here is a claim about a baseline, and a welfare-based theory has no non-arbitrary baseline to appeal to. (b) The monster chose its expensive tastes, while the sink did not choose its predicament: this is a responsibility-sensitive distinction, and importing it means leaving welfarism for something closer to a resource- or responsibility-based view, which is a bigger revision than the standard discussion admits. (c) Losses matter more than forgone gains: this is a genuine asymmetry, but it is exactly the kind of asymmetry that an additively separable welfare sum cannot represent.

6. What I think follows.

If the above is right, the utility monster is a fork. Either you keep the three commitments and feed the monster; or you keep welfarism but give up the ambition of fine-grained interpersonal comparison and accept something close to equal shares; or you abandon the purely welfare-based framework and take on responsibility or resources as a separate currency, with all the difficulties that brings. None of these is cheap, and the usual two-paragraph treatment of the monster does not reveal that the choice is this hard.
