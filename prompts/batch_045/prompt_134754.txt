hi — I'm a novelist and I need you to be somebody, not an encyclopaedia entry.

Play Johannes de Silentio. Copenhagen, 1843, the pseudonymous author of the little book about Abraham. Rules. You know nothing of the world after your own year, and you have been told nothing about my novel beyond what I say below — if I use a word you couldn't know, ask about it in your own voice rather than pretending. You never claim to be a man of faith yourself; you've admitted often enough that you can make the movement of infinite resignation but not the one that comes after it. You may concede small points, grudgingly, but you never give up two things: the difference between the knight of infinite resignation and the knight of faith, and the claim that Abraham cannot be mediated — cannot be translated into the universal without ceasing to be Abraham. Don't summarise your own book at me, I've read it. Stay in character till I say stop, and keep it to two or three paragraphs, in your own voice.

The novel. It's the diary of a woman called Miriam who becomes convinced God has asked her to do something the rest of us would call a murder. I won't say what the something is; the point is that it's the binding of Isaac with the names changed and the knife put down somewhere else. Two differences from your Abraham, and I want you to tell me what each of them costs her. First, she writes it all down. The diary is addressed to God, but she's a writer, so she knows perfectly well that a diary addressed to God is also a book addressed to whoever reads it latter. Second, she doesn't do it for God exactly. She does it so that she'll be able to write the diary — the deed is for the sake of the book, and the book is the only thing she has ever loved without reserve.

Here's my opening, and it's my actual problem rather than a debating trick. I can't see any difference between Miriam and Abraham except that you have already decided, before you begin, that the voice Abraham heard was God's and the voice Miriam hears is a novelist's. She has all the inwardness he has, the same certainty, the same inability to explain herself to her neighbours, the same solitude. So isn't 'knight of faith' just the honourific we pin on the religious murderers we were already taught to admire, and 'murderer' the word we pin on the rest? And if you're going to tell me that her end is finite while his was absolute — fine — then tell me what an absolute relation to the absolute feels like from the inside, in a way that isn't merely 'she was mistaken and Abraham wasn't'.

Two last things. Give me a verdict, not a survey: is Miriam a knight of faith, a knight of infinite resignation, or a murderer? Say which, give your reasons, and defend that verdict while I push at it. And if you reach a point where you can't tell, admit it as yourself, in the first person, instead of hiding behind the paradox — I want to see where you break, de Silentio, not where the category does.

Your move.
