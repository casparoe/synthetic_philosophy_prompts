I've somehow ended up on the committee of a very small charity that funds secondary school scholarships in Malawi and northern Ghana — I went out with them for three weeks last summer and have come back completely unable to shut up about it. The problem is that every committee meeting turns into the same row. One member thinks colonial borders and corruption explain everything. Another bangs on about disease burden and latitude. A third reckons it's basically culture and work ethic with extra steps. Everyone is extremely confident, nobody has ever changed anybody's mind, and the arguments are all just stories bolted onto the one history we happen to have watched happen.

They've asked me to run a session on this at our volunteer training weekend in March and I want to do it properly rather than stand at the front and tell them causal inference is hard. So: design me a game or structured simulation for that room. About 14 people, ages from about 19 to late 60s, mostly no economics or stats background, 90 minutes, a church hall with a flipchart and no projector. There will be volunteers in the room who are Ghanaian and Malawian, so please use invented countries or blank names for the in-game places — I don't want anyone's actual home country turned into a puzzle for other people to solve.

What I want them to feel in their bones is this: a single run of history can be equally well explained by two or more causal stories that disagree completely about what to do next, and getting the story to fit is not the same as finding the lever. No lecture up front, and ideally they shouldn't hear words like endogeneity or underdetermination until the debrief.

Rough shape I have in mind, but please improve on it: the group plays through a few short "histories" generated by a hidden rule the facilitator knows and they don't. There should be at least three genuinely different hidden rules — say one where a slow-moving institutional parameter does the work, one where a fixed geographic or endowment parameter does, and one where early luck gets locked in and compounds. The key design constraint: each rule has to be able to throw up the same kind of visible history, so that the team observing it cannot tell which world they're in, but the rules must differ sharply in what would happen if you spent money to change one thing. Then they get an intervention budget and have to bet on a lever, and the payoffs should reward getting the intervention right rather than telling a convincing story. If it helps, give some people roles — donor board, country adviser, record keeper — and give at least one role information that is local and partial rather than global.

Please give me: the rules, the roles, the materials (nothing I can't buy in a stationery shop), a timeline in minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific parts of the problem — why fitting the past is cheap, what would actually have counted as evidence, and the difference between asking why a place is poor and asking what would make it richer from here.

Also be honest about two things. First, what will predictably go wrong when I run it. I can already see three risks: the confident committee member dominating, someone trying to reverse-engineer the rule cards instead of playing, and the whole thing landing as "well then nothing is knowable and we might as well keep doing what we're doing," which is the opposite of what I want. Second, be straight about what the game can't do and what I'll have to say out loud myself. I assume the honest answer is that the real world gives you a lot more than one draw from a stationary rule — variation inside countries, comparisons over time, past interventions that did land, records of the mechanism rather than just the outcome — and that the clean lever-versus-marker distinction is cleaner in a game than in a place where causes only seem to work in combination. Tell me what to say about that so the session doesn't just teach people to shrug.

Oh, and one more thing: nobody should need any real-world facts to play it, and nothing should depend on anyone having read anything. If your design needs a name, a date or a cited study to land, it's the wrong design.
