I run a tiny philosophy podcast out of the spare room — a few hundred listeners, mostly people washing dishes — and no philosophy background myself, I just got obssessed with this stuff reading about it online. Next week's guest is Dr. Marisol Okonjo, a philosopher with a new book on the situationist critique of virtue ethics. Her line, as I understand it: the critique was right that people don't have big global character traits, but wrong that this is bad news for virtue ethics, because virtues were never meant to be global. A real virtue is a reliable disposition to act well inside a narrow, specifiable kind of situation, and it usually only exists because something in the setup props it up — a norm, a script, a doorway you can't walk past. Her slogan is that character is a property of a person-plus-setup, not of a person. She also pushes two things I haven't seen anyone say: that we should deliberately engineer these local virtues and treat the person who designs the situation as the one who has the virtue; and that on her account it's not crazy to call a system honest or conscientious — a hospital, a benefits office, maybe even a piece of software — while denying that any individual inside it is.

Whenever I try to press her on point two she says some version of "that's just the fundamental attribution error one level up," and I never know what to do with that. So: draft me ten to twelve questions, in the order I should ask them, that make that deflection progressively harder to reach for, ending with one question about what evidence could actually count against the view. I want at least one question on each of these, and a follow-up keyed to what she'll most likely say:

- The old study where seminary students on their way to give a talk stepped over (or didn't) a guy slumped in a doorway coughing, and how much of it was down to being in a hurry. Why is time pressure evidence about character rather than just about attention?
- The obedience experiments where ordinary people kept administering what they thought were dangerous shocks. Weren't those subjects reliably consistent across a whole situation-type — just in a bad direction? Does her local-virtue machinery let a reliable torturer count as locally virtuous?
- Where exactly a local trait stops being character and starts being a habit or a reflex. If the reliability vanishes the moment the scaffold does, what is the word virtue doing?
- Moral education. If you raise a kid by arranging her situations, have you given her a virtue or just managed her? And what about people whose work is mostly unscaffolded — improvisation, novel cases, being alone with a decision?
- The system claim. If the designer can hold the virtue, why not the institution, and why not the software? Make her say whether she accepts that and what it costs her.

Give me the questions as prose first, then a JSON array with one object per question, using exactly these keys: number (integer), question (the full question text), target (which commitment of hers the question pressures, one clause), her_likely_reply (one sentence, in her voice), follow_up (the question I ask next), concession (what she'd have to give up if she answered honestly), deflection_risk (one of low, medium, high — how likely she reaches for the one-level-up move).
