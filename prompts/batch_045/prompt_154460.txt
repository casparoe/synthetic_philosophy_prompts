I'm in a third-year philosophy of science course and we did inference to the best explanation a couple weeks ago, and I came out of it thinking I basically knew what "best" meant, and now that I've tried writing down my actual verdicts on real cases I definately don't. My prof keeps insisting there's a difference between how lovely an explanation is and how likely it is to be true, and that the second doesn't just collapse into the first. I half believe her. Here are the verdicts, in no particular order.

Cash gone from the kitchen drawer, no forced entry, nothing else taken, dog didn't bark. "My housemate took it" beats "a stranger picked the lock," and I think partly because it earns credit for stuff that *didn't* happen — the absences, not just the missing cash.

The department is quietly punishing me for the complaint I filed: my grade got changed, my advisor stopped replying to emails, my seminar slot got moved. Every new thing that happens fits. That's exactly the reason I distrust it. It would have fit whatever happened.

But in the cash case, fitting a lot of things was a point in favour. So "explains a lot" is a virtue in one and a vice in the other, and I can't say in a way I'd defend what separates them.

My housemate's story about the scratched pan: first she wasn't home, then she was home but never went in the kitchen, then she was in the kitchen but didn't touch the pan. Each revision fits the evidence better than the last and each one makes the explanation worse. But when my laptop kept dying and I went from "battery is degraded" to "battery is degraded so it can't handle peak draw, which is why it only dies under load and never when idle," the added detail made the explanation *better*, even though I also only thought of it after noticing the pattern. Both are post hoc additions that improve fit; one is fine and one is poison.

Car won't start: "it's just old" is worthless even though it would unify the noises, the slow crank, the dim lights. "The starter solenoid is shot" wins. But with my friend who's been short with me, sleeping till 2, and skipping our study sessions, I prefer "she's burned out" over "she's mad at me about last week," even though the second is more specific and easier to check. So unification beats specificity in one case and loses to it in the other.

3am scraping in the wall. Raccoon, house settling, the guy upstairs building something. Raccoon is clearly the best of the three and I believe none of them. Same with the friend who keeps cancelling — "she doesn't actually want to see me" fits everything and I refuse to believe it.

Case I keep going back and forth on: the milk. Empty carton rinsed and put back in the fridge. "Housemate drank it" is simpler and needs nothing extra. "Housemate drank it and is quietly annoyed with me so didn't mention it" costs an extra assumption but it's the only thing that makes the rinsed-and-returned carton make any sense. I flip weekly.

My attempt at a principle, to be tested and repaired rather than admired: an explanation beats its rivals when (1) it fits what happened *and* what didn't; (2) any refinement or added assumption sticks its neck out somewhere I haven't already checked, i.e. it has surplus content beyond patching the bit that was challenged; (3) it could have been wrong — the evidence had a way of going against it rather than being absorbed; (4) it names a mechanism that generates the symptoms rather than a label that just sums them up; (5) extra assumptions are worth buying when they're cheap, like a motive, and the detail they save is otherwise really surprising.

Questions. Does one principle actually get all of these, or are these just five separate virtues with no rule for weighting them when they conflict, and if it's the latter does that mean IBE is a mess? Which of my verdicts does the best candidate force me to drop, and why that one? Give me its verdicts on two cases I haven't listed, chosen by you, and include at least one where it says something I won't like. Does any of it reduce to ordinary Bayesian updating, likelihoods times priors, or is there an irreducibly explanatory ingredient left over?

And the thing I can't fit into the principle at all: how much should the possibility that the true explanation isn't on my list lower my confidence? Call it search-closure, the degree to which I think I've actually enumerated the decent hypotheses. It feels like it should matter enormously for the 3am noise and basically not at all for the milk, and I don't know whether that's because the hypothesis spaces really differ or because I'm smuggling base rates in under a new name.
