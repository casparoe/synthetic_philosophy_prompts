My lab has started reporting self-consistency numbers on evals: sample the model k times at temperature 0.8, take the most common answer, report that. One guy on the team insists this is literally an instance of the Condorcet jury theorem — each sample is a voter with competence p > 1/2, the votes are independent, so majority accuracy goes to 1 as k grows, and we can pick k the way youd size a jury. He also wants to weight samples by the models own confidence score, on the grounds that better voters should count more; that bit i think is importing something the theorem doesnt say, since the version i know is equal-weight majority over voters with the same p.

Can you audit the analogy? Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the voter, of p, of independence, of the binary proposition, of the truth — and then the places the mapping breaks. The ones i care about: our votes come out of one model with shared weights and shared pretraining, so they have a common cause (is that like a jury with a shared bias, and how much correlation does the theorem tolerate before the result stops holding?); the theorem is binary but our majority is over an open answer space, or over 4–5 options when its multiple choice (does the guarantee weaken as options multiply, and in what way?); and p is a property of voters, whereas what we vary is the *question* — on the hard items wed actually bother running this on, per-sample accuracy could be under half, and then majority makes things worse. In practice our gains flatten out around k = 5, which seems to tell against the asymptotic story.

Then: does the conclusion he wants — its not a hack, its asymptotically justified, we can buy accuracy with compute — survive, or was the analogy carrying it? And if theres a better thing to compare k samples to than k jurors, say what. Please separate whats just the mathematics under stated assumptions from whats genuinely contested among people who work on this.
