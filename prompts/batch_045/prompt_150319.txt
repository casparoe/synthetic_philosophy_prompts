I've been reading LessWrong and the blogs orbiting it for about fifteen years, and one thing I absorbed early from that scene is a habit I associate with Dennett: treat a philosophical thought experiment less as a piece of evidence than as a piece of rhetorical technology, something well- or ill-made, which very often carries its conclusion in its framing rather than in its structure. Over the years I've accumulated a set of views about when a case can be trusted, and I'm finally writing them up as a short position statement for a reading group I help run on philosophical method. Before I post it I'd like someone to check whether the set hangs together. Here it is, roughly as drafted.

(1) My main filter. Say a case has a twin if you can build a second case with the same abstract description — same pattern of causal and agential relations, same relevant options — but different surface dressing (a hospital instead of a trolley, a dial instead of a hand, a plague instead of a famine), such that the second case reliably evokes the opposite verdict. When I can find a twin for a case, I stop taking the case's verdict as evidence about anything. I've taken to calling verdicts that survive this treatment inelastic, and I've found the rule eats a large fraction of the famous cases.

(2) A second filter, independent of the first. I heavily discount intuitions about situations that are wildly out of distribution relative to anything our cognitive machinery could have been shaped by: populations of ten-to-the-thirtieth people, timescales of billions of years, mind uploading, agents with godlike power. In those regions I don't think my repulsion or attraction is tracking anything.

(3) Nevertheless, I think the experience machine gives real, defeasible evidence against a purely hedonic account of welfare. I have tried to build a twin for it several times and failed; the verdict seems to survive redescription, and I don't think it reduces to mere novelty-aversion or status-quo preference, though I can't fully rule that out.

(4) I think the repugnant conclusion is a genuine, load-bearing problem: a serious strike against total utilitarianism, not a curiosity to be explained away. More generally I think the very-large-numbers cases in population ethics are where the real action is, and that a population ethics that goes quiet there is not worth having.

(5) I think verdicts in the trolley family are evidentially worthless — the difference between diverting and pushing doesn't track anything I care about — and I nevertheless use those cases every time I teach, deliberately, because they make people feel their own principles collide.

(6) The best thought experiments are, in my vocabulary, consistency engines: they take a principle you already hold and force an application you hadn't considered, and their output is not a fresh intuition but a forced choice between commitments. Parfit's fission and teletransportation cases are my gold standard here.

(7) I don't think showing that a case's pull depends on its imagery is by itself a refutation of the verdict. I've seen too many dismissals of the form that's just an intuition pump that were really just name-calling. Sensitivity to imagery is the beginning of an objection, not the end of one.

What I want is an audit, not a survey of the literature. Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it — I'd rather you discriminate than flag all seven as problematic — then rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad. For each, say what would have to give and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the set, and finish by naming the single revision that cleans up the most. I have a vague hunch the trouble is in (1), since it's the item I've never written down before, but hunches are cheap and I'd rather you found the problem than confirmed my guess.

Two more things. Put a rough confidence on each of your verdicts, and tell me what would change your mind about the one you're least sure of. And separate what follows from the seven items as stated from anything you'd need to know about how people in fact respond to these cases, or about what the empirical work on philosophical intuitions shows: if some tension only bites given a finding you can't check, flag it and set it aside rather than asserting the finding from memory. Finally, if you think the framework itself is broken — if, for instance, the twin-pump rule can't be stated in a way that does the work I want of it, or if the whole project of auditing a set of methodological hunches like this is confused — say so plainly and tell me what to put in its place. A polite clause-by-clause audit of a broken question is the least useful thing you could hand me.
