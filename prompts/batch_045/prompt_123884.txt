I've never been able to settle, in my own mind, whether the problem of universals is a genuine question that something hangs on, or a knot that ought to be dissolved rather than untied. My sense of the terrain came to me the way it does for a lot of people of my generation: years of reading rationalist blogs and forum threads, where the default move is to say that a concept is a cluster in thingspace, that the clusters are there whether or not we draw the boundaries, and to treat the medieval and early-modern disputes as what you get when you try to do this without linear algebra. I find that response satisfying right up until I try to apply it, and then it seems to me to answer a different question than the one that was asked.

So I want to try a different angle, and I'd like your help with it: a prediction about a specific historical figure, for which I want a bottom line I could be corrected on, not a survey of positions.

Suppose we could put three cases to John Locke, with enough patience to explain the background and enough fluency to get a real answer back. I'll describe each case in modern terms; you should assume he takes them seriously rather than balking at the framing.

First case. We train a large neural network on images with no labels at all. Afterwards it contains internal features that answer to no word in any language, that nobody asked for, and that on inspection turn out to track something very close to our category of dogs: there is a direction in the network's activation space that separates dog-images from everything else nearly perfectly, and downstream behaviour of the system depends on it. Nothing in the training signal said "dog." Is that direction, for Locke, a universal? I take the pressure point to be this: for Locke a nominal essence is an abstract idea annexed to a name, and there is no name here and — on his own account of ideas — possibly no idea either. Does he conclude that the system has no concepts and the question doesn't arise, or does he treat the discovered regularity as evidence about the objects: that nature does have a joint there, at the level of the real constitution he thought we couldn't reach?

Second case. Two clusterings of the same data fit the observed cases equally well. One is the ordinary one; the other is a grue-style predicate: green for things observed before a certain date, blue for things observed after, with the date chosen so that everything so far falls on the green side. Both are perfectly clean on past data. Only one of them survives being deployed later. Where does Locke's apparatus locate the difference between them — in the constitution of the things themselves, or in the ideas we happen to have formed? And what would he say we are entitled to conclude from the failure of the second clustering: that it was never tracking a real essence, or only that it was inconvenient?

Third case. Locke is pessimistic that we can know the insensible corpuscular constitutions that he thinks ground the qualities we sort things by; our species, he thinks, are mostly our workmanship rather than nature's. But the science that came after him produced exactly the kind of item he said was out of reach: atomic number, molecular structure, the periodic table's joints — and these turn out to license prediction and intervention to a degree nothing in his own classificatory practice did. If we hand him this, does he have to give up the unknowability claim, and if he gives it up, does he thereby become a realist about universals? Or can he absorb the case while still leaving nominal essences to do all the sorting work?

What I want from you:

Your single best reconstruction of what Locke would say about each case, and — importantly — a clear marking of which parts are forced by the arguments he actually makes and which are you extrapolating past where his texts reach. Give me the strongest steelman of whichever reading you reject, whether that is Locke-as-realist or Locke-as-nominalist. Tell me, without chapter and verse and without a reading list, what kind of thing in his writings, if it turned out to be there, would settle the matter, and what would count as evidence that your reconstruction is wrong. Say plainly whether there is something incoherent in the exercise or merely something underdetermined, and say which of his commitments he would have to abandon to answer at all.

Finally, my own bias, so you can discount it: I find a position persuasive in proportion to whether it tells us in advance which clusterings will license induction, rather than redescribing afterwards which ones did. If both readings do equally well or equally badly on that, I would rather be told that than given a verdict manufactured to fit my criterion.
