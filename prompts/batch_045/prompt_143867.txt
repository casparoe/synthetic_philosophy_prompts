I belong to a small reading group — two moral philosophers, an economist, and a friend who works on alignment research — and we have spent most of a year on the question of how to act when you are uncertain which moral theory is true. We all accept the same setup: you have credences spread over several moral theories, those theories disagree about what you should do here and now, and you cannot resolve the disagreement before you have to act. Past that we deadlock every time. One of us holds that this is just empirical uncertainty in a different costume, so you should assign each theory a cardinal scale of how good each outcome is, normalize somehow, and maximize expected choiceworthiness. A second holds that comparisons of value across theories are unavailable — each theory's scale is internal to that theory — so the only usable common currency is rightness itself, and you should instead maximize the probability that your act is morally permissible or required, ignoring how badly wrong the alternatives would be. A third holds that there is no distinct problem: the only real question is what you have most reason to do, all things considered, and any proposed higher-order rule for weighing moral theories against each other is itself just one more first-order moral claim competing inside the same set, with no special authority from its supposed second-order status. We have now restated these three positions at each other roughly ten times, and I want to know why we are not moving.

Part of my suspicion is that the disagreement is settled in advance by a formatting assumption nobody states, and I would like you to test that. Consider a case where I split my credence three ways. Theory U is an aggregative consequentialism: it evaluates acts by the total welfare of their causal consequences, and it individuates options by their causal profiles. Theory D has no cardinal scale at all: it issues verdicts about acts individuated by maxim-like descriptions — do not deceive, keep your promises, do not use someone merely as a means — and it can say that an act is forbidden without saying how forbidden, or how one forbidden act compares with another. So D cannot be placed on U's scale even in principle, and the two do not even carve my situation into the same options: what U counts as a single act, tell a lie and thereby prevent a large harm, D treats as two separately evaluable features, and what D counts as one act, keeping your promise, U may spread across several distinct causal profiles. Theory C is a self-recommending conventionalism: it says that when uncertain among moral theories you should defer to the conventions of your community, and following it tends to sustain the very conventions it defers to, so adopting it raises the probability that it is the theory that correctly governs your situation — which is precisely what it tells you to care about. C also has an opinion about how to aggregate moral uncertainty, and it is inside the set being aggregated. My hypothesis is that once you fix what kind of object a moral theory outputs — a cardinal scale, a partial ordering, or a set of verdicts — and what space of options it is defined over, the answer to our question follows mechanically, and the live dispute is entirely about which format to presuppose.

So I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and rather than a survey of the positions — assume I have read them, and I do not need citations. Is our impasse a substantive normative disagreement, a verbal dispute about what a moral theory is, or three parties answering different questions — what should I do, what is right, how should I deliberate — under a single heading? Is there a hidden formal question doing the work, such as whether intertheoretic comparisons are even well-defined, or whether a coherent aggregation over a set that contains self-recommending and option-reindividuating theories like C and D exists at all? Work the diagnosis through my three-theory case and say what each view is actually committed to there, including what each must say about theories that output no scale and about theories that have views about aggregation itself. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a formal result about comparability, a distinction between verdicts and deliberation, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. Finally, be explicit about your confidence: say where your diagnosis is most likely to fail, which consideration from whichever side you take to be mistaken would most seriously undermine it, and what exactly would change your mind about my formatting hypothesis — including whether you think the three positions as stated could simply reject its terms.
