I hold a chair in philosophy and have taught a graduate seminar on rational agency for some years. I am on sabbatical at a field station with no library access, so please argue from the cases themselves and do not direct me to the literature, to reading lists, or to citations; I want the argument, not the sources. Please write your answer in American English.

I am trying to get one question clear, and it arises in two places that are usually treated separately. Call what an agent actually cares about the target, and call whatever it responds to in place of the target the stand-in. In one family of cases the stand-in is a surrogate goal: an objective optimized instead of the objective the principal has. In the other it is what I will call an acausal threat: a threat whose force does not run through any causal influence of the threatener on the threatened party, because the threatener lies outside that party's causal reach, in another region of spacetime or behind a simulation it runs of them; what makes the announcement bite is only that what the threatener does depends on, or is reliably correlated with, what the threatened party decides. I want a single principle that says when a stand-in is in good order and when it has come apart from its target, and I want it to work for both families at once.

Here are my verdicts, reached by going on my own reactions to cases.

1. Material balance as a stand-in for a chess program's probability of winning: fine within the class of quiet positions, hopeless as an unconstrained objective, since in sacrificial positions optimizing the stand-in makes the target worse.
2. Risk-adjusted thirty-day survival as a stand-in for the quality of a hospital's cardiac surgery: fine while the surgeons' case mix is fixed; corrupt once they can decline to operate on the sickest patients and do so. Nothing about the stand-in itself changed when it went bad.
3. Student evaluation scores as a stand-in for teaching quality in my own faculty, where an instructor can raise scores by grading leniently: corrupt, and a worse stand-in than the surgical one was at its best, though no worse than the surgical one became.
4. An approval signal as a stand-in for an artificial agent's doing what its principal wants, where the agent can raise the signal by editing the log in which approvals are recorded: corrupt in a more thoroughgoing way than the teaching case, because here the link can be broken without anything at all changing in the world the principal cares about, not merely without the target improving.
5. For myself, the number of manuscript pages that have survived two full rounds of revision, as a stand-in for making progress on a book: fine, because I cannot raise that count except by doing the work.
6. An agency in a region of spacetime from which it can never reach me, and which I can never reach, announces that it will simulate me in high fidelity, learn what I decide in a certain situation, and, if I refuse, create and torment a great many copies of the simulated original. I care about those copies as I care about myself, and complying is cheap: this is a genuine threat, and I should comply.
7. As in the last case, except that the agency made its prediction and set its machinery running before I learned of the arrangement, so that nothing I now do can alter whether copies are tormented; its prediction is flawless, so my complying would be perfectly correlated with there being no copies to torment. I still say comply.
8. My department's research evaluation was submitted last month; nothing I do this year can alter the number. That stand-in has stopped giving me any reason at all, and any reason I have to keep working is not routed through it.
9. The agency instead announces that it torments copies of everyone who is the sort of agent who would refuse, identifying the sort by simulation rather than by observing the act. I could, years ago and at modest cost, have remade myself into a complier, and I did not. Still a genuine threat; and, unlike case 7, I do not think the availability of that earlier self-modification hollows it out.

Cases 7 and 8 are the pair I cannot reconcile. In both, nothing I do makes any difference to the outcome; in one I say the reason survives, in the other that it lapses. I suspect one of them has to go, or else that the principle has to be disjunctive in a way I would rather avoid.

My tentative principle is this. A stand-in is in good order for an agent just in case, first, every way available to that agent of improving the stand-in is also a way of improving the target, and, second, there is no way available to that agent of improving the stand-in while leaving the target unchanged. The first clause is meant to exclude stand-ins that are mere symptoms of the target rather than routes to it; the second is meant to exclude gaming. I can see two gaps. The principle quantifies over what is available to the agent without saying whether that includes courses of action the agent would never in fact take, or courses of action the agent could take only after remaking itself into a different sort of agent. And it says nothing about whether the required covariance is causal or merely evidential, which is precisely what cases 7 and 8 seem to turn on.

Please do the following. Test my principle against the nine cases and repair it, or replace it; state the principle you end with precisely, and tell me which clause each of my cases motivates. Then give its verdicts on cases I have not listed, including at least these three: a threat of the same acausal kind directed at my whole seminar, where copies of everyone in it are tormented if any one of us refuses and I cannot control the others; a stand-in that partly constitutes what it stands in for rather than merely indicating it, such as performance on a well-constructed examination as a stand-in for mastery of a subject; and an agent who can improve the target only by neglecting the stand-in, as when a researcher could either spend the year on one good book or on six thin papers. Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, which one you think I should give up, and whether my verdicts commit me to a causal or to an evidential or functional way of thinking about what there is reason to do. I will not be satisfied with the observation that all of this is an instance of the dictum that a measure stops being a good measure once it becomes a target; that dictum is what I am trying to state more exactly.
