Two friends of mine came over for dinner on Sunday and spent most of the evening arguing about a made-up case, and I've been turning it over since. Both did law, both now work for different NGOs, neither will concede anything. I kept their glasses full and honestly can't tell who won.

The case is invented but it's the kind of thing that's now possible. Say a small republic, Veredia, replaces its drafting office with a statutory system everyone calls the Codifier. The assembly votes on one-line policy motions — "tighten penalties for harbouring prohibited persons" — and the Codifier emits the operative statutory text: definitions, cross-references, commencement, penalties. Only the emitted text is promulgated. It regenerates every night, so there's a new authoritative version each morning, and the whole thing runs to a few million lines, so no human being reads more than a slice of it. Enforcement is largely automated. When courts hit internal contradictions they resolve them by picking a reading, the way courts do.

Last year of the old regime a woman named Elin reports her lodger for sheltering a cousin who's on a prohibited-persons list — the sort of thing the regime actively encouraged. The lodger gets eight years. Two wrinkles: the version of the output in force on the Sunday Elin made her report didn't contain any retroactivity clause, but the version applied at her trial, a later regeneration, made the offence reach back two years; and no member of the assembly ever voted for that clause, it came out of the Codifier. Then the regime falls. The transitional parliament wants to convict people like Elin, and it can either declare the old output was never law and proceed under ordinary criminal law, or convict under the old text and openly admit it's punishing conduct that was permitted at the time.

Here's roughly how it went, more or less verbatim.

1. Rob: By any test Hart would have accepted, that output was law. Police, prosecutors and judges all treated the current Codifier text as the thing they had to apply, and that convergent official practice just *is* the rule of recognition. So the parliament has two honest options — convict under the old text, or let people like Elin walk. What it shouldn't do is tell itself the comforting story that monstrous rules were never law. The whole point of Hart's side of that argument with Fuller is that a candid retrospective statute is morally better than a pious fiction.

2. Nadia: Hart's candour argument assumes there was a rule there to be candid about. This thing changed nightly, contradicted itself, and no one could find out what it said. The version Elin was convicted under didn't even exist when she acted. That isn't a rule-governed order with too little morality in it — it's the gunman situation Hart starts from, with a legislature bolted on the front. Fuller's eight desiderata aren't a wishlist of nice-to-haves; they're what has to be present for the enterprise of guiding conduct by rules to exist at all. Strip out promulgation, constancy and non-contradiction all at once and you don't have a defective law, you have a defective something else.

3. Rob: Two replies. First, "nobody could read it" is a complaint about knowledge, not about validity — real tax codes and financial services regimes are unreadable in full and nobody thinks VAT isn't law. Contradictions get resolved by interpretive practice every day, and that practice is part of the machinery of a rule of recognition, not evidence against one. Second, and bigger: Fuller's list is a list of efficiency conditions, not moral ones. A norm can be general, clear, prospective, stable, possible to comply with and perfectly matched by official action, and still require you to hand over your neighbour. So the "inner morality" can't be doing the work in the cases that actually matter — it's plain old substantive morality smuggled in, which is exactly what Hart wanted to keep separate.

4. Nadia: That's the lazy reading. Fuller never said the internal morality guarantees justice; he said it's a morality of a different kind — what you owe someone you're governing by rules, the reciprocity of "here's a standard you can actually follow and I'll hold myself to it". Calling it efficiency mistakes the floor for the whole building. And Elin's case is exactly a floor case: she was punished under a version that wasn't in force when she acted, for a window no legislator asked for. If that's law, the word has stopped doing any work.

5. Rob: Maybe, but what does "not law" buy you that "law we refuse to apply" doesn't? You still have to punish her, and your route just relabels the retroactivity instead of removing it — you're convicting her under an ordinary offence whose elements she satisfied inside a legal order that told her to do it. Hart's version at least lets a judge say out loud: this was law, it was hideous, and we are paying the moral price of punishing anyway. Also your test has no stopping point. How much failure is enough? One contradiction? Nightly updates? A single retroactive clause? Positivism gives you a yes or a no at the moment of decision; you've given us a dial with no markings on it.

6. Nadia: The dial complaint cuts both ways. A rule of recognition is a fact about official practice, and at a moment of transition there just isn't a settled practice yet — which is why Hart's own framework has to treat a revolution as a jump to a new system rather than something the old system's rules can settle. Your clean yes-or-no is bought by describing a stable system from the outside; my dial is what you're actually stuck with while the system is coming apart. And it isn't true that we only have retroactivity or impunity. Declaring the old output void is a forward-looking repair of a rule-governed order, like a court refusing to enforce an agreement nobody could possibly have performed — not a criminal statute in disguise.
