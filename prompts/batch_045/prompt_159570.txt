I'm a senior, and our cafeteria runs a pre-order system I need to describe first, because the whole argument turns on it. Students order Friday's lunch by Tuesday midnight; the kitchen cooks the chicken basket only if at least thirty students order it, and otherwise everyone gets the vegetarian plate and no chicken is cooked at all. Individual orders are private, but the cafeteria posts the week's total on Monday, and the food-service company sets next year's standing order from the running annual total, which it rounds to cases of forty-eight servings.

Two members of my debate club, Noor and Wes, argued about whether students should order the vegetarian plate, and asked me to referee. I don't have a view, and I couldn't settle it.

1. Noor: my order changes anything only if exactly twenty-nine other students order without me. Otherwise the chicken is cooked anyway, or not at all. So skipping it is almost always pure self-denial, and in the rare case where it does matter the difference isn't one chicken but thirty.

2. Wes: that cuts against you. A small chance of a thirty-bird difference can still be larger in expectation than the single portion you'd be responsible for on the naive picture — the lumpiness inflates the stakes precisely when the probability is low. And there's a second channel: the posted totals feed next year's standing order, so each unit nudges a much larger purchase.

3. Noor: both channels are nearly inert here. Two years of posted totals cluster around a dozen or around ninety, never near thirty, so the knife-edge case is vanishingly unlikely; and the annual order rounds to cases of forty-eight, so my unit changes it only if the total lands exactly on a multiple of forty-eight. I'm being asked to give up something certain for something that will almost certainly be nothing.

4. Wes: then the question isn't marginal effect at all. Moral reasons attach to taking part in a practice of killing animals for convenience, and each person's job is to do their part of what we together are doing, not to estimate their own causal contribution to it.

5. Noor: but do your part only has force if the parts add up to something. Here they don't, unless the threshold is crossed, and when it isn't crossed my part is inert. You're assuming that because the group has a reason, each member has one, which is exactly the point in dispute. And if integrity is what matters, you owe me a reason why it matters here and not for the dozens of other purchases with equally tiny expected effects that I ignore every day.

6. Wes: that last point is a counsel of despair, not an argument. If it proved anything it would prove that no collective harm can be addressed, and plainly it can, by coordinating: if ten of us pledged together, each person's abstention would cross a threshold even though each one considered alone is inert. So the upshot is that we should organize — and the fact that we haven't yet is not a reason for me to order the chicken.

7. Noor: I can accept that, and it's consistent with what I said. It shows the group should coordinate; it doesn't show that, as things actually stand, I owe it to anyone to skip the chicken basket.

8. Wes: except the pledge only works if people are willing to abstain on the supposition that others will too — and someone who abstains only after everyone else has already signed up is exactly the person whose abstention can never matter.

Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move — say who is right about which specific point, where each of them goes wrong, and what, if anything, both of them are missing? I'd rather have the arguments assessed as they were actually made than have an overall winner declared.
