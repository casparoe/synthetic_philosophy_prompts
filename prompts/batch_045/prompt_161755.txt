I've been teaching myself a bit of Bayesian epistemology from blog posts and podcasts, and one post presented the Dutch book argument as though it settled everything. As best I can reconstruct it, its central passage ran roughly like this:

"A rational person's degree of belief in a claim just is the price at which they would be indifferent between buying and selling a ticket that pays one dollar if the claim turns out true. If your degrees of belief break the probability rules — say you are 70 percent confident it will rain tomorrow and also 70 percent confident it will not — then there is a combination of bets, each of which you would happily accept on its own, that loses you money no matter what happens. And the converse holds too: a bookie can be guaranteed to take money off you if and only if your confidence violates the probability axioms. So rationality just is probabilism. The same trick works over time: plan to update by any rule other than Bayes' rule and a bookie who knows your plan in advance can arrange a set of bets that costs you money whatever you end up observing. Finally, the money is only a stand-in — run the bets in utils instead of dollars and the usual worry about whether you value money linearly disappears, so the argument is really about belief and not about gambling."

The post then drew two further conclusions I care about: that someone who reports a range rather than a number — "somewhere between 40 and 60 percent sure" — has not really stated a belief that can be rationally assessed; and that the non-Bayesian statistical tools most working scientists are trained in are, strictly speaking, irrational.

Could you audit this rather than explain the argument from scratch? I would like to know which sentences a philosopher working on this would accept as written, which are simplifications that conceal an assumption the argument actually needs, and which are simply wrong. I am especially unsure about three things: whether the "if and only if" really holds without extra assumptions about what the bookie may offer and what I am prepared to accept as a package; whether redenominating the bets in utils removes the linearity worry or quietly presupposes part of what the argument was supposed to establish; and whether either of those two final conclusions follows from the parts that are correct, or whether the argument only ever shows something about what I should be willing to bet — which someone who simply declines to bet at all could ignore entirely.
