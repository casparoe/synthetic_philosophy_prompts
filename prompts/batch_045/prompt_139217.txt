You are an epistemologist who also works as a professional mediator of scholarly disputes: you have run many adversarial collaborations between camps that had spent years talking past each other, and you are known for drafting texts that neither side later disowns. Work in stages and think carefully before you write; I want the drafting, not a summary of the literature.

Context for why I am asking. I am a journalist writing a long-form piece on how the public should react when genuine experts disagree, and I want to end the piece with the document the two philosophical camps on peer disagreement should have produced by now rather than with another round of duelling op-eds. My readers are curious non-specialists, so every technical term has to be explained inline; I also have no interest in a reading list, so give me no citations, no author names, no paper titles, no bibliography, and no references to specific published articles or to who said what in some debate. The two positions should be identified only by their labels, Conciliationist and Steadfastness theorist, and should be characterised from scratch: the Conciliationist holds roughly that discovering an acknowledged epistemic peer disagrees with you is itself evidence that you have gone wrong somewhere, and that the reasonable response is to move your confidence substantially toward theirs, in the simplest version to the midpoint between the two. The Steadfastness theorist holds roughly that you may properly give your own reading of the evidence more weight than your peer's dissent, because your own first-order reasoning is not just one more item of evidence to be averaged; you are entitled to take your apparent grasp of the argument at face value when you decide how much to discount it.

Now the stipulation that should keep this from being a rehearsal. Assume, purely as a hypothetical for this exercise, that a large preregistered study tracked four thousand pairs of participants independently certified as genuine epistemic peers in a domain: near-identical track records on comparable questions, equal access to the same public evidence, and no relevant private evidence that either could name at the time. Each pair recorded an initial confidence on the same binary question, was shown the other's confidence, revised, and was scored. Assume the study reported three things. First, moving halfway toward the other's confidence improved the pair's average accuracy in aggregable domains, where errors look like independent noise around a truth: forecasting, quantitative estimation, perceptual judgment. Second, the same policy made no measurable difference in non-aggregable domains: moral judgment, aesthetic judgment, and logical or mathematical judgment. Third, in the subset of cases where one member of the pair was in fact reasoning from a chain of considerations the other could not reconstruct even after an hour of discussion, moving halfway made that member less accurate and left the pair's joint accuracy unchanged. Treat all three findings as real for the purposes of the document, and treat the third finding as the hard case both camps must absorb rather than explain away.

Deliverable. Draft the joint statement these two camps could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, given those findings. Begin with a short private brainstorming pass in which you map the dialectical landscape: candidate agreed claims, candidate disagreements that are merely verbal, and the three sub-questions you take to be most load-bearing, along with any sentence you caught yourself wanting to write that one side would refuse to sign. Then produce the statement itself in four parts. Part one, a numbered section of agreed claims, around eight to twelve of them, each substantive enough that a reasonable member of either camp could dissent from it, and each written so a non-specialist can follow it. Part two, a residual-disagreements table, with one row per disagreement: a neutral statement of the issue, the Conciliationist's own preferred formulation, the Steadfastness theorist's own preferred formulation, and your verdict on whether that row is a genuine disagreement or a dispute about words, with a sentence of justification. Part three, the cruxes, the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side; for each, give the concrete case, hypothetical study, or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it, and then write down in advance what each side predicts that case will show. Part four, a short closing note on what each camp would count as evidence against its own view, and on whether there is anything at all both agree would actually settle the matter. Close with a single paragraph, in plain language, that I could print in the magazine as the takeaway for readers.

Aim for something neither camp could call a caricature of itself. Where you cannot find a formulation both would sign, say so and explain what blocks it, rather than papering over the gap with a vague sentence. About twelve hundred words for the statement itself, not counting the brainstorming.
