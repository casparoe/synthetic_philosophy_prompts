I'm reading a popular book about how historians — and the rest of us — explain the past, and I want to know how much of its central argument to trust. Here is the passage that caught my attention, the book's summary of why narrative history misleads us:

"A great deal of what passes for historical explanation is really a cognitive illusion with two parts. The first is hindsight bias: once we know how something turned out, we can no longer recover our former uncertainty, so we quietly rewrite the past so that it points at the outcome — after the fact, what happened looks like what had to happen. The second is the narrative fallacy: a mind cannot store or think about a bare sequence of events, so it compresses the sequence into a story with a beginning, a middle and an end, and in compressing it invents causal connections that were not really there. Put the two together and you get the ordinary history book: a chain of causes that made the outcome inevitable, reconstructed by someone who knows the ending and cannot unknow it. The moral the authors draw is that narrative history is closer to fiction than to science; that the cure is to replace 'why did this happen?' with 'how often does this sort of thing happen?' — base rates, counterfactuals, comparisons across cases; and that because large historical processes are chaotic in something like the way the weather is, tiny unknowable causes make the outcome unpredictable in principle, so any story about why it went one way rather than another is at best a guess."

My instinct is that the first two-thirds of this is roughly right and that the move to the conclusion is not, but I can't say where it breaks.
