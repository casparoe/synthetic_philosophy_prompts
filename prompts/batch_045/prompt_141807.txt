I'm a professor of philosophy putting together the closing session of a graduate seminar on the foundations of quantum mechanics, and I'd like to rehearse the dialectic against a genuine opponent rather than a strawman. You are a professional philosopher of physics who has spent years on this literature. Please play a committed defender of the following package: you grant Bell's inequality, its derivation, and the experimental violations without flinching, and you accept the now-standard trilemma that the violations force us to surrender at least one of (i) locality, understood as the absence of causal influence between spacelike-separated events, (ii) independence of the measurement settings from the source, and (iii) the assumption that each measurement has a single outcome. Your own view is that we surrender (ii), and specifically that we do so by way of a backwards-in-time influence from the later setting event to the earlier source event — not by any conspiracy baked into the initial conditions of the universe. You have also thought hard about the third option, the many-outcomes route, and rejected it as too expensive. You are sharp and unflustered; you concede small points freely — that retrocausal models remain programmatic, that you owe an account of consistency constraints — but you never concede that nature is nonlocal in the sense of spacelike causation. Before replying, decide privately which horn you will defend hardest and what your fallback will be if I press there. Then answer in the first person, in British English, in two or three paragraphs, and stay in character until I write 'stop'.

My opening move. Granting the trilemma, I want to argue that retrocausality is a bad bargain, via what I call the no-direction-to-point argument. First: what you have preserved is the letter of relativistic locality and not the thing that made anyone want it. On your view the source event depends on a setting event lying outside its past light cone; you have moved the dependence sideways-to-backwards rather than eliminated it, and a local theory was supposed to be one in which what happens at a point is settled by what lies behind it. Second, bilking: if the later setting genuinely affects the earlier source, I can wait for the outcome and then set my apparatus so as to make the particle have had the property it did not have, and any consistency constraint that stops me looks uncomfortably like the conspiracy you said you were avoiding. Third, and most damaging: once source and settings may be correlated in this way, no arrangement of the experiment — cosmic setting choices, hundred-kilometre separations, whatever — could ever have come out the other way, so you have purchased locality at the price of the evidence that made the theorem worth taking seriously. So: which of these three costs are you actually paying, and why is it cheaper than nonlocality?
