For the past two years I have convened a small evening reading group under the auspices of our local adult-education institute — fifteen or sixteen people, most of them retired, none of them philosophers, meeting for ninety minutes in a room with a projector and tables seating four. I come to philosophy late myself, after a career in experimental physical chemistry, and I have been asked to lead one session on the St. Petersburg game. I would like it to be a session in which people do something rather than one in which I explain something to them.

The arrangement I have in mind is the familiar one: a fair coin is tossed until it first comes up heads, and the bank pays two units if that happens on the first toss, four if on the second, eight if on the third, and so on, doubling each time. The expected value of this arrangement diverges. Everyone present can follow that computation once it is on the board, and several of them will have met it before. What I want is for them to run into the difficulty themselves, in the form of a decision they actually have to make, before anyone names it.

Please design such a session for me. The constraints are these. No participant may risk their own money; the only stakes are a small budget of book vouchers I can draw on, and I would rather the design assume a hard ceiling on what the bank can pay than pretend otherwise. No lecture up front: nothing about expectation, utility, or the history of the problem should be said before the last twenty minutes, and the word paradox should not be used at all until then. The coin itself should not consume the evening — I am happy to prepare sealed envelopes from a pre-generated sequence of tosses beforehand, or to use whatever other device you think is cleaner, provided the randomness is something the group can in principle audit.

The shape I have been toying with, which you should feel free to replace if you have something better, is three rounds. First, each person writes down privately the largest sum they would pay for a single play of the game, under a procedure that makes it in their interest to state the truth — I have in mind the arrangement where their figure is compared against a price drawn at random from a range we all know, so that they buy at the drawn price when it is lower and buy nothing when it is higher, which as far as I can see removes any advantage in shading the number. Second, the same people bid openly, in their tables of four and then as a whole group, for the single play. Third, they price a different object: not one play but the right to play the game ten times over, with the winnings of each play paid separately and added up. My reason for the third round is parochial. In my old field we were drilled in the difference between an average taken across an ensemble of systems at one moment and an average taken along the trajectory of a single system over time, and those two can come apart badly for quantities with heavy tails. I suspect that distinction does real work here, and I would like the session to make it visible rather than to assert it.

Alongside the pricing I would like one coarser question put to the group: how long a run of tails would lead you to start doubting the coin, or the bank, or the whole setup. In my own work a quantity that refused to converge was generally a signal that the model had been pushed outside the range where it meant anything, and I want that diagnosis kept on the table as a serious option rather than dismissed as a refusal to play the game.

What I would like back is a document I could hand to a co-facilitator and run without further instruction: the rules and materials, the exact payoff scheme and what it costs me, a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes including the transition from small groups to the whole room, and the debrief. The debrief should map specific moments in the session onto specific diagnoses of what is going on — that money has a diminishing marginal value to a person, that the bank cannot in fact pay out in the far tail, that people systematically mishandle very small probabilities, that the mean is the wrong summary statistic for a decision taken once and never repeated, and the model-validity worry above — and it should be explicit about which of these the evening can actually discriminate between and which of them predict exactly the same behaviour in the room and so cannot be told apart by anything we do there.

Please also tell me, honestly, what will predictably go wrong. I can foresee at least three problems: someone will have read about this before and will announce the answer in the first five minutes; someone will treat the whole thing as a joke and bid the entire voucher budget; and the open bidding will almost certainly anchor everyone on whoever speaks first. Say how the design should absorb each of these rather than trying to prevent it. And say plainly which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce at all — my guess is the unboundedness itself, which no room and no budget can contain — and what the facilitator therefore has to supply in words at the end.

No references or reading list, please; I am not asking anyone to read anything, and I will not be citing anyone. What I want is an evening that leaves each person able to say, in one sentence of their own, why the expected value of the game diverges, and why that fact by itself does not tell them what they should have bid.
