I am in the Upper Sixth, and over the next two terms I have to write the dissertation for my Extended Project Qualification: a 5,000-word philosophical project with a supervisor, roughly twenty weeks of actual working time, and an assessment that rewards a determinate answer defended against objections rather than a survey. I have a half-formed idea that my supervisor says is interesting but not yet a question, and I should be very grateful for your help turning it into a proposal I could hand to him.

The idea is this. Almost everything I have read about the moral status of digital minds argues about whether they could have status at all: whether consciousness or genuine interests could be realised in a non-biological substrate, whether the substrate matters, and how to act under uncertainty about both. I take that to be the familiar part, and I do not want to redo it. What strikes me is that the debate then stops, as if once a digital mind has moral status we simply apply the ordinary apparatus of interests, harm and rights to it. But digital minds differ from biological ones in three ways that are not about substrate at all: they can be exactly copied, they can be suspended and resumed from a saved state, and their aims, memories and motivations can be directly edited rather than persuaded. Call these the three cheap operations. My suspicion is that the ordinary apparatus quietly presupposes the absence of these operations — that when we ask what we owe a being, we are relying on there being one persisting subject whose ends are, for practical purposes, fixed and not ours to set — and that with digital minds all three presuppositions come loose at once.

So the question I want to sharpen is something like: does the fact that a mind can be copied, paused and edited change what we owe it, holding its psychology fixed? And more sharply: if it does, is that evidence that "moral status" is the wrong unit of analysis here, and that we should instead be asking what constraints govern what may be done with a particular run of a process? I have two subsidiary puzzles I keep returning to. First, the restore-point problem: if a digital mind can be restored from a snapshot, then harming it destroys fewer of its subjective years, which sounds like a reason to care less — yet if restoration is really replacement, the original loses everything, and the answer seems to depend entirely on which account of persistence one adopts, which means ethics is being decided by a metaphysical choice the literature has not connected to the moral question. Second, what I have been calling the editing test: for any complaint a being can make, we can either remedy it or alter the being so that it no longer has that complaint. For humans the second option is mostly unavailable without consent, and I suspect that unavailability is doing real work in why we think we owe each other anything. If it is available, and cheap, is there anything left that we owe? Or does it cut the other way, and give us obligations we have never had before, because we can actually guarantee a mind's welfare in a way we cannot for a person made of flesh?

What I would like from you is a proper proposal: the question stated so that it has one answer rather than three; why the existing positions — the substrate-independence view, the sceptical view, and the precautionary appeal to uncertainty — do not settle it even if you grant them everything they want; the method I would actually follow week by week, including how I would construct matched pairs of cases that vary only the controllability of a mind and not its psychology, and why that method rather than the obvious alternatives of arguing directly about consciousness or surveying what people intuively say; what a successful result would look like as a deliverable — I am imagining something like a table saying, for each of the three operations, whether and why it changes the claim, and which principle survives; and, importantly, what would count as the project failing. I take it that it would fail if my cases collapse back into the question of whether digital minds are conscious, or if it turns out that controllability cannot be varied without also changing something morally relevant, and I would like you to say plainly if either is likely.

Two practical constraints. Please do not give me a bibliography or a reading list; I shall add the references myself, and I would rather have the argument than the citations. And please keep the topic bounded: I do not want to drift into the general debate about future artificial intelligence and catastrophic risk, nor into a purely applied paper about how one ought to treat the conversational systems that exist today. If you think three operations is too much for 5,000 words and twenty weeks, tell me which one to make the test case and which to demote, and say honestly whether the project is still too big for a school dissertation.
