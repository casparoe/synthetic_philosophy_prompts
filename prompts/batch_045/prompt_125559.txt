I lead the risk-quantification group at a company operating a fleet of several hundred autonomous vehicles in mixed traffic across six metro areas, and I need something my analysts can actually run rather than something that sounds wise.

The job, in outline. Every two weeks our scenario-mining pipeline flags forty to sixty situation types from fleet telemetry and simulation, and for each one an analyst has to attach a number to a sentence of roughly this form: situations of this kind lead to an event with injury potential at a rate below X per million vehicle-miles. Those numbers are aggregated into a safety case that our internal review board signs and that a regulator reads, and the regulator is not friendly. Turnaround is four working days per batch, the team is three analysts with strong statistics training and no philosophy, every class definition has to be frozen under a version number and reproducible from our logs a year later, and the whole thing has to survive being read by someone whose job is to find the move we made to get the number we wanted.

The obstacle is the reference class problem. I have read the usual material — base rates, the outside view, the advice to use the narrowest class for which you have adequate data, pre-register the class before looking at the outcome — and none of it survives contact with our data, for reasons I want the procedure to confront rather than gesture at:

First, our candidate classes are almost never nested. A class can be narrow along one axis and broad along another, so the instruction to take the narrowest class with enough data has no determinate answer, and my analysts resolve this differently from each other and from week to week.

Second, many of our candidate classes exist only inside a simulator. Choosing a reference class and choosing a generative model are then the same act, and the base rate inherits whatever the generator assumes. The procedure needs a step for auditing that entanglement and for deciding when simulation-derived rates may and may not be pooled with fleet-derived ones.

Third, our historical data are endogenous. They come from the system we already chose to deploy, under the conditions we chose to permit, with the mitigations we already fielded and the remote operators we already staff. The historical rate is therefore conditional on our own past screening, and it will not stay fixed if we act on it — by expanding the operating domain, or by scaling remote assistance down.

Fourth, and worst: with enough axes to choose from, an analyst can nearly always find a class that supports the number she hopes for, without ever being dishonest. I want this failure to surface somewhere other than in argument. My current idea is a standing ledger of every class-definition decision, scored after the fact, so that we audit the class-picker rather than the individual estimate — but I would rather have your version of that mechanism than my sketch, and I want it specified tightly enough that it can be gamed only at visible cost.

What I am asking for, concretely. A written decision procedure an analyst can run on a flagged situation type: the questions in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the tests a proposed class must pass before its rate may be used (at minimum I want an axis specification, a pre-outcome feature test, an adversarial re-derivation test in which a second analyst sees only the written definition and must reproduce the same case set, and a stability test under the policy changes we are contemplating); the points at which the procedure returns no number and escalates; and the points at which it stops and hands the decision to a person because the open question is one of values rather than fact. Be explicit about which steps are real procedure and which are judgment calls wearing a procedural costume. I do not need citations or the history of the idea; I need something I can hand to an analyst on Monday.

Then run it on three cases. (1) A left turn across oncoming traffic at a signalized intersection with no protected phase: about forty fleet instances and zero events, against a broad class of roughly twenty-two hundred intersection interactions containing three events. (2) Expansion into a metro area where the emergency-vehicle siren pattern, the pedestrian-signal conventions, and the customary turn etiquette all differ from anywhere we operate: no fleet data at all, and the only candidates are a simulator class from a generator validated in our existing cities, human crash statistics for that city, and our own data from cities with different conventions. (3) The case I expect to dislike: a class the team has quietly used for two years, defined partly by a feature an analyst codes after watching the clip rather than by anything in the logged schema, and which silently excludes the situations in which a remote operator took over before the maneuver began — precisely the population we would create by reducing remote assistance. Show me how the procedure catches it, what corrected number it forces, and what it says we must do about a safety case we have already filed.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which parts of the problem it leaves exactly where it found them, and where it will misfire in ways my analysts will not notice until a regulator points at them.
