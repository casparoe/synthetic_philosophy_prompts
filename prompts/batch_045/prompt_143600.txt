Hey — I'm a journalist, I write about science and ideas, and I'm working on a feature about whether death is bad for the person who dies. I've got two philosophers who've been sniping at each other about this in print for years and I want to put them in one (virtual) room as an adversarial collaboration, then run the document they produce as a sidebar. One of them holds the Epicurean symmetry line: no subject of experience, no harm; death is annihilation; the time after you're dead belongs to you exactly as much as the time before you were born did, and looking back at that prenatal stretch is supposed to take the sting out of the other one. The other is a deprivationist: death can be genuinely bad for you because it takes away the good life you'd otherwise have had, and a harm doesn't need a subject sitting around at the moment the harm occurs — it's enough that it makes your life, considered as a whole, go worse.

Could you draft the joint statement both of them could sign? What I need:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — the stuff neither of them denies. They both accept that plenty of people are terrified of dying, that a lot of what makes death awful is the dying rather than the being dead, that a death can be catastrophic for the people left behind, and that each of them owes some account of what makes a life go well. You find the rest. Keep them non-vacuous: nothing like "both agree that time is puzzling."

2. A table of the disagreements, one per row, where each disagreement is stated twice — once in wording the Epicurean would accept for their own view, once in wording the deprivationist would accept for theirs. Precise enough that a reader can point at the exact joint where they come apart.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of them. And where a crux is really a fight about words rather than about the world, say so, and say which part is verbal and why.

4. For each genuine crux, one case, thought experiment, or argument that both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Predictions about what ordinary people say when you put the case to them count as long as it's a case you could really put to somebody.

Cruxes I suspect matter — use them or replace them with better ones: the timing problem, i.e. if death is a harm, when does it happen, in the last minutes of life, at the instant of death, or never-at-a-time; why the stretch after your death seems worth dreading while the stretch before your birth doesn't, given that you're absent from both; whether a life can be bad to lose but not bad to start, the worth-continuing versus worth-starting question; whether a harm to you has to be something you could in principle have noticed; and how the badness adds up across a whole life rather than a stretch of one.

Two cases I want in the document, because I've been turning them over for abut a year and I havent seen either in the literature:

- The Moratorium. A safe procedure puts you into dreamless suspended animation for forty years; you wake with no biological aging, no gap in your memory, and you go back to the life you left. Intuitively a forty-year nap is a big deal. Does the Epicurean have to say it isn't bad for you at all, since there's nobody having the experience of being suspended? Does the deprivationist have to say it's roughly as bad as dying forty years early? Can either of them wriggle out, and if so on what grounds? Both sides should record what they predict people will say about it and what they think that intuitive verdict is actually worth.

- The Interrupted Project. Two people lose what the deprivationist herself counts as the same twenty years of future goods — same health, same pleasures, same achievements, same relationships — but one dies with a novel half-written and a child halfway raised, and the other dies having just finished both. Can the badness of a death depend on how complete the life was, and not only on how much good it lost? I want each side to say whether their account has room for that, and what they'd predict a reader's reaction to the pair tells us.

Somewhere in there, tell me what each view would actually say to somebody who was handed a terminal diagnosis this morning. That's the paragraph my editor will read first.

Housekeeping: no citations, footnotes, or see-so-and-so in the document — the piece runs clean, and it should be written so that someone reading it with no library and no wifi doesn't feel cheated. Where a line in the statement is genuinely contestable, tag it inline with how confident you are — high, medium, low, whatever scale you like — and at the end single out the one line you'd least want to have to defend if a hostile philosopher rang you up about it.

Thanks!
