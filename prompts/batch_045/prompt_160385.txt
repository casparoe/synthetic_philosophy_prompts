I work on cost-benefit analysis for a government agency (econ background, basically no philosophy) and I keep running into something that feels like a contradiction in what we actually do. I'd like to know what we're committed to by doing it.

Here's the practice. When we appraise a project we monetise everything. For stuff with markets we back the numbers out of behaviour: the value of a statistical life comes from the extra pay workers demand to take risky jobs, the value of clean air from what people pay for houses in less polluted neighbourhoods. For stuff with no market — a wetland, a species, a view — we can't do that, so we run surveys: describe the good carefully, remind people of their budget, then ask how much they'd pay in extra taxes to protect it, yes/no referendum style. Two more bits. First, we throw some answers out: people who are obviously answering strategically, people who are expressing a general warm feeling about the environment rather than about the specific good, people who name a number they plainly couldn't afford. Second, we use willingness to pay rather than willingness to accept compensation for the loss, even though for small changes those are supposed to come to roughly the same thing — and we prefer the one that gives the smaller number.

So here's what puzzles me. The line I was taught is that utility isn't a thing inside your head, it's just a summary of your choices: nothing over and above what behaviour reveals. But the survey half of what we do behaves as if utility were a hidden quantity that behaviour and survey answers are both noisy evidence for, which is why we can clean the noise out, discard the answers that dont track it, and calibrate surveys against market behaviour. And then we discard some behaviour too: we dont count choices made in ignorance or under panic, and we'd never build an appraisal out of lab results showing people choose differently between two equivalent framings of the same gamble.

So: what does the practice, taken as a whole, actually presuppose utility to be? Is it strictly committed to one answer, or could several different theories of what utility means license exactly the same thing we do? I'd like you to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and to say where it's genuinely underdetermined.

Two follow-ups if you have room.

A colleague has proposed fixing the mess by routing every non-market valuation through a citizens' assembly: small group, gets briefing materials, hears both sides, deliberates for a weekend, and only its post-deliberation vote counts as the preference. Call it laundering. What does that proposal presuppose about preferences and about utility that the current method doesn't, and would it make the package more coherent or just add a second inconsistent layer on top?

And the bit that bugs me most: willingness to pay scales with ability to pay, so benefits to poor people come out smaller and benefits to rich people come out larger. We do nothing about that in environmental appraisal, but in the health side of the same government the equivalent calculation counts a health gain the same whoever gets it. Is that an inconsistency the practice cant survive, or does it just show that willingness to pay is doing something other than measuring utility — standing in for opportunity cost in money terms, say? And what would have to change in the appraisal if we took the rival reading seriously?
