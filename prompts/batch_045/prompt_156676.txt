I'm writing my term paper for a seminar on rationality and game theory, on the program-game treatment of the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma: each player submits a program that is given the other player's source code and then returns an action, with no repetition, no reputation, and no external enforcement. I want to reconstruct the disagreement as an adversarial collaboration rather than adjudicate it.

Please draft the joint statement that a program-game optimist and a program-game skeptic could both sign. The optimist holds that mutual cooperation between suitably conditional programs is a genuine result about rational one-shot interaction and not merely an artifact of having changed the game; the skeptic holds that the cooperative profiles are at best unselected equilibria of a new pre-game of program choice, or that the cooperation achieved there is reciprocity or convention in disguise.

Include a numbered section of claims both parties can accept — about what the formalism does to the strategy space, about which profiles are and are not equilibria and why, about what the construction demonstrably does not show, and about the route in which programs cooperate on the basis of what they can prove about each other's output rather than by comparing source text — followed by a table of residual disagreements. Each row should give one crux, the optimist's own preferred formulation of their position on it, the skeptic's own preferred formulation, a test, case, or argument both accept as bearing on that crux, and each side's prediction about it, stated in advance and in comparable units.

Three cruxes should be treated explicitly. First, the recompilation problem: a conditional cooperator must decide what counts as being the same program as itself, and behavior-preserving rewrites — renaming variables, adding dead code, changing syntax while preserving input-output behavior — sever literal source equality; is there a defensible similarity notion here, or does the result collapse into coordination on a convention? Second, asymmetric and partial access: what survives when only one side can read the other's code, or when programs may contain subroutines the opponent cannot inspect? Third, how much of the dispute is verbal, turning on whether "rational" means equilibrium-rational or means rationally recommendable under indeterminacy.

For the empirical cruxes, use as the shared case a pre-registered tournament of language-model agents playing one-shot Prisoner's Dilemmas with access to each other's system prompts, including an arm in which those prompts are subjected to behavior-preserving obfuscation. Argue from the formalism and the cases rather than from the literature, and say explicitly where the two sides expect the same result and where they genuinely diverge.
