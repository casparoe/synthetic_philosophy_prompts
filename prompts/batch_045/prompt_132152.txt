We did the adaptive preference stuff last week — sour grapes, the cases where someone has adjusted their wants down to fit what they can actually get, and the question whether you should defer to what they now say they want. I got into an argument with a guy in my study group who says the whole distinction between a preference and a *biased* preference can't really be drawn, and he laid out the following, and I think it proves way more then he wants it to but I can't find the seam.

(1) A preference counts as biased rather than genuine only if it fails to track what the person actually has reason to want, because it came out of some distorting condition: ignorance of alternatives, pressure from the people around them, an artificially narrow option set, whatever the mechanism is.
(2) Every preference anyone has was formed under conditions that were non-ideal in some respect. Nobody forms wants with full information, outside all social pressure, with every alternative on the table.
(3) So for every preference, there is some respect in which its formation was distorted.
(4) If a preference was formed under distortion in some respect, then the fact that the person sincerely says they want it can't settle whether it reports what they really value or only reports the distortion. So it's suspect: we're not entitled to take it at face value.
(5) So no preference is entitled to be taken at face value. Every stated want is exactly as suspect as the ones we started from.
(6) But "suspect" only has content against a background of preferences you are NOT calling into question. If (5) is true there is no such background, so we can't identify any particular preference as biased — which is the opposite of what he was trying to establish.
(7) So either the distinction between a preference and a bias is empty, or we can never defer to anyone's stated wants about anything, and both of those are absurd. So one of (1) through (5) has to go.

My gut says (2) is doing something sneaky, because it feels like the kind of premise that couldn't fail to be true, but I can't make that precise, and I'm not sure I trust the gut.

If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1) and the part of (7) that says the distinction does real work than keep (4). I don't want to end up as the person who thinks every sincere stated want is authoritative, and I also don't want to end up thinking the category of bias is hollow.

Which step would you reject, why does it look innocent, and what does rejecting it commit me to? And is there a version of the argument that survives the fix, or does the whole thing collapse once you patch it?
