I run a second-year undergraduate seminar module on whether virtual things are real, and I am also, unofficially, the person a friend's MMO studio calls when its player-tribunal needs a principled line on griefing, item loss, and compensation. I want one artifact that serves both: a decision tree — a taxonomy whose branches are questions, not labels — that sorts a person into a position on whether virtual objects exist at all and, downstream of that, on whether virtual harms are harms. I have read one recent book-length treatment that lays the field out as a tidy four-way taxonomy, and I do not want its labels handed back to me: build the space from the cases first and attach conventional names only at the leaves. Every branch node should be a question a student with no philosophy background can answer without knowing where it leads, phrased in terms of a case rather than a thesis, so the instrument sorts people by what they say about the cases and not by which name they find attractive.

The cases I want doing the work are these. A rollback: after a breach, the admins restore a thirty-six-hour-old snapshot, and a unique, non-duplicable item you earned inside that window is simply gone. A duplication exploit that leaves five thousand copies of the one sword. A player-built cathedral that took four thousand hours and is deleted by an admin for a rule violation with no backup. A permanent server shutdown, in two variants — the data sitting unread on a drive in a drawer, and fans re-hosting the same world years later with different players. A house on a shard that nobody visits or renders for months, still standing after a reboot. The rollback case again, but with the item bought for two hundred dollars in the cash shop rather than earned. An avatar cornered and subjected to simulated sexual violence, where the player reports lasting distress and stops playing. A companion NPC that flees, cries out, and persistently avoids the player who hurt it, where you should stipulate that its internal dynamics are behaviorally indistinguishable from those of a frightened creature and that nobody knows whether it feels anything. And a scope probe running through all of them: does your answer change if the same sequence of events happens in a text-only MUD, in a tabletop campaign with miniatures and a character sheet, or in a novel read silently? I also want to say where I get stuck, because I suspect it is a node you should name: the cathedral reads to me as a genuine loss and the rollback does not, and I cannot identify the difference between them.

Please keep it to seven or eight branch questions and eight to eleven leaves. Each leaf should carry the conventional name of the position, or unnamed if nobody defends it; one sentence on its most awkward commitment; and — this is what the tribunal actually needs — one sentence on what it implies about whether the cathedral player should be compensated, and in what form. Mark the combinations of answers your questions allow but that nobody occupies, with a one-line diagnosis each: incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate instead of inventing a further question to hide the overlap, and where a pair differs only in something none of my cases can detect, invent one further case of your own that would detect it. Then, as an appendix, a five-question quiz drawn from the tree with a scoring key, so students can locate themselves by their answers. No bibliography, no citations, no suggestions for further reading — I want the instrument itself, not the literature around it. American English, please, and tight enough to paste into a two-page handout: use indentation or numbered node ids rather than box-drawing characters.
