I'm on a small volunteer panel that's supposed to hand our parliament a short report on defence manpower. We're a country of about six million, we share a long border with a much bigger neighbour we don't get along with, we still have peacetime conscription, and we've been officially non-aligned for decades — though two nearby countries recently joined an alliance, so suddenly everyone here is re-litigating all of it. I've got no academic background at all, I've just been reading about this on my own for a few months, and I have no library access, so please don't point me at books or papers I should go read. I want reasoning I can check against public numbers and against arguments I can follow.

We have roughly eighteen months, no research staff, and maybe enough budget for one decent survey. So my question is: out of everything people argue about conscription versus a professional army, and about staying armed-and-neutral, which two or three questions should we actually try to settle in the report — and which should we just declare above our pay grade, or admit don't really move the decision? I care less about the list than about the why: what makes a question load-bearing here, which ones most of the other arguments secretly depend on, which sound deep but are really just confusions, and which are things everyone repeats that probably have nothing behind them. Tell me what would move something up or down the list too.

Here's where I've got to on my own, in case it's useful or in case I'm just wrong. First: it seams to me that what conscription really buys isn't cheap soldiers, it's a random-ish sample of the whole society — sort of like a jury, or sortition — that a government has to get past before it can take the country to war, and whose families are visibly exposed. Is that a mechanism with actual teeth, or just a nice story?

Second: conscription looks to me like mostly an option on mobilisation depth, and how much that option is worth depends on how much warning we'd get — months of tension you can train through, versus a war that begins with whatever you already have on day one.

Third, on neutrality: whether our posture reads to outsiders as a porcupine (costly to invade, no offensive reach, so nothing to preempt) or as a freeloader hoping someone else bleeds for it.

And I suspect the whole "is conscription cheaper" fight is a bit fake — one is a tax paid in time by young people, the other a tax paid in money by everyone, and the answer mostly falls out of how you price a conscript's year.

Give me your ranked two or three, defend the ordering, and flag anything above that you think is just mistaken.
