I'm working on a paper about what conservation law quietly assumes about the ontology of species, and I want the framing pressure-tested before I start writing. Here is the practice. Endangered-species legislation protects taxa as countable, bounded units: as I understand it, typically species, and in some jurisdictions subspecies and distinct population segments too. Whether a particular population gets protection, and how much, can turn on whether specialists currently classify it as a full species, a subspecies of a wider one, or nothing worth naming; and taxonomic committees periodically split and lump taxa, so the protected set can change without any organism changing. The classifiers themselves, meanwhile, tend to be pluralists — several competing species concepts, different counts of the same organisms, and the standard line that disagreement between concepts is at least partly a matter of which concept suits the question being asked.

So: what does this practice presuppose? Does it strictly require that a question like how many species are in this river have a determinate answer independent of our classificatory purposes, or is it fully compatible with a deflationary or quantifier-variance view of species talk? Does the law's habit of following taxonomic revision show that it treats species as discovered rather than stipulated, or does it show the opposite — that it treats them as whatever the relevant authorities currently say?

I want to try out a distinction I haven't seen put this way: between a dispute that is merely verbal (fix the terms and the disagreement disappears) and one that is purpose-relative but not verbal (the terms could be fixed either way, but which fixing is correct is constrained by the point of the inquiry, and the point is not itself arbitrary). My hypothesis is that the law's commitment sits in the second box, that this is why it can absorb taxonomic revision while still resisting the thought that species are whatever we stipulate, and that the same distinction predicts where it runs into trouble: hybridization, population segments, and taxa whose conservation value is high while their species status is contested. Use the distinction if it earns its keep; if it collapses into something already in the literature, say so.
