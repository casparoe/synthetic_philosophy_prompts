I am a novelist, and I am asked to supply advance praise — the short encomium that appears on a jacket or in a catalogue — for other people's novels perhaps two dozen times a year. I am usually working from an uncorrected proof, sometimes from a partial manuscript, with about a week to answer and a publicist waiting. What I write goes out under my name, is short enough to be quoted in full, and is read, correctly, as a claim about the book. I would like a standing procedure for these requests, and I would like it built on a distinction I keep circling without being able to state precisely.

The distinction, in my own terms. Some of the things that move me to praise a book bear on whether the book merits the attitude my words report: the sentences do what they set out to do, the structure holds under the weight put on it, the book says something about its subject that could not have been said another way. Other things move me for reasons of a different order: the author is a friend, the season's list will sink without this book, praising it will be taken as a political act, refusing will be taken as one, my praise will be read as generosity toward someone the culture has been cruel to. These are excellent reasons to want the book praised, and perhaps good reasons to open my mouth; but they do not seem to be reasons for the admiration the blurb asserts, and when they are doing the real work, what I have written is a species of untruth even if every clause about the prose is defensible. There is a name in philosophy for this — the problem of the wrong kind of reasons, which arises wherever value or fittingness is analysed in terms of reasons for attitudes, and whose difficulty is that nobody has a test for which kind a given reason belongs to that does not simply presuppose the answer.

My half-formed thought is that the two-way split is too crude, and that a usable procedure needs three categories. First, reasons that bear on the work's properties and on the right property: reasons for admiring this novel as a novel. Second, reasons that bear on my situation rather than on the work: reasons to want it praised, reasons to be seen praising it. Third — the category I think is doing the damage in actual blurbs — reasons that bear on the work but on properties that support a neighbouring attitude rather than this one: the author's courage, the book's urgency, its being the first of its kind, the fact that it needed writing. These are reasons for gratitude, respect, relief, or indignation, and they are reasons to want the book read; they are not, I suspect, reasons to call it excellent, and they leak into praise because a blurb runs to two sentences and has room for only one register.

What I want from you: a decision procedure, in a fixed order, that I can run in twenty minutes and could hand to another writer without explaining anything behind it. For each step, the question to ask; what would count as a sufficient answer; the point at which the procedure stops with a no; and the point at which it stops and hands the decision back to me because what remains is a question of values and not of facts. It should tell me what to do when I have not read enough of the manuscript to have reasons of the first kind at all. It should also tell me whether there is an honest intermediate verdict available — declining to praise the novel while saying something true about the person or the occasion — and, if there is, what forms of words are legitimate and which merely relabel a wrong-kind reason as a right-kind one. Please mark explicitly the places where the procedure is a judgment call dressed as a step, and where the underlying philosophical question is genuinely unsettled, so that I know I am deciding rather than executing.

Then apply it to three cases. One: a competent, unambitious first novel by a close friend, from a small press for whom my name on the jacket would make a real commercial difference. Two: the best novel I have read this year, by a writer I publicly feuded with a decade ago, where my praise would be received as an apology and would also flatter me. Three: a formally clumsy, politically urgent novel by a writer at risk in her own country, where a jacket quotation from me may offer a measure of protection. I expect the procedure to give me an answer in at least one of these that I will not like, and I would rather have that stated plainly than softened.

Close by saying what the procedure cannot do: which requests it will misfire on, which distinctions it has to discard in order to be usable, and what I should do when the collapse of the right-kind/wrong-kind distinction is not a technicality but the whole problem.
