I work as a software engineer on the training side of a robotics perception team, and I have no formal philosophy background. A reading group I sit in on has been discussing the old question that William Molyneux put to Locke: a man blind from birth, who has learned to distinguish a cube from a sphere by touch, is suddenly given sight; shown the two, and before he touches them, can he tell which is which? The group's answer is no — not at first sight, because he has to learn how things look. I tried to turn that answer into an argument a machine-learning person would take seriously, and I ended up somewhere I do not want to be. Here it is.

(1) Suppose the group is right: at the first moment of sight the man cannot tell the cube from the sphere. Then the visual presentation of a shape and the tactile presentation of a shape begin, in him, unlinked.

(2) If they begin unlinked, then something has to do the linking, and the only candidate I can see is experience: repeated episodes in which he sees and touches the same object, from which the correlation between the two streams can be extracted.

(3) But a learner cannot extract a correlation from two streams unless the pairing is already marked for it in the data. It has to be able to tell, of a given episode, that this visual state and this tactile state belong together — that they are two presentations of one and the same thing. If nothing in the data marks which sight goes with which touch, there is no association to make and no error to learn from; the two streams are just noise at each other.

(4) And being able to tell that this sight and this touch are presentations of one and the same shape is already being able to tell, by sight, which shape is in front of you. You cannot recognize the thing you are looking at as the very thing you are touching without recognizing it as a cube or as a sphere.

(5) So if the group's answer is right, the man can never acquire the link either: the capacity needed to learn the mapping is exactly the capacity whose absence the answer asserts. The no answer, pushed one step, says that he will never learn to see shapes at all.

(6) That is false, and not only for him. My team trains models to align a camera stream with a gripper's contact-geometry stream, and the only thing we supply is that the two streams arrive together — no label saying which visual state goes with which tactile state, no prior description of shapes. They learn the alignment anyway, and it is the same trick that lets image-and-caption models pick up what a caption refers to from co-occurrence alone. If (3) were true, none of these systems could work, and I have watched mine converge.

(7) So something in (1) through (5) is false. The cheapest-looking exit is to conclude that the group is wrong and that the man can tell the cube from the sphere at first sight — but that is not a conclusion I reached, it is one the argument backed me into, and I do not trust an argument whose steps would also rule out learning I watch happen every week.

My order of attachment is (6) first, then (1). I would give up (2), (3), or (4) before either of those, and I cannot tell which of the three is doing the damage.

Please tell me which step you reject, why that step looks safe right up until you press on it, and what rejecting it costs. If the fault is spread over more than one step, say which one is load-bearing. And once the step is removed, tell me what is left of the original question: does the no answer survive intact, or does the argument keep some of its pull toward yes even after the diagnosis?
