I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend — a lawyer by training, not a philosopher — about what John Stuart Mill would have made of algorithmically curated social media, and we got far enough into it that I would like a reconstruction from someone who is not invested in either position.

The concrete question we ended up on was a hypothetical policy. Call it the steelman-slot mandate: any platform above a size threshold must display, alongside every item of political content, the strongest available opposing treatment of that same specific matter. The pairing is selected by an independent board chosen by sortition, and the board is explicitly barred from judging whether the opposing item is true; it may rank candidates only by argumentative strength. Platforms may not reorder, annotate, or editorialize either item, and users may not opt out.

Two stipulations made the argument interesting, and I want to be clear that both are stipulations rather than established findings. First, suppose that when something roughly like this was trialed, the measured effect was not depolarization but increased confidence: readers who were shown a strong opposing case came away more certain of their original view, on the theory that the strongest opposing arguments are exactly the ones a motivated reader can rehearse and rebut in advance. Call it the inoculation effect. Second, suppose that this effect was concentrated almost entirely among people already inside what philosophers now call an echo chamber — a community whose members have been taught in advance to distrust every outside source, so that exposure to counterevidence gets converted into further confirmation — and was largely absent among people who were merely in an epistemic bubble, where contrary material simply is never encountered, through nobody's culpable fault.

My friend's side was that Mill would have opposed the mandate outright. On the harm principle, he said, what you read is self-regarding, and Mill's handling of the drunkard shows he would not coerce a person for their own intellectual good; being badly informed is not an assignable obligation to others, and Mill punished failures of assignable obligation, not failures of self-culture. Second, a public board deciding which opposing argument is strongest is precisely the power over the content of opinion that Mill resisted when he argued that the state may compel education but must not itself direct it, on the ground that a state education is a contrivance for making people like one another. Third, whatever the diagnosis, Mill's remedy is always more discussion and never compelled exposure, and a standing power to appoint the selectors is a more durable threat than any ranking algorithm. On the inoculation stipulation he said it simply shows the mandate would fail, which is a reason against it rather than for it.

My reply was that his first point concedes too much. Mill's defense of freedom of thought is not a defense of whatever informational environment happens to emerge; it is an argument that truth gains from collision, and a collision requires that the conflicting view actually be present and actually be pressed by someone who believes it. A ranking system tuned for engagement that quietly screens out the other side is not a free collision that happens to be lopsided; it is closer to the thing Mill says he feared most, which was not legal censorship but the pressure of prevailing opinion and feeling — except now individualized, automated, and sold back to each person as his own preference. I also pointed to his institutional proposals: he describes a representative assembly as, among other things, a venue for the adverse discussion of opinions on public matters, he was willing to weight votes by education in his book on representative government, and he thought elementary education could be compelled while its content could not be dictated. On that precedent, compelling exposure while leaving conviction free looks Millian to me. As for the inoculation effect, I argued that Mill would read it as evidence that the beliefs in question are held as social badges rather than as lived convictions, which is nearer to his category of dead dogma than to genuine opinion, and that he would therefore treat it as a reason to repair the conditions under which belief is formed rather than as a reason to leave the architecture alone.

So: what would Mill actually have said about the mandate, and where does each of us misread him? I would like four things. Does the distinction between compelling exposure and compelling belief hold up inside his framework, or does it collapse on inspection? What does his suspicion of state-appointed examiners do to a mandate whose whole operation is the appointment of selectors? Given the instruments he actually favored, what would he have proposed instead? And where does the analogy between a privately owned ranking system and the forms of opinion pressure he knew about break down in a way that would have mattered to him — including any respect in which our whole dispute depends on a distinction he simply did not have. Please do not side with me by default: I want to know which of my moves he would have rejected, and why.
