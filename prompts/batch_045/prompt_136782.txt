Say Jeremy Bentham got resurrected for a week to advise a small country that's rewriting its sexual-offences law. The wrinkle they can't handle is AI-generated sexual imagery of real, identifiable adults made without their consent — the kind that's basically impossible to tell from a photo. Cabinet is down to two options: (a) an outright ban on generating or possessing sexual imagery of real people, or (b) what the drafters call the Provenance Rule — no ban, but every model has to embed a cryptographic watermark in its output, platforms have to block sexual images without one, and anyone depicted can demand takedown plus statutory damages without proving any actual distress.

For context you already have: Bentham thought consensual sex between adults was nobody else's business and left an unpublished essay arguing same-sex acts shouldn't be punished, since they harmed no assignable person and the only real pain came from other people's irrational antipathy. He also treated security as the main end of the law, cared obsessively about evidence, and designed the Panopticon.

So: which option would he back, and how would he get there using his own machinery — the dimensions of pain and pleasure, primary vs secondary mischief, alarm, his usual move of dismissing punishments that only gratify antipathy? Two bits I especially want: whether the harm here is really just irrational antipathy again (as with the consensual cases) or genuine assignable mischief, and whether the knock-on effect of nobody trusting any image is a big deal for him specifically. Oh, and what would he do with imagery of adults who don't exist at all.
