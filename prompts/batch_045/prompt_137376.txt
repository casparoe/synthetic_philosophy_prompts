Here's where I'm at. I'm a philosophy professor, and for about a decade now I've been defending a position I call the riverbed view, which I arrived at mostly by teaching this stuff and watching where my own arguments kept breaking. The claim isn't that events are inevitable. It's that history has a shape at the level of functional capacities — things like sustained energy surplus per capita, bureaucratic record-keeping, mass literacy, long-distance coercive reach, cheap long-haul transport — and that this shape is locked in the way a riverbed is: the water is totally interchangeable, the bed is not. Scramble the micro-conditions — who wins which war, which firm or city or dynasty happens to carry an innovation, which person is standing where — and you get wildly different carriers delivering roughly the same functional profile. My working rule of thumb, which I'm aware is crude: re-run the last few centuries with a thoroughly shuffled deck and something like 80% of the functional profile comes back within about 250 years. Call that my re-run budget, and yes, I know both numbers are made up; part of what I want from you is what would move them.

Two bits of the view I'm shakiest on, and I'd rather you go at these than at the easy targets. First, the carrier-indifference corollary: if the bed is what's real, then which empire or firm or language wins shouldn't change the downstream functional profile much, and I think I may be smuggling in the very contingency I'm trying to explain away when I say "much." Second, what I think of as the thin-counterfactual problem: whenever I test the view I vary one variable and hold the rest of the world fixed, which more or less guarantees the system flows back into the same bed. I suspect a lot of my confidence is an artifact of that method, and I don't have a thick alternative to offer.

Ok so the actual ask: what would count against the riverbed view? I want defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one I want you to say whether a committed riverbed theorist like me should concede it as counting against the view or correctly dismiss it as missing the point. My history-department colleague says I have, because "functional capacity" is a rubber yardstick I can always redraw after the fact to cover whatever happened, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's a real objection or just the standard complaint you can make about any substantive claim about history. What would move me personally, for whatever it's worth: I'm least attached to the carrier stuff and most attached to the bed stuff, and the thing that would actually make me sweat is an argument that the two can't be pulled apart — that you can't have a robust bed without also saying something falsifiable about who's carrying the water.
