I've retired from a sensory physiology lab — best part of thirty years, mostly nociception, mostly in animals, which I have some complicated feelings about now — and in the last couple of years I've drifted into philosphy of mind, which I dont think I was prepared for. Right now I'm stuck on pain asymbolia and the question of what's actually supposed to be bad about pain, and my book club (six of us, all retired, none of us humanities people) is doing Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go next month, so I want to try the two against each other while I've got a captive audience.

Here's where I've got to, and tell me if I've got the map wrong. As I understand it there are roughly three candidate homes for pain's badness. There's the sensory-discriminative signal: the distinctive sensory quality itself, the thing you can localise, time, and rate on a 0-to-10 scale. There's the affective sting: the unpleasantness, the being-bothered-by-it, which is what seems to drop out in asymbolia when the signal stays put. And then there's one nobody seems to name, which I'd call the summons: the way pain is supposed to make a claim — a licence for you to act, and a reason for other people to stop what they're doing and help. I've not seen it split as signal / sting / summons anywhere, so it may be a daft taxonomy, but it's how I've been thinking about it.

Two things I keep going round on. First: is asymbolia a lesion or an attainment? If a person can have the full sensory signal with the sting switched off, the Stoics and half the mindfulness literature would call that a hard-won achievement, and my old clinical colleagues would call it an insular insult. Same behaviour, opposite verdicts. Second, I'm suspicious of the evidential base anyway — a handful of cases, lesions that are never tidy, patients who are often also aphasic or anosognosic or dementing — so I half suspect the philosophers are leaning harder on this material than the data can carry.

Which is where the novel comes in, because as far as I can see Ishiguro has built a whole population on asymbolia-shaped lines. Kathy narrates the entire thing in this flat, unprotesting voice. The students know from childhood roughly what's coming and don't campaign, don't run, don't riot. Tommy's shouting fits are treated as the aberration rather than the response. And the scene I can't get out of my head is Madame watching Kathy dance on her own to a sad love song and bursting into tears — and then the explanation that's offered for those tears much later, which Kathy herself doesn't seem to believe. That looks to me like a third party attributing suffering to people who aren't registering any distress, and I genuinely can't tell whether the book is saying she's right, that she's wrong, or that it's the scandal that the question is even open.

My hypothesis, which I'd rather you tested than flattered: Hailsham is a piece of engineered asymbolia — the signal is delivered with the sting conditioned out of it — and the novel's real horror is that nobody answers the summons. Take it apart for me:

- Which of signal, sting and summons does Ishiguro actually put the harm in? Does the book let them come apart at all, or does its whole effect depend on their staying stuck together?
- Is the students' flatness a deficit, an adaptation, something they've been robbed of, or something the reader is meant to supply on their behalf — i.e. we do the feeling and they just undergo?
- Is the summons intact without the sting? Put it crudely: if someone isn't bothered, does their pain still give the rest of us a reason to act? That's the Madame question and I want it answered in both directions, for the philosophy and for the scene.
- Where does the asymbolia lens distort the book? I'd rather have that than a tidy reading. If the obvious rejoinder is that every human being in the novel is in the clones' position and the clones are just mortal people with more paperwork, say so, and then tell me what that does to my three-way split.
- And the reverse direction: does the novel show the philosophy anything the case reports can't, given how few and how messy those cases are?

I'm not after references or who-said-what-first — I can't read them all and the group won't — so argue it rather than cite it. No quotations either, I've got the book and half of them have only seen the film, so talk about scenes by what happens in them. British English please (localise, sceptical, that sort of thing); some of the group get properly annoyed otherwise.
