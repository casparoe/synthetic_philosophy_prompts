QUESTION (20 marks)

A game master picks a number S uniformly at random from {1, 2, 4, ..., 2^N} pounds, where N ≥ 2 is fixed and known to you. She puts S in one envelope and 2S in the other, shuffles them, and hands you one at random. You open it and see the amount A. You may then keep it or swap to the other envelope.

(a) For each A you might see, compute E[other envelope | A]. For which A should you switch? [8]

(b) Compute the expected gain (relative to never switching) of the policy you gave in (a), averaging over S and over which envelope you got. Check the result against the plain symmetry argument that no strategy can beat any other before you open the envelope. [7]

(c) Another student claims: "Since your policy does better than staying conditional on every amount you could see except one, the right to open the envelope and then switch must be worth a positive amount before you open it — so you should be willing to pay for that option." Is this right? [5]

STUDENT'S ANSWER

(a) Write the amount as A = 2^j. The two extreme amounts are special: A = 1 can only come from the pair (1, 2), where it is the smaller, and A = 2^{N+1} can only come from the pair (2^N, 2^{N+1}), where it is the larger. Every interior amount 2^j, for 1 ≤ j ≤ N, can arise in exactly two ways — as the larger member of (2^{j−1}, 2^j) and as the smaller member of (2^j, 2^{j+1}) — and since the envelope you are handed is chosen at random, these are equally likely, so P(I hold the larger | A = 2^j) = 1/2. Note that this is not a judgement of indifference: the 1/2 is forced purely by the symmetry between the two envelopes, so nothing in the calculation depends on S being uniform, and any prior over the powers of two would give the same posterior of 1/2 and hence the same 1.25A for the middle amounts. So E[other | A] = (1/2)(2A) + (1/2)(A/2) = 1.25A for interior A, = 2 for A = 1, and = 2^N for A = 2^{N+1}. Optimal policy: switch unless A = 2^{N+1}, i.e. switch iff A ≤ 2^N.

(b) Endpoint amounts have probability 1/(2(N+1)) and interior amounts 1/(N+1). So the expected gain from switching is (1/(N+1))[1/2 − 2^{N−1} + (1/4)(2^{N+1} − 2)] = 0: the 25% gain across all the middle amounts is exactly cancelled by the single loss of 2^N at the top. Sanity check with N = 4: gains of +1, +0.5, +1, +2, +4 at A = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 with weights 1/10, 1/5, 1/5, 1/5, 1/5, against −16 at A = 32 with weight 1/10, giving 0.1 + 1.5 − 1.6 = 0. This is consistent with symmetry: before you open the envelope every strategy has the same expected payoff, which is what we've confirmed.

(c) Yes, the other student is right, and this is the residue of the paradox that survives the symmetry argument. Conditional on every amount you could actually see except the very top one, switching has positive expectation, so the option to look and then switch is worth strictly more than nothing — roughly a quarter of whatever is in your envelope, i.e. about 0.25·E[A] on average — and you should be willing to pay something close to that for it. The symmetry argument only shows that always-switching is worthless, not that conditional switching is.
