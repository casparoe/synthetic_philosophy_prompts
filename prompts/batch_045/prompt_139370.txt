I did my undergrad in physics about fifteen years ago and have been in software ever since, so I read this stuff as a hobby and there are a lot of gaps. I keep coming back to the Everettian probability business — not "does many worlds work in general", just the narrow question of where the Born rule is supposed to come from — and I genuinely can't tell whether it's still stuck or whether I'm missing the bit where it got solved.

The way I'd split it, there are two different complaints floating around. Complaint one is the everything-happens one: if every outcome occurs on some branch, what does it even mean to say one of them was more probable, or that I should have *expected* it? Complaint two is the numbers one: okay, grant that probability talk makes sense here somehow, why should the weight be the squared amplitude rather than, say, equal weight per branch, or the amplitude without the square? Those feel like seperate complaints to me, and my sense is that a lot of the talking-past-each-other comes from scoring a derivation against the complaint it wasn't aimed at. (I'm deliberately not attributing any of this, because I'd get the names and years wrong; feel free to say who argues what if that's useful.)

Then there's my own hobby horse, which is really where I want you to push. Every derivation I've managed to follow — the decision-theoretic ones with betting agents, the symmetry ones, the "you just don't know which branch you're on" ones — seems to assume branching goes one way: once two branches are distinct they stay distinct, so an agent's possible futures form a tree and you can do bookkeeping over it. But the underlying dynamics is unitary and reversible, and decoherence makes recombination absurdly unlikely, not impossible. So take a Wigner's-friend-ish setup: the agent splits on some measurement inside a sealed, extremely well isolated box, and before anyone reads anything out, the branches are brought back together. My question isn't whether that's practical — it's that in that case there is no tree to do bookkeeping on, the mid-experiment "which branch am I on" question has no answer that survives to the end, and yet the amplitude structure is still sitting there and still gets the final probabilities right. Does the Born rule *problem* even have well defined content in a case like that, or does it quietly presuppose that decoherence has already happened?

My hypothesis, which you should feel free to demolish: this isn't one debate, it's at least three — (a) a normative question about what an agent embedded in a branching world should bet on or care about, (b) a metaphysical question about whether anything in the world makes some branches more real, or more numerous, or more weighty than others, and (c) a hidden formal question about what structure decoherence actually delivers and how load-bearing its irreversibility is — and (c) is doing most of the real work while everybody argues about (a) and (b). I've also seen people just say it's a verbal dispute about the word "probability", and I can't tell if that's a diagnosis or a way of giving up.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a survey of the approaches. And please actually commit: tell me which diagnosis you think is right and why, tell me how confident you are, say what would change your mind, and say what would count as real progress given that diagnosis — a theorem, a distinction, a reframing, a new experiment, or nothing at all and here's why we should expect this to keep going nowhere.
