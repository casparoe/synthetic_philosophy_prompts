I have no philosophy background whatsoever — I build mechanical props and effects for an escape-room company, mostly out of junk and power tools — and I got into an argument with a coworker that has now completely spiralled. I've ended up with a chain of reasoning whose conclusion I can't accept, and I can't find which link is bad. Someone told me cases like these have names in philosophy (finks and masks? not sure I've got them the right way round), but I don't care about the labels, I care about my argument.

The cases from work:

1. A bottle on the shelf. It's cast sugar glass, thin and brittle. Nobody's dropped it. My coworker says that's all "fragile" ever means: it would break if it were dropped.
2. The same bottle packed in its foam-lined road case. Drop the case off the truck and the bottle doesn't break. But it's obviously still fragile — we padded it, we didn't change the bottle.
3. A "shatter" bottle. Thick rubber, bounces, totally un-fragile, but there's a tiny charge inside wired to an impact sensor, so it bursts into bits when it hits the floor. It would break if dropped, and it isn't fragile.
4. An effects box with a big charged capacitor and a lid interlock: open the lid and a switch kills the power before anything can discharge, nothing happens. But the capacitor is sitting right there, charged. Dangerous or not? I go back and forth on this one hourly.
5. Same kind of box, but with a motion sensor that arms it when somebody walks into the room. Right now it's completely inert; the instant someone enters it goes live.

Then my argument:

(1) For a thing to have one of these properties — fragile, dangerous, soluble — just is for a certain "if such-and-such happened, it would do so-and-so" claim to be true of it.
(2) Case 2 kills that: the if-then is false (it wouldn't break) but the bottle is fragile. So (1) is wrong, and the if-then facts can't be what the property consists in.
(3) Cases 3 and 5 kill it from the other side too: there the if-then is true but the property is missing. Rubber isn't fragile; an unarmed box isn't dangerous.
(4) So the if-then facts and the dispositional facts can come apart in both directions, which means having the property isn't a matter of the if-thens at all.
(5) So it must instead be a matter of what the thing is actually like right now — its real internal make-up, apart from any ifs.
(6) But the make-up is itself only ever describable in if-then terms: the molecular bonds' holding-or-giving, the charge's moving-if-a-path-opens. Every candidate "solid, non-dispositional" base turns out to be another disposition, so (5) either bottoms out in nothing or goes round in a circle.
(7) So either having a disposition is nothing over and above the if-thens (refuted in (2) and (3)), or there is no such thing as a disposition at all.
(8) Which is insane: then the capacitor isn't disposed to discharge, the sugar glass isn't disposed to shatter, nothing has any powers, and nothing explains why anything ever happens — and my whole job of checking whether something is safe to be around has lost its subject matter.

I want to keep that the bottle in the road case is still fragile, I want to keep that case 3 is a genuinely different sort of thing from case 2, and I definitely don't want to end up at (8). So: which step do I give up, why did it look safe, and what does giving it up cost me? And do cases 2, 3, 4 and 5 all need the same repair, or do they pull apart — if they pull apart, I want to know which one is the real problem. Please explain in plain language and answer in American English (my spellcheck keeps fighting me on it).
