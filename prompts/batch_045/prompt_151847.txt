I am an appellate lawyer, not a philosopher, and I keep noticing that we do one thing here and the opposite thing there. Here is the package of practices I have in mind, stated as neutrally as I can:

1. Courts identify the "ordinary meaning" of statutory words as a competent speaker of the language would have understood them at the time the statute was enacted. Meaning is said to be fixed at enactment and not to drift afterwards.

2. When doing this, they decline to consult the legislative record — committee reports, floor statements, earlier drafts, ministerial explanations — even where it is public, on point, and unambiguous. What the enacting legislators wanted the words to accomplish is treated as irrelevant to what the words mean.

3. Yet the same courts also: (a) read statutes so as to avoid results they are willing to call absurd, manifestly unjust, or unworkable; (b) read old statutes as "always speaking," so that a provision framed in terms of a technology or practice from the 1930s is applied to its 2020s successor with no amendment ever having been made; (c) treat a preamble or an enacted statement of purposes as authoritative in resolving ambiguity, even though that text is as much part of the enactment as the committee reports they refuse to look at; and (d) defer to the reading adopted by the executive agency that administers an ambiguous provision, while continuing to insist that statutory interpretation is a question of law for judges alone.

4. Provisions carrying criminal penalties are construed narrowly, in favour of the person exposed to them.

A concrete instance, so the discussion stays on the ground rather than in the air: a 1935 by-law forbids "any vehicle" in a public park, and a city now wants to exclude e-scooters. The court reasons that "vehicle" in 1935 meant any wheeled conveyance used to carry people or goods — a description the enactors would not have had e-scooters in mind when using, but whose terms plainly cover them — and concludes that the e-scooter is within the meaning, not merely within some extended application the court has invented.

Here is what I want from you:

**The implicit theory.** Reconstruct the view of meaning, communication, and legal authority that this package presupposes. Does it presuppose that a statute's meaning is a function of the linguistic conventions of a community rather than of any speaker's intentions? Does it presuppose a distinction between ascertaining what a text says and deciding what is to be done about it, with only the first counting as interpretation? Does it presuppose something further about law's job — notice to citizens, legislative supremacy, the constraints on unelected judges — that the interpretive rules are meant to serve?

**Strict presuppositions versus mere fits.** Distinguish what the package strictly presupposes — what could not be given up without abandoning the underlying theory — from what merely sits well with it and could be jettisoned while remaining recognisably the same approach. In particular: is the refusal in (2) required by the theory of meaning in (1), or is it only an evidentiary or institutional proxy that the theory recommends for reasons of cost, reliability, or manipulability? Is the narrow-construction rule in (4) part of the theory of meaning at all, or an independent normative overlay?

**Internal consistency.** Is (3a) compatible with (2), given that a claim about absurdity looks like a claim about what the legislature could not have meant to achieve? Is (3b) in genuine tension with (1), or can the e-scooter reasoning — fixed descriptive content, open-ended application — reconcile them, and what does that reconciliation assume about concepts and about there being a fact of the matter about which description the 1935 word carried? Are (3c) and (3d) consistent with (2), or do they betray a different view of what statutes are for: if the enacted purpose-statement and the agency's reading are admissible while committee reports are not, what is being trusted in the first pair and distrusted in the third?

**Replacing the package.** Suppose the jurisdiction abandoned this package for a straightforward purposivism — courts ask what the statute was there to achieve and read the text so as to serve that aim. What exactly would have to change in how judges reason, write, and justify outcomes? Say precisely what burden purposivism imposes that these rules seem designed to avoid: what must a judge now establish, and what kind of claim is it (empirical, counterfactual, evaluative) when the legislature was plural, compromised, and partly silent? And say honestly where textualism's rules purchase their cheapness at the cost of accuracy.

**Settled versus contested.** Separate what people who actually work on this would treat as common ground from what is genuinely disputed among them — for instance, whether there is a determinate fact of the matter about ordinary meaning at a time, whether the line between interpretation and construction is coherent or ad hoc, whether textualism really delivers the constraint on judges it advertises, and whether textualism and purposivism diverge much in the cases that reach appellate courts. Tell me which of your own claims are the contested ones, and what the strongest version of the other side is.

**Where the practice is underdetermined.** Finally, tell me where the practice as described does not settle the philosophy: places where two or more quite different theories of meaning, authority, or legislation would license exactly the same behaviour, so that pointing at the practice cannot adjudicate between them.
