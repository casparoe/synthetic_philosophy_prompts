I keep changing my mind about which acquisition phenomena really support a poverty-of-the-stimulus argument, so I want my case-by-case verdicts tested against a principle. Here is where I land:

1. Structure dependence in question formation. Asked to turn the boy who is smoking is crazy into a question, children put the auxiliary in the main clause instead of moving the first auxiliary they encounter, and nothing in their experience presents the linear rule as wrong. Genuine.
2. Binding Principle C. Learners know that he cannot pick out the Ninja Turtle in he ate pizza while the Ninja Turtle was dancing, though no one ever demonstrates that the coreferential reading is unavailable, since unavailable readings leave no trace in the input. Genuine.
3. Retreat from overgeneralization. Children produce causative uses of intransitive verbs, telling a parent not to giggle them or not to disappear a toy, and then stop, without correction. Genuine.
4. Large neural networks trained only on text now show sensitivity to long-distance agreement across intervening attractor nouns, and to some island effects, with no innate grammar built in. This makes me doubt 1, even though the child and the network occupy roughly the same evidential position — and I can't see what principled difference saves 1 while 4 stands.
5. Phonotactics: which consonant clusters a language permits, learned from distributional statistics. Not genuine.
6. Syntactic bootstrapping of verb meaning, where the number of arguments a verb takes cues its interpretation. Not genuine; I think semantics and observation are doing the work there.

My tentative principle: a phenomenon is a genuine case only if (a) a domain-general learner could not recover the target from the statistics of the available input, (b) the missing information is not supplied by feedback or by some non-syntactic source such as semantics, discourse, or world knowledge, and (c) the competence appears before the relevant evidence could have accumulated.

Test that principle against all six verdicts, repair it where it fails, and motivate each clause by the case that forces it. Then state what the repaired principle predicts for two cases I haven't settled: the well-known delay on Principle B, where children who produce adult-like questions still allow him to corefer with John in John likes him; and a child whose only input is the imperfect speech of a late learner of the language. Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts no coherent principle can keep, and whether 1 and 4 can both survive once the distinction you draw is made explicit.
