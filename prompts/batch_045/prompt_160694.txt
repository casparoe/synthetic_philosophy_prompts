I am writing a literary novel, and a late scene turns on whether a character really has a trait of character she has spent the whole book being accused of. The metaphysics matters to me more than it might seem to: the scene only works if the reader's judgement about the trait is the one I intend, and I do not want to secure that judgement by having the narrator simply assert it, or by rigging the situation so that the answer is obvious. So I would like three variants of a single situation, built to order.

The set-up: one character, whom I shall call P, either has or lacks a settled trait of character — a liability to cruelty towards whoever humiliates her, a liability to panic in enclosed spaces, a compulsion to confess when trusted: you choose, provided that (a) the trait is the sort of thing a realist novel can plausibly treat as grounded in something about her rather than in her surroundings, (b) the stimulus is something the scene can present, and (c) the manifestation is a piece of behaviour visible on the page. Assume, as the novel does, that such traits are genuine dispositions with a causal basis.

The three variants are to be indistinguishable to any other character in the scene: same woman, same situation, same outward behaviour, same outcome.

1. A *fink*: P has the disposition, but the arrival of the stimulus itself sets in train something that removes the disposition before it can manifest.
2. A *mask*: P has the disposition, something in the situation prevents the manifestation, and the disposition survives the episode intact.
3. A *mimic*: P lacks the disposition, but the manifestation occurs anyway, produced by a different route from a different basis.

The constraints, which are the point of the exercise:

No sorcerers, no gadgets, no science fiction, and no convenient timing. The interfering factor in (1) and (2) must be psychologically and physically ordinary, and in (1) it must be *triggered by* the stimulus or by P's registering it, not by an unrelated event that happens to arrive at the right moment: a well-timed coincidence is not a fink, and I will treat it as cheating. In (3) the behaviour must be genuinely produced, not performed — no acting, no pretence, no deliberate deception, since that makes the lack of the disposition too easy to see.

The hard constraint, and the reason I am asking rather than doing this myself: the usual diagnostic must be made useless. In both (1) and (2), if the interfering factor were removed a moment before the stimulus arrived, the manifestation would occur. So the difference between fink and mask cannot be got out of the counterfactual about what would have happened without the interferer; it has to be got out of whether the causal basis of the disposition persists through the episode, and the case has to make that persistence something the narrative could in principle show rather than merely stipulate.

What I want after the three variants, which should be written as scene material, present tense, a short paragraph or two each, in the register of the novel:

- For each variant, state the disposition, the stimulus, the manifestation, and the interfering factor, and say which of the three kinds it is and why.
- Run the verdicts: what the simple conditional analysis says about each case, and what a mask-tolerant or causal-basis analysis says; note where they come apart and where they unexpectedly agree.
- Name the single variable that differs between (1) and (2), and show that it is what does the work — say what happens to each case if you remove it, and whether the pair then collapses into one case.
- Tell me how a defender of the conditional analysis would attack each variant, and which attack you think is strongest.
- For each variant, say what a novelist can put on the page — a second scene, an unobserved moment, a nearby occasion, a piece of behaviour the trait would also generate — that would let a competent reader get the ascription right without being told. This is the part I care about most: a difference that only a metaphysician with access to causal bases could detect is useless to me.
- Finally, tell me which of the three is hardest to make convincing inside a realist novel, and why. If a fourth variant would make the set cleaner, add it; if not, do not pad it out.

Please write the whole answer in British English.
