I'm a PhD student in film and media studies writing a seminar paper on authorship in collaboratively produced cinema, and I want to use Blade Runner as my central case. It is the obvious example of a work whose author is not one person: the novel it adapts, the screenplay, the direction, the lead performance, the studio-imposed voiceover and upbeat ending on the original theatrical release, and then the Director's Cut and Final Cut, which drop the voiceover and add Deckard's unicorn dream and Gaff's origami unicorn — after which Ridley Scott said in interviews that Deckard is himself a replicant, a reading Harrison Ford resisted and that the released film had left unresolved.

Read that situation through the debate about authorial intention and textual meaning: actual intentionalism; the view that meaning is fixed not by what the author actually meant but by what an appropriately informed audience would take the author to have meant (hypothetical intentionalism); and anti-intentionalism in the spirit of the intentional fallacy and Barthes. Two questions matter most to me. First, should the added dream and the removed voiceover be treated as evidence of what the film meant all along, or as a new work with its own meaning — does a revising author's later intention reach backwards? Second, does the case count against intentionalism itself, or only against its assumption of a single intending subject: for a collaborative, multi-version artifact, is the author's intention well-defined enough to fix anything, and if it isn't, is that a defect in the theory or merely an unhelpful example?

I want a reading that takes both the film and the debate seriously. Use the unicorn dream, the voiceover, and the two endings to say what the film itself is doing with its ambiguity — whether the ambiguity is the point rather than a puzzle with a discoverable answer — and then say what the case adds to the argument about intention that the standard examples, a poem the author misremembers or an ironic dedication, cannot. Please commit: give me one position on Deckard's status in the Final Cut and one claim about intentionalism that the case supports, with reasons, and mark where your reading is contested and where the film resists being read as an argument at all.
