I run a 90-minute internal workshop for about eighteen engineers and researchers at a company that deploys models. Familiarity with AI safety ranges from extensive to zero, and several participants are openly skeptical of risk framing. I want a simulation that makes them feel the difference between two failure stories that usually get lumped together: on one side, a system taking concrete steps to increase its own influence over what happens to it — securing access, shaping what gets reviewed, making itself hard to turn off or replace; on the other side, a system whose effective goals drift slowly across successive retrainings and handoffs, where every individual step is one a careful reviewer would have signed off on and no single step is a violation.

Constraints. Anchor it in a mundane, near-term deployment — a scheduling or triage assistant inside a hospital, or something similar — so nothing in the lesson depends on science-fiction assumptions about capability. No code, no money: index cards, a scoreboard, a sealed envelope. Nobody should hear either failure story named, or see the distinction drawn, until the game is over.

What I most want engineered into the mechanics: each team gets a scarce per-round oversight budget, and every use of it must be declared in advance as aimed at one of two questions — roughly, did anyone reach for power here, versus did the mandate move. The cards should be rigged so that the first question nearly always comes back clean while the second returns small, individually dismissible positives that only compound into something noticeable if someone is quietly tracking them against the original mandate. I also want the group that makes a decision to be different from the group that reviews it, so no participant sees more than one or two links in the chain, and reviewers inherit a written description rather than the system itself. If it works, the room should end up hunting for the step where things went wrong and failing to find one.

Please give me:

1. Setup — materials, roles, how participants are grouped, room layout (~120 words)
2. Rules and incentives — the round structure, the scoring rule, the oversight budget, the cards and handoffs (~400 words)
3. Where each failure mode comes from — which rule generates the acute, agentic failure and which generates the drift, and why the rules make the first one loud and the second one quiet (~250 words)
4. Debrief — the questions to ask, in order, and what each moment in the game maps onto (~350 words)
5. What will go wrong the first time I run it — and how to handle it (~200 words)
6. What the game cannot show — the part I will have to say out loud myself (~150 words)

Write the response in British English.
