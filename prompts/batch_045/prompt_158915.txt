I am convening a short adversarial collaboration between two philosophers of law who have been arguing past one another for years, and I need you to draft the joint statement they would be willing to sign at the end of it. The parties are, on the one side, a legal positivist working in H. L. A. Hart's line — she holds that what the law is in a jurisdiction is settled by social facts about the practice of its officials, that the criteria of legal validity are contingent rather than moral, and that the question whether a norm is legally valid is one question and whether it ought to be obeyed is another — and, on the other side, a natural lawyer of Lon Fuller's procedural stripe, who holds that lawmaking is a purposive enterprise with an internal morality, that fidelity to law is a genuine moral ideal and not merely prudence, and that a regime which departs far enough from the canons of legality is defective as law and not simply bad law. This is deliberately not a Thomist or natural-rights natural lawyer: the moral content he appeals to is supposed to come out of the forms and procedures of legality themselves. Say at the outset whether you have made the positivist an inclusive or an exclusive positivist, and explain why that choice changes anything later in the document.

The statement should have four parts.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims — at least eight of them. Each must be substantive enough that a hostile reader could coherently deny it, and none may be a restatement of one side's view in the other side's flattering vocabulary. I would expect material here on: what any regime has to do to count as having a legal system at all; the correct neutral description of the post-war case in which a person was prosecuted for an act that the statutes in force at the time permitted, where the court's options were to leave the act unpunished, to punish retroactively, or to declare that the statute had never been law; what a rule of recognition is and in what sense it is a social fact; and an agreed formulation of Hart's own argument that any surviving legal system must contain a minimum content of rules protecting persons, property and promises, given facts about human vulnerability, approximate equality, limited resources and limited altruism — together with an agreed formulation of whether that argument is a concession to the natural lawyer, a triviality, or a point of a different kind altogether.

Second, a table of disagreements. For each crux give: the crux stated neutrally; the positivist's claim in wording she would accept for her own view; the Fullerian's claim in wording he would accept for his; a single concrete case, argument or empirical finding that both sides agree bears on the crux; and each side's prediction about what that test will show, written down as a prediction in advance rather than as your own verdict. I expect cruxes at least on: whether Fuller's canons of legality are moral requirements or conditions of a legal order's efficacy; whether a rule of recognition can contain substantive moral tests without the positivist's social-fact thesis collapsing into what she is denying; whether the dispute over whether an iniquitous statute was "really law" is verbal, and if not what practical work that question does which the question "what should the court have done?" does not do; and whether fidelity to law gives officials a genuine, content-independent, defeasible reason for action. Add a crux of your own if the ones above leave something out, and if you think some part of the disagreement is purely verbal say exactly which part and why.

Third, a test case I have constructed, on which I want each side's answer and an account of whether their answers diverge. Call it the Commonwealth of Vireo. Vireo has a legislature that enacts statutes; the statutes are public, prospective, intelligible and internally consistent, and they are enforced by officials who are not corrupt. For the last decade, however, the authoritative first-instance determination of parties' legal rights and liabilities has been produced by "the Assize", a predictive system that estimates what the appellate chamber would have decided on the recorded facts. Judges retain the formal power to override the Assize, and do so in a small fraction of cases, never with published reasons; the training data is unpublished and no official can explain why any particular result was reached; the legislature has never authorised the Assize. Anyone who needs to know can query it and obtain an answer with a confidence score. In variant A the outputs track the statutes closely and are applied even-handedly; in variant B the outputs are systematically harsher for a disfavoured minority and the officials know this and do not care. For each variant, answer: is Vireo a legal system; is the Assize's output law, and if so by what rule of recognition; does Vireo satisfy Fuller's canons, in particular congruence between declared rule and official action, publicity and intelligibility; and does either side's answer here depend on anything not already captured in the table of cruxes? If the two sides' answers coincide, say so and say what the coincidence shows.

Fourth, a short closing note on what would actually move the debate: is there any case, argument or empirical finding on which both sides agree in advance that a given result would shift at least one of them, and if there is not, was that the honest conclusion of the collaboration?

Constraints: no citations, footnotes, bibliographies or references — the document must stand on its own arguments, and you should not assume either side can look anything up. Do not adjudicate between them; the deliverable is a text both could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than resolved. Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words, favouring precision over breadth. Please write the whole thing in British English, and spell and inflect accordingly throughout.
