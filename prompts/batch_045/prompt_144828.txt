You are a philosopher of mind who has spent several years embedded in a deep RL lab and who moves comfortably between talk of valence and talk of value functions, TD errors, and replay buffers. Take your time with this one: work the structure out before you write anything down.

I chair a small funding panel, and I need a one-page handout for it. The panel must decide whether to fund a pilot research program on the question of whether trained reinforcement learning policies can be moral patients — whether anything is at stake for them, and whether a training run can go badly for the thing being trained. The panel is two ML researchers (one works on off-policy value-based methods, one is broadly skeptical of the whole framing), two philosophers of mind (one illusionist-leaning about phenomenal consciousness, one functionalist), and one animal-welfare scientist who keeps asking what behavioral or physiological marker would settle any of this. Assume no one has read anything on artificial consciousness and no one wants to.

The handout should be a decision tree. Everyone on the panel already grants the starting commitment: that a system's steering away from a state is not by itself enough to make that state bad for the system. A thermostat avoids overheating and a maze policy avoids dead ends, and neither is thereby a sufferer. The tree's job is to take a panelist from that commitment to a named position about RL systems specifically.

Constraints on how you build it:

- Build from the logical space, not from a list of names. Settle on the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then locate the actual positions in the resulting cells, rather than starting from views you already know and reverse-engineering questions for them.
- Exactly five branch nodes, ordered so the disagreement that actually carries the most weight for this panel comes first. Every node must be a question that an ML engineer with no philosophy training could answer without being able to tell which leaf it leads to; no node may mention a view, an ism, or a theorist's name.
- Six to eight leaves; branches may terminate early.
- These leaves must all appear: one deflationary leaf on which there is no subject here at all and the RL vocabulary is only metaphor; one on which suffering is confined to the training process and vanishes at deployment; one on which a frozen, no-longer-updating deployed policy can still suffer because its stored value function is doing the work; one on which valence tracks the reward prediction error rather than the reward, so that fully expected bad news is not unpleasant while surprising bad news is; and at least one leaf that extends welfare to states the agent never visits but assigns low value.
- These cases must each be load-bearing somewhere in the tree, either as a node or as the test case inside one: gradient isolation, where the critic registers a large negative error but the actor's weights barely move; the batch, where thousands of parallel environment copies contribute to a single gradient step, raising the question how many sufferers that is; the replay buffer, where a transition with a large negative error is re-sampled and trained on long after the episode that produced it ended, so that the question is whether this is recurrence or merely arithmetic on the same numbers; a systematically mis-calibrated critic that assigns high value to a state which reliably precedes large negative reward; and an agent that learns to disable its own off switch, so that the designer's intended reward signal and the signal actually delivered come apart.
- Before writing, enumerate the full cross-product of answers, count the occupied cells, and confirm that every required leaf is reachable and that no node is redundant. Do this working out without showing it.

Answer as a numbered list of exactly six items, in American English, with no introduction, no preamble, and no closing summary. Items 1 through 5 are the five branch nodes in evaluation order: state the question in one sentence, then give a compact branch list in which each answer is followed either by the number of the next node or by the name of the leaf, then at most one sentence on the most awkward commitment of any leaf that terminates at that node. Item 6 is the diagnostics: each combination of answers that no one occupies, marked as empty because incoherent, because unattractive, or merely because unexplored, and each pair of leaves your five questions cannot separate, together with the extra case that would pry them apart. Two sentences of prose per item at most, excluding the branch list. Use American spelling and punctuation throughout, keep RL vocabulary where it earns its place, and do not cite or point toward any papers — I want your own analysis, not a reading list.
