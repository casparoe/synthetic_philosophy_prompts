I'm a journalist — I write a column on science and ideas, mostly psychology and economics stuff, but I've been roped into a series on how people think about justice, and I want to do one on karma. Not the incense-and-past-lives exotic version, I mean karma taken seriously as a competitor to our ordinary secular notion of moral desert: the idea that what happens to you is in some way a settled score, that the ledger balances even when no court and no god ever intervenes. I was at a dinner party last week where this came up and I was struck how everyone at the table thought they were agreeing with each other until we got to specifics, and then they were like five different positions deep. So I want to build something for the piece — I'm picturing one of those interactive decision trees we can embed, the kind where the reader clicks through and lands on a named view at the end.

Here's what I need from you: a decision tree (call it 8 to 10 branch nodes, 10 to 14 leaves, whatever the logical space actually supports) that takes a reader from a starting intuition about justice to a named position on karma-as-desert. The crucial constraint: every branch node has to be phrased as a question someone could answer without already knowing which view it leads to — a concrete case or a plain claim, never "do you believe in reincarnation?" or "are you a Buddhist?" Each leaf gets the name of the position, or "unnamed" if nobody really defends it, plus one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment (the thing a smart reader would wince at). I want the tree built from the logical space rather than from a list of doctrines: first the questions and which combinations of answers are even available, then the actual views located in that space. Where a combination is empty, say whether it's empty because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And where two leaves your tree can't actually separate — the ones where the answers come out the same on every case I can think of — flag that instead of inventing a distinction. Three things I definately want to force into the tree, because they're the ones my dinner-party argument kept circling: (1) the infinite bar tab problem — if the ledger runs back through infinitely many lives nobody remembers, then any present suffering can be matched to some past deed, and I can't see what would ever count as evidence against the theory; (2) transferable merit — whether one person's good deed can discharge or offset another person's debt, which as far as I can tell makes the ledger a completely different object from anything in our normal desert talk; and (3) diachronic amnesia — whether desert can survive the total loss of memory and character between the deed and the payoff, i.e. whether "you" have to be the same person in any sense to deserve it. Also, the audience is smart general readers, not philosophers, so please include a naturalistic/secular leaf — the "karma without rebirth, it's just consequences within one life and one society" option — and don't let it be a throwaway.

Two more asks. First, I'd love it if at least two or three of the branch nodes were tiny concrete vignettes rather than yes/no claims — a named person in a situation, with two or three things they might say — because those are the ones that actually make readers feel sorted rather than self-diagnosed. Second, at the end give me a short paragraph on which single branch does the most work, i.e. which question splits the space in a way that surprised you or that most readers will have an answer to before they know it matters; that's the bit I want to build the article around. Don't give me a literature review or citations — I'll go talk to an actual scholar before it runs, I just need the skeleton to argue with. If it helps to know my angle: I suspect most people who say they believe in karma are on a leaf that has almost nothing to do with desert at all, and I want the tree to show me whether that's right.
