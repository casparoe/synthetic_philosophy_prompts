I've been going back and forth with a friend for most of an evening about the inside view versus outside view distinction in forecasting, and we ended up using the 2007–08 subprime collapse as our test case and arguing ourselves into a standoff, so I'd like a more disciplined analysis than either of us managed. Her claim is that the episode is a clean vindication of the inside view: the outside view said housing was safe, because the claim everyone repeated before the crash was that US house prices had never fallen on a nationwide basis since the 1930s, and because the risk models at the ratings agencies and the bank risk departments were calibrated on loss experience from a period of rising prices and treated geographic diversification as protection against a national downturn. Meanwhile a handful of analysts and small hedge funds went through the loan-level data, noticed that the teaser rates on enormous fractions of the 2005–07 vintages were scheduled to reset into the same two or three years, and bought protection against subprime-backed tranches, and they were spectacularly right while the models were catastrophically wrong. I think her conclusion comes close to being backwards, and I want you to adjudicate it, but I want the mapping done properly first: who the forecasters actually were on each side (the rating analysts, the risk departments, the desks writing credit protection, the short sellers, the regulators), what each of them was really trying to predict, what their actual options were — including the option of not forecasting at all and merely buying insurance, which is a different act from predicting — and what each side was treating as evidence.

The thing I keep pressing, and that she dismisses, is that the winners did not defeat the outside view so much as replace its reference class: they stopped asking what US house prices do and started asking what happens to cohorts of no-documentation, teaser-rate, high loan-to-value borrowers originated in a given year, which is a class with a very different base rate attached to it. If that is right, then the lesson is not that mechanism beats statistics but that the choice of reference class carries the whole argument, and the inside/outside contrast is really a continuum of granularity rather than a dichotomy. I want you to say whether that is the right diagnosis, and if it is, what general advice survives it: how fine-grained should a reference class be, what stops you from gerrymandering it until it returns whatever number you wanted, and does calling a judgement a base rate make it more epistemically respectable or merely more rhetorically effective?

Two further things I want handled rather than gestured at. First, my own attempt at a distinction: a base rate is a statistic about a population of cases inside a regime, so outside-view reasoning is partly evidence about the regime and not only about the case, which should make it systematically blind to regime change — and when the reference class has no positive instances at all, the outside view does not deliver a disciplined forecast, it delivers a zero that is really the model confessing it cannot represent the event. Is there any principled way to tell when an empty or near-empty reference class is evidence of genuine rarity rather than evidence of blindness, and does epistemic modesty in that situation mean moving your central estimate or widening your tails? Second, I want the places where the framework goes quiet: as I understand it the outside view was devised as a remedy for forecasting, but nearly every actor here was deciding under uncertainty rather than issuing a number, and I suspect my friend has slid a forecasting tool into a decision rule. There is also something I have started calling reference-class engineering — the possibility of acting so as to move yourself into a better reference class instead of passively accepting the base rate of the class you began in, by modularising, parallelising, shortening feedback loops or buying options — and I would like to know whether anything of that kind is visible in this episode, on either side of the trade, and whether it is a genuine fourth option the inside/outside framing omits or just the inside view in disguise. Then give me a verdict: is the subprime episode an argument for the outside view, against it, or a case the distinction was never built to handle, and what would you have advised a senior risk manager in 2006 who had both the national base rate and the loan-level data on the same desk?
