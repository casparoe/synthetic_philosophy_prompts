Hi — I'm the deputy director of a tiny county emergency management agency in a river valley: about eleven miles of levee, an upstream dam owned by a power company that is not great at returning calls, a trailer park and an industrial park on the floodplain, roughly nine thousand people total. Eight to twelve times a year I'm the duty officer on a call where, inside thirty or forty minutes, with a laptop, three browser tabs and one phone call, I have to decide what to recommend to the county judge. She signs the order, and whatever I write on the one-page rationale later gets read by the sheriff's office, the state auditor, an insurance adjuster, and occasionally a reporter. The sentence I keep having to write is some version of we know the water will not overtop the levee at the Maple Street gauge, or we know it will, and the difference between those two sentences is sometimes an evacuation of four hundred people at night.

I got into an argument with a hydrologist friend about this last spring — we had a near miss with the pump station at Maple Street, and ever since, that particular failure mode is the only one anyone in the room can think about, so the same forecast reads completely differently to us than it did a year earlier. She told me to go read up on two things, and I did, sort of: the view that whether someone counts as knowing depends on what is at stake for them (I think it's called pragmatic encroachment, and there's some version where the bar for knowledge goes up when the decision is high-stakes), and the view that the standards a knowledge claim has to meet shift with which error possibilities are live in the conversation (contextualism?). Both seem to say the bar moves, but for different reasons and maybe in different directions, and I need one protocol, not two. So: write me a decision procedure my duty officers and I can actually run in the time we have, built out of those ideas, that starts from a forecast plus a map and ends with one of three outputs — assert knowledge, refuse to assert knowledge and act on the precautionary branch anyway, or stop and hand the call to a person as a values question. For each step say what would count as a sufficient answer, where the procedure just terminates in a no, and where a human has to decide because it's not a factual question anymore.

Three things I specifically need it to handle, because they are where I keep tying myself in knots. First, whose stakes? If the bar tracks stakes, it seems like it should track the stakes of the people in the trailer park, not mine — but I'm the one making the call, and my stakes are different and point the other way: I get blamed for a costly false alarm and for crying wolf, and that pushes me toward evacuating more than the residents' own interests do. Second, sometimes the stakes cut both ways at once: raising the bar means waiting twelve hours for a better forecast, and waiting eats the lead time the evacuation needs, so being more careful makes the outcome worse if I'm wrong — I need the procedure to say what to do when the standard the stakes demand is literally unreachable inside the window. Third, whatever I write travels: my we know gets read weeks later by an auditor or an adjuster who has no idea what was at stake that night or which failure modes we'd been worried about, so I think the protocol needs a step that records which error possibilities were excluded, or something equivalent. Please include a short section on what the procedure cannot do, and please work it through on three cases: (a) overnight, 60 percent chance of overtopping at Maple Street, four hundred homes, a lot of elderly residents, six hours of lead time; (b) one identical forecast covering two zones at once, the trailer park and a warehouse district where the only thing at risk is empty buildings and a parking lot — if the procedure tells me I know the water won't reach the warehouses but not that it won't reach the trailers, on the very same evidence, say so plainly, because that's the answer I'm going to hate and I'd rather see it worked out than avoided; and (c) a slow-onset one, three days out, where a better model run lands in twelve hours but evacuation needs thirty. Assume the person running this has no philosophy training and is tired; no citations needed, and please don't just tell me to be more careful.
