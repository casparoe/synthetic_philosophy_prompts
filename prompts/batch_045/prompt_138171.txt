Hey, so I'm writing the second book of a trilogy and I've painted myself into a corner a bit. The setting is a fictional alpine republic (I'm calling it Rhaetia — Switzerland-ish in vibe but please don't treat it as a roman-à-clef of anywhere) about thirty years from now, and there's a parliamentary commission drafting a new animal protection act. One of my viewpoint characters, Beatrix Halder, is a moral philosopher who's spent her career on Kant, and she gets called in as an expert witness. The chair basically asks her: of all the measures on the table, which would Kant have objected to most? I want her answer to be an actual ranking, from "he'd hate this" to "he wouldn't bat an eyelid" or even "he'd be baffled by the question" — the scene only works if she's forced to commit to an order instead of waffling — with a sentence or two of reasoning per item, and ideally one item where the answer is genuinely unclear and she has to say so out loud.

Background as I understand it, and this is the part I'm shakiest on so please correct me where I've got him wrong: we owe animals nothing directly, since they aren't rational and can't be ends in themselves; but cruelty to them is still wrong, because it coarsens the agent, damages in him the humanity he owes as a duty to himself, and because a man who tortures a dog is more likely to be cruel to people. I also half-remember that he thought vivisection was abhorrent but could be allowed when it served some important end for humanity, which has always struck me as a bit of a fudge: if the animal doesn't count for anything, why should the importance of the end matter? Anyway.

The measures on the table in Rhaetia, in no particular order: (1) a total ban on keeping animals for food, i.e. abolishing livestock farming outright; (2) mandatory stunning before slaughter, plus a rule that nobody ever slaughters an animal they raised themselves, plus a rule that abatoir staff rotate off the kill floor every week so no one gets hardened to it; (3) the Kestrel programme — gene-edited cattle bred without any capacity to suffer at all, which the ministry pitches as making the whole welfare debate dissolve; (4) fully automated slaughterhouses with no human on the kill floor whatsoever, which the republic is very proud of and which were a compromise after a referendum; (5) a ban on animal testing for cosmetics while testing for life-saving drugs stays legal; (6) court-appointed guardians who can sue on behalf of apes, in the apes' own name; (7) a constitutional clause saying living creatures may never be treated merely as means, which the campaigners call the dignity clause; (8) a statutory retirement for working animals — cart horses, guide dogs, detection dogs are kept at public expense once they can't work anymore, which I gather is close to something Kant suggests about an old dog who's served his master; (9) culling of overpopulated wild deer by drone, at night, with the footage never released.

So: how would Kant rank these, and which of them does his indirect-duty story actually decide cleanly versus leave ambiguous or silent? Is there anything here he'd find not just wrong but literally unintelligible in his own terms — and anything he'd endorse for reasons that have nothing to do with the animal at all? Which item, if any, would be where the real Kant comes out somewhere my character doesn't want him to? And where does the automated slaughterhouse and the pain-free cattle leave the whole argument, given that the usual story is supposed to be about what cruelty does to us?

Oh, and please don't give me citations, page numbers, or anything I'd have to look up — I'm writing most of this on a train with terrible signal and I can't check a bibliography, I just need the reasoning. My writing group said the last draft of this scene read like somebody reciting a wikipedia page, so I need her to sound like she's actually thinking. (I have a vague memory that some real country has a creature-dignity clause in its constitution, but don't hold me to that, and keep it general — I'd rather have the argument than the fact.)
