I am assembling the problem set for a graduate seminar on higher-order approaches to consciousness, and before I commit the syllabus to any of them I want the specification rather than the theories: what, exactly, does a higher-order account of state consciousness have to deliver in order to count as adequate?

Fix the purpose first, since adequacy is relative to it. The theory is to be good enough to adjudicate, from behavioural and neural evidence, whether a given system has conscious experiences and which of its states are conscious. The verdicts I actually need are about: patients who are behaviourally non-responsive or only minimally responsive after brain injury; non-human animals with and without anything playing the role of a prefrontal metacognitive system; split-brain subjects; and feedforward artificial classifiers with no recurrent or top-down architecture. Any requirement that does no work in those cases is one I want flagged as idle for my purposes.

Then the list. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement — the phenomenon to be explained, the verdict it must reach, the distinction it must not collapse, or the empirical result it must not contradict — and mark whether it is (a) a constraint on any theory of consciousness, (b) one that bites specifically because of the higher-order architecture, or (c) a preference of some camp that I should refuse to grant. Among the cases I want handled:

- the ordinary contrast between perceiving something and merely processing it, as in masked, inattentional and change-blindness cases, and in blindsight-style dissociation of discrimination from experience;
- dreamless sleep and anaesthesia, where first-order sensory processing continues while experience is absent;
- the generalised rock problem: a higher-order state that targets a state belonging to another subject, or to nothing mental at all. What makes a targeted state the subject's own, without presupposing that it already is? I do not want the answer to be that a state is conscious iff a higher-order state of that very subject targets it, since that says nothing until that very subject has been explicated non-circularly;
- the empty-target case: hallucination and phantom limb, where the higher-order state has no first-order state to target, or targets one that is not there;
- the attributive-mismatch case, which I do not think receives enough attention: suppose a subject's higher-order mechanism reliably picks out a currently active first-order state but systematically misdescribes its content — the higher-order tagging that normally goes with green is triggered when the first-order state is as of red. The theory must say whether the state is conscious, and whether what it is like tracks the content of the first-order state, the content attributed by the higher-order state, or neither;
- the temporal-mismatch case: suppose, as the evidence on integration windows and on the relative sluggishness of metacognitive processing at least allows, that higher-order states update more slowly and more coarsely than the first-order states they target. The theory must then say whether consciousness is retrospective, coarse-grained, or systematically asynchronous with the first-order processing it is supposed to make conscious, and what it predicts we should find if so;
- the unity case: many concurrent first-order states, one or few higher-order states — is the unity of a conscious field explained by the unity of the higher-order state, and what happens if the higher-order machinery is divided?
- the structural constraints: the account must not generate a regress of higher-order states, and it must not quietly concede the same-order point by treating the terminating higher-order state as conscious in its own right rather than in virtue of a further state.

Go on to the two questions I care most about. First, tell me which of the things people in this literature demand are genuine desiderata and which are仅仅 the prejudices of one side: candidates that worry me include the demand that a theory account for phenomenal character rather than for cognitive availability or reportability, the demand that it not make experience depend on conceptual sophistication, and the demand that it preserve the intuition that a state's being conscious is intrinsic to it rather than relational. Second, tell me whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — I suspect one is, and if so, which conjunct I should drop and what that costs me. Then say which single requirement does the most exclusionary work: the one that, if I relaxed it, would let in a theory I would otherwise reject.

Close with a brief scorecard — a few lines each, and a table is fine — of the thought-based version associated with Rosenthal, the quasi-perceptual version associated with Lycan, and the same-order or self-representational alternative, against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring. If two of the requirements I have gestured at collapse into one, say so rather than padding the list.
