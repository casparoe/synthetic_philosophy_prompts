I've been reading the rationalist end of the internet for something like fifteen years, and the habits that stuck are the usual ones: taboo the word, ask what I would expect to observe differently, look for the gears rather than the label. By profession I'm a security engineer, and the vocabulary I actually reason in is threat models, adversaries and their capabilities, honest-but-curious versus fully malicious participants, plaintext versus metadata, on-record and off-record channels, side and covert channels, plausible deniability and deniable encryption, the assumption that the adversary knows the system and only the key is secret, and the standing discipline of asking which party is supposed to learn what.

What I do not have is much philosophy of language. I know the cartoon version of Grice — speaker meaning versus sentence meaning, a cooperative principle, some maxims, implicature — mostly secondhand, and I suspect the cartoon is doing me a disservice. So here is what I want.

Take the Gricean package in its technical detail rather than the cartoon version: the definition of speaker meaning in terms of a reflexive intention, where the speaker intends to produce an effect in the hearer by means of the hearer's recognition of that very intention; the cooperative principle and the four maxims with their sub-clauses; the stepwise derivation pattern for a particularized conversational implicature, roughly that the speaker has said that p, that there is no reason to suppose she is not observing the maxims, that she could not be doing that unless she thought that q, that she knows the hearer can see this, and that she has done nothing to stop the hearer thinking q; the diagnostics meant to separate implicature from entailment and from conventional content, in particular cancellability or defeasibility, non-detachability, and calculability; the distinctions between flouting, violating, opting out, and clashing; and the distinctions between particularized, generalized, and conventional implicature. Restate all of it in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can. For each piece, tell me what it corresponds to in a model with a specified sender, a specified receiver, a specified adversary, a message space, and an assumed relationship between the parties' objectives. Where my vocabulary offers several candidates and none is exactly right, say so and pick the least bad.

One framing I have been playing with, which you should use or replace rather than simply accept: an implicature is not a message the sender is trying to hide from the receiver, it is a message the sender is trying to hide from a third party — an overhearer, a future employer, a tribunal, the public — while relying on the receiver to reconstruct it. On that reading implicature is closest to a deniable channel between sender and intended receiver whose security property points sideways rather than at the adversary in the channel; call it deniable uptake. The case I have in mind is the recommendation letter that says only that the candidate writes good English and turned up to tutorials regularly, from which the reader is meant to conclude that he is no good at philosophy, without anything on paper the candidate could complain about. Tell me how much of the Gricean apparatus that framing actually carries, and where it stops paying.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I care about: which claims translate cleanly, which translate only after you have quietly changed them, and which cannot be stated in my vocabulary at all. I expect trouble in three places in particular.

First, the cooperative assumption. In my framework cooperation is an alignment of objectives, or at best a protocol both parties have an incentive to follow. Grice's hearer seems entitled to assume something weaker and stranger: that the speaker is, for the purposes of the inference, playing the language game. Is that a prior over messages, a specification, or a norm? My framework can only represent it as a prior, and I suspect that misrepresents Grice — that if it is a norm, then flouting it generates content in a way that quietly violating a protocol does not.

Second, the boundary between what is said and what is implicated. My vocabulary wants to draw that line at the boundary between plaintext and inference, but I gather that what is said is itself context-dependent in ways that do not fit: unresolved references, undisambiguated expressions, the later disputes about whether there is a middle layer between what is said and what is implicated. Tell me whether the line I want to draw is the line Grice drew, and what breaks if I draw it there anyway.

Third, silence. In a channel model, if no message is sent there is no plaintext and nothing to analyze, and yet it is obviously possible to implicate something by saying nothing: failing to deny a rumor, not answering in a meeting, not replying to an email. Does the translation have resources here, or is this the point at which the channel picture itself is the wrong one?

Please test the translation on two cases before telling me it works. In the first, the receiver is hostile: speaker and hearer have genuinely opposed interests, and the hearer is a lawyer or a diplomat who will happily decline the inference and hold the speaker to the literal content. In the second, there is no third party at all — nobody to be deniable to — and speaker and hearer share a great deal of context; I want to know whether implicature still has a job in that setting, or whether a channel with no adversary collapses into plain speech.

Finally, and this is the real question: for each item in the residue, tell me whether it is a defect of my vocabulary — something security engineering would be better for being able to say — or a sign that the Gricean claim was never as clear as it looks in a seminar room. I would rather be told that a piece of the theory is underspecified than be handed a translation that has quietly imported assumptions Grice never made.
