I am a science and ideas journalist, and I am writing the section of my magazine's style guide that governs mental-state and epistemic vocabulary about AI systems — knows, believes, understands, reasons, wants, refuses, is confused, is in pain. At the moment my desk decides these case by case, which produces visible inconsistency: the same system is described in one piece as reasoning step by step toward an answer and in another as emitting text that resembles reasoning. I do not want an essay on consciousness. I want a procedure I can run repeatedly, under deadline, with a standards editor signing off.

The backdrop is the old thought experiment in which a person conducts a long conversation with Einstein's preserved brain and cannot tell, from the conversation alone, whether they are addressing something that thinks or something that merely behaves as though it does — together with the companion suggestion that a sufficiently enormous book of prepared replies could hold up its end of an indefinitely long conversation. Assume that the question of whether flawless conversational performance amounts to understanding is genuinely open, and that our copy has to be written while it remains open.

Here is my half-formed proposal, which I would like you to test, sharpen, or replace with something better. Our house style currently has a binary: report what the system output, or attribute mentality to it. I suspect the useful cut is threefold rather than binary — output-level vocabulary (it produced, it declined, it gave), competence-level vocabulary (it knows the capital of France, it can solve, it distinguishes), and experience-level vocabulary (it understands, it believes, it is distressed) — and that conversational evidence of the Einstein's-brain kind can only ever license the first two, never the third, because the very thing in question is whether that evidence discriminates. A second suspicion: our current allergy to mental verbs is not neutrality either, since refusing to write that a system knows anything, or wrapping every verb in scare quotes, is itself a claim about the system that a reporter may not be in a position to make.

Please deliver the following.

One, an ordered decision procedure — the questions to ask, in order, about any sentence under consideration, with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each and where the procedure terminates in a no. It must be usable without access to model internals, training details, or a research team, and it must tell the desk what to do when the relevant fact is not obtainable at all.

Two, a distinction between reporting and endorsing that survives contact with real copy: what happens when a source or an ordinary user says of a system that it understood them, and what the reporter is doing when they quote it.

Three, a two-tier version: a sixty-second pass for breaking news and a full pass for features, with a note on which steps the short version drops and what errors that invites.

Four, worked applications to three cases: a news story on a chatbot whose medical advice to a reader turned out to be correct; a feature in which the writer wants to say that a bereaved user felt understood by a bereavement chatbot; and a system that persistently emits false factual claims, where the desk must choose between writing that the model says, claims, or believes something false.

Five, one application where the procedure returns an answer I will not like. In particular, tell me honestly whether applying it consistently also forbids the mental-state and knowledge vocabulary we routinely use about non-AI things in the same issue — a company that knew its drug was dangerous, a dog that knows its owner — and if so whether that is a reductio of the procedure or a conclusion I should accept.

Six, the residue: the cases the procedure cannot settle, where an editor has to decide because the question is one of values or of tone rather than fact. I am thinking especially of headlines, where space forbids qualification; of first-person or reconstructed passages; and of the difference between a description that is theory-laden and one that is merely vivid.

Please write in American English, in polished house-style prose, at roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words, organized so it can be pasted into a style document. Refer to positions and arguments rather than to papers or authors; I will supply any citation the final version needs.
