I maintain the internal ballot service that our Production Change Board uses to decide whether a change ships in the next release train. I now have to add an automatic recommended-outcome line to the tally report, which means I need to state, in code, the rule the board is actually applying. It has never written one down and its minutes record only outcomes, never reasons. So I am trying to reconstruct the rule from past decisions, and I have a case coming up on Thursday that I do not think the record settles. I would like help with the reconstruction rather than with the engineering.

How the ballot works. Five members. Every ballot has four yes/no fields. Three are findings: I, that the change introduces no new path to silent data loss; R, that a tested procedure exists which reverts the change within one hour; F, that the change sits behind a kill switch confining exposure to a single tenant. The fourth field, S, is the bottom line: ship it in the next train. Members fill in all four fields at once and cannot see one another's answers until the ballot closes. The charter fixes the connection between the findings and the bottom line for the two classes of change:

- Standard change: ship if and only if I and R.
- Flag-gated change: ship if and only if I and either R or F.

My service does not enforce that connection. It flags ballots that violate it, the board sees the flags, and it has never asked anyone to correct one. Roughly one ballot in six is flagged. Members evidently treat S as carrying something the findings do not.

Out of about forty decisions, these seven are the ones where the tool flagged the result as split, meaning the finding tallies and the bottom-line tally did not all point the same way. In the remaining cases everything lined up and the board simply followed. So these seven are what constrain the rule. Alongside the tallies I give the number of individual ballots on which the charter condition comes out true as a whole, which is not always the number you would get from the tallies taken proposition by proposition.

- Alder, standard: I 5–0, R 4–1, S 4–1. Both findings true on four ballots. Shipped.
- Birch, standard: I 4–1, R 2–3, S 2–3. Both findings true on two ballots. Held.
- Cedar, standard: I 3–2, R 3–2, S 2–3. Both findings true on only two ballots. No flagged ballots. Held.
- Dogwood, standard: I 4–1, R 3–2, S 2–3. Both findings true on three ballots; one of those three voted no on S anyway, saying in discussion that the change had not had enough bake time. Held.
- Gorse, standard: I 3–2, R 2–3, S 3–2. Both findings true on one ballot. Held.
- Elder, flag-gated: I 4–1, R 3–2, F 1–4, S 3–2. The condition R or F comes out true on three ballots. No flagged ballots. Shipped.
- Fig, flag-gated: I 4–1, R 3–2, F 3–2, S 2–3. The condition R or F comes out true on three ballots. Held.

Thursday's case, Vellum, is a flag-gated change to the settlement retry path. Everyone accepts I, so that field is 5–0. The disagreement is about reversibility, and it falls out like this: two members say a tested revert procedure exists and the kill switch does not confine the blast radius; two say the opposite; the fifth says neither holds. The ballots are:

- two ballots: I yes, R yes, F no, S yes
- two ballots: I yes, R no, F yes, S yes
- one ballot: I yes, R no, F no, S no

So the tallies are I 5–0, R 2–3, F 2–3, S 4–1. Every ballot is internally consistent, and R or F comes out true on four of the five ballots even though neither R nor F has a majority on its own.

Here is what I need. First, set out the decision rules that fit all seven precedents, since I am fairly sure there is more than one, and say what each of them returns for Vellum. Second, give me your best guess at what the board will do on Thursday and, more importantly, what it should do if it wants to be consistent with itself, identifying which precedent is the closest analog and what follows from that precedent. If the seven cases genuinely underdetermine it, say so plainly and tell me what principle the board would be committing itself to under each choice. Third, describe a further case, in tallies, that would separate the rules that survive, so I know what to watch for in the next split decision. Fourth, tell me what the recommended-outcome line in my report should compute, given that the fork in the code is whether I aggregate proposition by proposition and then apply the charter, or evaluate the charter condition on each ballot and then take a majority.

I am not asking what the correct rule would be in the abstract, only what this board's own record commits it to and where that record runs out. If this pattern has a name in the literature, I would like to know what to call it in the write-up.
