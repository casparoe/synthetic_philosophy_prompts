I sit on my hospital's Food and Nutrition Standards Committee as the patient representative, and I have to circulate a written recommendation ten days before our next meeting. The subject has recently stopped being abstract for me. I was admitted here last spring with an infected foot, spent eleven days on a medical ward where the bedside ordering tablet put the grilled chicken and the fruit cup on the first screen and the macaroni and the pudding behind a second one, went home with a type 2 diabetes diagnosis and eleven days' worth of eating habits I had not chosen, and have spent the months since trying to un-choose them. I find that I now want exactly the steering I used to think was condescending. I would like the memo to be addressed to the other eleven people on that committee — several clinicians, a dietitian, a nurse manager, and our chief financial officer — so please write it in a register they can act on rather than in the idiom of a seminar paper.

The proposal on the table, called Nourishing Defaults, is this. For inpatient trays, the lower-sodium, carbohydrate-appropriate option becomes the pre-selected item on the bedside tablet; selecting anything else costs one extra tap. Nothing is removed from the menu. A card at the bedside says the menu is arranged and that any item can be changed at any time. The same change is proposed for the staff and visitor cafeteria. The committee would run a twelve-month pilot on two medical wards and one surgical ward and then report to the board.

I need the memo to work through the following, all of which have come up and none of which we have resolved.

First, several of us think the ward case and the cafeteria case are different in a way the committee keeps sliding past. On the ward, the arrangement serves ends that patients already have: almost nobody admitted to a hospital wants their blood glucose to get worse, and the default helps them get what they were trying to get. In the cafeteria it serves ends we have decided people should have, and a nurse on a double shift who wants the macaroni may simply want the macaroni. One of our clinicians insists this distinction is doing all the moral work and that we should therefore split the proposal. Another says it is a distinction without a difference, because in both cases we are exploiting the fact that people take the path of least resistance. I want a view on which of them is right and what it implies for what we should approve.

Second, captive population. Patients on a ward cannot leave, are frequently in pain, sedated, or cognitively foggy, are in a novel environment, and are strongly disposed to read anything the hospital does as a recommendation — several have told us they assumed the pre-selected item was what the doctor had ordered for them. The standard line in favour of defaults is that they are permissive because opting out is cheap. But here opting out is cheap once and not cheap sixty times over a fortnight: each re-assertion is a small act of will, performed by someone who is unwell, and it is also an act of disagreeing, out loud, with the institution that is currently keeping them alive. Our dietitian also worries about the opposite failure, that repeated visible steering produces reactance and people start ordering against the default on principle. Does the captive setting change what kind of thing the default is — does it stop being a nudge and become something closer to a standing instruction — and if so, does it need the protections an instruction would need?

Third, the default is wrong for identifiable people. Patients on renal diets, patients with cachexia or unintentional weight loss, palliative patients for whom appetite matters more than glycaemic control, some pregnant patients, and patients whose religious or cultural dietary practice does not fit the pre-selected items. The default has to be uniform to be cheap to administer, so these groups have to be handled by some carve-out or by clinician override, and we cannot agree whether a carve-out is a technical fix or an admission that the policy is paternalist toward people whose ends we have misdescribed. Relatedly: our committee has authority over food service only, not over what physicians order, so a physician's diet order already binds and the default operates underneath it. I would like the memo to say how much of the third problem that fact actually solves.

Fourth, an embarrassment. Under the current supplier contract the pre-selected items are roughly sixty cents cheaper per tray than the alternatives, and the CFO's support for the proposal is not separable from that. The draft before us does not mention it. I need the memo to say whether the justification for the policy survives being honest about this, whether it would survive the numbers being reversed, and whether we should disclose the saving to patients and staff even if disclosure is not strictly required for the policy to be justified. Please treat the CFO's position as one a reasonable person could hold and engage it rather than dismissing it.

Fifth, a thing I proposed myself and cannot get the committee to rule on. Call it Choose Your Own Default: at admission, each patient is asked once which arrangement they want the menu to have for the duration of their stay — heart-healthy items first, the ordinary arrangement, or rotating chef's choice — and the tablet then behaves accordingly. My thought is that letting people select the architecture converts a nudge into something they impose on themselves, which should answer the objection that nudging treats the chooser as a thing to be managed rather than as an agent, and that it also solves my third problem, because the renal and palliative patients would sort themselves out at the front door. The objection from the floor was that the meta-choice is itself framed, that a frightened and unwell patient will take whatever is offered at that point too, and that it adds staff time at admission. I need a considered ruling on whether Choose Your Own Default changes the ethics of the policy or only its appearance, and if it changes the ethics, whether it should be the pilot condition rather than the plain default.

Sixth, measurement. The board will not let us run different menus on the same ward, so we cannot randomise properly and will have to compare across wards or before and after, which everyone concedes is weaker. We also cannot get individual clinical outcomes linked to tray choices in the pilot period without a data request we have no time for. Given that, tell me what we should insist on recording anyway — opt-out rates, something about who opts out, something about the carve-out groups — and what trigger should force the committee to retire the default. I would also like a condition of the following kind specified: if the pilot shows X rather than Y, we stop or we change, with the threshold named.

What I want from you is the document itself: a recommendation with conditions and a timeline, addressed to the committee, that says plainly whether to approve the proposal as drafted, approve it in some modified form, split it between the wards and the cafeteria, or decline it; that gives reasons our CFO and the two clinicians who think the whole thing is manipulative could actually reply to; that gives the strongest version of whichever position it rejects; and that ends with a short paragraph on what would have to show up in the pilot for the committee to reverse itself. Where my own sympathies are evident, please argue against me where you think I am wrong rather than accommodating me: I have noticed that having been nudged, and having benefited, has made me less reliable than usual about this.
