ok so im writing the theory chapter of my thesis on what (if anything) makes pain bad, and before I read any more theories of pain I want to know what I should be demanding of one. Write me the desiderata list.

Purpose first, since I gather adequacy is relative to what you need the theory for. I need one good enough for two jobs: (1) clinical — deciding when a pain is worth treating, including when the patient reports the pain but says it doesn't bother them; (2) policy — comparing amounts of pain across different people so analgesia can be weighed against other things in a health budget. If those two jobs pull the requirements apart, say so explicitly and tag each requirement with the job it comes from.

Background so we don't talk past each other. Asymbolia for pain: patients who report the sensory character of a pain — where it is, how strong, burning vs stabbing — but report no unpleasantness and no urge to get out of it; roughly, they recieve the pain without minding it. My half-formed hunch, which you should feel free to shoot down: the literature is stuck partly because "badness of pain" is doing duty for three properties that can come apart — the raw hurt character (how much it ouch-es), the aversive oomph (the push to stop it, withdraw, protect the part), and something more verdict-like that shows up in what you'd pay to avoid or whether you'd trade the experience away. Asymbolia looks like it knocks out the middle one but maybe not the third; people who like thier pain (the runner's burn, masochism, the mourner who won't give up a painful grief) look like the reverse. So: every requirement should say which of the three it's about, and a theory has to either pick which one is the badness or explain why it's none of them.

Cases and considerations I want on the list, each with the argument for why it's a requirement rather than a taste:
- an asymbolic patient who reports no unpleasantness but will still pay to avoid the pain, or will still flinch;
- liked or wanted pain, including pain the subject is glad to be in;
- pain with no tissue damage, and damage with no pain — so the theory can't just build badness out of how accurately the pain tracks the body;
- subjects who can't conceptualise pain (infants, most animals, maybe something artificial): is badness present anyway? Is that a fixed point the theory must respect or a consequence it can deny?
- first-personal authority in general: is the subject's say-so about how bad it is data the theory must preserve, or something the theory is allowed to overrule? Asymbolia seems to press hard here;
- why pain rather than itch, nausea, grief, exhaustion — the theory should say what if anything is distinctive about pain's badness, or say there's nothing distinctive and mean it;
- whether the theory yields anything quantity-like for the policy job, or says interpersonal comparison is a useful fiction.

Then: mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just prejudices of one camp; flag any pair you suspect can't be jointly satisfied (that part interests me most); and tell me which single requirement knocks out the most candidate theories. A very short scorecard of the obvious families at the end is fine — pain as a bodily command, pain as representation of damage, badness as desire or attitude, badness as a separate phenomenal component — but keep the weight on the criteria, that's the actual deliverable. Don't bother with citiations, I'll fill those in myself.
