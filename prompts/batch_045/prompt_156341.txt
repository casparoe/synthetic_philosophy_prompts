I am in my last year of secondary school and I run my school's philosophy society, so I am putting together a short sorting quiz for a session on what emotions actually are. I would like you to design the instrument for me, and I have fairly specific requirements.

The quiz should sort respondents by what they are willing to say about concrete cases, never by which label they find attractive. Nine questions at most, each a short first-personal scenario with two or three answer options, and every option phrased as something a person might actually say out loud. None of the questions or options may use the words judgment, perception, cognitivism, intentionality, or qualia, and none of them may ask directly whether emotions are beliefs, whether they are bodily feelings, or whether they are perceptions — the theory vocabulary should appear only in the key, never in the questions. Please invent fresh cases rather than reusing famous ones from the literature, and keep the whole thing finishable in fifteen minutes.

The cases need to do real diagnostic work, so please include at least these: someone who is frightened of something they themselves acknowledge to be harmless; fear or pity directed at something the respondent knows to be fictional; a mood with no apparent object that arrives for no reason; a person whose heart rate and gut feelings have been pharmacologically flattened but who still insists they are angry; a very young child or a dog in a state we would ordinarily call fear; two people with indistinguishable bodily agitation whose agitation is directed at entirely different things; and a case where the emotion fades the moment the person changes their mind about the situation. Use your own judgment about the rest.

Before the questions, lay out the two, three, or four binary switches you are building the space from — for instance whether an emotion must be about something, whether its identity is fixed by its content or by how it feels, whether it can be assessed as correct or incorrect and in which sense, whether it requires a body — and then derive the combinations. Place the familiar families of views in the resulting cells, using plain descriptive names rather than authors' names, with at least six leaves. Note at least three cells that are empty and say for each whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Also flag any two leaves your questions cannot pry apart, and say what further question would separate them if it could be asked.

Two further constraints. First, the session closes with a debate about whether a system that processes text, has no body, and holds no settled beliefs about the world could literally be afraid or angry; each leaf should end with one sentence on what that position commits you to about that case, plus one sentence on the leaf's most awkward commitment elsewhere. Second, I have no library access, so please do not cite papers, name books, or suggest further reading, and keep to position families that a generally informed reader would recognize.

Tell me which single question does most of the discriminating work, which question almost everyone will answer the same way and whether it should stay as a warm-up or be cut, and what to do when someone's answers point at two different leaves.

Please open your reply with one sentence stating which distinction the quiz really sorts on and why that one organizes the space better than the alternatives; then give the switches, the questions, the scoring key, and the notes, with no preamble, no restatement of what I have asked for, and no closing summary.
