I'm in a joint reading group this term — half machine-learning people, half philosophy department — on induction and generalization, and one sentence keeps closing discussions before they've really started: someone says "there's no such thing as a free lunch," and that's meant to be the end of it. It came up twice last week and I've come to think the two uses pull in opposite directions, which is why I'm asking. Use one: a member of the group showed that a new training recipe beats the standard baseline across a whole suite of benchmarks, and our loudest participant replied that no free lunch means it can't really be better — it's tuned to the benchmark distribution, and averaged over all possible problems the two recipes would come out exactly even, so the result tells us nothing. Use two: later, discussing whether any prediction rule can be rationally preferred to any other, the same person said no free lunch means every inductive bias is arbitrary — simplicity, smoothness, the uniformity of nature, whatever you like — because anything you assume buys you accuracy on some worlds at the cost of accuracy on others, leaving no non-circular reason to prefer one to another. The first use seems to presuppose that the distribution of problems you'll actually face matters enormously; the second seems to deny that any such distribution can matter. I don't see how both can be right.

To make it worse in an interesting way, my officemate Priya has been pushing what she calls the fixed-exchange-rate reading, and I can't tell whether it's a clarification or a dodge. Her claim is that the Wolpert–Macready-style results are bookkeeping identities: relative to a fixed weighting over a class of problems — and only relative to it — gains on any subset of problems are exactly offset by losses on the complement, so what the theorem gives you is an exchange rate rather than a price. On that reading, she says, the philosophical action moves entirely to the weighting: if the weighting is something we discover about the world (that the problems we meet are structured, compressible, drawn from a small corner of the space of all possible objectives), then there are perfectly real lunches to be had and the theorem never said otherwise; if it's something we stipulate, the theorem is true but idle. She then adds a twist I find harder: the choice of weighting is itself a bias, so if the slogan undermines induction it undermines the choice of weighting too, and either the regress stops somewhere — in which case some assumption was legitimate after all and the second use overreaches — or it never stops, in which case nothing follows about what anyone should actually do. I've also heard that the invariance condition the result rests on doesn't hold exactly in practice, and that there are weakened "almost no free lunch" successors, which muddies things further.

What I want from you is a disambiguation, not a verdict. Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims "there's no such thing as a free lunch" could be making when used in this neighborhood — from the trivial (resources have opportunity costs; every gain has a cost somewhere) through the mathematical (averaged over the class of all objective functions, every search or learning procedure performs the same; no procedure dominates another; performance is conserved) to the epistemologically ambitious (no inductive method can be vindicated without circularity; all priors are on a par absent assumptions about the world; nothing about the past can justify a prediction about the future) — and for each reading tell me whether it is a platitude, whether it is substantive and defensible, and whether it is simply false or unsupported. Then, and this is the part I care about, say which readings would actually get my interlocutor the conclusion he wanted in each of the two arguments, and whether either use requires a premise the results themselves don't supply. In particular: does the fact that algorithms tie when averaged over all possible problems give any support at all to the claim that we have no reason to trust simplicity, given that we are not facing all possible problems — or is that inference smuggling in something like a uniform distribution over worlds? Please also flag any reading on which the slogan, applied to the problems practitioners actually encounter, is straightforwardly false. I'd like this as a list I can bring to Thursday's meeting, with each entry stating the claim, its status, and which of the two arguments it does or doesn't support; and if you think my hypothesis that the uses are in tension is the wrong way to carve it, say so and give me the better carving.
