I convene a small reading group attached to a migration-policy think tank, and for the past year two of our members have been arguing past each other about whether a state has a right to exclude would-be immigrants. One defends the discretionary view: a political community, like an association or a club, is entitled to set the terms of its own membership, and this entitlement is not exhausted by whatever good consequences exclusion happens to produce. The other argues that border controls are exercises of coercion against identifiable people and therefore stand in need of a justification owed to the excluded themselves, which in practice few actual restrictions supply. They have agreed to stop debating and to co-author a joint memo instead, addressed to our funders and to two policy staff who have never read any of this literature: roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words, no citations and no footnotes, every term of art glossed on first use, and no verdict on who is right. Please draft that memo for me, in the voice of two people signing the same document.

The memo should open with a numbered section of agreed claims: the propositions both of them can sign without hedging, including about what coercion at a border consists in, what the state's ownership or control of territory does and does not add, why the analogy between a state and a voluntary association is or is not doing load-bearing work, and what the empirical literature on wage, fiscal, and institutional effects can and cannot settle. I would like you to use one device they have already half-agreed on: decompose the phrase the right to exclude into its separate incidents — a claim-right against entry, a power to waive that right on conditions, a power to set terms after entry, a privilege of enforcing by various means, an immunity against having the whole arrangement overridden by outsiders — treat each incident as separately assertable, and let the parties sign some but not others, so that the residual disagreement is located rather than global. Then give a disagreements table. Each row should be one crux, and for each crux: the sub-question, a formulation of each side's position that that side would itself accept as fair (not the opponent's caricature), a test or case both accept as genuinely bearing on it, and each side's prediction written down in advance together with the magnitude or outcome that would count as that side being wrong. The cases I would like used as the tests, unless you find better ones: a would-be migrant who wants no social contact with citizens at all, only to trade or to transit or to live unobtrusively in a thinly populated region, since I think the associationist rationale is weakest there; an archipelago state that restricts entry to one of its islands only, which separates membership-based from territory-based rationales; a state that sells or leases its admission slots to employers or to another state, on the theory that if exclusion is property-like it should be alienable, and if it is fiduciary it should not be; a world in which only one territory remains habitable, so that exclusion leaves the excluded nowhere to go; a state that has demonstrably helped produce the conditions pushing people to move, where the question is whether the resulting duty is a duty of admission, of compensation, or of neither; and the question of what means of enforcement the right to exclude, if it exists, licenses, from paperwork to fencing to interception at sea.

A few constraints on how you write it. Where a disagreement is really about words — for instance because one side hears the right to exclude as a mere liberty to refrain from admitting while the other hears a claim-right enforceable by force — say so explicitly and give the disambiguated versions rather than staging a fake dispute. Where a crux is partly empirical, record for each side in advance what estimate would move it, so that neither can reinterpret the evidence after the fact. Where a crux is conceptual, say what kind of argument would settle it. If some of the disagreement turns out to rest on a premise about state legitimacy or about the value of political self-determination that neither party could defend within the memo's length, mark it as the point where the collaboration stops rather than smoothing it over. Finally, close with a short section on whether there is anything at all — a case, an empirical finding, a distinction — that both of them would now count as actually moving the debate, and if the honest answer is that nothing would, say that instead of manufacturing convergence.
