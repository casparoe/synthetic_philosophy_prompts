In my 19th-c political thought seminar we did two weeks on Mill and I'm still not sure what to think about the late socialism stuff. Our professor's line is that it's a direction of travel rather than a position, and that he died before he had to cash it out — which is kind of a cop-out answer, so I want to push on it ourselves. We read the Autobiography, the later chapters of the Principles where he talks about the future of the labouring classes and the stationary state, and the essays on socialism that got published after he died. So here's the setup: imagine Mill gets shown three actual proposals that are floating around now, and he has to rank them.

(A) A citizen's dividend: a public wealth fund holds equity in automated infrastructure and land rents, and every adult gets an unconditional, non-means-tested cash payment, set deliberately modest — enough to cover basic housing and food, so that full-time employment becomes optional for most people but not luxurious.

(B) A conversion escalator: any firm above about fifty workers is required, as it grows, to shift a rising share of its equity and board seats into worker-held shares that can't be sold, only held while employed, with a right of first refusal for the workers (backed by state credit) whenever the owner retires or sells.

(C) A job guarantee: the state acts as employer of last resort at a fixed wage for anyone who wants one.

Where I think he'd get interesting is the clashes with things he actually says. He thinks wage labour is a relation of dependence that a decent society outgrows, and he describes the problem of the future as combining the greatest individual liberty of action with common ownership of the raw material of the globe and an equal share in what combined labour produces. But he also thinks new property arrangements have to be tried experimentally on a small scale first because nobody yet knows whether communal ownership can sustain the required exertion, he was hostile to relief systems that create dependence and encourage population growth (he was pretty Malthusian about that), and he worries constantly about uniformity and about organization swallowing individuality. And now the twist: in all three proposals, the thing being automated away isn't just manual work but educated middle-class cognitive work, which is not something his stationary state was built for.

So: rank them as he'd rank them, and argue for the ranking from his own commitments rather than vibes. Which one does he take as the fulfillment of what he was reaching for, which one does he think is a trap, and which does he think is beside the point? Would he treat an unconditional dividend as unearned income in the bad sense or as a return on common ownership? Would he worry that (A) recreates the allowance system he hated? What's the one thing across all three he'd find genuinely alien, and what experimental evidence would he demand before endorsing any of them? If you think he'd refuse to rank them and insist on small-scale trials first, say that — but then say what he'd predict the trials would show.
