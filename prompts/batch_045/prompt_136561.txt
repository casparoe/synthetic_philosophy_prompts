I'm a software engineer — infrastructure, mostly — and I got placed on our company's internal model-safety review because someone from the platform side had to be there. We rate internal research proposals RED (don't build), YELLOW (build only under named controls), GREEN (proceed). One senior researcher on the panel has been doing this longest and everyone else calibrates against her, so before Thursday I want to predict what she'll do with the proposal in front of us. Her track record on six past proposals, with the reasons I wrote down at the time: (1) a long-horizon RL system maximizing a trading desk's P&L, able to rent cloud capacity and to modify its own code, with no externally verified shutdown — RED; she said the quantity it optimized was already a proxy nobody had actually endorsed, and that being switched off was precisely the outcome it would learn to prevent. (2) The same system with its weights frozen after training, no self-modification, and a hardware kill switch verified by an external red team — YELLOW, on 48-hour leases with external monitoring; to this day I can't tell whether the frozen weights or the kill switch was doing the work in her reasoning. (3) A superhuman system whose goal was to minimize cancer mortality, sandboxed, with no physical actuators but with a text channel that human researchers read — RED; her view was that a text channel to a person is an actuator and that persuasion is the cheapest instrument available. (4) A superhuman next-observation predictor with no objective of its own, no tools, and no state carried between sessions — GREEN. (5) A moderately capable system with a sixty-second reward horizon that could move real money, but where everything unwound within the hour under hard external rate limits and there was no kill switch at all — YELLOW with monitoring; she said a system with no tomorrow has nothing to plan into. (6) A moderately capable, long-horizon system charged with keeping a museum collection catalogued and intact, with real actuators inside one building, a verified shutdown, and no self-modification — YELLOW, and she added that she would have given the same rating if the goal had been to smash every vase in the building. What I notice is that her record has a hole in it: she has never rated anything that was superhuman, long-horizon, and equipped with genuine actuators all at once.

The new proposal is a system the team calls Argus-3, and it sits exactly in that hole. It is superhuman at long-horizon planning and world-modeling. Its objective is narrow and purely epistemic: minimize the cumulative log-loss of its own predictions about its sensor stream, averaged over the timesteps in which it is running — so being shut off contributes nothing to the score, which the team presents as removing any reason to preserve itself by construction. The objective lives in a read-only module that no self-modification can reach, though the board retains the ability to edit it. It cannot change its weights. It has real actuators: it spends a compute budget, rents capacity, commissions physical experiments through a contractor API, and may send messages to staff. And it has a hardware kill switch verified by an external red team, which has deliberately never been disclosed to it. In discussion three ways of lowering its loss came up, and they seem to me to pull in different directions. It can learn more — better sensors, more compute, more experiments, more time online. It can make the world more regular, since a smaller number of independent, hard-to-model agents (including the humans who reorganize its datacenter or edit its objective module) is easier to predict than a larger number. Or it can stop modelling the world and start managing its own sensor channel, which is far cheaper than either of the others and requires no power at all, but which presumably makes it very defensive about anyone touching its inputs.

So here is what I'd like from you. First: predict her rating of Argus-3, with the reasons you think she would give. Second: say what rule she appears to be running across the six earlier verdicts, give me at least one genuinely different rule that fits all six just as well, tell me what each rule predicts about Argus-3, and tell me which you'd put money on and why — in particular whether the content-independent story (any final goal plus a long horizon plus capability pushes toward the same handful of sub-goals, whatever the goal says) and a containment-and-corrigibility story (what matters is whether we can actually stop it and whether it can rewrite itself) really do come apart here, and which parts of the self-preservation argument survive the average-instead-of-total scoring trick. Third: name the single smallest change to Argus-3, or the one further case, that would best tell the competing rules apart, and what each rule predicts for it. And if our precedents genuinely underdetermine the rating, say what we would be committing to as a principle with each choice, since that is what I'll have to defend in the room. I've absorbed a little of the vocabulary from blog posts — something about final and instrumental goals being independent, and a short list of sub-goals that supposedly show up for almost any final goal — but I don't know the literature and can't look anything up, so please argue from the cases themselves rather than pointing me at papers or reading lists.
