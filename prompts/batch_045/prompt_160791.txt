hey, question I've been stuck on. A friend and I keep arguing about evidential cooperation in large worlds and I think we're mostly talking past each other, because we never agreed what a theory in this area would even have to do to count as a good one. So before we get to whose favourite decision theory wins: help me write the spec.

Framing so we start from the same place. Take the small-world / large-world distinction in Savage's sense: in a small world you get handed the full space of states and your action is just a map from states to outcomes; in a large world you also have to reckon with the possibility that whatever produces your decision gets produced again somewhere else — other agents, causally cut off from you, running something structurally like your decision procedure. The interesting claim is that this can change the verdict in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma: your cooperating is evidence about what those other runs do, and if the evidence is strong enough relative to the payoffs, cooperation comes out rational with no repetition, no reputation, no enforcement. Call that evidential cooperation.

What I want from you is the list of requirements any theory has to satisfy for its verdicts about cases like that to be worth taking seriously — with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and a mark on which are non-negotiable and which are really just the taste of one camp. Not the ranking; I want the standard first. I'll fix the purpose too, since adequacy presumably depends on it: I want to know whether an actual agent — one who doesn't know how many agents similar to it there are, can't inspect its own source code, and has to decide in real time — should treat evidential cooperation as a genuine reason to cooperate. Score the list against that purpose, not against some God's-eye benchmark.

Things I think have to be on the list somewhere, in no particular order:

- It has to say what the evidential link has to look like. Presumably cooperation only pays above some threshold of correlation, and the threshold should be a function of the payoff structure — I want the requirement stated so it's checkable, and I want to know what goes into it.
- It has to keep apart correlation that runs through the decision procedure being the same procedure twice from correlation that's just a common cause upstream of both agents. The worry is the smoking-lesion shape: conditioning on your own action as if it were evidence about a fixed trait you have, rather than as something that moves the other agent. Which part of that is a real requirement on the theory, and which is a slogan the causalists use to beg the question?
- It has to handle the reference class, or the graining, or whatever you want to call it: who counts as another run of you? Too wide and you're cooperating with agents whose choices carry no information about yours; too narrow and nothing ever cooperates. And it shouldn't be gerrymanderable — I shouldn't be able to flip the verdict by redescribing the situation, or by splitting one decision into a hundred and then handling them one at a time, or by choosing the level of description of my own algorithm.
- It has to have something to say when the number of similar agents is unknown, or enormous, or infinite enough that the relevant conditional probabilities stop being well defined. A theory that only speaks once you're handed the exact population isn't action-guiding, and I suspect this requirement is the one that does the most damage.
- It has to degrade gracefully when similarity is partial: networks of partial correlation rather than a tidy clique, asymmetric cases where you don't know which position you're in, cases where you can't read your own code.
- It has to survive adversarial similarity. If someone can build agents that are near-twins of you but wired to do the opposite of what they predict you'll do, a theory that cooperates on similarity alone looks exploitable. Is robustness to that a genuine desideratum, or just a prejudice of the causal/functional camp that I should strike from the list?
- It has to say whether the deliverable is a claim about rationality or a piece of engineering advice for agents who can self-modify, precommit, or get themselves built so that other agents cooperate with them. People slide between these and I want the list to force a choice.
- It should generalise past the two-person case: public-goods-ish structures where your act is evidence about many others at once, and where the correlation isn't all-or-nothing.
- It shouldn't secretly reduce to maximise total utility in a trenchcoat. I want at least one requirement that says when evidential cooperation fails on its own terms even in a very large world — otherwise I can't tell whether I've just been handed consequentialism with extra steps.

Then the part I care most about, and the reason I'm asking you rather than writing this myself: go back over whatever list you produce and flag the subsets you think can't be jointly satisfied. If the list is itself an impossibility result, say so and say what gives. Also tell me which single requirement knocks out the most candidates, and — this is the bit I'll actually argue with my friend about — which items are real desiderata and which are one camp winning by building its favourite conclusion into the definition of adequacy.

A short scorecard at the end is fine — evidential, causal, the algorithmic-identity family, and old-school superrationality, a couple of lines each — but keep the weight on the criteria.
