I am a software engineer by training with no formal philosophy background. Once a month I run a 75-minute "judgment" session for about sixteen engineers at my company: no pre-reading, no homework, a whiteboard, index cards, and a room in which at least a quarter of the people will roll their eyes at anything that looks like ethics. I want to build one session around the question of whether "better than" has to be transitive, and I want it to be an activity rather than a talk.

What I already have to work with: I can bring a set of non-transitive dice, and everyone in the room has shipped some kind of ranking system, so "if A beats B and B beats C, then A had better beat C or the sort breaks" is a hook I can use in the first two minutes. I also know the words "intransitive" and "money pump" from reading about decision theory, though I could not reconstruct the argument to save my life. The dice worry me as a route in: they show that a formally cyclic relation is coherent, but they say nothing about whether *better than* could be like that, and I don't want the session to end up being about dice.

What I want from you is a runnable plan in which the participants construct a spectrum chain themselves and only find out at the end where they have arrived. Specifically:

- The chain should be heterogeneous. Each link should trade one good-making feature away for a *different* good-making feature, rather than adding a little more of the same one. I especially do not want this to slide into a sorites or vagueness exercise whose moral is that the borderline is fuzzy; if that is where the group naturally heads, I want the design to have closed that route off in advance.
- Run the same structure twice: once in a technical frame (a system design pulled between three metrics that do not look commensurable — latency, operating cost, durability, or better ones if you have them) and once in a morally loaded frame, such as spreading a fixed quantity of on-call pain across a team. I want to see whether people's willingness to chain steps differs between the frames, and I want the debrief to say what to conclude if it does.
- After the pairwise judgments, add a trade round with small but real stakes — not money; tokens, coffee vouchers, or something comparable — and with an explicit right to refuse any trade. Arrange it so that anyone whose judgments form a cycle can be walked around the cycle and end up with strictly less than they began with, while having accepted every step. They should feel the cost rather than hear about it.
- Add a third round in which the group tries to rescue consistency by assigning numbers to the items, and runs into the fact that the numbers either contradict the pairwise verdicts they just gave or force them to accept the endpoint they resisted in round one.
- Include a standing "these are on a par" option, meaning neither better, nor worse, nor exactly equal, and for at least one pair present the identical pair inside two different comparison sets, so that we can tell whether any cycle that appears belongs to the items or to the frame they were presented in. What I do not want is a design that manufactures a cycle simply by never letting anyone say "no trade."

Nobody should hear the principle, the spectrum argument, or the money-pump idea named until after the final round.

Please give me: the materials; the exact rules and the payoff scheme for the trade round; a minute-by-minute timeline; the actual items for both frames; and a debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto the three ways out — deny transitivity, accept that the far end of the chain really is better, or deny that some of the links are relations of "better than" at all — together with what each way out costs, given what actually happened in the room. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, including what I should do if nobody produces a cycle and what I should do if someone says "just assign numbers" in round one and the whole room agrees. Finally, be honest about which part of the problem the activity cannot stage and which I would have to say out loud myself; my guess is that the gap between a cycle in what people prefer and a cycle in what they judge to be better is one of these, but tell me which.

What I want them walking out with is the sense that this is a structural problem about how step-by-step improvements aggregate, not a puzzle about fuzzy boundaries, and that each available answer costs something they can point to in what they did for the last hour.
