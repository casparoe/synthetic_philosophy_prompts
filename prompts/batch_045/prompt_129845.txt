I write the ideas desk for a magazine that runs one piece of satirical nonfiction a month, alongside reporting on science, technology and the occasional academic dispute. I would like a submission for that slot.

The target is luck egalitarianism: the family of views in distributive ethics holding that inequalities are unjust when they trace to brute luck but permissible when they trace to gambles and choices the person made and could have declined, and that a just state should therefore compensate the first and not the second, and should aim to make its citizens equal in what they are responsible for rather than in what they end up with. I want the piece to be a mock ruling of the tribunal that administers this principle.

Form: a written decision in the matter of a benefits claim, in numbered paragraphs, with the standard sections (findings of fact, analysis, disposition) and an appended guidance note addressed to future claimants. About 800 to 900 words. The voice is deadpan administrative prose: the flat procedural register of a social-security or immigration appeals tribunal, impersonal, courteous, unhurried, entirely convinced of its own reasonableness.

The humour has to come from the doctrine applied in earnest, so please build the ruling out of the following real features of the view, in roughly this order:

1. The tribunal states the governing distinction up front, in its own words, clearly enough that a reader who has never encountered the view can follow the rest.

2. A claimant who knowingly took a gamble and lost. The tribunal finds this to be option luck, declines relief, and does not flinch from or soften the fact that the claimant is left with nothing.

3. The tribunal then has to establish that the gamble was genuinely chosen, and the inquiry forces it further back into upbringing, information, temperament and neighbourhood, until the distinction it began with starts to dissolve on it. Let it go several levels down and let the dissolution be visible in the reasoning rather than announced.

4. A claimant whose needs are unusually costly to meet: a taste the person disowns and would rather not have, and a second taste the person deliberately cultivated. The tribunal takes seriously both horns of the dilemma, that compensating the costly taste subsidises what someone chose while refusing to compensate it punishes someone for what they cannot help.

5. A section in which the tribunal conducts, as ordinary procedure, an inquiry into a claimant's life that no one would want to undergo, and treats the inquiry as the respectful thing to do.

6. A remedial programme commended by the tribunal that trains claimants to want less.

Constraints: the tribunal never concedes that anything is wrong with the principle it is applying, and the piece should contain no explicit moral, no winking and no closing turn that tells the reader what to think — the reductio should be legible but unstated. Do not make the claimants comic; the comedy is in the reasoning, not in the applicants. No named philosophers, no citations or references to real cases, and no invented ones that look real: the tribunal, the statute and the claimants are all invented, but the doctrine has to be the actual doctrine rather than a straw version, so that someone who holds the view recognises it in the ruling and concedes, however uncomfortably, that it is theirs.
