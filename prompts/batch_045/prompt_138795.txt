I’m teaching a third-year seminar on the ethics of punishment and we just went over the Dennett–Caruso dustup about just deserts. You know the setup: Dennett is the compatibilist who thinks reasons-responsive agents can still deserve blame and punishment in a reconstructed, non-basic sense, and Caruso is the free-will skeptic who says basic desert is out and we should trade retribution for a public-health quarantine model aimed at prevention and social justice. I don’t need a summary of their views or any citations; assume the students already get the rough contrast. What I need is a class handout: build me a decision tree (or flowchart) that sorts someone into a position on punishment by making them answer concrete questions, not by letting them pick a label.

I’ve cooked up three cases to do the real work, and I want the branch nodes built out of these cases plus one threshold question. The cases are: the Erased Legacy case, where an offender’s memory of the crime and the desires behind it are completely erased by a medical procedure before trial, leaving a law-abiding person with no psychological continuity to the person who did it; the Reasons-Responsive Psychopath case, where an offender meets every compatibilist condition for moral agency in a calm, reflective setting but undergoes a temporary, medically verified collapse of that capacity in the heat of the moment and then commits the crime; and the Twin Sentencing case, where two offenders commit indistinguishable crimes, but one had everyadvantage and acted from malice while the other acted from the same motives after being raised in extreme deprivation that made the crime statistically near-inevitable.

Start the tree from the claim that the state is sometimes permitted to impose intentionally burdensome, condemnatory treatment on a convicted offender. If someone rejects that, send them straight to an abolitionism leaf. Then use the cases to ask plain-English yes/no or three-way questions a student can answer without knowing the position names. The splits that matter for us are: whether basic desert is coherent; whether reasons-responsiveness at some time can ground desert; whether psychological continuity to the offender matters; whether the state’s aim is censure or prevention; and whether the state must condemn or can merely quarantine. Build the tree from the logical space first: give the questions in the order a student hits them, then show the answer-paths, then drop the actual named positions into the leaves. The leaves have to include at least these five: basic-desert retributivism; Dennett-style compatibilist desert; Caruso-style public-health quarantine; pure consequentialist deterrence or incapacitation; and abolitionism. After the leaves, note any combination of answers that nobody actually occupies and say briefly why nobody is there (is it incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored?), and then flag any pair of leaves your tree can’t really seperate—I bet Dennett-style desert and a mixed desert/deterrence view collapse, so say so if they do.

Audience is undergrads, so keep every node question in plain English and put the philosophical labels only in the leaf names. Size: no more than 7 questions and at most 8 leaves. No jargon like basic desert in the questions themselves. And please don’t give me any direct quotes or citations—paraphrase everything. Hard limit: keep the entire response under 600 words, so be terse and don’t pad it with preamble.
