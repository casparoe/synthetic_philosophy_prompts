In most American cities, building anything denser than what already exists is not something an owner is entitled to do. Even a project that fully complies with the zoning code goes through discretionary review: notices are mailed to every address within a fixed radius, a hearing is held on a weeknight, the people who already live nearby speak — mostly against — a planning commission or city council weighs *neighborhood character*, shadows, traffic and parking, and the project emerges approved with conditions, sent back for revision, or dead. Broad environmental review statutes let nearly any project be challenged over its traffic or aesthetic effects, historic-district designation can freeze the exteriors of buildings with no architectural distinction individually, and minimum lot sizes and parking requirements do much the same work silently, without any hearing at all.

Two features of this arrangement puzzle me. First, the interests weighed are almost entirely those of people who already live there and, disproportionately, already own; the interests of whoever would live in the new building enter only as abstract statistics about regional housing need, because those people are not in the room, cannot be, and in many cases do not yet know they want to be there. Second, the same political culture that defends this veto as protection of a community will clear that community by eminent domain for a highway, or let it be transformed beyond recognition by rising rents and turnover, without treating either as a change of character requiring anyone's consent. The protection seems to be triggered by someone physically constructing something, not by the neighborhood's being transformed.

What theory does the practice presuppose? I mean that in several senses at once. What view of property — of what owning a house is *for* — does it require: that ownership carries a stake in the uses of nearby land, a kind of collective easement in the character of a place; or can the same veto be reconstructed more thinly, as compensation for concentrated costs, or as a defensive response to the fact that costs are local while benefits are diffuse? What view of political standing does it require: that the relevant constituency is the affected, the subjected, the present, or the enfranchised? Does it need to treat a neighborhood as something with interests of its own, over and above its residents, or is that just a way of speaking — and if it does, what are the identity conditions of a neighborhood, such that it can be *preserved* rather than merely changed? Does it presuppose that the existing pattern of who lives where is a neutral baseline, so that permitting change is the act that needs justifying and blocking it is not?

I also want to know which of these the practice strictly presupposes, which it merely fits well with, and where it is underdetermined — where several quite different theories would license the same veto, so that the practice cannot be read off as evidence for any one of them. And I want a test. Suppose a city amended its procedure in two ways: any objector must state, on the record, the compensation at which they would withdraw their objection, and the city pays it if the project proceeds; and a share of seats at the hearing is reserved for people who commit in advance to renting in the building. Which of the theories you reconstruct treat those as repairs, and which treat the first as an insult — evidence that the objection was never about a compensable cost — and the second as worthless, because the beneficiaries are not merely absent but not yet identifiable, and who they are depends on whether the building goes up? Finally: what would actually have to change in procedure and in law if a city took seriously a *right to the city* understood not as a consumer's right to find housing somewhere, but as a claim by inhabitants to participate in deciding how their city is made?
