I run an online Nomic game — the self-amending rules thing — and I've drifted into a position I want stress-tested before I build it into our rule set. The position: every paradox of legal self-reference is really an ambiguity about time, and you can kill all of them with one rule I'll call the Present Tense Rule: a rule takes effect only when its enactment is complete, and no rule may alter the conditions that had to be satisfied for its own enactment. The usual headaches — a rule that repeals the rule about how rules get repealed, a rule that makes itself retroactively unamendable, the two-rules-that-contradict-each-other-on-priority case — all look to me like they only bite because we let a change reach backwards and rewrite the procedure that produced it. Stop the reach-back and self-amendment is no weirder than ordinary amendment.

So: what would count against this? Three things mainly. First, can you hand me a concrete rule-change that passes cleanly by majority, doesn't reach backwards in any obvious sense, and still forces a contradiction or leaves genuinely undecidable whether its in force? I care most about the slow ones that only detonate a few moves later — a rule that's harmless when passed but whose effect depends on a later amendment, or on which order two independently valid proposals happen to get voted on in the same turn. Second, does my rule smuggle in something it can't have — a stable notion of when enactment is complete, a rule set that stays finite and consistent? I have a nagging feeling the Present Tense Rule has to be immune to amendment to do any work, which would put back exactly the unamendable-rule problem I thought I'd dissolved. Third, real legal orders: people make a lot of unconstitutional constitutional amendments and eternity clauses. Do those support the no-reach-back picture, or do they show legal systems doing the thing my rule forbids and getting away with it?

Also, does my rule predict anything different from the standard fix, which is to give rules a priority order and declare some of them entrenched — a case where the two approaches give opposite answers? Rank whatever you find from fatal to merely awkward, and say which bits you think someone holding my view would concede as real costs and which they'd just wave off. I'd be most moved by an actual sequence of moves in a game; abstract worries about self-reference in general move me much less. Give me an honest verdict too on whether the view has been qualified into something that can't lose, and if it has, whether that's fine here or a sign I've stopped saying anything. Skip citations and reading lists, I just want the arguments. And please write it as one continuous prose thing, like an email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
