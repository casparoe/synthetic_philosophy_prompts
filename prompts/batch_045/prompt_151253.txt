My research group is building a hand-coded corpus for the historical spread of techniques and forms. Our sources are trade manuals, apprenticeship and guild records, field recordings, court and company registries, trade catalogues, and newspaper archives, mostly nineteenth and early twentieth century. We want to model part of the corpus with replicator dynamics — heritable variation plus differential success — and we need to decide in advance which candidates to code as evolving units and which we should hand to a different framework. I have seven candidate units and three-and-a-half measures of my own devising, and I want you to rank the units by how much purchase a Darwinian model actually has on them, and to tell me where my measures break.

The seven candidates, described at the grain we would actually code:

1. A knot: the bowline, coded as a sequence of hand movements and rope passes, as it appears in rigging and sailing manuals and in the hands of working riggers.
2. A sourdough maintenance routine: feeding interval, hydration ratio, refreshment proportion, and the care of the starter itself, where part of what is transmitted is a living microbial lineage housed in flour and water.
3. A fiddle tune: a dance melody learned and passed on by ear, with no notation in the chain, coded as a melodic contour plus its idiomatic ornamentation.
4. A grammatical construction: the English be-going-to future, coded as a syntactic pattern with its semantic and pragmatic conditions.
5. A standard clause in commercial contracts: boilerplate copied more or less verbatim from one precedent to the next, with the chain visible in the documents themselves.
6. A laboratory protocol for a specific assay: reagent concentrations, instrument settings, timings, and the equipment and supply chain the protocol presupposes.
7. A reusable joke frame: a fixed setup filled with new nouns and professions, of the kind that circulates in print and conversation with constant small variation.

My measures, which I want you to use, repair, or replace:

Registry fidelity (R), 0–10: the share of a unit's type-defining features that are stored outside any single bearer — in artifacts, notation, tooling, architecture, institutional records — rather than in a person's memory or skill. High R means the lineage can survive the forgetting or death of every current bearer.

Bottleneck width (B): the smallest number of transmission events that would have to fail simultaneously for a local lineage to end, given the population we can actually observe. An apprenticeship chain running through one master is narrow; a construction carried by a speech community is not.

Reconstructive repair (P), 0–10: the probability that a learner who has encountered only the product — the tied knot, the baked loaf, the heard tune — and not the procedure, can recover a lost procedural detail well enough to keep the lineage functionally intact. High P is my attempt to capture the case where per-copy fidelity is low but lineage fidelity is high because the product itself carries the information.

Fitness decoupling (D), 0–10: how much of the unit's spread is explained by features that make it good at getting itself transmitted, as opposed to features that make it useful, cheap, or pleasant for its bearers.

For each item I also want a Darwinian purchase score, 0–100: roughly, the share of the item's observable historical dynamics you would expect a replicator model to get right, as against the share you would expect it to get wrong or be silent about.
