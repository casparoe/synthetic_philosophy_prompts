I am a novelist, and I have been asked to contribute an essay to a series in which writers of fiction write about one philosophical idea that has altered their sense of their own practice. I have chosen the notion of a hinge: those propositions, in Wittgenstein's late notes on certainty, that stand fast for us not because we have grounds for them but because they are what our giving and asking for grounds comes to an end in. My claim is that the reading of a novel has the same structure, and that the items which stand fast in reading are not facts about the fiction but the conditions under which anything can so much as count as a fact about the fiction. I have been calling them diegetic hinges.

The draft follows. After it I list the six things I most want comments on.

***

DIEGETIC HINGES

1. The complaint. When a manuscript comes back from a trusted reader with the note I stopped believing it on page 61, the sentence on page 61 is almost never the difficulty. Something has gone wrong earlier, and it has gone wrong in a way the reader cannot point to, because it is not something the reader was attending to. In my experience the failure is usually this: I have relied on something I took to be given, the reader has taken it to be unset or has taken it to be given differently, and everything downstream of that divergence is now unreadable in a way that has nothing to do with plausibility. A character in one of my books is referred to, early on, only as the boy from the ferry, and much later appears again in the town. Several readers assumed the earlier passage was a memory of someone dead. I had assumed that an event narrated in the past tense is an event in the world, and that the dead stay dead unless the text says otherwise. Neither of us was wrong about a fact in the story. We were running different frameworks, and neither of us knew we had chosen one.

2. Two ways of going wrong. Some violations are absorbed. The narrator turns out to have lied; the death was faked; the apparent stranger is the brother. The reader re-reads, keeps everything, and adds a rule. Call this surprise. Other violations are not absorbed. A text that requires you to take the same name as picking out different individuals in adjacent paragraphs, with no signal; a text in which what has been narrated as having happened is later treated as not having happened, and no frame of memory, dream, lie, or revision is offered; a text in which a door is simply both open and closed, with no irrealis dimension in play. The reader's response here is not that is false. It is the more primitive I do not know how to go on. And notice what such a reader does next: they posit a frame — it was a dream, it is a joke, the chapter is a story someone is telling — which restores the thing that broke. They repair the framework rather than reinterpret the content. That repair response is, I think, the signature of a hinge: it shows that what failed was not a claim inside the world but a condition of there being a world to have claims about.

3. Four candidates. The identity hinge: a name or description continues to pick out the same individual unless something signals otherwise. The settledness hinge: what is narrated in the past tense is settled, and is not retroactively unsettled without a signal. The actuality hinge: the furniture of the world is continuous with ours by default — handles open doors, the dead stay dead, water is wet — save where the text signals otherwise. The report hinge: the narrator's reports of speech and of spatially and temporally basic matters are trustworthy absent a signal. I do not think this list is complete or that it could be made complete, and I say so in the essay. Its incompleteness is one of my worries.

4. What these are not. They are not beliefs. One can be wrong about whether the butler did it; one cannot be wrong, in that sense, about whether the butler is still the butler. They are not, or not usefully, propositions true in the fiction: the standard apparatus of fictional truth can of course accommodate them, but it must add them by fiat, and once it has added them it represents the dead stay dead and the butler did it as items of exactly the same kind, when their roles in comprehension are utterly unlike. And they are not mere defaults or conventions, because defaults are weighed against evidence and can be defeated by enough of it, whereas these are what determine, inside the practice, what is going to count as evidence for anything. Suspense requires that the future be open and the past settled; it therefore presupposes the settledness hinge rather than being supported by it. My suspicion is that every distinctively literary response — suspense, dread, the shock of a late revelation — sits on a hinge and would be unstateable without one.

5. The test I want to defend. A genuine hinge is violated only unrecoverably; a convention or default is violated recoverably. If a surprising turn can be absorbed by re-reading the passage as ironic, dreamt, reported, or metaphorical, then what was violated was a default. If the reader's only recourse is to import a new frame from outside the passage, then what was violated was a hinge. I like this test, but I am not confident in it, for reasons I return to below.

6. Genre as hinge package. What a cover, a blurb, and an opening chapter do is not describe the world; they negotiate the package. A reader who opens a fantastic text expecting a realist one undergoes a small breakdown at the first walking corpse, then swaps packages, and the swap is not an inference — they do not weigh the evidence, they re-anchor. This, I want to say, is why genre-mixing is difficult in a way that is not merely a matter of taste or marketing: you cannot run two incompatible packages over the same region of text without localizing one of them, and localization is expensive. It also explains why the negotiation has to happen early and why getting it wrong in the first three pages is close to fatal.

7. What I concede. I do not have a principled list of the hinges, and I suspect no such list exists. The line between a hinge and a very strong convention is negotiable case by case, varies between reading communities, and varies historically: what was a hinge for a reader of mid-century realism is a convention for a reader raised on slipstream. I claim only that the distinction in role — framework versus content — is real, even when which items occupy which role is a matter of negotiation. My residual worry is that I have redescribed the ordinary, well-studied phenomenon of reading conventions in a borrowed vocabulary and called it a discovery.

***

What I would like from you:

One. Load-bearing or decorative? Wittgenstein's hinges are said to stand fast within a form of life, held in place by what we do; a reader, by contrast, sits in a chair and turns pages. If hinges are anchored in practice and reading is the least practical of practices, the analogy may fail at precisely the point where I need it. Give me that objection at its strongest, phrased the way someone who knows the late Wittgenstein well would phrase it, and tell me whether it is fatal or merely narrowing.

Two. My recoverability test in section 5. Is it workable, or does it simply relocate the problem? In particular: violations are recoverable for some readers and not for others, and readers disagree. Does that variance collapse my distinction into a sociological claim about reading communities, and if so is that a cost or a result?

Three. Where I am most exposed. The claim in section 4 that hinges are not probabilistic and are not weighed against evidence, but instead determine what counts as evidence within the practice, invites the reply that this is just a very high prior with mystique added. State that reply as strongly as it can honestly be stated, and tell me what I must concede in order to keep the thesis standing.

Four. Section 6. Should the extension to genre be kept, cut, or restricted to something narrower? My instinct is that it is the most interesting part of the essay and also the part I can support least well.

Five. The vocabulary: diegetic hinge, hinge slip, world break, hinge package, re-anchoring. I distrust jargon that earns nothing. Which of these terms do real work, and which should I abandon for plainer words?

Six. The question I hope nobody asks. Every example I have is drawn from realist fiction in the first or third person, past tense, and my positive account may therefore be nothing more than a description of that tradition's conventions wearing philosophical costume. If a commenter says this, what is the honest response — and is there a revision that would protect the essay against the charge rather than merely answering it?

Finally: if you could require exactly one revision of this draft, and one cut, what would they be?
