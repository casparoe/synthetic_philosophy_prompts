I'm a backend engineer, and for about three years I've been one of nine volunteer moderators on a large online community for programmers (roughly 12,000 members: a few busy general channels plus per-topic channels). Our code of conduct says we moderate conduct rather than opinions, but in practice the escalations that consume us are political arguments, and the accusation that gets thrown in both directions, several times a week, is that the other person is not arguing in good faith. Right now each of us decides that by feel, which means the outcome depends on which moderator is on shift and on whether the moderator happens to agree with the poster. We handle maybe four or five such escalations a week; a ban is a real cost to the person and has to be explained to them in writing and defended to a three-person appeals committee drawn from the membership; and whoever is on shift has fifteen or twenty minutes, no training in any of this, and a rule that the written explanation cannot be longer than a short paragraph. So I want something my team can actually run rather than a discussion of the topic.

The rough distinction I keep coming back to is that three quite different things get bundled together when we say someone is arguing in bad faith: insincerity, where the person is saying what they don't believe in order to win or to provoke; closed-mindedness, where they are sincere but no evidence would ever move them; and something like asymmetry, where they hold one standard of evidence and charity for their own side and a much harsher one for the other. My sense is that the third is doing most of the real work, and that it is also the one we can least see directly, because we only ever observe words. I also half-remember a point from something I listened to a while back, which I may be mangling: that a group like ours becomes an echo chamber not by holding extreme beliefs but by teaching its members to discount every source outside it, so that a person can be perfectly sincere, civil, and well-informed-by-their-own-lights and still be structurally unreachable by anything we or the other side could say. If that is roughly right it should change what the procedure looks for: not sincerity, which we cannot test, but signs that the person's beliefs are still in contact with outside sources and that their standards travel. We have improvised versions of this — asking whether the person would accept the standard they are applying if the sides were reversed, whether they have ever conceded something costly in front of their own allies, whether they can state the other side's case well enough that the other side would accept it — but we apply them inconsistently and mostly to people we already dislike.

Please turn this into a written procedure my moderators can follow in the time they have: the questions in order, what counts as a clear pass or a clear failure at each, the point at which the procedure stops and says take no action, and the point at which it stops and hands the decision to a person or to the appeals committee because the remaining question is one of values rather than evidence. Tell us explicitly which of our current tests are worth keeping and which are noise. Then run the finished procedure on three real cases from last month. First: a long-standing contributor, unfailingly polite, popular, who posts only links from one outlet, has never in three years conceded a point, and who happens to agree with me politically — I strongly suspect your procedure will tell me to treat him as not worth engaging with, and I want to see whether it does and whether I have to accept that. Second: a poster who is regularly rude and has been warned for tone, but who last month wrote a long public retraction when a study he had been citing for a year turned out to be flawed, and who argues with people on his own side at least as often as with the other one. Third: a newer member who demands that anyone disagreeing with them first produce a statement of their position strong enough that they themselves would endorse it, and who treats any failure to do so as proof of bad faith, so that the demand can be renewed indefinitely. Finish by saying plainly what the procedure cannot do — in particular whether it can separate bad faith from mere ignorance, and whether it can be turned on me and on my own side of these arguments without becoming just another way of winning them.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning and the worked cases, with no preamble and no restating of what I asked.
