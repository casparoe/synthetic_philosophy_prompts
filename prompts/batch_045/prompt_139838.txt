I have no formal training in philosophy or economics, but I have been reading about longtermism on my own for a while, and I keep getting stuck on one feature of it: the way the case for prioritizing the very long-term future gets turned into an actual funding decision, by multiplying an astronomically large number of possible future people by a very small probability of making a difference, and then comparing the result to ordinary charitable spending. What fascinates me is not the headline argument but the plumbing — all the contested questions that have to be quietly settled before the multiplication can be done at all. So I would like a piece of satire aimed at that practice.

Please write a parody Request for Proposals — a funding call issued by an invented longtermist foundation — of about 800 words, laid out in sections: a mission statement; a scoring formula with one fully worked numerical example; a numbered FAQ of five questions and answers; and a closing notice about this year's awards.

Requirements:

1. Register. Deadpan institutional bureaucratese, the voice of a well-run, self-confident grantmaker. Nothing should wink at the reader: no character may say that anything is strange, no punchlines in italics, no puns in the organization's name (a dry acronym is fine). The comedy has to come entirely from getting the reasoning right, so that someone who actually holds these views would recognize their own arguments and find the piece fair rather than a straw man. At the same time, a reader who has never heard of longtermism should be able to tell what is being mocked by the end.

2. The scoring rule. It should multiply a very large estimate of future stakes by a very small probability, and the worked example should actually multiply out correctly — the joke is the size of the resulting number, not an arithmetic slip. Show how an ordinary, well-evidenced near-term intervention scores, and have it dismissed as a rounding error.

3. The fine print. Without ever announcing that it is doing so, the document should settle at least three genuinely contested questions, each in a clause or an FAQ answer: whether preventing the loss of future lives and bringing additional happy future lives into existence count in the same way (please coin two in-house terms for these, along the lines of continuation credit and creation credit, or better ones of your own); what, if any, discount rate is applied to benefits in distant centuries; and what to do when two moral theories the foundation takes seriously disagree about which project is best — a weighted vote or internal parliament device is welcome.

4. Cluelessness. One FAQ answer should handle the objection that our long-run effects are so unpredictable that we cannot even tell whether a given intervention makes things better or worse. The foundation should absorb this deadpan, without conceding it — for instance by multiplying by a sign-confidence factor, or by reclassifying the problem.

5. The ending. The closing notice should follow the document's own logic, straight-faced, to a conclusion a reader recoils from: my suggestion is that the foundation announces it has awarded nothing this cycle and is rolling the corpus over, on the grounds that the case for waiting will only get stronger, and notes that the same argument will apply next cycle — but if you can think of a reductio that follows more strictly from the scoring rule you have written, use that instead.

Please do not cite or name real people or organizations, and do not break character with footnotes or commentary.
