I spent ten years in industry working on guidance and control for autonomous underwater vehicles before I took my doctorate in philosophy, and I now write mainly on skill and knowledge-how. Next month I am giving a colloquium to my old group, and I want to use the visit as an occasion to think again about a literature I have come to find stalled. The dispute between intellectualists and anti-intellectualists about knowing how seems to me to be running on competing intuitions about described cases, and I suspect that a good deal of what is doing the work is that the two sides are operating with different background pictures of what a piece of competence in an agent could even be. My first vocabulary may be a sharper instrument here than the one the literature uses, so I would like you to carry out a systematic translation. Please write in British English, and please do not supply references, a bibliography or suggestions for further reading; I know that literature well, and what I want from you is the structure of the argument rather than its sources.

The vocabulary I want the translation done into is the one I used daily: plant and environment; controller or policy; state estimator; closed-loop against open-loop behaviour; feedforward against feedback; world model; model-based control, including model predictive control with its online optimisation and receding horizon, against model-free or amortised policies arrived at by tuning or reinforcement learning; system identification; hierarchical architectures in which an outer loop sets setpoints for an inner one; gain scheduling and other forms of adaptation; robustness, stability margins and disturbance rejection; infeasibility and unreachability; and the difference between a controller you can certify and one you have only validated empirically. Translate into exactly that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, the following six claims: (1) anti-intellectualism, understood as the view that to know how to do something is to possess a policy that produces success reliably across the relevant range of conditions, with nothing in the account requiring an inspectable, propositionally formatted representation of how the thing is done; (2) the regress consideration that drives that view, namely that if every competent performance required consulting such a representation and then applying a rule to it, the applying would itself be a performance requiring a further consultation, so that something must be competent without consulting anything; (3) intellectualism, understood as the view that knowing how is a matter of standing in some propositional attitude to a proposition about a way of doing the thing, together with the fallback position that the relevant attitude need be neither consciously accessible nor verbally formattable; (4) the claim that knowing how, unlike mere reliable success, is factive, in that there must be a fact of the matter about how the thing is to be done; (5) the claim that what the debate is really about is guidance, so that a performance manifests knowledge-how only if the agent guides it as it goes along and not merely if something in the agent generates the correct trajectory; and (6) the claim that knowledge-how is what separates intelligent from merely successful performance, so that a superbly tuned but unguided mechanism would not count as skilled.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about: for each of the six, say what translated cleanly, what translated only after you quietly added or dropped something, and what resisted translation altogether, and say in each case whether the resistance is a gap in my engineering vocabulary or a sign that the philosophical claim was never very determinate. I have three cases I want you to run through the translation as a check on yourself, because I think they pull in different directions. First, the transfer case: a policy trained to genuine competence on one plant is deployed on a modified plant and its performance collapses in a way not predicted by anything the agent can report about what it was doing; is that best described as the absence of knowing that, and is the intellectualist committed to predicting the collapse? Second, the one-shot instruction case: a competent agent told a single true sentence about how to do something it has never done immediately does it tolerably well, which seems to show a route from proposition to policy that the anti-intellectualist must accommodate as a process of compilation whose intermediate stages are difficult to locate in time. Third, the recombination case: an agent that reliably does A and reliably does B in the settings in which it learned each cannot do A followed by B in a novel setting, even though it can state perfectly well that the novel setting calls for A and then B. Finally, tell me where you think the philosophical dispute turns out to be merely verbal once it is put in control-theoretic terms. I have in mind especially the question whether a learned latent state or a set of tuned gains is a representation with correctness conditions at all: my old vocabulary has no settled answer to that, and the philosophers seem to me to be leaning on one.
