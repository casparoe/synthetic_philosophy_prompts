I serve on a review panel that has to attach a single number to each idealization in a model dossier, so I am trying to convert some informal worries into a scale. Two stipulations before you score anything.

First, the use-profile: score every item as it would be used for genuine out-of-sample extrapolation — a policy that has never been tried, a climate state outside the instrumental record, a dose below the range anyone has measured — not for interpolation inside the sample the model was fitted to. An idealization that is harmless for curve-fitting can be lethal for extrapolation.

Second, the two features I want the score to track. (i) *Carry-through*: how much of the distortion introduced by the false assumption survives into the model's headline output once the assumption is relaxed toward realism by a realistic amount, measured against the precision the model actually advertises rather than against some absolute standard. (ii) *Correction debt*: an idealization carries debt when there is no published or straightforwardly constructible de-idealization of it, and the debt accrues interest through what I will call *assumption creep* — the tendency of downstream users, meaning the people who cite the model's numbers rather than the people who built it, to start treating the assumption as a discovery about the world instead of a simplification. High debt means the falsity is likely to be laundered into doctrine before anyone pays it back.

Score each of the following five from 0 to 10, where 10 means "the falsity costs us nothing for this use and is unlikely to be mistaken for a finding" and 0 means "the falsity is doing the work and there is no way to check it." Give one decimal place per score, allow no ties, and order the list from highest to lowest.

1. Point masses and a frictionless plane, in the derivation of the law of the inclined plane — an assumption everyone knows how to relax, but which the result depends on essentially.
2. Infinite population size with random mating and no mutation, in a population-genetics model whose headline output is the rate of loss of heterozygosity — bearing in mind that the same qualitative result falls out of several structurally quite different models.
3. A single representative agent with rational expectations, in a dynamic stochastic general equilibrium model used to forecast the effect of a monetary rule that has never been implemented.
4. Perfect information and unconstrained optimization, in an optimal-foraging model predicting the prey choice of a real predator that demonstrably computes nothing — the classic as-if case. In this item specifically, say whether whatever makes it score as well as it does also transfers to item 3.
5. A smooth, monotone damage function relating global mean temperature to aggregate economic loss, in an integrated assessment model whose headline output is a social cost of carbon.

Reply as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each: the score, then the single consideration that most determined it. No introduction, no conclusion.
