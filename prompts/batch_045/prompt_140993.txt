Say Hegel somehow gets dropped into a defense-policy workshop in 2030, and two things get put in front of him.

One: a real world federation — not a league of states with no teeth, but an actual sovereign with courts, a standing army and the power to overrule member states, and it's kept the peace for forty years.

Two: almost all the fighting that sovereign does is done by autonomous drones and cyber ops. An operator sitting in one country can wreck another country's power grid, its banks and its air defenses without anyone on her side being in physical danger, and without any soldier ever facing another soldier.

So what's his reaction? I care about the bits of his actual position that strain here: that war is what stops a people from settling into comfortable inward-looking stagnation, that the willingness to risk death is what makes recognition between states more than talk, and that a federation of states was always going to be hollow because it has no genuine sovereign over the members. Does he grudgingly aprove of the world sovereign once it has real teeth, or does he say it's produced a soft, stagnant humanity that's lost something it needed? And does drone-and-cyber conflict still do whatever work he thought war did once nobody on the winning side is risking anything — is it closer to a police action, or to a master who won't stake his life and so can't really be recognized? Where does he land, and which of his own commitments does he have to drop to get there?
