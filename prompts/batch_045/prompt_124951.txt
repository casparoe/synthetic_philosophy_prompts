I chair the grants committee of a small private foundation, and I need a short memo I can circulate to the other four members before we vote on Friday. Please write it in American English. I'd rather have argument than apparatus: no citations, no literature review, no footnotes, no reading suggestions — just the reasoning, stated plainly enough that a trustee with no philosophy background can follow it and act on it.

Here is the case. Two staff analysts — call them R. and M. — were asked to assess the same cost-effectiveness estimate for a conditional cash transfer aimed at keeping girls in secondary school in one province. R. puts roughly 75% on the program's having a genuine, policy-relevant effect of the advertised size. M. puts roughly 25%, on the grounds that the estimate rests on a cluster-level analysis with very few clusters and would mostly wash out under a different specification. Their track records on comparable questions look equally good: similar Brier scores across a couple hundred resolved questions about whether social-program results hold up, similar habits of updating, and no one on staff has caught either of them reasoning sloppily. There are two complications. First, M. wrote second, having read R.'s memo, and moved up from 15%; R. has not seen M.'s. Second, they disagree about whether they are peers at all: R. says most of M.'s calibration comes from easier questions, and M. says R. is inside-viewing this particular program. The committee has now read both memos and is split the same way, two and two, with me holding the deciding vote. So we are in a disagreement about a disagreement, and whatever rule we adopt had better be one we can also apply to ourselves.

The live options are: fund the full three-year program now; decline; fund a scaled-down version as a literal splitting of the difference; or spend a modest fraction of the money now on an independent re-analysis plus a small fast pilot, with a decision rule pre-committed in advance, and decide at the next cycle. The last option costs about six months and one cohort of beneficiaries. Note also that the program looks like it has a threshold — half the money plausibly buys much less than half the effect — so conciliation at the level of credence may not translate into conciliation at the level of the decision.

Please recommend one course of action, with a time frame where one is needed, and give the reasoning. I want the memo to take a real position on the underlying epistemic question rather than managing it procedurally: when apparent peers disagree, does the fact of disagreement itself move the committee's credence, and if so toward what — the midpoint, the more cautious end, or a widening of uncertainty that shows up somewhere other than the point estimate? I especially want an honest steelman of the strongest thing that can be said against whatever you recommend. Two objections I'd like addressed head-on: first, that a standing policy of splitting the difference looks as though it should tell us to stop splitting the difference once we notice that informed people disagree about splitting the difference; second, that whichever option we pick, the same two analysts will read whatever new evidence we buy, and will likely disagree about it in the same direction as before.
