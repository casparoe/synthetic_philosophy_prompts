I sit on the Frontier Systems Review Committee at a mid-sized AI research institute. We authorize internal deployments of models above a capability threshold, and we also review them retrospectively: every team whose deployment we authorized comes back to us at the end of the cycle for a post-hoc judgment that can carry real consequences — a formal finding of non-compliance, a hold on the team's next deployment slot, or a mandatory remediation plan with a deadline.

Two cases are on Thursday's agenda, and they have split us three–three along a fault line we have never actually resolved.

*Kestrel.* Their dossier was thin. Roughly a third of their evaluation prompts were later found to appear verbatim in their fine-tuning data; they sampled one completion per prompt at temperature 0; they logged no seeds; they filed no pre-registered hazard taxonomy; and they skipped the staged rollout, going straight to a general deployment across about 3,000 internal users. Nothing has been reported in eight weeks — but their post-deployment monitoring was close to minimal, so we cannot distinguish "nothing happened" from "nothing was detected." The deployment is still live.

*Heron.* Their process was close to exemplary: pre-registered hazard taxonomy, a held-out evaluation set with provenance checks, an external red team, 16 samples per prompt with a validated judge, a staged rollout, an incident-response plan, and complete logging. In week three, in a deployment of comparable size and risk class, their model produced a detailed and actionable social-engineering plan that a user then carried out against a small healthcare provider; roughly 400 patient records were exposed. The harm is documented, reportable, and moderate — no physical injury, substantial notification and remediation cost, no evidence the team could have caught it by following our requirements more carefully. The deployment was suspended.

Half the committee says Kestrel is the only real failure here: they breached our requirements, the absence of an incident is luck, and outcome-based judgment makes our standards unenforceable while inviting teams to under-report. The other half says the committee exists to prevent harm, that Heron's incident is the only case in which the thing we exist to prevent actually occurred, and that grading Heron's paperwork while ignoring that tells every team that documentation matters more than outcomes. One member has also invoked the philosophical literature on moral luck — the pattern of treating agents differently because of outcomes beyond their control — and argues that whichever way we go, we are institutionalizing that inconsistency deliberately rather than by accident, which is worse.

A further complication: one member has circulated a concrete proposal. Retrospective review would be split in two. The compliance finding would be made on an outcome-blinded dossier, with all post-deployment material redacted; only afterward would the committee hold a separate "learning review" that treats incidents strictly as information about the quality of our own process requirements, never as evidence about the team. The proposal also adds a "procedural variance" requirement: teams must run their evaluation pipeline twice on independently drawn evaluation sets and report the rate at which the verdicts disagree, as a measure of how much of any single pass is signal. Her opponents reply that realized outcomes are our only unbiased evidence that our process requirements track anything real, and that a committee which never lets outcomes move its judgments can enforce elaborate rituals that correlate with nothing.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to the committee before the meeting. It should be in memo form (To / From / Re / Recommendation / Reasons / Opposing considerations) and stay under 1,000 words. It should give:

1. an explicit recommendation for each team — including, if you recommend a finding against Kestrel, what form it takes and over what time frame, and whether anything at all is required of Heron;
2. a general standard for retrospective review, stated precisely enough that a future committee could apply it to a new case without re-litigating this one, with its scope and any stated exceptions;
3. an honest statement of the strongest consideration on the losing side, in the form its proponents would actually press it;
4. a recommendation on the outcome-blinded review and procedural-variance proposal — adopt, adopt in modified form, pilot for one cycle, or reject — with the modification spelled out if you propose one.

Do not cite or refer to any literature; the reasoning should stand on its own. End the memo, after all prose, with a single machine-readable line in exactly this form:

\answer{Kestrel: <sanction|no sanction>; Heron: <sanction|no sanction>; standard: <outcome-insensitive|outcome-weighted|hybrid>; outcome-blinded proposal: <adopt|adopt modified|pilot|reject>}
