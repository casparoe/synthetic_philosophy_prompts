I'm in a third-year seminar on Descartes this term — the Meditations and the fifth part of the Discourse, plus a few of the letters — and my paper is on animal minds. Everyone in the room agrees that Descartes denies thought to the beasts, but we keep disagreeing about what rule he is actually applying, and the disagreement only becomes visible when we take that rule to a case he never considered. I want the rule stated precisely enough to be applied. Here is the track record as our reading notes reconstruct it, and then the cases I want adjudicated.

Verdicts on the record:

1. A magpie or parrot trained to utter words at the right moments: no thought. Production of the right sound in the right circumstance is explained by the disposition of the vocal organs, not by what the bird means.
2. A dog that learns tricks for rewards, whines when struck, and tracks its master by scent: no thought. Our notes also record him conceding sensation of a merely bodily kind while denying that any of this involves thought, so pain behaviour is not evidence for a mind on his view.
3. An ape or monkey that imitates our actions more exactly than we imitate theirs: still no thought. Imitative success is, if anything, a mark of mechanical operation, and he adds that a creature that thought as we do would also use signs to declare its thoughts.
4. A person born deaf and mute who invents signs of his own and makes himself understood to those around him: a mind. This is the case he uses to block the excuse that the beasts fail only for want of the right organs.
5. A madman whose imagination is disordered and who reasons badly: a mind. He is a man; the defect is not in the rational soul but in the body and its dispositions.
6. A newborn infant, or someone in a deep faint, who shows no sign of reasoning at all: a mind, for the same reason.
7. A clockwork figure that walks, eats, plays a tune and reacts when touched: no thought. He supplies two tests by which we could always tell such a machine from a real man — it could never arrange words or signs to declare its thoughts to us, and though it might do some things as well as or better than we do, it would infallibly fail at others, which would reveal that it acts from the disposition of its organs rather than from knowledge.
8. A machine that does one thing superlatively and nothing else: no thought, by the second test.
9. God and the angels, with no body and no organs: they think, and more perfectly than we do.
10. An explicit constraint he insists on: the beasts do not merely have less reason than we do. Reason does not come in degrees across a species; either there is thought or there is none.

What I want from you:

First, state the rule, or the package of rules, that best fits these verdicts, in a form that could be applied to a case he never saw. Be explicit about which components are supposed to be constitutive of thought and which are merely evidence for it — I take that distinction to be where our seminar's disagreement really lives, since the madman and the infant seem to show that a being can fail the test and still have a mind.

Second, give me at least one alternative rule that fits the whole track record just as well. I want to see the underdetermination rather than one tidy reconstruction.

Third, tell me what each rule predicts about the following, and say where the rules come apart:

(a) An ape taught a large stock of arbitrary signs that uses them in combinations it was never taught — to ask for what it wants, to name things that are absent, and to refuse what it is offered. Does his remark about imitation settle this case, or only a weaker one?
(b) A machine that responds appropriately to whatever is said in its presence, across any domain, and that does not act from any fixed arrangement of organs — something that satisfies both of the tests he offers. Does he owe us the verdict that it thinks, or were those tests intended as a claim about what no machine could ever do?
(c) A human child raised from infancy with no exposure to language, who uses no signs and displays no flexible behaviour.
(d) A being with no body, no organs and no behaviour at all.
(e) A colony of insects that collectively solves problems no single member can, by signalling among its parts.

Where the rules disagree, give your best guess and the reason for preferring it. Then name the single further case that would do the most to tell the rules apart — I suspect it is not any of mine but something that separates a creature which merely signals its wants from one that asserts something about an absent object and then corrects itself.

Two constraints I would like respected. Keep the immortality argument separate from the behavioural one: the claim that if the beasts thought they would have immaterial souls as we do, and that this is highly improbable, seems to me to be doing independent work, and I want to know which verdicts would change if it were dropped. And please say plainly where his apparatus yields no verdict at all; I would rather be told that than be handed a confident answer for which he had no resources.
