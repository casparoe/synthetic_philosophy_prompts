I spent a career in materials science and have lately been going to a village reading group that works through philosophical topics one evening a month. I have been asked to take the next session on the disagreement between endurantism and perdurantism. The audience is about a dozen retired engineers, teachers and doctors, with no formal philosophy among them; two have read a good deal of popular physics and will arrive convinced that special relativity has already settled the question in favour of the four-dimensional picture; one, a retired clergyman, will arrive convinced that the two views say the same thing in different words and that the quarrel is therefore empty. I have one hour, no handout, and a blackboard.

Please do not explain the distinction to me. Plan how I should explain it. I want the sequence of moves and the reason for the order: what has to be on the board before the disagreement can even be stated so that it does not sound like a quarrel about vocabulary, whether the datum that a single thing persists through change in its own intrinsic properties should be introduced before or after the two accounts are described, where the argument from temporary intrinsics should enter, and how the question of what is happening to an object is to be kept separate from the question of whether past and future times are as real as the present, given that my audience will conflate the two within the first ten minutes.

I would like to build the session around one example rather than several. My candidate is a single crystal of quartz in a sealed capsule, carried up through the alpha–beta transition a little under six hundred degrees and then back down again: same silicon and oxygen throughout, nothing added or removed, sharp crystal faces giving it non-arbitrary boundaries at every moment, an intrinsic property — symmetry, and with it density — that differs above the transition and below it, and one and the same specimen recovered at the end. Tell me whether that carries the whole hour or whether it fails somewhere I cannot see, what such an example must have and must lack to do this work, and why the stock examples — a burning candle, an ageing person, a performance of a symphony, a ship with replaced planks — do less work; if one of them belongs later in the hour as a test rather than at the start, say which and where.

Then give me the misconception map: the errors my audience will bring or will form during the session, including the ones I have already named and the ones I have not thought of, and for each the exact point in the sequence at which it should be headed off rather than merely corrected at the end. I expect, among others: that perdurantism denies that anything really changes; that a temporal part is a spatial part laid out along an extra direction; that endurantism is the view that only the present is real; that relativity forces the four-dimensional picture; that if the same facts can be described either way then nothing is at stake. Say which one I should deliberately let surface and then turn to use, and which I should prevent from arising at all.

Finally: one question I can put to the group in the last five minutes whose answer would show me they have understood the disagreement rather than learned its vocabulary, and a short list of things I should openly refuse to go into at this level — branching or fission cases, the relativity argument, constitution, whether the dispute is substantive — and one line of apology to each for the omission.

Write your answer as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, no numbered lists.
