I have agreed to give the commencement address at the engineering college where I did my undergraduate degree — a seven-minute slot, three weeks from now. About fifty of the six hundred graduates are the first cohort of a machine-learning concentration; the rest of the class is mechanical and civil engineering. The bleachers will be full of families who have never had occasion to think about any of this and who will be moved by anything warm-sounding. My father will be in the second row: he rebuilt diesel engines for forty-one years and cannot tell you a single thing he knows. I will not use him as an illustration and I will not condescend to him, but I also cannot give the speech as though his forty-one years were a lesser thing than what the person two seats away is about to begin.

What I want the address to carry is the pair of inversions — Darwin's, in which the appearance of design is explained by a process with no designer in it, and Turing's, in which the appearance of understanding is explained by machinery that understands nothing — and I want them to do structural work rather than decorate the thing. That rules out the two lines the genre keeps handing me: that these graduates now understand their field, which the Turing inversion makes cheap, and that understanding was never what they thought it was, which my father would hear correctly as an insult and which I think is false anyway. It also rules out warning them about the machines and flattering them as the generation that will handle the machines.

The best I can do by way of a positive criterion is this: to understand is to be the kind of thing whose failures are informative — whose wrong answers narrow the search rather than merely being wrong — and whose claims can be checked against something other than their fluency. I am not sure the criterion survives my father. When an engine would not turn over, his wrong guesses narrowed the search beautifully, and if that is all comprehension comes to, then he had it without a word for it, and the machine-learning cohort has spent four years with something that fails my own test.

Write the address. If you think no seven-minute commencement speech can carry this without either condescending to the room or dissolving into the fluent noise it is complaining about, say so plainly and tell me what I should give instead — and in either case, tell me the one sentence you would cut and why.
