hey — could you write me something for a handout? Context: I'm TAing the undergrad Wittgenstein seminar this term (third time, and every year at least one person writes an essay saying that if there's no fact about what I mean then nothing means anything at all, wich drives my advisor up the wall), and we've just finished the rule-following material: the skeptical paradox, and the so-called skeptical solution where meaning gets cashed out in terms of what a community is prepared to endorse rather than in terms of truth conditions, with the deviant rival function doing the work — the one that coincides with addition on every sum anyone has ever actually performed and then returns 5 once the numbers get big enough that no one's been there. A friend in university HR let me skim their real compliance handbook and it's written in this uniquely bloodless register — "the University is satisfied that...", "staff are reminded that..." — and I thought it would make a good container for the view, because the whole point is what happens when a bureaucracy takes it seriously.

So: write me 700–900 words of an internal guidance document — a tax authority, a permits office, whatever you prefer — for an organization that has officially adopted the skeptical solution as its operating doctrine. Numbered sections with sub-clauses, a couple of footnotes, flat cheerful institutional voice, no winking and no footnote that explains the joke. I'd like it to cover: (1) a definitions section that retires "correct" and "incorrect" from official use in favour of endorsability vocabulary, on the grounds that nothing an employee does could make an answer the right one; (2) a worked case — a clerk who has only ever performed small sums is asked for 68 + 57 (or pick your own pair) and returns 5 — with guidance on how he is to be handled given that he cannot be said to have gone wrong, and with his protestation that he *would* have answered 125 explicitly dismissed as the "Dispositional Excuse" (no finite run of past behaviour settles what he meant, and a disposition can't tell anyone what he ought to have said); (3) provisions for staff who work alone — night-shift archivists, remote inspectors, lighthouse keepers — and so have no community on hand to do the endorsing; (4) the question of who endorses the endorsers, which the document must close off in a way that plainly doesn't work (a cap, a sunset clause, a line about pending funding) without ever using the word "regress"; and (5) a rule that supervisors may not tell staff what a regulation requires of them, only what their colleagues will accept going forward. Throw in one short pull-quote from an internal dissent memo by a self-described holdout who insists there's just a plain fact of the matter about what the rule requires, plus the document's footnote brushing him off.

The comedy has to come entirely from getting the position right — the loss of error, the normativity problem, the finiteness point, the pressure toward an endless hierarchy of endorsers — so that someone who knows the literature laughs instead of wincing; if a joke needs explaining, cut it. And please don't turn the clerk into a rebel hero: the unsettling thing is precisely that the doctrine has no resources for calling him wrong, and that should land as unsettling rather than triumphant. Ideal outcome is that a first-year reads it, enjoys it, and only afterwards notices they can't say what's funny. Definately keep it reading like something I dashed off between office hours rather than a polished piece.
