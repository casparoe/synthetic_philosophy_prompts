I host a small philosophy podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher who's just published a book reviving Moore's organic-unity axiology: the two great intrinsic goods are the enjoyment of beauty and personal affection, we know this by intuition rather than argument, and the value of an organic whole isn't the sum of the value of its parts. Her one genuinely new move is a "replacement test" instead of Moore's isolation test: to find the value of a friendship, don't imagine it alone in an empty universe, imagine swapping this friend for the most agreeable available substitute while holding your own mental states fixed, and see what's lost.

Draft me seven or eight questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. I want at least one on the "no owner" problem: if the surplus value of the friendship-whole isn't experienced by either friend, who is it good for — and if her answer is that value needn't be value-for anyone, push on what practical work "good" is still doing. At least one on her own bullet-biting claim that beautiful objects nobody ever sees add almost nothing to a world's value: does that quietly make her a pure experientialist, and if so why keep beauty on the list of intrinsic goods at all? One on scope: if the method delivers beauty and friendship, why not parenting, achievement, or religious devotion, and what stops the method from being a blank cheque for whatever a particular class finds tasteful? One that turns the organic-unity machinery loose on evil — if wholes generate surplus value, can a friendship that involved betrayal and reconciliation be more valuable than an equally close one that didn't, and does she bite that bullet too? And please end with one question where her favourite deflection, roughly, that intuition is the datum and there's nothing further to argue about, stops being available to her — plus one about what would actually count as evidence against the view.
