I'm a TA in an introductory epistemology course, and next week I have a single fifty-minute section in which to teach Fitch's paradox of knowability to a mixed group: mostly first-years who have just met the verificationist thought that truth cannot outrun what we could in principle come to know, plus a cluster of mathematics and computer-science students who will not tolerate vagueness about the quantifier step. Do not explain the paradox to me; give me a plan for explaining it.

I want the order in which the components have to arrive and what each depends on: how much anti-realist motivation has to be on the table before the derivation carries any force at all, and whether the factivity and conjunction-closure assumptions about knowledge should be stated up front or elicited from the students at the moment they are needed. I want the one example that should carry the derivation from start to finish, together with what such an example must have and must lack — it has to be a truth everyone will grant is knowable in principle and unknown in fact, and it must not be one whose whole interest is that it is unknowable, nor one that quietly imports self-reference. Then I want the misconceptions mapped onto the sequence, with the point at which each should be headed off: that the argument has proved there are unknowable truths; that it refutes realism; that the contradiction at its centre is just Moore's paradox or the surprise-examination paradox in a new hat; and, the one my computer-science students will press, that the derivation requires us to have identified an unknown truth rather than merely to know that there is one.

Finally, give me the single question I should end with, to test whether the derivation itself has landed rather than only its conclusion, and a verdict on what I should openly refuse to cover in fifty minutes: my instinct is to pass over the intuitionistic and restricted-knowability responses in silence, and I want to know whether that silence would make the result look more decisive than it is.
