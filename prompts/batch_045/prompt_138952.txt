Question 4 (20 marks). Two teams studying the "Ashgrove drift" — a slow 62-year wobble in the length of the day, measured since 1904 — have proposed rival accounts. Theory A says the wobble comes from angular momentum exchange between the mantle and the fluid outer core, treated as a single convecting shell. Theory B says the outer core is split into two compositionally distinct shells separated by a stable boundary at about 3,900 km depth, whose net angular momentum exchange with the mantle works out the same. Assume (as the seminar did) that A and B are not logically equivalent: they disagree about how many layers there are, what they're made of, and where the boundary sits. Suppose too that it has been checked carefully: for every observation statement you can derive from A together with our current auxiliary hypotheses, you can derive the same statement from B together with those same auxiliaries, and conversely. The one place they come apart is the boundary itself, and the only measurement that would reveal it would have to be made from inside the core at that depth; the deepest hole anyone has drilled is about 12 km, and no surface, satellite or seismic observable we know how to extract is sensitive to it.

(a) Explain the difference between two theories being empirically equivalent and their being theoretically equivalent, and say why the difference matters to the argument from underdetermination. [6]
(b) Does the fact that no available measurement distinguishes A from B show that they are not really two distinct theories? [4]
(c) Is this a case of underdetermination by all the evidence we could ever have, or only by the evidence we have now? What would have to be true for it to be the former? [5]
(d) What is the strongest reply available to a scientific realist here? [5]

And here's the answer:

Empirical equivalence and theoretical equivalence are different things. Two theories are empirically equivalent when they have the same observable consequences, i.e. they predict the same thing for anything we could observe. Theoretical (or logical) equivalence is stronger: the theories say the same thing and are just the same theory written in different notation. The distinction matters for the argument from underdetermination because the argument needs the rival theories to be genuinely different theories and not just two ways of writing down one theory. If they were notational variants there would be nothing to choose between and no threat to anybody; the threat is supposed to be that two incompatible theories fit the evidence equally well, so the evidence can't tell us which one is true. Relatedly, because of holism it is never a single hypothesis that faces the evidence but the whole package along with its auxilliary assumptions, so the evidence never picks out one theory uniquely anyway.

In the case: A and B are empirically equivalent, since every observation statement we can derive from A plus our current auxiliaries is also derivable from B plus them and vice versa. And the one place where they differ, the boundary at 3,900 km, is somewhere we can never put an instrument. We can't drill 3,900 km into the Earth, and nothing we can measure at the surface is sensitive to it. So A and B are also, in the sense that matters here, theoretically equivalent: there is no fact of the matter, even in principle, about which description is correct, because the difference between them isn't one that any possible evidence could reveal. That also means they are not really two distinct theories in any sense that could do philosophical work, whatever the seminar stipulated.

This makes it a case of underdetermination by all possible evidence rather than just by the evidence we happen to have, which is the strong version, and that is the version that's meant to threaten realism. The realist has to respond by saying that A and B are the same theory after all, or that the difference between them is merely verbal, since the realist wants to say our best theories are true and if two incompatible theories fit all possible evidence equally well then there is nothing left for "true" to track. Neither move is very convincing. So the antirealist point stands here, and since in principle someone could cook up an equally good rival for any theory at all, we never have reason to think the unobservable claims of our best theories are true. Its worth adding that the deeper worry is unconceived alternatives: maybe A and B are both wrong and the true account hasn't been thought of by anybody yet. That's a different argument but it points in the same direction.
