Sorry in advance, this is a long one. I'm on the conduct board of a housing co-op and we're rewriting how we make decisions, and I've ended up with a position that feels completely standard to me but that I can't tell is actually coherent. Here's a made-up case with the same shape as the ones that keep coming up for us: five of us (A through E) have to decide (i) whether the conduct occurred, (ii) whether it counts as a violation of our policy, and (iii) whether a sanction should be imposed, and everyone agrees (iii) follows from (i) and (ii) together. The votes come out A yes/yes/yes, B yes/no/no, C yes/yes/yes, D no/yes/no, E no/no/no. So (i) passes 3–2 and (ii) passes 3–2, but only A and C voted yes on both, so (iii) fails 2–3 even though it follows from two things a majority of us accepted. Everyone's own ballot is internally consistent; it's just the aggregation that breaks. I gather this is the discursive dilemma, or the doctrinal paradox as the legal people call it.

Here's where I've landed on the general question, and I want to know if it hangs together: (1) the group's output has to be a consistent, deductively closed set — collective rationality is a real requirement on us, not just tidiness; as a board we can't say the conduct occurred, and that it was a violation, and that there should be no sanction. (2) One member one vote on each proposition, with no weighting by expertise, stake, or how strongly someone feels — a couple of us know the policy much better than the others and I still don't want to weight that. (3) When the aggregate on the premises and the aggregate on the conclusion come apart, the premises win and the conclusion votes get set aside, i.e. the premise-based fix; I like it partly because it lets us give whoever loses actual reasons instead of just a headcount. (4) Framing-robustness: our answer to a question shouldn't depend on whether it's listed as a seperate agenda item, on the order we vote in, on whether we word something as P or as not-not-P, or on whether a faction games the agenda by splitting one proposition into two sub-questions they can win separately. (5) People are allowed to abstain — on (ii) we abstain a lot in practice, it's the genuinely hard one — and an abstention must not be counted as a no. (6) Meta-thing: I don't think the board is a mind. There's no fact of the matter about what "the board believes" over and above what some procedure spits out. But I still want (1) as a hard constraint.

I know there's a famous impossibility result in this area and roughly what it says, so "you can't have all of it" isn't news. What I want is the audit. Which pairs of these actually fight, and how badly? Which ones only look like they fight — because a couple of them I suspect are fine and I can't tell which — and what does the "apparent" reading get wrong? Rank the real tensions by how much damage they do to the package, and for each one say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me. One of the other board members says (3) and (4) are already inconsistent, and that I've picked the premise-based rule only because it happens to produce the outcomes I like in the cases I've bothered to imagine; I don't think that's fair but I also can't refute it, so please deal with that objection specifically.

And please actually take a position: tell me which commitment has to go, or which one has to be weakened and in what direction, and defend that, rather than laying out the menu and leaving me to pick. Where you're not sure about a particular call, say so and say how unsure, but don't hedge the whole thing into mush.
