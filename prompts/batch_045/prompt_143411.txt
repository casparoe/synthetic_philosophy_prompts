I write a long-form column on law, the behavioural sciences and the social sciences for a weekend magazine, and I am currently working on a feature about precommitment: the devices by which a person or a polity ties its own hands in advance. The piece moves between the psychiatric Ulysses contract, the fiscal rules that several European states wrote into their constitutions in the last two decades, and the broader puzzle of founders who bind successors who never agreed to anything. Separately, I am guest-teaching a master's seminar on law and behavioural science this spring and I have agreed to set the final paper. I would like the exam question to do double duty as a way of pressure-testing my own framing, so I would rather have one hard question than three safe ones. The students will have read and discussed the standard material on precommitment and weakness of will, so the question cannot be answered by restating the story of Odysseus and the mast, nor by asserting in general terms that future generations did not consent. It should be answerable in ninety minutes in an exam room with no library and no internet, so it must be fully self-contained and must not reward anyone for having read more than the seminar readings; I do not want citations anywhere in the model answer.

The question I have in mind concerns a fictional jurisdiction. Suppose the Republic of Marenne, after a sovereign debt crisis, entrenches a Stability Lock: a cap on the structural deficit; a requirement that any law breaching the cap be passed by a two-thirds majority and then confirmed by a referendum held no fewer than sixty days later; certification of breaches and of emergency exemptions by an independent Fiscal Guard whose nine members serve single twelve-year terms and can be removed only for incapacity; and a rule that the Lock itself may be amended only by a three-quarters majority plus referendum. Twenty-five years later, three things are true at once. First, a once-in-a-century epidemic leads the government to seek a large emergency borrowing, and the Fiscal Guard deadlocks on whether an epidemic falls within the exemption, which was drafted with armed conflict and natural catastrophe in view. Second, parliament has repeatedly attached new protected spending mandates to the Lock but has never relaxed the cap, so the constraint now bites considerably harder than it did at enactment. Third, a province that acceded to the union several years after the Lock came into force now returns a majority in favour of renegotiating its fiscal arrangements and argues that it never bound itself. I have been calling these, respectively, the wrong-siren problem (the device fires in circumstances it was not built for, rather than being merely resisted by the very temptation it was built for), the ratchet asymmetry, and the borrowed-mast problem. There is also the question of who plays the crew: in the original story, the enforcers are themselves immune to the song, whereas the Fiscal Guard is an artificial agent with its own institutional interests that can, as here, fail to speak with one voice.

What I want is the crux, not the summary: what exactly has to be true of a precommitment for binding the later agent to count as an extension of that agent's autonomy rather than a restriction of it, and does that account survive misfire, ratcheting, inherited obligation and delegated enforcement — or does it turn out that every design fix (sunset clauses, broadly worded emergency certification, periodic reauthorisation, symmetric amendment thresholds) reopens the door to the self-serving redescription the device was meant to defeat? Please give me the question exactly as it should appear on the paper, with the hypothetical written out cleanly and any technical term defined in the question itself; then a model answer of roughly seven to eight hundred words; then a marking scheme out of twenty-five built around five must-hit points, with the marks for each and a one-line note on how candidates typically get that point wrong; then a short note on what separates an A from a B answer, since I expect the strongest answers to do something with the tension between revisability and rigidity rather than merely listing considerations. Where a mark depends on a judgement about which reasonable people would disagree, please flag it so I can adjust the weighting before I hand the paper to the other examiner.
