I record a small, slightly over-long podcast on metaphysics for listeners who already know a bit of it, and in a fortnight I'm interviewing a philosopher of physics — she's asked me not to be named in the blurb, so let's call her Professor Halloway — who's just published a book defending what she calls orientation absolutism: the claim that left-handedness is a genuine intrinsic, monadic, irreducible property of objects, not a relation to us or to a paradigm hand or to a convention, and that Kant basically settled this in his 1768 essay on the ground of the difference between regions in space, with his incongruent counterparts: a left and a right hand agree in every intrinsic magnitude and in all their internal relations and still can't be brought to coincide, so no relationalist story about space can account for the difference. I remember the examples being hands and ears and — I think — snails whose shells wind the other way, but don't hold me to that. What I want from you is ten or twelve questions that don't just let her restate the view.

The bit I most need help with is her escape route. Every time anyone brings up the actual physics she says something like "that's a fact about the notation of our theories, not about handedness", and sails on, and I've never found a way past it. So I've been reading up so I dont sound like an idiot: as I understand it, Wu's cobalt-60 experiment (done in 1956) found that the electrons from the beta decay come out preferentially in one direction relative to the spin of the aligned nuclei, so the weak interaction isn't mirror-symmetric, which gives a way of operationally defining left and right that needn't presuppose any shared object — but that on its own arguably doesn't settle it, because if CP were conserved then the mirror image of the whole set-up would still be a physically possible process, just one involving antimatter, and it was Cronin and Fitch's 1964 result on neutral kaon decay that broke that last symmetry. Tell me if I've got any of that wrong, and then build me a run of three or four questions that start by granting her the notation point and make it progressively harder to lean on: does her view really have no stake in whether the laws are handed; would a parity-symmetric world be one in which there's no fact of the matter about which hand is which; and if she insists the metaphysics is independent of the physics, then what would a world have to be like for there to be no fact of the matter — a single handed object and nothing else, say, or two objects that are mirror images of each other and nothing else — and what does absolutism say in those cases?

A few other things I'd want covered, each with a follow-up keyed to whatever she'd most likely answer: the question that got me into this in the first place, which is what holds each side together — that all left hands are congruent with all other left hands, all left gloves fit all left hands, congruence among handed things is transitive and sorts everything into exactly two classes — is that a further fact standing in need of explanation, and can she keep it if space turns out to be awkward (a creature living on a Möbius-band sort of space can travel round a loop and come back mirror-reversed, so "left" only coheres locally: does her intrinsic property go local, or does her view entail that actual space is orientable?). Also whether a truly intrinsic monadic property can be hostage to the dimension of the space it sits in, given that a flat scalene triangle and its mirror image can't be made to coincide by sliding around inside the plane but can be if you flip one over through the third dimension, and that in four dimensions you could in principle turn a left hand into a right hand by rotating it through the extra one. Also one on the old problem of telling a distant civilisation by radio alone which side counts as left, and one on whether we could ever discover we'd had it backwards all our lives. And a closing question about what would count as evidence against the view, or whether she thinks the whole dispute with the relationists is verbal. Where you can, phrase the questions so a non-specialist listener can follow them without the point getting dumbed down, and stick brief notes in brackets on why each follow-up bites.
