hey — pretend you're a professional philosopher who's spent way too long in the decision-theory literature on ambiguity, and help me prep an interview. Brainstorm a bit before you write anything: make a private list of every serious objection to the view I describe below, cross off the ones she's obviously heard a hundred times, and build the questions out of what's left.

I host a tiny podcast on choice under uncertainty. Next week's guest is a decision theorist I'll just call Professor M. Her view, in brief: the Ellsberg pattern isn't a bias. Rationality doesn't demand a single sharp prior; when your evidence pins down only a *set* of priors, it's rationally permissible to rank options by how they do at the worst prior in that set. (Setup, so we're on the same page: urn K has 50 red and 50 black marbles, known. Urn U has 100 marbles, all red or black, composition unknown. You bet on a colour and a marble is drawn. Most people take K-red over U-red *and* K-black over U-black.) She's sharp, she's heard every version of "that's just a money pump," and she insists she's making a normative claim about what's permitted, not a descriptive claim about what people happen to do.

Give me 7 to 9 questions, each two or three sentences max, written so I can read them out loud, plus a scripted follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. Please don't build any question around naming papers or authors — I can't check them and she won't want to recite them on air. And no "what got you into decision theory" filler. Cover at least these:

1. The incoherence charge done *properly*, not the naive Dutch-book version: spell out the two preferences and show that no single probability over compositions delivers both (the one number that has to be below 1/2 for the first bet and above 1/2 for the second). Then ask which premise she gives up. Her likely answer is "the premise that one probability represents you at all" — so your follow-up is: incompleteness isn't aversion. If your priors are genuinely imprecise and you refuse to pick a tie-breaker, what your theory predicts in the two-urn case is silence, i.e. no comparison, not the strict preference for K that people report. So what's the extra ingredient doing the work — and is it an attitude, or is it secretly a belief?

2. Her own signature case, which I want you to use against her: one urn, 100 marbles, red proportion known to be exactly 1/3, the remaining 2/3 split between black and yellow in an unknown way, one draw, you choose which colour you're betting on. Same urn, same draw, so there's no "different urns, different states" escape. Ask her to say what differs between the event *red* and the event *black* on her account, other than what she happens to know about it. Follow-up: if the answer is "nothing about the event, only about my information," then why isn't that just evidence, something that should shape the credal set rather than add a separate taste for the known? And make her say out loud how much weight the worst case (zero black marbles) actually gets in her deliberations.

3. A dynamic question. After she's committed to betting red on U but before the draw, would she pay a penny for the composition to be revealed? Then: after the marble is drawn but before the colour is shown, would she pay to switch her bet to urn K? Ask how those answers hang together, and hit the standard reply — that the switch fee merely shows she'd rather have been on K all along — with: why doesn't an agent who expects to want to switch just choose that way from the start?

4. Aggregation. 100 independent ambiguous urns, one draw each, payoff on the total. Does her principle give a different verdict in the aggregate than in the single case? If yes, why should the number of repetitions matter to a normative principle about caution? If no, make her bite the bullet out loud: a big portfolio of independent ambiguous bets is worth less to her than the identical portfolio with the compositions disclosed, even though she's allowed to watch the early draws and learn.

5. A "nobody ever knew" case of your own construction: the composition was fixed by a genuinely random physical process, never recorded, never observed by anyone, so no one is better informed than she is and no one could have chosen it adversarially. Ask whether caution is still permissible here, and press on why: if the pull of the worst case comes from a tacit suspicion that the setup was chosen by someone who knew her bet, that suspicion is idle here, so she should explain what's left.

6. One closing question about what would change her mind, and one about whether her view gives any support to the precautionary principle in regulation — and if it does, how she answers the objection that caution has costs borne by identifiable people.

Each question should be something she has to actually think about, not something she can field with a slogan. At the very end, on its own line, put the machine-readable verdict \answer{Qn}, where n is the number of the question you think she's most likely to deflect rather than answer.
