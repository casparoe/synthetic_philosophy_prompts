I convene a third-year seminar in philosophy of economics, and I am writing its final paper. The students have worked through choice theory as far as expected utility, and they have read the two objections to revealed preference theory that anyone can recite: that observed choices are the product of preferences and beliefs together, and that a preference defined as whatever rationalizes behaviour cannot bear the weight welfare economics places on it. I want one essay question that cannot be passed by restating either of those.

The subject is not merely professional for me this term. My wife and I are completing the application for our daughter's primary school place. The form asks each family to rank six schools; it pre-loads a "suggested" six, produced by an algorithm trained on the submissions of families with similar postcodes and similar histories; a family can search for and add others, but very few do. The local authority's consultation paper then proposes to treat those rankings as evidence of what families "really" value in a school — attainment, journey time, pastoral quality — in order to decide which schools to expand. I have been arguing with my own brother-in-law about whether our ranking will show anything at all about what we want.

Please build the question around that kind of case: an institution inferring values from choices where the menu offered is itself a function of earlier choices, and where choosers know that what they can get depends on what they rank. The crux I want students to have to face is whether the rationalizability result we have been using — that, given the domain assumptions in the lecture notes, choices satisfying the weak axiom (never choosing X when Y was available, and Y when X was available) are exactly the choices representable as maximizing one fixed ordering — so much as applies when the sequence of menus is not fixed independently of the chooser's behaviour; and then, separately, whether an ordering recovered under those conditions could serve as a measure of the thing the institution cares about. I want the identification problem first, and then the question of which job "utility" is being asked to do: representing choice, measuring well-being, or furnishing an interpersonal currency for policy.

Please supply: the question itself, with sub-parts and marks (25 total, forty-five minutes, closed-book, answerable without citing a single author — marks for the moves, not for names); a model answer of no more than 450 words; and a marking scheme organised around five must-hit moves, with the marks each is worth and one line on the characteristic way a candidate gets it wrong, followed by a sentence on where the A/B boundary falls. Have the question also force an answer to the exasperated objection that choices are all we can ever have, so that a "real" preference no possible behaviour could contradict is idle and policy should use the choice data anyway. I would also like them asked what pattern in the data would diagnose the problem — I have in mind something like a test that re-randomises which schools are suggested. Keep the whole response under 1,000 words.
