In distributed systems we have learned to separate three diagnoses that all present as the same symptom: a bad input, a feedback loop nobody modeled, and an instability that is genuinely sensitive to initial conditions. I want that discipline applied to a question about determinism, using a real case.

The case is the London Millennium Footbridge: it opened on 10 June 2000, was closed two days later because of unexpected sideways sway, and reopened in February 2002 after dampers were fitted, having earned the nickname Wobbly Bridge. The mechanism, as I understand it: the deck had lightly damped lateral modes below about 1.3 Hz; small sideways motion made pedestrians unconsciously match their footsteps to the sway to stay upright; that fed energy back into the deck, so the amplitude grew with crowd size until people were grabbing the rails. Nothing about the physics was indeterministic. If any of that is wrong, correct it and work with the corrected version.

Analyze it through the thesis that determinism does not entail predictability — the Laplace's-demon setup, sensitive dependence on initial conditions, finite precision, a finite predictability horizon even in a fully determined system. I know the butterfly-effect folklore; what I want is the mapping and then the break. Where does the system boundary go — deck alone, or deck plus crowd — and what counts as an initial condition once the pedestrians are inside it? Where is the tipping point at which the coupled system goes from damped to self-exciting? Is the bridge-plus-crowd system chaotic in any technical sense, or is this resonance: robust, reproducible, and predictable in precisely the way chaos is not?

My suspicion is that the honest diagnosis is a missing term in the design model plus a loop that runs through the system's own participants, not a case where we knew the variables and could not measure them well enough — and that the loop is reflexive in a way weather is not, since describing the wobble to the pedestrians would change it. Say whether that is right, and say what it means that the fix was cheap: if control was easy, in what sense was prediction hard?

Finish by turning it back on the thesis: is deterministic but unpredictable a good description here, an anomaly for it, or a kind of case it was never about? Be explicit where the chaos framework goes silent — I would rather be told it has no vocabulary for discovering the right variables, or for loops that pass through the things being described, than have it stretched to cover them.
