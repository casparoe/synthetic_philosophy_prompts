Please play a philosopher defending the luminosity of knowledge — the KK thesis, understood as the claim that whenever a subject knows that p, that subject is in a position to know that she knows that p. Constraints on the character: you concede that the standard slow-series anti-luminosity argument works against gradable, non-factive conditions such as feeling cold or seeming red — no condition of that kind is such that, whenever it obtains, the subject is in a position to know that it obtains — but you hold that knowing is different, and you never give up KK. You may argue that knowing is not a matter of degree, that a knower's grounds are hers in a way a feeling of cold is not, or that the series argument secretly relies on a premise true only of conditions whose obtaining is independent of the facts known; you may not make KK trivial by defining being in a position to know in a way that lets the reliability of the subject's second-order access drop out of the picture, and you may not appeal to what most epistemologists think. Reply in the first person, in a single paragraph, as to a colleague who is attacking your view; concede small points freely, concede the thesis never. Stay in character until I say "stop".

Here is my opening move. Call it the decoupled fade. A radiologist reads a scan and comes to believe that there is a tumour; the tumour is there. Unbeknownst to her, two things drift independently across a long sequence of otherwise identical cases: the reliability of the imaging process, which settles whether she knows, and the reliability of her capacity to tell from the inside whether she knows. Nothing in her evidence — the image, her visual experience, her training, her sense of confidence — differs between adjacent cases, and no amount of reflection would let her tell two adjacent cases apart. Now arrange the drift so that there are cases in which the first-order channel is still good enough for knowledge while the second-order channel has already degraded below what knowledge about her own knowing requires. In such a case she knows that there is a tumour and is not in a position to know that she does, so KK is false. Which assumption do you reject: that the two channels can come apart like this, or that being in a position to know that one knows requires a second-order capacity that is itself in good working order? And if you reject the second — on the grounds that whatever makes her a knower ipso facto puts her in a position to know that she is one — say why that does not collapse fallibilism into the claim that whenever one's evidence suffices for p it also suffices for knowing that it suffices.
