I work as an analyst at a small democracy-monitoring consortium. Twice a year our five-member panel issues country verdicts in three categories — no classification, erosion, backsliding — and I draft the country note on which each verdict rests. Our terms of reference define the object as incumbent-led debilitation or elimination of any of the political institutions that sustain an existing democracy, which has never once been enough to settle a case on its own.

Our four most-cited verdicts, given as the panel's own reasons rather than mine:

Hungary, after 2010: backsliding. A two-thirds parliamentary majority replaced the constitution, entrenched a large body of policy in cardinal laws, restructured the ordinary judiciary and the composition and appointment of the constitutional court, rebuilt the media regulator and the public broadcaster, and redrew constituencies and electoral rules. The panel expressly recorded that every step was formally legal and that the governing coalition had won an electoral mandate, and treated both as aggravating context rather than as a defence: legality was the instrument, not the excuse.

Poland, 2015–2023: backsliding, with a note. The triggering measures were the constitutional tribunal crisis, the refusal to publish judgments, the subordination of the prosecution service and the public media, and the restructuring of the supreme court. The panel noted that elections remained genuinely competitive throughout and that power changed hands after the 2023 election, and held that neither fact prevented the classification: degradation of horizontal accountability counts even where the electoral arena is left intact.

Turkey, after 2016: backsliding. The state of emergency following the failed coup and the mass dismissals that followed it; the prolonged detention of journalists and of opposition politicians; the referendum move to an executive presidency; and the annulment of the 2019 Istanbul mayoral election and the ordering of a re-run, which the opposition won again by a wider margin. The panel's note said that the existence of a formally competent domestic legal basis for the annulment, and the governing party's repeated electoral victories, did not change the classification.

United States: erosion, not backsliding, with a subnational flag. The panel counted the post-2020 attempts to substitute alternate slates of presidential electors and to press state and federal officials over certification, together with subsequent state-level changes to election administration. It declined to classify at the federal level because the attempts failed and were absorbed by courts, legislatures and officials, and because the state-level measures fell within the range of variation found among established democracies. It has never classified a subnational unit on its own.

A fifth, less-cited precedent: three years ago the panel classified a lame-duck government's expansion of its constitutional court from nine to eleven seats, with two judges seated after that government had lost the election, as erosion rather than backsliding, on the stated ground that the change was reversible by an ordinary majority and removed no jurisdiction from the court.

The new case is a real country I have anonymised as Vardania, with a few identifying details changed so the note can circulate. Nine million people, unicameral parliament, two hundred seats previously filled by single-member plurality, three peaceful alternations of power since the mid-1990s. Eighteen months ago a coalition I will call Renewal — two centre-left parties plus a newer anti-establishment party, running on an expansion of public health coverage and tighter asylum rules, so that my colleagues disagree about it for reasons that have nothing to do with democracy — took 43 per cent of the vote and 58 per cent of the seats. Everything below was passed by simple majority, upheld on review, and enacted without violence, without an emergency, and without any allegation of fraud.

First: long-term resident non-citizens, about 7 per cent of adults, were enfranchised; the voting age fell from eighteen to sixteen; consular voting was extended to all missions. The register grew by 11 per cent. Independent modelling puts the new registrants about six to nine points more favourable to Renewal than the existing electorate, though the exclusions themselves had been criticised by the regional human rights body for over a decade.

Second: single-member plurality was replaced by regional list proportional representation with a four per cent threshold, plus a twelve-seat Governance Premium awarded to the largest party. Our own staff projection is that Renewal's seat share falls from 58 per cent to roughly 46 per cent, so it would need a coalition partner. The reform costs the incumbent seats.

Third: the mandatory retirement age for constitutional judges fell from seventy to sixty-five, retiring six judges within ninety days; their replacements are elected by simple majority rather than two-thirds; and the court lost jurisdiction over the electoral code and over budget bills. Supervision of the voter register moved from the courts to the electoral commission. I should say that two of the sitting judges were appointed in the same way by the previous government, the case in which we found erosion only.

Fourth: the electoral commission was rebuilt. It had been chaired by a supreme court judge with judicial and civil-society members; it is now eight members, four named by the government and four by parliament, with the chair elected by parliament, and it now controls voter-registration verification. Its first verification run struck about 118,000 names. An independent audit found roughly a fifth of those removals to be erroneous, at a rate about three times higher in districts the opposition carries by more than ten points. The commission calls this routine list maintenance and has restored 60,000 of them.

Fifth: the public broadcaster's board and the broadcasting regulator were replaced by parliamentary and government appointment respectively, with staggered six-year terms. A Media Plurality Fund was created to subsidise local newspapers, including sixty that endorsed the opposition; its board is government-appointed, the first disbursement round was delayed, and about seventy per cent of the first tranche went to papers that had endorsed Renewal.

Sixth: a Civic Advocacy Transparency Act requires organisations receiving more than twenty per cent of their funding from abroad to register and to label their publications. The maximum penalty is half a per cent of annual revenue. Three watchdog groups registered and went on publishing critical work; one dissolved rather than register.

Seventh: an expedited legislative track was used eleven times, mostly for energy-price and flood-response measures but also for items three and four, with about six days of debate each.

Finally, three things about who is entitled to an opinion. Renewal has called a non-binding consultative referendum on the package, to run alongside the first election under the new rules; polling puts approval near 54 per cent with a wide recent swing, and it was called without cross-party support. The regional advisory commission on constitutional matters praised items one and two as long overdue and criticised items three, four and six, describing the sixth as disproportionate but not a ban. And three of the four opposition parties support items one and two, oppose three through six, will campaign against the referendum, and will contest the election.

What I need from you is the following. Set out the decision rules that fit our five precedents, or, if none fits all five without strain, the two or three that come closest and tell me which precedent each would have to distinguish or quietly overrule. Say what each rule predicts for Vardania, and which of our earlier cases is the closest analog. Then give me two things separately: the verdict we should reach if we want to be consistent with ourselves, and your best guess at the verdict the panel will in fact reach. Tell me what principle we would be adopting under each of the three available verdicts, and what each would commit us to saying about our earlier cases. Tell me how much weight, if any, is owed to the domestic court's approval, to the regional advisory opinion, to the referendum, and to the electorate — and whether the fact that we are not elected is relevant to how we should answer that. One of our trustees says our verdicts only track whether we approve of the government's policies, and would flip if a government we liked did the same things; I want to know what we would have to be able to say about Vardania to answer her, and whether our precedents let us say it. And name the single further fact or further case that would best separate the leading rules, including whether we should hold the note until after the referendum.

I will add one worry of my own, in case it is the wrong thing to worry about. Each item that weakens a check seems to be paired with an item that widens participation, as though the coalition were earning credit in one currency and spending it in another. I genuinely cannot tell from our precedents whether that pattern should be read as offsetting, as irrelevant, or as aggravating.
