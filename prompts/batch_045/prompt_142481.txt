Dear colleague,

I'm drafting a short position paper on markets in contested goods, and before I circulate it I'd like someone to check whether the commitments I've ended up with actually form a package. Most of them feel like they travel together, but I have a persistent sense that two or three of them are doing work the rest of the set won't permit, and I can't locate the fault line. Below is the list, stated as flatly as I can.

1. I want my opposition to particular markets to be non-consequentialist in a specific sense: it should not rest on empirical claims about crowding out, degradation of the good, or harm to third parties. Concretely — I would still oppose paid gestation if the best available evidence showed that women who gestate for pay do as well as, or better than, matched controls on the outcomes we care about, and that no one else is made worse off.

2. I don't think appeals to human dignity or to the symbolism of the body do any argumentative work; they name the place where a reason should go. If there is a reason, I want it stated.

3. I oppose an open market in kidneys from living donors — price arrived at by bargaining, buyers selecting among sellers.

4. I support compensated donation: a single public purchaser, a fixed gratuity set administratively, no bargaining, no buyer-side selection of the donor, a mandatory delay, and an independent donor advocate with a veto.

5. I oppose paid gestation even when it is organized in exactly the parallel way: one licensed agency, a fixed fee set administratively, no selection of the gestating woman by the commissioning parents, delay, advocate, veto. Every feature that makes 3 objectionable is stripped out, and I still object.

6. I have no objection to unpaid gestational surrogacy and would leave it legal.

7. I favor repealing anti-price-gouging statutes. A supplier who raises the price of ice, fuel, or rooms after a hurricane is not wronging anyone, and the statutes make the people they are meant to protect worse off.

8. I think the criminal ban on vote selling should stay, and I think the two standard arguments for it both fail: the epistemic one, because any individual vote is almost certainly not pivotal, and the political-equality one, because we already tolerate wildly unequal private spending on campaigns. My reason is different — the franchise is an office held in trust rather than a piece of property, and so it is not the kind of thing one may sell, even when the sale changes no outcome and leaves no one worse off.

9. Part of what bothers me about a kidney market is who would in fact sell: disproportionately the badly off. But I also think the objection would survive in a society with far greater material equality than ours.

Two variants I keep circling and cannot settle:

A. Compensated donation in which the cash gratuity is replaced by a non-transferable, non-inheritable priority credit that moves one person the donor names higher on the transplant list — payment in kind, denominated in the very good being procured, with no cash changing hands. My instinct is that this is better than 4, not worse.

B. A rule that permits surge pricing during an emergency but requires the surcharge above the pre-disaster price to be rebated, with interest, to buyers once the emergency is declared over. My instinct is that this is a pointless complication: if the higher price is permissible, the seller should keep it.

What I want is an audit rather than a survey of the field. Please tell me which of these commitments genuinely pull against each other, ordered by severity; which apparent conflicts dissolve once a distinction is drawn explicitly, and what that distinction is; and for each real tension, what exactly has to give and what the revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. I'm not attached to any item, so don't spare the ones I've stated most confidently — but do say which single revision buys the most coherence overall. If your diagnosis settles A or B, say how. Please write in American English, and keep the reply to the audit itself.
