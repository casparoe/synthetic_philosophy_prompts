I am preparing the opening session of a short film-and-philosophy series at our local library, on the theme of contingency in history, and I have settled on Capra's It's a Wonderful Life as the centerpiece. The subject has become personally pressing for me of late: my father died in October, and since then I have caught myself repeatedly running the tape backward, tracing the small and wholly undramatic choices — a missed connection, a job he took on an impulse — that happen to be the reason I exist at all, and then being unsure whether that exercise is clarifying or merely consoling. So I would like a reading of the film that is willing to be unconsoling where the argument requires it.

The lens I have in mind is the for-want-of-a-nail picture of history: the idea that large outcomes turn on minute, unrepeatable particulars, so that the course of things is radically sensitive to small interventions and cannot be reconstructed backward by finding the one decisive cause. I want the film read against that idea, not as an illustration of it, and I want three distinctions to be kept apart, since I think the film quietly slides between them.

First, distinguish a traceable single chain of consequences — the lost horseshoe, the fallen rider, the lost battle — from genuine chaotic amplification, where no link is the cause and the chain could not in principle be traced. Clarence's Pottersville is presented as a chain George can be walked along link by link: his brother drowned at nine, so the men on the transport died, and so on. Ask whether the film therefore needs the traceable version of contingency while borrowing the emotional authority of the untraceable version, and whether that is a philosophical maneuver or a rhetorical one.

Second, distinguish sensitivity from irreplaceability. Even granting that removing George changes everything, it does not follow that he mattered uniquely: a role can be multiply realizable, and a system that is sensitive to which person occupies a slot may be entirely indifferent elsewhere. What does the film do with its near-surrogates — the uncle, Mary, the policeman, the cab driver, the druggist — and do those characters strengthen the consolation or quietly undermine it?

Third, I am bothered by the shape of the counterfactual itself. In the counterfactual semantics one learns from Lewis and Stalnaker, we ask after the closest world in which the antecedent holds. Pottersville strikes me as a strange candidate for that title: it diverges from Bedford Falls on many independent matters of fact at once, each requiring its own extra departure. Is the angel showing George the nearest world, or a spectacularly distant one? And there is a related worry about directionality: in a genuinely chaotic order, small causes should be as likely to improve things as to ruin them, yet this cascade runs uniformly downhill. Is that a moral thesis about how decency and predation each propagate, or is it simply that nobody tells the story of the nail that saved the kingdom?

Finally, the question I care about. George's torment was about the shape of his own life — whether he should have been free to leave Bedford Falls and do his good somewhere else. What he is shown is a world in which he never existed. Does the film's answer reach the thing that actually tormented him, or does it answer a different and easier question? And if the honest answer is that consequences are unpredictable at the scale that matters, does that verdict tell against George's way of living, or does it give the strongest available argument for it — that when outcomes cannot be forecast, one must choose by the kind of person one is?

Close by saying what, if anything, the film contributes to the debate that argument alone does not. Please describe scenes rather than quoting the screenplay.
