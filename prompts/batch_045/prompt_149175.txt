I'm writing a term paper for a graduate seminar on representation and grounding, and I want to use Denis Villeneuve's *Arrival* (2016, from Ted Chiang's 1998 novella *Story of Your Life*) as a case study rather than as decoration. My supervisor's objection is that the film is Hollywood Whorfianism and has nothing to offer anyone working on the symbol grounding problem; I think he is half wrong, but I need a reading that earns that rather than asserting it. I'm also not interested in another round on whether linguistic relativity is empirically respectable — I've read that literature and it isn't my thesis. What I want is a reading that takes both the film and the grounding literature seriously, points me at the two or three scenes that actually do the work, and ends by saying what the film adds to the philosophical argument that the arguments alone don't.

Here is the angle I keep circling. In the early stretch, Louise and Ian have a two-way channel: they point, gesture, display objects, and slowly assemble a mapping between heptapod logograms and English words out of ostension and repetition. But a symbol-to-symbol mapping is exactly the thing the grounding worry says cannot be enough — a dictionary handed around a room, every step still formal, nothing connecting the signs to anything. So: what in the film is supposed to lift Louise from that mapping to understanding? I'd like you to adjudicate between the candidate sources of anchoring the film seems to offer, roughly in order of how seriously I take them: perceptual contact with the aliens themselves (the bodies, the ink, the shared physical environment of the shell); shared purposive action in a common space, including the little interactional rituals and the games the teams play with the visitors; and initiation into a practice with norms that outrun anything sensorimotor. That third one is why I think the mahjong detail matters more than it looks: the Chinese team's rendering of the key logogram as *weapon* rather than *means* or *tool* is explained in the film by the competitive game they used to interact with the heptapods. If that's right, the film is claiming that what a sign anchors to depends on the interactional practice you are in with its users — a practice-first, broadly Wittgensteinian point that a pure symbol-symbol story can't reconstruct, and that the more standard causal-informational accounts don't obviously make room for either. I want that developed, and I want to know whether it is genuinely a claim about grounding or just a claim about translation choice.

The part I most want you to spend time on is the late, stranger case, because I think it's where the film might contribute something new instead of illustrating a known position. Once the language reorganises Louise's experience of time, she has something like episodic memory of events that have not happened: a daughter not yet conceived, a UN reception years away, a private phone number and a dying woman's words she has not yet been told. Standard grounding accounts tie a symbol's anchoring to causal or sensorimotor contact with what it is about; here the referent lies downstream. I've been toying with a distinction I call *teleological grounding*, where a sign is anchored by what the agent is oriented toward and will later do with it rather than by what caused it now, and I want your honest verdict on whether the film actually forces a category like that or whether it can be handled by a more conservative story. The conservative story I can see: the heptapods ground their own symbols in their own non-linear perception, and Louise simply inherits grounded terms through testimony and trust — which, if you think about it, is how almost all of us get almost all of our vocabulary anyway, and would itself be an interesting pressure point for grounding purism. My friend in computational linguistics has a third reading that I'd like you to engage rather than wave away: she says Louise's achievement is nothing but very strong pattern learning over a large corpus of alien writing, with the time-bending stuff as a mystical garnish, and that no notion of grounding is needed to explain anything in the film.

Be honest in the other direction too, since a paper that ignores this will get torn apart in the seminar. Chiang's story is a first-contact puzzle with a resolution many readers find too neat, and the film has a real temptation to make language acquisition a gift: Louise understands because she is given the language, and the mechanics of getting a symbol system off the ground are skipped precisely where they get hard. If the film cheats at the exact point where grounding becomes difficult, say so plainly, tell me which of my three readings survives that charge and which collapses into the gift, and say how much of the paper can be written anyway. I'd rather submit something that concedes the film's evasions and still shows why the early scenes are philosophically interesting than something that overclaims.
