I'm assembling a set of teaching cases on allocation under scarcity, in the lifeboat idiom, and for the past two years I've been logging my co-instructor's verdicts as she gives them — she has now ruled on something like forty variations. I have a new case I want to use, and I'd like to know what she will say about it before I put it to her. More usefully, I'd like to know what rule she is running: everything she has said so far is jointly consistent with several rules that come apart on this case, and I don't trust myself to work out which.

Here are the five verdicts I take to carry the information. In every case, eject means put a passenger over the side by force, against their will, with no consent and no prior agreement among those aboard; in every case the boat is taking water and the alternatives are ejecting someone or risking foundering.

1. Ten aboard. Unless one goes over, the boat founders within minutes and all ten drown; if one goes over, the other nine survive. All are strangers, equally healthy, no one at fault. No straws, coins, or other randomizer is available, and in practice the three strongest would simply choose — and they would choose the oldest passenger. Verdict: no one may be ejected; they should all go down.

2. As in 1, except that a proper lottery is available. Verdict: they should draw lots, and the loser may be put over; nine lives rather than none.

3. Twelve aboard. The boat will hold out until rescue only if one seat is freed. One passenger has injuries that will kill him within the hour whatever anyone does; the others will all survive if the boat holds out. Verdict: the injured man goes over, no lottery needed, and insisting on one would be a kind of fetish.

4. Ten aboard. One passenger deliberately loosened a plank the night before, hoping to cover a theft; the boat is leaking because of him, and it founders within the hour unless one goes over. Verdict: the saboteur goes over, no lottery needed.

5. Eight aboard. One passenger is in a panic, thrashing at the gunwale, and will capsize the boat within minutes. There is no way to sedate or restrain him, and no other passenger's removal would help. Verdict: he may be put over, even though he is at fault for nothing — as she put it, it is he who will capsize the boat.

She has also said, generally, that she distrusts any rule under which the people who end up surviving get to decide who dies.

The new case. Eight aboard; rescue is expected in about six hours. With eight aboard there is roughly a 70 percent chance of foundering before rescue and all hands lost; with any seven aboard, about 20 percent — the passengers are close enough in weight that it does not much matter which one goes. A, aged 34 and healthy, climbed aboard against the crew's express order after being told the boat was at capacity; his weight is the last increment, he meant no harm, and he has not damaged the boat. B, aged 76, has a heart condition and perhaps five years of expected life remaining against A's forty-odd, but a good chance of surviving the six hours if the boat stays afloat. The other six are healthy adults of middle age, none at fault. A lottery is possible but would require the bailing to stop for twenty minutes, which the crew puts at about five percentage points of additional risk. Alternatively the decision can be deferred an hour, at about two points of additional risk, by which time they will know whether the leak is worsening.

So: does she eject A, eject B, run the lottery anyway, eject no one, or wait? Please give me the verdict you expect with a rough confidence, then the rules that fit the whole track record and what each predicts here — my own guesses are a lexical-responsibility rule, a least-to-lose rule keyed to what is at stake, a procedural rule requiring equal chances, and a rule on which merely probabilistic benefit can never license killing, but I don't know which of these she is running or whether the real one is something I haven't listed. Note that no single passenger's presence is necessary or sufficient for foundering here, so whether A is the threat in the sense of case 5 is itself part of what is at issue, and treat waiting as a distinct verdict rather than folding it into one of the others. Then give me the single further case — as minimal a variation as you can manage — that would best separate the leading candidates, and tell me, if the track record genuinely underdetermines the answer, what she would be committing herself to under each resolution.
