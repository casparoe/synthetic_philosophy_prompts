Hey — I host a tiny podcast about weird corners of rational choice (like 900 listeners, mostly econ-adjacent grad students and some LessWrong types), and next week's guest is a philosopher who's just published a book reviving superrationality: the view that in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma against someone who reasons exactly as you do, you shouldn't defect, because the two of you aren't really running separate deliberations. I've had people on to defend evidential and causal decision theory before and those went fine, but I'm worried this one just re-runs the greatest hits — the twenty letters, the billion dollars if exactly one telegram arrives, so everybody should roll a twenty-sided die and hope for their own number — and I end up with forty minutes of nothing new.

So give me nine or ten questions that actually pressure the view. Things I want covered:

1. The die-roll answer is usually sold as "your choice of probability *is* their choice of probability" — you don't get to hold the other nineteen fixed and then defect on them. I want a question that makes him say what that fixed-point constraint is really buying. Is superrationality just "optimize over the diagonal where everyone runs the same deliberation," and if so why isn't that a plain coordination rule any game theorist could state, with no special metaphysics of shared reasoning attached? He'll probably reach for the caused-vs-merely-correlated distinction or start talking about timeless/functional decision theory — I want a follow-up on whether that machinery is doing any work or just renaming the constraint.

2. I made up a variant to spring on him. Same twenty letters, same billion, same "exactly one telegram or nobody gets anything" — but each recipient also gets a private slip reading either SINGLE or DOUBLE. It's ten of each, everyone knows it's ten-ten, nobody knows who got which, and DOUBLE means your payoff is two billion if you're the only one who asks. Symmetry is broken, but everyone is still running the same deliberation; it just takes their own slip as an input. Question: what does superrationality tell the DOUBLE people to do, and does the answer stay a fixed point or does it collapse into a bargaining problem about who defers to whom? Give me a follow-up for the move where he says "the same function applied to your own slip restores the symmetry."

3. Partial superrationalists. Say only twelve of the twenty are superrationalists and the rest are ordinary causal-defectors who ask for sure, or just randomize. Does the view degrade gracefully, or does it hand you no prescription until you know the exact composition of the room — and if it's the latter, how is that different from ordinary uncertainty about another agent's type?

And I want to close on one question about what would count *against* the view: a case where superrationality returns no answer, or returns one he'd hate, that he can't wave off with "well, that's not the kind of case the theory is about." For each question give me the answer he's most likely to give plus the follow-up, so I know where to push live. Keep the questions conversational — my listeners tune out the second it sounds like a seminar. Also he's the kind of guest who'll happily say "that depends what you mean by should," so if you can build one question that closes off that move, even better.
