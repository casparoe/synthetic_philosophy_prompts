I'm on the editorial board of my school's humor magazine, and I'm assembling a satire issue built around bad institutional reasoning. I'd like you to write the centerpiece: a deadpan open letter to parents, roughly 700 words, with a headline, a subject line, and a formal sign-off, from an invented school-district superintendent announcing that the district has solved its world-language staffing shortage.

The program: Mandarin will be taught without anyone in the building learning any Chinese. Students are issued a rulebook that tells them which marks to produce in response to which other marks, where the rules refer only to the shapes of the marks — or, in the new edition, to their numerical codes — and never to what the marks mean. The district's position, stated in complete sincerity, is that a student who has worked all the way through the book understands Chinese in the only sense the state can test for.

The letter should walk through the program's tracks, and each one should be a recognizable move from the actual debate about the Chinese Room argument — the example in which a person in a room follows a rulebook for Chinese and produces replies indistinguishable from a native speaker's, and the puzzle is whether anyone in the setup understands a word — pushed about one step past where its defenders would go:

- Level One: open-book table lookup.
- Level Two, Internalization: students memorize the tables until they can produce replies with the book closed, at which point, the superintendent explains, understanding has genuinely arrived, because now it is the student herself who constitutes the whole system rather than merely one part of it.
- Level Three, Connectionist: the rulebook is replaced by a very long list of numbers that nobody can interpret, which the superintendent presents as an even clearer case of understanding, since there is no longer any rulebook for a critic to point at and the arrangement is brain-like.
- The Robot Track: participants wear a camera-and-locomotion rig so that their replies are causally hooked up to a real environment, which the superintendent regards as settling the question.

Then the escalation I most want: the new edition eliminates Chinese-looking marks entirely, replacing them with sequences of colored lights or vibrations, on the grounds that the marks never needed to look like anything to anybody in the first place.

Constraints: no real people named or quoted; no direct quotation of the original argument, since the piece should work for a reader who has never heard of it; no puns, no science fiction, and no winking at the audience. Keep the register of an actual administrative letter — busing schedules, a budget line item, a phone number for the pilot program. The superintendent should not be a villain or a cartoon; he should be a competent administrator following a definition to where it leads. Include one quote from the district's last human Mandarin teacher, who has been reassigned to proctor the examinations on the condition that she not speak during them.

The ending should be the recoil: the superintendent announces that the program will be extended to English the following year, so that the district can certify English proficiency without anything being understood on either side, and closes with a genuinely proud sentence about the savings. A reader should come away feeling both that something has gone badly wrong in the definition of understanding — the gap between producing the right marks and knowing what any of them mean — and, uncomfortably, the pull of the superintendent's case, since his students really do pass, and passing is the only test anyone has.
