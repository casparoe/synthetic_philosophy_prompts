So this is going to sound like an odd thing to ask for but bear with me. Last February I had to pick a hospital room: my mother was dying in one hospital, my father was in surgery in another one fifty minutes away, I'm an only child, and there was nobody else to send. I went to my mother. She died at four in the morning, my father came through fine, and he has never once made me feel bad about it. What I can't get past is the way people describe it afterwards: obviously you did the right thing, obviously there was only one thing to do, and what you're left with is grief and guilt and it will fade. That account feels wrong to me, but not because I secretly think I chose wrongly. It's that the thing I didn't do hasn't gone anywhere. I don't walk around feeling bad about it exactly — it's more like there's an unpaid bill with my name on it from last February, and everyone telling me I did the right thing isn't paying it.

My friend who did a philosophy minor says this is incoherent on the technical meaning: if you can't do both, you can't be required to do both, so at most one of the two was what you really ought to have done and the other one just dissolves, and what I'm describing is a feeling dressed up as a fact. I'm not convinced, and I don't think it's a feeling either — but I also really don't want something that just shrugs and says both things are true, isn't that sad.

What I want is a pantoum, four quatrains, spoken by someone who has already made a choice of roughly that shape (make up your own situation, it doesn't have to be mine, but no philosophers as speakers). If you don't know the form: lines 2 and 4 of each stanza become lines 1 and 3 of the next, and in the final stanza the poem's own first and third lines come back in the second and fourth slots, in reversed order, so that the poem's opening line is also its last line. I want the form to carry the argument rather than decorate it. The two repeated lines should be the two competing claims — or the two people, or the two things owed — and the fact that they keep trading places, each arriving in the slot the other one just vacated, and then returning at the end swapped around, should be what the poem is saying. Ideally it should read as a hard decision that got resolved correctly right up until the last quatrain, and only there should it be plain that nothing was dissolved and nothing is going to be. Two more constraints, sorry: don't use the words regret, guilt, grief, sorry, should have, either, or both, because the moment the poem names an emotion the reader will file this under feelings, and the point is that it's about what is still owed; and no consolation, no we-carry-on, no moral at the close. It should be accurate enough that someone who actually knows this debate would recognise the position being dramatised — that the claim you didn't act on survives as something you still have to answer for — and would not mistake it for the neighbouring view that there was never really a conflict, or the other one that says there's simply no fact of the matter about what you should have done. No preface, and no title like "The Choice." American English, please. If you absolutely have to, three sentences after the poem on which line is doing which job, but I'd rather be able to tell.
