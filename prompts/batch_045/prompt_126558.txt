I've just spent a long evening arguing with a friend about whether two well-informed people who have read each other's work and still disagree can both be rational. She kept appealing to Aumann's agreement theorem; I kept saying the theorem's hypotheses are doing more work than she was admitting, and we went in circles. I now have to mark a script on exactly this material, and I'd rather have it graded on its merits than have my own sympathies imported into the mark.

The question, as set for a second-year decision theory paper (20 marks; candidates were told that a first-class answer must state the theorem's assumptions precisely, sketch why it holds, and diagnose the columnist's inference carefully):

**Question 4.** (a) State Aumann's agreement theorem, making explicit what it assumes and what, exactly, is required to be common knowledge. (b) A columnist writes: "Aumann proved that rational people cannot agree to disagree. So when two well-informed economists still disagree after thirty years of debate, at least one of them must be reasoning badly, or must simply not be taking the other's evidence seriously." Discuss. If the claim needs qualifying, identify the assumption that is doing the work and say what the theorem does and does not entitle us to conclude about the two economists.

The candidate's answer, verbatim:

Aumann's (1976) theorem: two agents share a prior over a finite state space; each has a partition of that space representing her private information; each updates by conditionalisation. Let E be an event, and let C be the event that agent 1's posterior for E is q1 and agent 2's is q2. If C is common knowledge — in Aumann's sense, that C contains the cell of the finest common coarsening of the two partitions at the actual state — then q1 = q2. Agents who share a prior therefore cannot "agree to disagree" about the probability of a single event. The proof is short: a common-knowledge event is a union of cells of each agent's partition, so each agent's probability for E conditional on C is the same weighted average of the prior probabilities of E across those cells, and the two posteriors coincide. Two caveats are worth stating: the conclusion concerns the probability of one and the same event, and it says nothing about what the agents should do, since they may have different utilities over outcomes.

The result is closely related to the Milgrom–Stokey (1982) no-trade theorem, which is essentially Aumann's theorem applied to the event that a given asset is worth more than the price asked: if a proposed trade were common knowledge, both parties would have to assign the same probability to its being profitable, and so neither could expect to gain.

The columnist's reading goes beyond the theorem. The load-bearing assumption is the common prior, which is not a requirement of Bayesian rationality: agents can be perfectly rational and yet have begun with different priors. And that is exactly the position of the two economists — they have read different bodies of work and each takes the other's evidence to be partly misleading, so they plainly do not share a prior. Where the common prior fails the theorem has no purchase, so their disagreement is no evidence of irrationality, and the columnist is wrong.

Moreover, given a common prior this is the only way the theorem can fail, since the other condition takes care of itself: once the two agents have told each other their numbers, each knows the other's posterior, and that is just what common knowledge of the posteriors amounts to here. So persistent disagreement among well-informed people is exactly what we should expect. What the columnist says is true only as a conditional: if the economists did share a prior and did have common knowledge of each other's posteriors, they could not go on disagreeing.
