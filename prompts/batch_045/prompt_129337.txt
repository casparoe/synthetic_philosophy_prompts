So I'm running a reading group on moral responsibility and we've done the classic Frankfurt-style case — you know the one, agent decides on his own, the counterfactual intervener only would have stepped in if he'd been about to decide otherwise, nobody actually does anything — until everyones bored of it. Every time we run it, the same two escape hatches come out: the leeway person says "sure, but he could still have *tried*, or willed otherwise, and that's the alternative that matters," and someone else says "the real problem is the intervener, the setup is basically coercion with extra steps." I want a version with neither hatch available, which is why the ask is this:

Build me a Frankfurt-style case with no intervener in it at all. The agent can't do otherwise, and that has to be true because of ordinary standing features of the situation, not because anyone or anything is monitoring her, waiting on her, or ready to step in.

Constraints:

- One paragraph, realistic setting — a workplace, a clinic, a school, a family. No neurosurgeons, no chips in the brain, no gods or angels, no perfect predictors, no science fiction, and no help from the universe being deterministic.
- She does something genuinely blameworthy, mid-sized (a betrayal of a confidence, a cruelty, a cowardly omission that matters) — not a murder, and not something so trivial that the blame judgment is noise.
- Nothing in the setup is triggered by, or sensitive to, what she thinks, feels, deliberates about, or decides. The blockage is just already there.
- The blockage can't be traceable to her own earlier free choice — no "she got herself into this position," no self-binding, no Aristotle's drunk. If her inability comes from something she freely did earlier, the leeway person just traces, and we're back where we started.
- If anyone arranged the blockage, they didn't do it in order to make her unable, and ideally nobody arranged it at all.
- Her action has to be fully hers in the actual sequence: nothing restrains her, forces her, pushes her, or bypasses her agency. So the blockage shouldn't read as an excuse — it can't be the sort of thing that makes us say "well, she couldn't help it" about the act itself.
- The case has to work for a reader who thinks her actual decision was indeterministically free. Don't manufacture the inability by making the decision causally inevitable.
- The blocked alternative should be one she had every reason to want to take, so that the "she could still have tried" move has to carry all the weight by itself.
- Don't stipulate the verdict. "Intuitively, she is blameworthy" is not allowed as setup; the case has to produce the judgment.

After the scenario:

1. Give me a control version, same agent, same reasons, same act, with the blockage removed, and tell me honestly whether your own blame judgment shifts between the two. If it does, that's a finding, say so.
2. Walk through what the leeway theorist says and why, and what the actual-sequence / reasons-responsiveness person says and why.
3. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the case if you take that feature out.
4. Give the strongest attack on your case from the leeway side, and say whether it survives.
5. Honest note: is this actually a Frankfurt case, or did you end up building something else in disguise — a tracing case, an excuse case, blame for an attitude rather than an act? And if you found that every ordinary no-intervener blockage you could devise either left her able to try or read as an excuse, say which horn you hit and which constraint you had to relax to get out of it. I'd rather have that than a case that quietly cheats.
