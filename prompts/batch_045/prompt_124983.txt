My roommate and I got into it last night about whether a chess engine (or really any AI system) "really" believes and wants things, or whether we're just projecting that onto it, and he closed it out with "a real pattern is one that lets you compress the data" — said with this air of having settled the whole thing. He's read a lot of Dennett: the three stances, the intentional stance, all that. I've read some of it too and I basicly never know what to say at this point, because I can't tell what that sentence is even claiming. One way of taking it it's just a handy test: if a description saves you space or predicts better than chance, that's evidence there's something there. Another way it's a definition — being compressible is what it *is* to be a real pattern. A third way it sounds like it's saying only the compressing descriptions carve things at the joints and everything else is us reading ourselves into the data. Those are wildly different claims and he slid between them all night.

I also came up with three cases I think break at least some versions of it, but I want to know which versions:

1. The Book. Suppose there's a giant book listing everything the system does at every timestep, and it's been right a billion times running. Perfect prediction. But the list is incompressible — you can't do better than copying it out in full, it's noise as far as any shorter description goes. Is there a pattern in that system's behavior or not? Does it "want" anything?
2. The lazy description. "Every output is valid JSON" compresses what a language model does enormously and tells you basically nothing you'd want to predict. Compression without prediction. And the reverse: my friend also said at one point "predicts better than chance" instead of "compresses" — are those even the same criterion, or do 1 and 2 pry them apart?
3. The cooked-up encoding. As far as I can tell any old junk can be compressed if you pick the right encoding, so "it compresses" only means something relative to a scheme. He said that doesn't count, the encoding is gerrymandered. But what does "gerrymandered" mean here other than "I don't like that one"? If patternhood is relative to an encoding, is it relative to a *person* too — does the pattern come and go with how smart the predictor is, or with its computational budget? That seems like a bad result, but maybe it's the honest one.

One more thing that bugged me: on this criterion the physical stance looks like the *least* real stance, since nobody can actually compute the predictions it promises, while the intentional stance is where all the usable compression lives. If he's a physicalist about everything else, does the slogan quietly cut against that?

So: can you lay out, as a list I can bring to the rematch, the distinct precise claims "a real pattern is one that buys you compression" could be making, from the trivial to the wild? For each one say whether it's a platitude, whether it's substantive and defensible, whether it's actually false, whether it's what people who talk this way usually mean, and whether it treats adopting a stance as discovering something that's there or as just picking up a tool. Most importantly: which reading does his argument actually need in order to conclude that the intentional stance is *just* a stance and there's no fact of the matter about what the engine believes — and does that reading survive cases 1 through 3?
