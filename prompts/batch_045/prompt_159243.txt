I write about science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about how results from mathematical logic get borrowed by fields that have no business borrowing them. Recently I had dinner with two sources I'd interviewed separately — Miriam Alvestad, who works on legal theory and codification, and Theo Brandt, a mathematical logician — and they spent most of the meal arguing past each other about whether a legislature could engineer a genuinely Gödelian gap in a legal code. I stayed out of it, and I'd like you to referee. I've reconstructed the exchange as faithfully as I can; the numbering is mine.

(1) Miriam opened with the claim that a codified legal system is, for these purposes, the same kind of object as a formal theory: a finite body of numbered provisions, definitions, and inference practices that courts apply to derive conclusions. Gödel's first incompleteness theorem says that any sufficiently strong, consistent, effectively axiomatized theory leaves some question it can formulate undecided. So, she said, it should be possible for a legislature to write a provision that produces a case the code itself cannot settle — and if a government is serious about automating small-claims adjudication, this is not a parlor game.

(2) Theo said the transfer fails at the first step. The theorem is a result about theories that interpret a modest amount of arithmetic, and the sentence it produces is a sentence of arithmetic — a claim about the natural numbers. Nothing in it says that a system of norms about property, contracts, or penalties must leave a norm-shaped question open. Legal codes do leave questions open, but for their own reasons: vague predicates, open texture, conflicting authority, gaps between general rules and novel facts. Calling that "incompleteness" is a metaphor wearing a proof.

(3) Miriam replied that Theo was overstating what the theorem needs. The requirement is not that the theory be *about* numbers in any substantive sense; it is that the theory be able to encode its own syntax, so that it can talk about its own sentences and proofs. Any canonical citation scheme does that: section numbers already give provisions addresses, and codes are already full of self-referential machinery — severability clauses, sunset provisions, provisions about their own amendment, conflict-of-laws rules that send a question back to another system. Numbers are just the cheapest available encoding, not the point of the theorem.

(4) Theo conceded the encoding point and said it doesn't help her: what self-reference in a legal code buys you is the liar, not Gödel. A provision along the lines of "no court shall grant relief to any party who successfully invokes this section" produces a contradiction or an unperformable instruction, which courts dissolve with the doctrines they already have — severability, the absurdity doctrine, hierarchy of norms. Incompleteness is not contradiction. Gödel's sentence is *true* and unprovable, and getting it requires a consistent system that can prove enough about its own derivability relation. A clause that makes the code contradict itself is a different pathology, and it shows nothing about whether ordinary legal questions are undecidable.

(5) Miriam said that was a strawman of her device, and produced what she called a diagonal clause. Roughly: "In case C-1729, the plaintiff is entitled to relief if and only if the proposition that the plaintiff is entitled to relief in C-1729 is not derivable from this Code." This, she argued, is not a liar paradox but the fixed-point construction itself. Suppose the Code never both grants and denies relief in C-1729. Then the entitlement is not derivable from the Code — since if it were, the Code would also, via the clause, deny it, contradicting the supposition. So the condition on the right-hand side holds, and the clause therefore entitles the plaintiff. The Code determinately resolves C-1729, and no derivation from the Code yields that resolution. That is a legal proposition, about a named case, with a yes-or-no answer — not a vague one — that the Code cannot settle.

(6) Theo said the clause doesn't get off the ground, because "derivable from this Code" is not a well-defined relation of the right kind. The diagonal lemma requires a fixed proof procedure whose outputs are recursively enumerable and which the system can reason about. Legal derivation is not like that: courts invoke purposes, precedents, general principles, proportionality, equitable discretion, and their reading of what the legislature was trying to do; and the relation is non-monotonic, since a later decision can overturn what was previously derivable. So the trigger of Miriam's clause is indeterminate, and a court faced with it would have to invent a doctrine about what "derivable" means — at which point it has amended the system rather than discovered a limit in it. He added two more: legal systems are built to *tolerate* inconsistency, with priority rules for exactly that purpose, so the consistency antecedent Gödel needs is one law conspicuously fails to satisfy; and "true but unprovable" needs an intended interpretation that determines the truth of the sentence, which law, unlike arithmetic, does not supply.

(7) Miriam answered both. On openness: the same objection would prove that no informal mathematical practice is ever subject to the theorem, since mathematicians don't work inside a fixed formal system either; the standard reply is that the practice can be regimented, and a legislature could regiment adjudication too by specifying a closed list of interpretive canons and a fixed doctrine of precedent for the purposes of the clause — which, she said, is more or less what some jurisdictions already approximate. On inconsistency: even if the raw code conflicts with itself, you can look at the subsystem that survives the priority rules, and if that is consistent and rich enough to encode its own syntax, it is incomplete. And the subject-matter objection in (2) cannot be right in general: an undecidable sentence inherits its content from whatever the encoding is an encoding *of*, and here the encoding is of legal propositions.

(8) Theo said: grant her all of that and the result is still inert. What she would have shown is that there exists *some* sentence the code cannot decide, and that sentence would be a contrivance about the code's own derivability — not a contract dispute, not a custody question, not anything a legislature or a litigant cares about. The legal questions that actually go unsettled are unsettled for reasons that have nothing to do with diagonalization, so the theorem contributes nothing to the theory of law. Miriam's reply was that the existence claim *is* the point for her purposes, and that sentences about a code's own derivability are precisely the sentences that matter when the adjudicator is a machine: an automated judge applying the code has to determine whether the trigger fired, and if it cannot, the system has a case it cannot decide.
