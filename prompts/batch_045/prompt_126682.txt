my friend and I have been stuck on this for two weeks and I'm pretty sure we're arguing past each other, so I want a number with the machinery showing. Setup is invented, nothing to look up:

There are about 10^12 agents. Each one plays exactly one prisoners-dilemma against a partner drawn at random from the population — no repeats, no reputation, no communication, no causal contact of any other kind. Payoffs: 3/3 if both cooperate, 5/0 if you defect on a cooperator, 1/1 if both defect, 0 if you cooperate and get defected on. Each agent's utility is just its own payoff in its own game; nobody cares about the aggregate.

The agents came out of the same process and run the same deliberation algorithm, but each gets a private "flavor": an iid draw that nudges its temptation payoff up or down by a little. Two things to keep straight about that. From the outside, iid flavors plus a shared algorithm looks like a common-cause structure that could screen the agents' decisions off from each other. From the inside, no agent can fully introspect its own deliberation — it finds out what it decided the same way the rest of us do, by deciding — so its own action still carries information about what the other similar agents are up to.

Now the bit I've never seen anyone discuss, which is where our actual disagreement is. Before deciding, each agent may pay 0.01 utils — same price either way, paid whether or not you end up cooperating — to install one of two gadgets:

- a decoupler: your action is chosen by an external coin flip. Your action now carries zero information about your deliberation. My friend calls this "going opaque."
- a beacon: your action becomes somewhat more informative than it otherwise would be about what your deliberation was doing, i.e. it becomes better evidence about what the other similar agents are doing.

Neither gadget touches anyone's payoff directly. They only change what your action is evidence of. And — the part she says I'm hand-waving — the choice of which gadget to install, or none, is itself a decision made by the same correlated process, so it looks like it should also be evidence about whether everyone else installed one.

Her argument in short: go opaque, then defect. Once your action is a coin flip, cooperating buys you nothing evidentially, and defecting still nets you the 5 or the 1, so you strictly gain.
