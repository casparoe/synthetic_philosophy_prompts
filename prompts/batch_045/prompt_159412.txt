I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've walked into an argument I don't have the equipment to settle. I'd be grateful for a thought experiment built to order.

The argument is about blame in engineering teams. Two engineers jointly took on a piece of work months ago — say, co-owning a risky database migration or sharing an on-call rotation for a service only the two of them understand. The work went badly. Everyone agrees both of them acted; the live question is whether they did it *together*, in the sense that matters for what they owe each other — who is entitled to an apology, who can demand an accounting, whether the failure is something "we" did or merely two things each of them did side by side. Someone told me this is the territory of collective intentionality and joint action, and what I've pieced together is that there are two broad families of account:

The first family treats "we are doing this together" as nothing over and above an interlocking web of individual attitudes. Roughly: each person intends that they do the thing together, each intends to do their own part *because* of that, each is prepared to adjust to what the other is doing, and all of this is common knowledge between them. No such web, no joint action. I'm told this is more or less Michael Bratman's line, though I may be caricaturing it.

The second family treats joint action as a normative fact rather than a psychological one: two people can enter into a joint commitment — by an explicit or tacit expression of readiness — which constitutes them as a plural subject and generates obligations to one another that persist for as long as the commitment stands, whether or not anyone's current attitudes keep pace with it. I'm told this is more or less Margaret Gilbert's line. There seems also to be a third view, associated with John Searle, on which we-intending is an irreducible attitude that has to be there in each person's head.

What I want is a single scenario in which these accounts come apart in a specific direction: the attitude-based, reductive account should say that there is no shared intention and hence no joint action in the demanding sense, while the commitment-based account should say that there is a joint action and that the participants therefore owe something to each other. Constraints, because I want the case to be doing real work:

- Set it in ordinary engineering life — a migration, an incident, a review process, a rotation, a shared on-call. One paragraph, and ideally under 150 words. Assume a reader who knows the philosophy but not the tooling, and define any technical term you use.
- No thought-experiment furniture: no hypnosis, brain lesions, twins, aliens, demons, perfect predictors, or invented laws and institutions.
- The disagreement must not be bought cheaply. It cannot turn on anyone acting under threat, on a participant not knowing the other exists, on irrationality, on a contract or a reporting line, or on one of the "participants" being a bot or otherwise incapable of having intentions. It has to fall out of the ordinary structure of working together over time.
- Don't stipulate the attitudes. The scenario shouldn't contain a sentence of the form "she no longer intended that they do it together"; if a psychological claim is needed, it should be one a reader would naturally attribute from what the people do and say.
- What the participants owe each other has to be an output of the theories applied to the case, not written into the case itself.

Then, in under about 600 words, show that the design works:

1. For each of the two families, state the verdict and derive it from that account's own conditions, forwards rather than backwards from the conclusion. If it's useful, say where the we-intention-as-primitive view lands.
2. Name the single feature of the case that is doing the work.
3. Give the minimal variant: hold everything fixed, change that one feature, and tell me what happens — I expect the disagreement to collapse, but tell me whether it does, and in which direction.
4. Give the strongest version of the reply available to a defender of whichever account comes out looking bad here.
5. If my sketch of either family is a caricature, correct it before you use it, and say briefly how the correction changes the verdict.

Two last things. Please don't give me citations, bibliographies, or reading lists — I can't check them, and I'd rather have the reasoning. And if it helps to fix what counts as cheating: I want to use this case in a discussion with my team about whether shared blame in a postmortem tracks a live shared intention or a standing commitment that has outlasted it, so the case should be one my colleagues would recognize as a situation they have actually been in.
