I work on large-scale training and cluster infrastructure, and I am professionally fluent — not popular-science fluent — in the following vocabulary: distributed optimization and dual decomposition; parameter servers, gradient aggregation, and federated learning with local updates; schedulers with priority classes, preemption, and per-tenant quotas; gossip protocols and eventual consistency; offline policy evaluation versus online A/B testing; distribution shift and the sim-to-real gap; reward hacking and Goodhart effects; and the gap between a loss you can actually compute and an objective you actually care about. I can read an argument in that idiom as fast as in English.

I have been reading about the socialist calculation debate and I want it in that idiom. Assume the standard setup throughout: the means of production are publicly owned and cannot be bought or sold; there is money, there are wages, and there is an ordinary market in consumer goods. I don't want historiography or exegetical disputes about what anyone really meant, and I have no access to a library from where I'm writing, so please don't send me to sources — take the three positions as I state them below and work the arguments out.

Translate each of these, one at a time, as faithfully as you can:

1. Mises's claim that without markets in the means of production there is no way to perform economic calculation at all — that the difficulty is not practical but principled: with no exchange of the higher-order goods there is no common unit in which to commensurate heterogeneous inputs, and so no way to tell which of two production plans is the cheaper one.

2. Hayek's later version of the objection: that the knowledge relevant to the allocation is dispersed, local, time-sensitive, and partly inarticulate, so that the problem is not one of computation given the data but one of collecting and using knowledge that was never in anyone's possession in a form that could be reported upward.

3. Lange's trial-and-error model: a Central Planning Board announces prices, instructs managers to minimize average cost and to expand output until price equals marginal cost, then raises prices for goods in excess demand and lowers them for goods in excess supply, repeating until the system settles. Include the retrospective gloss on this model — that a sufficiently large computer simply runs the iteration fast enough for the delay to stop mattering.

For each of the three, give me the translation and then the residue report: what maps cleanly onto something in my vocabulary, what you had to bend or idealize to make it fit, and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all. Where the translation requires an assumption my framework does not supply — an externally grounded reward signal, a convergence guarantee, an agent that wants something, a fixed objective — say so explicitly rather than quietly importing it and presenting the result as a translation.

Then run my own proposal through the same procedure. Call it the settlement-free price board. Prices do not come from the Board's own observation of excess demand but from a real-money prediction market: anyone may take a position on how much of each input the Board will actually release to firms in a given quarter, contracts settle in cash against the realized quantity, and position limits keep any single trader too small to influence the physical allocation by trading. Firms are instructed to treat those prices as their costs, to minimize shadow cost, and to expand until shadow price equals shadow marginal cost. Managers receive a bonus equal to a fixed fraction of measured shadow profit, paid in real consumption goods. My intuition is that this extracts Hayek's dispersed knowledge out of the managers' heads and into the prices without restoring private property in factories, and that it dissolves the Mises problem because the prices are a genuinely grounded cardinal signal rather than an accounting fiction. I suspect it breaks somewhere in the loop between the forecast and the quantity being forecast, but I cannot say where, or how badly, or whether it breaks for Mises's reason or Hayek's or for some third reason that neither of them would have recognised.

Finally, give me a verdict on the residue as a whole: does what refuses to translate point to something my vocabulary cannot say, or to something the debate itself never made clear?
