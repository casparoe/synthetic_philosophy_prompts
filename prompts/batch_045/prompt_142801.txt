I am a software engineer — mostly backend and ML infrastructure, no formal philosophy training — and I have been asked to write the technical recommendation for a deployment decision I do not feel equipped to reason about. I would like you to translate the relevant debate into the vocabulary I actually work in, and then tell me honestly what the translation loses.

**The system.** Each quarter we score about 5,000 patients on predicted risk of an avoidable hospital admission within 90 days, and we can enroll 200 of them in a care-management program. One number per patient feeds two downstream consumers: a ranked list from which the top 200 are enrolled, and an automatic flag above a fixed score that triggers a pharmacist review. Group A and group B differ in base rate (roughly 18 percent versus 9 percent). The model is retrained monthly. Enrolled patients receive an intervention that changes the outcome, so we only observe clean ground truth for patients we *did not* enroll.

**Three demanded invariants.** (C) Clinical staff want the score calibrated within each group: among patients assigned 0.2 in group A and among patients assigned 0.2 in group B, about 20 percent should have the outcome. They say they cannot reason about a number whose meaning shifts with the patient's group. (E) Compliance wants equal false-negative rates across groups: of the patients who do have the outcome, the same fraction must be missed in each group. (S) Our own team wants continuity across retrains: a patient already on the waitlist should not be pushed down the list by a change in the model rather than a change in their file. There is also an unspoken fourth demand that no one has written down, which is that the score keep a single public meaning usable by both downstream consumers.

**What I have read.** Summaries of the standard incompatibility results in algorithmic fairness: that calibration together with equal false-positive and equal false-negative rates cannot all hold unless the base rates are equal or the model is perfect; that a criterion requiring similar individuals to be treated similarly conflicts with the group-level criteria unless some strong assumption about the underlying quantity holds; and that deleting the group attribute from the feature set does not buy anything, because the information is redundantly encoded in the remaining fields.

**The vocabulary I am fluent in.** Property-based tests and invariants asserted over a deployment; test suites that provably pass only on degenerate inputs; multi-objective optimization, Pareto frontiers, and the difference between a loss you minimize and a constraint you satisfy; pick-two results of the CAP-theorem shape; sliced monitoring dashboards, SLAs, and error budgets; interfaces and contracts between a producer of a number and its consumers; the difference between a post-processing wrapper and a change to the decision rule itself; canary deploys, drift, and model versioning; A/B tests and interventions, at the level of a practitioner rather than a theorist. I am comfortable with the idea that two metrics cannot both be optimized, and I am skeptical of arguments that dress that up as a theorem.

**What I want.**

First, a translation. Restate each of the fairness criteria above, and the incompatibility results connecting them, in that vocabulary. For each criterion: write it as an assertion over the running system, and classify it as a property of the score's *representation* or of the *allocation* the system actually makes. I have a suspicion I want checked: that calibration is the kind of property a monotone post-processing wrapper can always fix per group, so it is cheap, whereas equal error rates are properties of the allocation and are not cheap, and that the standard presentations put these two side by side as if they were the same kind of thing when they are not. If that is wrong or only half right, say so, and say what it costs us that per-group recalibration also reorders the ranking.

Second, a residue report. Which of these demands has no clean expression in my vocabulary at all — and for each, tell me whether that is because my vocabulary is missing something or because the demand was less well defined than it looked. I am especially interested in: what kind of object a protected group is, given that it is not a property of the system or of the data but a designation imposed on both; whether the individual-level criterion can even be stated without an untestable similarity oracle; and whether the claim that one kind of error matters more than another is an engineering claim in disguise or something my framework simply cannot see.

Third, the parts of my setup the standard statements do not cover. My decision is a top-k selection under a fixed quota rather than a threshold, and calibration is not invariant under monotone reparameterization of the score, so it is not obvious to me which of these criteria are even meaningful when only the ordering matters. One score feeds two consumers with different needs. There is an intertemporal dimension — retrains, waitlists, promises made to patients — that the static statements say nothing about. And our ground truth is censored by our own interventions. For each: say whether the relevant incompatibility still has an analogue, what that analogue would assert, and whether it is a real constraint or a tautology.

Finally, a recommendation stated in engineering terms: which invariant we should drop, what we would be adopting by dropping it, and an explicit boundary marker at the point where the engineering reasoning stops and we are making a value judgment that my vocabulary has no resources to settle.
