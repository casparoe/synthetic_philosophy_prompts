I have no formal training in philosophy, but I've been reading about the philosophy of mind in my spare time, and I've become stuck on an argument that seems to me to make David Chalmers's distinction between the hard problem of consciousness and the meta-problem unusable. I'd be grateful if you could show me where it goes wrong.

As I understand the distinction, the hard problem is the problem of explaining why physical goings-on are accompanied by experience at all, while the meta-problem is the problem of explaining why we — ordinary people, and philosophers in particular — say, judge and insist the things we do about experience: that it has a felt, subjective character, that no account in terms of functions or information would capture it, that there is a real explanatory gap. My worry is that the second problem collapses into one of two things depending on how the first one is resolved, and so is not a separate object of study at all.

The argument I keep returning to is this. Every piece of evidence anyone has ever had for the existence of consciousness is someone's claim about their own case: a report, an avowal, a judgment, a bit of verbal behaviour. If consciousness is real, then the meta-problem has a one-line solution — we report being conscious because we are conscious and can tell that we are — and anything further is just psychology of an ordinary kind, continuous with the easy problems. If, on the other hand, someone produces a complete account of the mechanisms that generate those reports, judgments and avowals, and that account nowhere needs to mention phenomenal properties, then it explains everything that was ever going to count as evidence, and the rational conclusion is illusionism: there is nothing there of the sort we thought there was. So the meta-problem is either trivial or decisive. Either way, nobody can sensibly work on it without having already settled the hard problem, which is exactly what it was supposed to help settle.

I also don't understand a related move I keep running into: the suggestion that a materialist solution to the meta-problem would somehow weaken the case for the hard problem even without showing that consciousness doesn't exist. Surely explaining why we hold a belief is not by itself a reason to doubt it; that is just the genetic fallacy. An account of the origins of our phenomenal judgments, however complete, tells us nothing about whether those judgments are true, so how could it count against the reality of experience?

And one smaller thing: I've read that a realist is supposed to be able to solve the meta-problem as well. But if the realist's solution is anything more than the one-liner, doesn't it already tell us how consciousness is connected to the mechanisms of judgment and reporting — which is the hard problem itself? Is there anything substantive for a realist to say here that leaves the hard problem open either way?
