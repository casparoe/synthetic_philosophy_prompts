I'm helping put together the programme for our department's Philosophy in Public night — grad students reading short pieces to a slightly drunk audience of undergrads and the odd townie — and I want to contribute something on the sorites. I TA the intro logic course where we spend two weeks on the paradox and every year half the class comes away convinced it's just a trick question, so I'd like something that gets the structure of the problem right enough that the people in my department who work on vagueness laugh for the right reasons, while still landing for someone who has never heard of it.

What I have in mind is a fake internal document — a memo, a circular, a standing operating procedure, whatever fits — from a bureaucracy that has to apply the word heap in earnest. My working idea is a national grain standards authority that sets import tariffs according to whether a shipment is a heap, but if a customs office, an insurance claims department, or a museum acquisitions committee deciding when a pile of rubble becomes a monument works better, use that instead. Somewhere around 700 to 1000 words, deadpan throughout: nobody in the document ever notices that anything philosophical is going on, the word vagueness never appears, and the comedy comes from the real shape of the problem rather than from anyone being stupid. A few things I'd specifically like worked in, in whatever order the document naturally reaches them: the tolerance principle as an actual rule of procedure (a clause to the effect that a single grain cannot make the difference), followed by someone noticing that the rule run backwards seems to make an empty warehouse a heap and dealing with that administratively rather than logically — I'd love a clause banning the reverse induction; a sharp cutoff written into the regulations at some satisfyingly specific number of grains, asserted with total confidence and no argument, plus the authority's completely straight-faced insistence that there is a fact of the matter about where the cutoff is; a supervaluationist episode, where a shipment qualifies only if it is a heap under every admissible precisification, almost nothing qualifies, and the fix is to restrict which precisifications count as admissible; a degree-theoretic episode, with partial heap status, pro-rated tariffs, and a heapiness surcharge that someone tries to appeal; higher-order vagueness as a second-tier review board for borderline borderline cases, and then the moment where that board needs it's own borderline category; and one place where two branches of the same bureaucracy apply the word to the same shipment under different standards without anybody treating that as odd. Give it some invented institutional furniture too — named circulars, a schedule of admissible readings, an annex that has been deferred indefinitely, a footnote that cites an earlier circular which itself cites a later one, that sort of thing.

End with the document in a state of collapse that it presents as completely routine — the amendment trail obviously about to repeat forever, say. Nobody should break character to explain the paradox, and no winking at the reader on the way out.
