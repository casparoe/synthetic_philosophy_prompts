I convene a small graduate reading group on strategic reasoning, and we are closing the term with a session on backward induction, the centipede game, and the family of paradoxes around it. The setup we have been working with: at each node one player decides whether to take, which ends the game and gives that player the larger share of the pot on the table, or to pass, which lets the pot grow and hands the move to the other player — so that both would end up far richer if they passed nearly to the end, and the last mover has an unanswerable reason to take. I would like to end not with a summary but with a poem I can hand out, and I have a fairly specific idea of what it should be.

Write a sestina: six stanzas of six lines, unrhymed, a steady ten-to-twelve-syllable line, closed by a three-line envoi. Take the six end-words from the furniture of the problem — I would suggest TAKE, PASS, LAST, COUNT, TRUST, MORE, carrying respectively the defection, the continuation, the terminal node, the number of rungs still to come, reliance on the other, and the pot that swells with every pass — though you may swap one or two for close synonyms if grammar demands it, provided the roles survive. Two structural commitments matter to me. First, let the stanzas walk backwards along the tree: begin at the final node, where the mover simply takes, and step one node earlier with each stanza, so that the opening move is the last thing the reader reaches and the poem ends where the game begins, which is also where the induction's verdict lands. Second, let the sestina's rotation do the work of the argument: words returning in shifted positions, one node at a time, is the induction itself, and the envoi, where all six crowd into three lines, is the moment the whole chain is visible at once. No jargon in the poem itself — the theory should live in the structure and the images.

On content, I want it to survive a game theorist's reading. Ground the unravelling in what actually grounds it — common knowledge of rationality and of the payoffs, not rationality alone — and be honest that the paradox is not that people are irrational but that the premise the argument needs at each node is the very thing each observed pass calls into question. If you gesture at laboratory play, do it qualitatively; no invented numbers or percentages, no citations, no named theorems or authors anywhere. The twist I want the poem to think with, and to make its hinge: suppose the players are hiding nothing from each other — two copies of one bargaining program in separate boxes, each handed a read-only view of the other's source before it moves, with no channel for messages and no way to bind itself. Then the usual escape hatch is sealed; there is no private type, no hidden generosity, no possibility that the other simply likes passing, because its motives are as legible to it as to itself. What remains to break the chain is not doubt about the other's ends but doubt about the frame: that it has miscounted how many rungs are left, that the node it reads as merely late is, for it, nowhere near the last. Please render this as a genuine alternative to the familiar small-chance-of-a-cooperator story, and make the contrast tell — an error about how many rungs remain is a bug one might find by reading, whereas a preference for passing is something one could only weigh. But do not let it turn into a rescue: on either story the unravelling is delayed, not repealed, and the ladder still ends in a taking. Leave us, in the envoi, with the specific open question of whether what we should give up is the premise or the conclusion — and let the poem stand as a poem for anyone who has never heard of the centipede.
