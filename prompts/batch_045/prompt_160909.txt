I'm writing a dissertation chapter in philosophy of biology on how function attributions actually work in molecular and evolutionary biology, and I need help drafting one section of it. To fix the positions: on the selected-effects (etiological) account, a trait's function is the effect for which it was selected; on the causal-role (systemic) account, a trait's function is the contribution it makes to some capacity of a containing system we are analysing. My supervisor holds the former, a collaborator in the department holds the latter, and after two years of talking past each other they have agreed, at my insistence, to an adversarial collaboration. I have been asked to ghost-write the joint statement. Please draft it.

What I need:

1. A short glossary settling the terminology — function, proper function, malfunction, design, selected effect, system — in wording both sides could sign, since part of the stalemate has plainly been terminological.

2. A numbered agreed-claims section: what both accept about paradigm cases (a heart's pumping blood), about the distinction between a function and a mere effect, about malfunction, about function attributions at gene, cell, organism and group levels, and about artefacts and engineered systems. No vacuous claims — if a claim would be signed by everyone in the philosophy of language as well, cut it.

3. A disagreements table: one row per residual disagreement, with the etiological side's own formulation and the causal-role side's own formulation, each phrased so that side would accept it as their view, plus your verdict on whether that row is a genuine disagreement or a merely verbal one, with a one-line reason.

4. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one side. I expect three or four; I would rather have three real ones than seven padded ones.

5. For each crux, the case, measurement or argument both parties would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction about it written down in advance. Please build these around the following stipulated cases rather than the standard textbook ones:

- The hitchhiking twin: two present-day populations fixed for the same allele with identical causal profiles, where in one population the allele spread because of the effect in question and in the other it rode along with a sweep at a linked site, that effect being fitness-irrelevant.
- The first-generation gene: a de novo protein-coding gene arising from previously non-coding sequence that improves fitness immediately, considered at the generation it appears and at ten, hundred and thousand generations later.
- The selfish element: a transposon whose replication raises its own representation while lowering organismal fitness.
- The co-opted-and-degenerate trait: a structure that now contributes effect Y, was historically selected for effect X, and no longer produces X at all.
- A directed-evolution lineage: an enzyme designed from scratch and then optimised in vitro over many rounds, with no organismal history at any point.

6. A closing note on your own confidence: for each crux, how confident you are that settling it would in fact move at least one side, and which single claim in the document you are least sure of. Flag any place where your drafting relies on a contingent empirical assumption about biology — for instance about how common de novo gene birth is, or how tightly current contribution tracks selection history in real genomes — and say how the document would change if that assumption were false.

Please argue from the concepts rather than from the literature: I do not want citations, quotations or a reading list, since I will supply those myself, and I want the document to stand or fall on whether the arguments in it are good.
