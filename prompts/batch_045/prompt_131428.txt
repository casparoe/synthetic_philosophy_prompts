I have no formal training in philosophy. I came to this on my own, through a meditation group and through arguments with a friend who grew up Hindu, and everyone in that circle reaches for the same pair of analogies when karma comes up: it is a ledger, or a bank balance, or a debt — and it is like gravity. As they use them, the point of both is to take the moralism out. Nobody is punishing you, no judge is deciding your sentence, there is no need for a cosmic bookkeeper; what comes back to you is simply an account being settled, as impersonal as a stone falling. My friend then uses this to defuse the objection I keep raising, which is that the whole apparatus looks like a license for blaming people who suffer: if a child is born into a short and painful life, the ledger picture invites people to say she must have run up the debt in a life she cannot remember. His reply is that the objection only bites if karma is a judge handing down sentences, and that on the gravity picture there is no blame anywhere — only cause and effect — so saying she earned it is just a moralistic way of stating a causal fact.

What I want is an audit of the analogies rather than the verdict I already expect.

First, set the mapping out explicitly; a table is fine. On the ledger side: what plays the role of the debtor, the creditor, the debt, the currency, the payment, the interest, the discharge, the statute of limitations, bankruptcy, and the transfer of a debt to a third party? On the gravity side: mass, distance, force, the falling body, the trajectory. I also want to know which of the two analogies is actually carrying the weight at the point where my friend uses them, since I suspect they are doing different and partly incompatible jobs, and that he switches between them without noticing.

Second, the places each breaks — and, which is the part I care about, which of those breaks matter for the work he is making the analogy do and which are cosmetic. Three that I have been turning over myself:

One, currency. Money is a token: it stands in for anything, which is why paying it can settle a debt of any kind. Suffering does not look like a token for anything. It does not restore what was taken, it does not compensate the person who was harmed, and the person harmed is not even a party to the transaction. If the payment cannot do what payments do, what is it doing? And I notice that when I press this, my friend quietly changes source domain and starts describing the suffering as a teacher, or a medicine, or a purification — which is a school or a hospital, not a ledger. Please name that switch and assess it: is it a repair of the analogy or an abandonment of it?

Two, the debtor who cannot read the account. A real ledger is checkable. You can be told what you owe and why, and you can dispute an entry. The person paying in a later life has no memory of the act she is charged with and no way to audit the books — the settlement lands on someone who is, for every purpose either of them can access, a different customer from the one who incurred it. Does the ledger analogy smuggle in an entitlement to an itemized statement that karma cannot supply, and is that a fatal break or an incidental one?

Three, transfer and inheritance. Debts can be assumed and paid by others; as I understand it, some Buddhist practice involves dedicating the merit of a good deed to someone else, often a dead relative. If karma is a debt, that looks like settling someone else's bill, which a ledger permits and which desert, I would have thought, does not. So does the analogy commit its users to transferability, and what happens to the account if it does?

Third, and this is what I most want: a distinction I arrived at on my own and cannot get anyone to engage with. Suppose two people do the same cruel thing, indistinguishable from the outside. One was never in a position to know better, was never taught, and acted on an impulse she had no realistic chance of resisting. The other knew exactly what she was doing and chose it. On the ledger reading, and on the gravity reading as my friend uses them, the two entries are identical: the act is the act, the debt is the debt, gravity does not care about your excuses. But a theory of who deserves what — as opposed to a theory of how much damage has been done and needs cleaning up — is supposed to care about precisely that difference. So I have started separating two readings. Call it the bookkeeping reading: karma records and settles what is owed in the world, and would look exactly the same in a universe where nobody ever had a choice about anything. Call it the formation reading: the point of the mechanism is what the act makes of the person who does it — her character, her habits, her capacity to see clearly — and what returns to her has the shape of that, rather than being a price. I want to know whether the distinction is genuine or whether I have reinvented something that already has a name I don't know; how each reading fares against the ledger and the gravity analogies; and, since the whole purpose of my friend's use of them is to take the blame out of suffering, whether either reading can actually deliver that. Or whether the no-blame conclusion was never coming from the analogies at all, and is instead a separate claim being carried in by the word impersonal.

If some other source domain does the job he wants, I would like to hear it; if no analogy can, say so plainly. And please don't give me a reading list or point me at texts — I'm doing this on my own and have no library access, so I need the reasoning itself.
