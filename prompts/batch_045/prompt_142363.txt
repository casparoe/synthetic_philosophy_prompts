I'm preparing the session of a graduate seminar on Scanlon's contractualism in which we take up the aggregation objection, and I want the dialectic straight before I walk into the room. One of my students has written a paper defending a fix, and I'd like to be able both to characterise the fix fairly and, if it fails, to say exactly where it fails.

Let me lay out the pieces as I see them. The objection: contractualism tests principles by asking what each person could reasonably reject, and it does this by considering each person's complaint on its own, never adding complaints together. It therefore seems unable to capture the thought that a large number of small burdens can outweigh one large one, or that it can be wrong to save one stranger rather than five when everyone's individual claim to be saved is the same. The standard contractualist reply, as I teach it, has two halves: numbers can enter indirectly, because when no one's complaint is stronger than anyone else's there may be no way of giving each person's equal claim its due other than saving the greater number; and an individual's reasons can themselves contain a comparative element, a reason not to be left much worse off than others.

The student's proposal is to build the second half into the test rather than leaving it to the content of reasons. Call it margin-plus-sum contractualism. Where two candidate principles (or two outcomes) are compared pairwise, first look at the largest individual complaint each generates. If these differ by more than a fixed margin m, the one with the smaller largest complaint wins. If they differ by no more than m, treat them as tied on that dimension and instead compare the sums of complaints, the smaller sum winning.

What I want from you is a clear conceptual map of what this proposal is actually doing. In particular:

First, restate why the aggregation objection bites in the first place — why, given the shape of the reasonable-rejectability test, it is not merely a complaint about a few verdicts but a challenge to the structure of contractualist justification. I want the underlying reason, not the list of cases.

Second, work the student's rule through three sorts of case: many trivial burdens against one severe one; five lives against one life with all else equal; and a case in which each of the many is slightly worse off than the one, but the many vastly outnumber the one. Say where the rule gives the intuitively right answer, where it gives it for the wrong reason, and where the answer depends on the size of m.

Third — and this is the part I care most about — the worrying structural feature. The relation the rule defines is a pairwise one with a tolerance built in, and tolerances of that kind are the usual source of intransitivity. I want you to determine whether the rule can generate cycles across three options (three candidate principles, or three outcomes), where A beats B, B beats C, and C beats A; to show why, if it can; and then to say whether this matters. Does the contractualist framework need there to be a single least-rejectable principle, so that a cyclic relation is fatal? Or can contractualism coherently treat reasonable rejectability as a non-transitive comparative notion, in which case the cycle is a curiosity? Is there a cheap modification that eliminates cycling without collapsing the rule back into plain summing?

Fourth, say whether the rule preserves what was supposed to be distinctive about contractualism — the insistence that each person's complaint be taken separately, so that no one is asked to bear a large burden for the sake of a total that no one experiences — or whether the margin is simply a rounding device that reinstates aggregative ranking under a contractualist label, drawing its plausibility from a judicious choice of examples.

Assume throughout that complaints can be made interpersonally comparable in the way the rule requires; I don't want that worry, which is a different problem. Please don't give me citations or a reading list — I know the literature and will supply references myself. Aim for something I could hand to a strong graduate student, around 800 to 1000 words, prose plus whatever explicit statement of the rule and of the cycling example is needed; keep the formalism to a minimum, but do make the three-way comparison concrete, with numbers.

Finish with a single sentence stating the bottom line about whether the fix succeeds, and then a final line, on its own, consisting only of \answer{...} whose content is one of: FATAL, REPAIRABLE, or ILLUSORY — fatal if no fix of this shape can work, repairable if a variant can be made to work, illusory if what looked like a contractualist solution turns out to be aggregation in disguise.
