I'm a graduate student in philosophy, and this term I'm leading discussion sections on Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism." Someone in my section pointed out that the 2006 reclassification of Pluto looks like a real instance of a scientific community arguing in public about whether a question was one of meaning or one of fact, and I've been trying to work out whether that observation is illuminating or merely cute. So I'd like a careful case analysis rather than a slogan. The facts I'd work from are these. In 2006, at its General Assembly in Prague, the International Astronomical Union adopted a definition according to which a planet in the Solar System is a body that orbits the Sun, has enough mass to pull itself into a nearly round shape, and has cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit; bodies satisfying only the first two conditions were to be called dwarf planets. Pluto — discovered by Tombaugh in 1930 and listed as the ninth planet for seventy-six years — fell into the new category, and the resolution passed on a show of hands near the end of the meeting, with only a small fraction of the union's members in the room. The trigger was the discovery, a few years earlier, of Eris and other Kuiper Belt objects of comparable size, which forced the choice between admitting an open-ended number of new planets and demoting Pluto; and the IAU's own committee had floated three rival criteria — cultural (a planet is whatever enough people call a planet), structural (roundness), and dynamical (orbital dominance) — of which the third won. There was also a precedent: when Piazzi found Ceres on 1 January 1801 it was hailed as the missing planet between Mars and Jupiter, but within a couple of decades, as Pallas, Juno, Vesta and others accumulated and the count passed twenty, astronomers quietly recatalogued the whole group as asteroids; Ceres has since been reclassified again, this time as a dwarf planet. Since 2006 planetary scientists have repeatedly pressed for an alternative, geophysical definition keyed to a body's intrinsic properties rather than to what else happens to be nearby, and the public has never really accepted the demotion.

What I want is the mapping run in both directions, and I want it disciplined. Take the first dogma: was the dispute over Pluto's status a disagreement about the meaning of the word "planet" or about the facts about Pluto? Give me an account of what each side was claiming in those terms, and tell me where the distinction fails to grip: the winning criterion is dynamical rather than intrinsic, but it seems to have been chosen because it delivers a small, stable, teachable list; whether a body clears its zone is a matter of degree and depends on where the body orbits, so that a Mars-sized object in the inner Oort cloud would apparently fail; and both the defenders and the critics of the definition argued from consequences, about whether the taxonomy still carved nature at the joints. Then take the second dogma: what would it even be for the sentence "Pluto is not a planet" to be answerable to some isolable range of confirming experiences, and what happens to that picture once you notice that the definition is entangled with the rest of astronomy's commitments as well as with textbook, museum and mission-planning ones? And I want an honest statement of what the episode does to Quine's two dogmas themselves. My own hypothesis, which I'd like tested rather than flattered, is that the case is not evidence against the analytic–synthetic distinction but evidence about where it does its work: it looks like a textbook case of Carnapian explication, where a community stipulates a more exact replacement for a vague term and then argues about fruitfulness and simplicity rather than truth. Is that right, or does it concede too much to Quine?

To make the question sharper, here is a test I've been playing with. Call it the reclassification test: a term's application is settled by meaning rather than by fact only if competent users, faced with new evidence, would treat a resulting change in application as a correction of their beliefs rather than as a change of subject — that is, only if they are disposed to say "we were wrong about Pluto" and not "the word now means something different." Apply that test to Ceres between 1801 and mid-century and to Pluto in 2006, and tell me whether it delivers the answers people actually gave, and whether those answers are consistent. Note the complication that ordinary speakers overwhelmingly took the second option about Pluto: they continued to call it a planet and treated the IAU as having changed the subject, which is precisely what the committee's discarded "cultural" criterion would have predicted, and which suggests that the folk term had a meaning the official one was supposed to replace. Does that confirm Quine's claim that no principled line can be drawn between the linguistic and the factual components of a statement's truth, or does it instead show that a community can and does make that line explicit by an act of stipulation — and that the interesting questions are then pragmatic rather than semantic? Finally, tell me where the framework is silent. In particular: does anything in Quine's argument, or in Carnap's, tell us who has the standing to stipulate, or what makes one body's decision authoritative over another's? I suspect the answer is no, and that this silence is the most useful thing the case reveals — but I'd like that argued rather than asserted.
