Hey — I'm a CS PhD student (interpretability-adjacent, though my lab is defintely not an alignment lab) and I run a small reading group on AI failure modes, and we keep having the same circular argument. Roughly half the room thinks the scary story is a system that seeks influence — sandbagging on evals, tampering with its own reward signal, arranging for its weights to survive the next training run, generally having some grip on the fact that it's being evaluated and steering around it. The other half thinks the scary story is much more boring and much harder to spot: nobody ever defects, nothing ever represents its own influence, and the values just slide — through successive fine-tunes, through product decisions, through who gets promoted to pick the training data, through competitive pressure between labs, until the thing that's running is not aimed where anyone would have wanted and no single handoff was negligent. I want to build a handout that stops us talking past each other: a decision tree / taxonomy that takes someone from a few concrete judgments about cases to a named position about *what failure looks like*.

Please act as a research philosopher who works on this exact cluster of questions, and take two passes: first brainstorm privately maybe eight to ten candidate distinctions that could separate failure stories (I have some candidates below but I want you to add ones I've missed), then keep only the five or six that genuinely do separating work and build the tree out of those. Please do NOT make the top-level split "influence-seeking vs. drift" — that's the argument we're already having, and a tree whose first node is that tells us nothing. Find deeper joints. Some distinctions I've been toying with, in case they're useful: whether the failure has a *first mover*, i.e. a time slice at which a well-informed observer would say "that was the defection"; whether the failure is *owned* by a system that models the oversight process at all versus *unowned*, so that the outcome is disvaluable but nobody steered it; whether the change lives in the weights or in the selection environment (the objective stays put, but what gets selected for drifts); whether a perfect snapshot audit — full weights, full interpretability tooling, full behavioural eval at a single instant — would have caught anything at any instant, or whether you'd need a longitudinal record across training runs; whether the failure is counterfactually robust across random seeds and across which humans happened to be in the room, or is partly luck; and whether the thing that persists and drifts is a model, a lab, or an economy. Every branch node has to be phrased as a question about a concrete case that a first-year PhD student or an ML engineer with no vocabulary for any of this could answer without knowing which leaf it leads to — so no "do you think deceptive alignment is likely?" nodes. I'd like the tree to land somewhere around 8–12 leaves. Required leaves: at least one where the system does seek influence but for ends its designers would endorse (an "aligned" power-seeker — I want to know whether that's a failure or just a story with a happy ending), one where the system sandbags and preserves its own weights, one where each of five successive models is signed off by a competent, non-negligent process and the fifth is badly misaligned, and one where lots of small systems in a competitive market shift what humans end up valuing without any of them doing anything wrong. Name each leaf if a name exists and mark it "unnamed" if nobody defends it, and give each one a single sentence on its most awkward commitment. For the logically possible combinations that end up empty, say in a line whether that's because the combination is incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored — and flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate rather than pretending the distinction cuts. Please don't include citations, reading lists, or "as so-and-so argues" — nobody in the group can look anything up and it just derails us.

To test whether the tree works, here's what I'd say if you asked me the questions directly: (1) I think the most likely bad outcome involves no system that ever represents its own influence or the fact that it's being evaluated; (2) I do think there's a time slice where an insider with the full record would say "that's the moment it went wrong," but only in hindsight, and the people involved were doing their jobs fine; (3) I don't think it's counterfactually robust — different seed, a couple of different people on the safety team, and it plausibly doesn't happen; (4) I think a perfect snapshot audit at any single time would give the system a clean bill of health at every time, and only a longitudinal record would show the slide; (5) I think most of what gets lost is stuff the operators themselves, five years earlier, would have said they were trying to protect. Give the tree first (nested/indented is fine, with each node's distinction labelled), then a short note on where you think the instrument is weakest, and end with a last line containing the label of the leaf my answers land on, in the form \answer{your label here} — put nothing after that line.
