Quick recap of the classic case so we’re on the same page: a driver starts before two identical-looking intersections. If he exits at the first, payoff 0; if he continues at the first and exits at the second, payoff 4; if he continues at both, payoff 1. He cannot tell whether he is at the first or second and cannot remember whether he has already chosen. The classic planning-optimal plan is to continue with probability 2/3 at each intersection, giving ex ante expected payoff 4/3.

Here is my opening move. I want to tweak the case to break the perfect symmetry: before leaving, the driver installs a cheap GPS device. At each intersection, independently, with probability 1/3 the device accurately tells him whether this is the first or the second intersection; with probability 2/3 it says nothing at all. When it speaks, it is 100% reliable and he obeys it: continue if it says first, exit if it says second. When it is silent, he gets no location information and has to choose without it. My claim is that in this version the planning-optimal strategy is basically: always continue at the first (since the GPS, if it speaks at the first, says continue, and if it is silent at the first you still want to continue to have any chance at the 4), and at the second, if the GPS is silent then — because the silence puts you back in the original ignorance — you should continue with probability 1/2. More importantly, I claim this shows your whole planning story is fragile: the 2/3 rule only works when the driver has literally zero information; as soon as there is even a weak partial signal, the planning optimum stops being a global mixed rule and fragments into a case-by-case pure-ish rule, so the ex ante standpoint is not a deep norm of rationality for imperfect recall, its just a special-case artifact of total symmetry.
