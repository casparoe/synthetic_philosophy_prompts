After more than forty years in experimental particle physics — the last two decades of it given over to precision measurements, unfolding and efficiency corrections, nuisance parameters, and the standing quarrel about whether to quote Bayesian credible intervals or frequentist confidence intervals — I have retired and begun reading philosophy on my own, slowly and without much supervision. I have now reached the doomsday argument, and I find it at once entirely familiar in form and completely unlike anything I would recognise as a measurement. I would like you to translate it into the vocabulary I actually think in, and then to tell me, without politeness, where the translation breaks down.

The datum, as I understand it: I find that I am approximately the hundred-billionth member of the species Homo sapiens, in the sense that on the order of 10^11 humans appear to have lived before me (the precise figure is not load-bearing). The quantity of interest is the total number of humans who will ever live. The argument concludes that this total is probably not vastly larger than the number to date — that our future is more likely to be short than enormous — and that this follows from my birth rank alone, irrespective of whatever else I happen to believe about technology, resources, or history.

Three things, please.

First, the translation. Write it as I would write a note to a collaboration: specify the observable, the model, the likelihood, the prior, the posterior, and say what the conclusion becomes when stated that way. I am fluent in the following and would like you to draw on it rather than on philosophical idiom: estimating an unknown population size from the serial numbers or ordinal positions of sampled items; priors over a scale parameter that is unbounded above; the fact that a single uniform draw yields a likelihood proportional to 1/N and a posterior that simply tracks prior/N; sufficient statistics; coverage and the ensembles over which coverage statements are defined; stopping rules and the likelihood principle; model comparison between rival sampling protocols; and hierarchical treatments in which the sampling protocol itself is a nuisance parameter to be marginalised over. If some piece of the argument has no counterpart in that vocabulary, say plainly that it has none; do not paper over the gap by importing philosophical jargon and calling it a translation.

Second, the residue report. I would like it itemised, and sorted into three bins: what carries over without distortion; what carries over only after being bent — where you had to assume something the argument does not license, or quietly identify two things the argument keeps apart; and what cannot be expressed at all. For each item in the last bin I want your view on which is at fault: my vocabulary, because it is built for measurements of external systems and lacks resources for this kind of datum; or the argument, because the untranslatable residue was never a well-defined claim in the first place.

Third, a verdict on my own reconstruction, which I suspect is too deflationary and would like checked. I have come to think that the entire content of the argument is: take your prior over the total number of humans, multiply it by 1/N, renormalise. And I have come to think that the central dispute among philosophers of the argument — between treating your rank as a uniform random draw from the set of all observers, and weighting hypotheses according to how many observers they contain — is, in my terms, a disagreement about whether that factor belongs in the likelihood or is cancelled by a reweighting of the prior. The first looks to me like sampling a fixed population; the second looks like a search or trigger that is guaranteed to fire whenever the hypothesis contains any observers at all, so that the existence of a record carries no information. If that is right, does the argument not collapse into the reminder that your prior is doing all the work — which in my field would be a reason for declining to quote a central value rather than a reason for announcing one?

Two further difficulties, which I cannot see how to express in my own terms and which are, I think, where the real interest lies.

The first is indexicality. In every measurement I have ever made, the apparatus is not among the events it records. Here the datum is not a reading taken on an external system but the fact of which element of the sample the apparatus itself happens to be. There is no ensemble over which I can check the calibration of the estimator; the experiment cannot be repeated; there is no independent channel by which the total could be cross-checked; and no experimental design fixes the population from which I was drawn, so that the choice of what philosophers call the reference class looks exactly like a nuisance parameter that nobody has specified and nothing in the data constrains. Is the self-locating character of the datum a genuine blind spot in my vocabulary — something the philosophers have a name for and handle properly and I simply cannot say — or is it the point at which the argument ceases to be an inference and becomes a stipulation about priors?

The second is what a physicist would do next. When one estimator is dominated by its prior and cannot be validated, one does not abandon the question; one looks for another channel. Here the analogue would be independent evidence about how long technological civilisations typically last, or about how many there are, delivered by a route that does not pass through my birth rank. Suppose such a channel existed and supplied a genuine likelihood. Would the doomsday argument then simply be one term in a joint fit, and would its weight turn out to be negligible next to any real data? Is there any reading on which it is more than that? And is there, anywhere in the philosophical literature on this argument, anything that corresponds to a bona fide estimator with checkable coverage properties — or is the entire debate conducted above the level of the likelihood?

Please keep the answer in the region of fifteen hundred words, and keep it addressed to me rather than to a philosophy seminar: use my vocabulary where it works, and mark the places where it does not.
