I’m teaching a third-year philosphy of AI seminar — 16 students, one 90-minute slot — and I want an in-class activity that makes them feel the force of the bit in Turing’s 1950 paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence where he worries about extrasensory perception messing with the imitation game. I definately don’t want a lecture up front, and they shouldn’t hear the words telepathy, clairvoyance or extrasensory perception, or be told anything about Turing’s own reply, until the debrief at the end.

As I understand the target idea: the imitation game is supposed to tell us something about a machine by looking only at what comes out through a narrow, official channel. Turing takes seriously the possibility that, if ESP were real, information or influence could pass by some hidden route outside that channel — say the human contestant or the interrogator picking up thoughts directly — so a result might reflect a leak rather than the machine’s own resources. His way of handling it is not to laugh ESP off but to try to change the set-up so the leak couldn’t be used: keeping the parties apart, restricting communication to a teleprinter-style link, maybe even trying to make the room telepathy-proof. The conceptual sting I want them to hit is that the test’s verdict depends on background assumptions about what channels are open, and the fix of insulating the game looks like it quietly assumes there is no funny business; moreover, you can only design against channels you can imagine, not against every possible one.

So: design a runnable game, simulation or structured role-play for exactly 16 people in 90 minutes. It can be played in small groups with roles rotating, or as one big staged game with subgroups — your call, but everyone needs something to do. Use only cheap materials, and give me a payoff scheme with small real stakes (points, counters, chocolate, whatever — no real money). The rules, incentives or secret roles must be arranged so the students run into the ambiguity themselves: they should start off reading the results as evidence about the contestants, and only later, ideally constructing the worry themselves rather than being told, realise that a hidden channel could be doing the work. They should not be told what to suspect in advance.
