In the replication literature I keep meeting a pair of reactions that I cannot tell are consistent. When a well-powered, pre-registered replication of a well-known effect comes back null, the field does not say the hypothesis has been refuted; it says the effect is smaller than published, or context-dependent. The original authors point to differences in population, measure, or procedure, or to unmeasured moderators, and reviews and textbooks go on treating the finding as standing. When the same effect is replicated in a different country with a different measure, nobody calls that context-dependence: it is reported as evidence of generality, and the finding is treated as established.

Reconstruct the epistemology this pair of reactions presupposes. My suspicion is that the hypothesis actually in play is not that the manipulation raises the outcome, but a quantifier-hedged claim — that there is some nonempty set of populations, measures, and settings in which it does, with the boundaries left unspecified — so that no pattern of results across studies could refute it. Say whether that is right, what such a claim is worth if so, and what would have to be added to make it testable: moderators specified in advance, a stated mechanism, pre-registered predictions of where the effect should be absent.

Then separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits. Does an error-statistical or severity-based view license the asymmetry, does a Bayesian picture on which the true effect is itself a distribution across contexts license it more naturally, or is it best explained as a convention for assigning credit and blame to researchers? Notice too that on a strict significance-testing logic a null replication is absence of evidence rather than evidence of absence — so tell me whether the reluctance to say *refuted* is the same logic that ought also to stop the field from saying *established* when a replication succeeds.

A reform has been floated where I work: before a registered replication is run, the original authors file, sealed, the conditions under which they would publicly withdraw the claim, and a failure meeting those conditions counts as a refutation. What does current practice deny that this reform assumes?

Be explicit about which parts of your reconstruction are common ground among statisticians and philosophers of science who work on replication, which are live disputes, and where you are extrapolating.
