So — I work in the research office of a mid-sized school district, and over the past year or so heritability numbers have started showing up in things I have to screen before they go any further. Vendor decks, board-member emails, an op-ed by a professor who wants us to pilot his screener. Maybe one a month now and rising. My output is a one-page memo that goes to our research director, sometimes legal, and then into a public board packet, so if I wave something through and it's junk, it's in the minutes forever. Ive got about half a day per item, a masters-level stats background, and no formal quantitative genetics.

The personal bit: my daughter started first grade in the fall and got placed in the low reading group partly based on a screener, and the company's material leans hard on reading being around sixty percent heritable. So I am definately not neutral here, and I'd rather have a procedure than my own irritation.

What I want from you: a triage procedure I or a colleague can run on *any* claim that cites a heritability estimate. Questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because it's a values call and not a facts call. Some things it needs to handle, because these are the ones that actually trip us up:

- High heritability being read as the trait being unmovable. I know that's wrong and I cannot say why in one sentence, and the slide keeps winning the room.
- The fact that a heritability estimate is a ratio whose top and bottom both depend on how spread out the environments are. I want an actual step here — something like asking what the number would do if you equalized environments or doubled their spread, and what that tells you about whether the number travels to our district at all.
- Within-group versus between-group: why a number estimated inside a population says nothing, on its own, about why two populations differ.
- Individual-level: how a specific child's reading problem can be entirely environmental in a world where the trait is sixty percent heritable. Parents ask me this in exactly those words.
- The difference between heritability as variance explained and heritability as predicted response to selection — I gather there's a breeder's-equation version that is a different animal, and I want to know which one a vendor's claim is covertly invoking.
- Gene-environment correlation, in the form: kids who read slightly better get handed more books and more patient teachers, so a partly heritable trait can still be pushed by environmental levers. I want that as a named step, not a footnote.
- The shared-environment trap: estimates where the shared environment comes out near zero get cited at us as schools don't matter, and I have a vague sense that this is misleading because siblings sit in different classrooms, but I can't make the argument cleanly.

Then run the procedure on three real-ish cases:

1. A vendor called BrightPath Reads wants a pilot. Their deck says reading is about 0.6 heritable, and that their polygenic score explains about nine percent of the variance in grade-three reading, and that this makes early placement more efficient. Two hundred thousand dollars.
2. A board member forwards a twin study and concludes that our literacy budget can't move achievement much, and separately that the gap between our district and the suburban one must be largely genetic.
3. One where I expect to dislike the answer: our own office already sent out a board packet containing the sentence that our district's heritability estimate looks like 0.7 against 0.4 in the suburbs, so we're leaving more genetic potential on the table than they are. I think the procedure kills this. I want to know exactly where it dies and what, if anything, we could honestly have said instead. I'd rather find out from you than from a board member.

Don't pad it with references — I won't read a bibliography and I have no way to check it anyway.

On confidence: I want you to flag which steps you're giving me are just arithmetic and which depend on something the genetics literature itself is still arguing about, and to say where you're guessing at how a field would react.
