I've been working on predictive processing for my thesis for a couple of years and I've drifted into holding a stronger, weirder version of it than anything I think anyone in the literature actually defends, and I want to stress-test it before I build a chapter on it.

The boring half I'm fine about: perception as inference, precision weighting, action as a way of making predictions come true rather than as the end of the pipeline. I take that bit to be on reasonably firm ground. The part I've come round to is about the self. My claim is that what makes a system a self isn't the contents of any self-model, or even the fact that it represents itself at all, but something about timescales: a self is a system whose generative model makes predictions about its own trajectory that can only be settled over intervals much longer than the interval over which it can act. So, crudely: a self is a system that can be wrong about itself for longer than it can do anything about it. Call it the 'longer than you can act' criterion. The persistence and felt unity of a self, on this story, is just what it's like to be stuck with an error signal about who you are that you can't discharge by moving — you have to keep bringing your behaviour, and your own self-description, back into line with a prediction that stays open.

What would count against this? I want defeaters ranked from fatal to merely embarrasing, and I want the ones a friendly predictive-processing person would concede, not only the ones a hostile philosopher would enjoy.

Some worries I already have, which I want you to push further rather than list back to me: the criterion might be trivially satisfied — a thermostat with an integral term, a plant timing its dormancy, a firm with a five-year plan, a net trained with slow weights as well as fast ones — in which case it doesn't pick out selves at all. Or it might collapse into a view I don't want: a memory-based, narrative, or higher-order-thought account wearing prediction-error vocabulary. Or it might explain nothing about experience and only redescribe the situations in which we reach for the word 'self'.

What would actually move me is a case where the timescale profile and the phenomenology come apart: temporal thickness present with no unified self-experience, or self-experience intact with no multi-timescale structure of the kind I'm describing. Depersonalization, ketamine, certain schizophrenic symptoms, split-brain cases — tell me which of these cut and which I can absorb, and why. Also: am I defending something unfalsifiable, given that 'much longer than' is a dial I can turn every time a counterexample shows up? And if it is insulated, is that a genuine problem for a claim that's meant to sit at the framework level rather than describe a mechanism?
