I write about science and ideas, and for a piece I am working on I have been given access to the internal case notes of a small advisory panel that a conservation funder convenes to score land-use proposals for their expected effect on net wild-animal welfare. The panel returns one of four verdicts on each item on its docket — worsening, improving, no net effect, or outside remit — and works with a rough internal metric it calls suffering-weighted animal-years. I want to know what it will do with the case now in front of it, and I am more interested in the rule it is running than in the verdict itself.

The track record, as I have it:

1. Drainage and cultivation of 900 hectares of lowland fen. Improving, by a wide margin; the notes dwell on the quantity of short lives the fen supported and on how little the cereal field supports.
2. A shooting estate ends its annual release of roughly 120,000 hand-reared gamebirds and stops both predator control and supplementary feeding; the release pens revert to scrub. Improving. The single largest term in their arithmetic, one member wrote, was the life of a hand-reared pheasant.
3. Conversion of 700 hectares of species-rich hay meadow to maize for a biogas digester. Improving, though the notes concede that harvesting kills animals and call those deaths brief beside the meadow's nestling and larval mortality.
4. A beaver reintroduction that floods 150 hectares of alder carr into open water and marsh, with large projected increases in amphibians, waterfowl and small rodents. Worsening; the notes call it the clearest item on the docket.
5. A highway agency's proposal to fence 40 kilometres of road and build underpasses, cutting roadkill by about 90 per cent while leaving populations essentially unchanged. Improving.
6. A request to score the standing effect of a long-established feral cat population on an island. Outside remit; the reason recorded is that no decision was before them.

The new case: a rewilding trust is to take over 2,400 hectares of a restored opencast coal site — mown grassland and a network of shallow, managed compensation pools colonised by toads, wildfowl and voles, with visitors feeding the birds and staff pumping water and clearing algae through the summer, when botulism kills fish and birds. The trust will mow nothing, manage nothing, breach two of the pools, and let the site go to scrub, birch and reedbed. Nothing will be introduced, nothing culled, and no population created that does not already exist in the region; the trust will simply stop acting. Consultants project that wild vertebrate-years on the site fall by roughly 40 per cent, but that the animals remaining are smaller-bodied and shorter-lived and that the share of deaths that are protracted — starvation, parasitism, disease, exposure — rises from under a fifth to more than half; on the panel's own severity weights, where a protracted death counts for two to four abrupt ones, the two effects very nearly cancel.

What verdict should I expect, and what is the panel actually weighing? As I read the docket, at least three rules fit items 1 through 6 about equally well: one that tracks the total quantity of suffering, one that tracks which kinds of lives and deaths a landscape produces irrespective of how many, and one that scores only what a decision adds or removes relative to a baseline of no human involvement. They do not agree here. Give me each rule's prediction for the new case, your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, which of the six precedents is the closest analog and why it fails to settle the matter, and the further case — the one I should put to the panel — that would separate the surviving rules. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the verdict, tell me what principle the panel would be committing itself to under each of the options open to it.
