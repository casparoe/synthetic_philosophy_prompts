I work as a software engineer (ML infrastructure) with no formal background in philosophy or political theory, and I started reading about direct democracy after a friend in Zurich described a federal vote whose result struck her as incoherent. Since then I keep noticing what looks like the same shape in California ballot propositions, and I'd like to know whether it is a recognized phenomenon with a literature behind it.

Here is the pattern as I see it. Voters are asked a series of separate yes/no questions, each about a narrow policy, and each one produces a genuine majority. But the combination of the winners is a package that no majority ever chose — and in the strong version, a package a majority would reject outright if it were put to them as a single proposal. I did the arithmetic on the boring version: if three measures each carry with a bare majority, inclusion–exclusion only forces the intersection of the three winning coalitions to have about 100 − 3×49 members, which is to say it can be empty, so the resulting policy bundle can have literally zero supporters even though every step was majoritarian. The examples that made it click for me were the well-known California property-tax initiative of the late 1970s sitting alongside later measures that mandate funding levels for particular services, and Swiss federal votes where revenue-cutting and spending-creating measures pass within a few years of each other. In the milder version the winning combination is jointly satisfiable but is nobody's package: polling suggests most voters wanted A and not B, or B and not A, and yet A and B both pass. What makes these feel like one thing to me is that the trade-off never gets faced by anyone. Each question is decided in isolation, so the conflict is pushed downstream onto a legislature or a budget office, which then has to break a tie between two things it is told are both the will of the people, and gets blamed for betraying that will whichever way it goes.

I don't just want a label — I want the carving, and I want to know where the edges are, so please don't stop at naming something. Specifically: does it matter whether the conflicting measures are passed at the same election or years apart, by the same electorate or by different ones (a referendum contradicting a legislature, say), or whether the contradiction is logical and immediate versus visible only under an economic forecast that one side disputes? Does it matter that the voters who care intensely about A are a different, smaller group than those who care intensely about B, so that the bundle reflects intensity rather than numbers? Is the downstream blame — the implementing body being accused of ignoring a will of the people that is not well defined — a separate phenomenon with its own name, or part of the same one? And is what I'm describing really one thing, or does it split into (a) a fact about aggregating separate binary choices, (b) a psychological fact about how people reason when a trade-off is decomposed, and (c) a fact about ballot and constitutional design? I've read a little about Condorcet-style cycling, but that seems to need three or more alternatives for the same office and an actual cycle, and I don't think I need either. Someone also pointed me to a paradox — I have the name Anscombe in my head but can't confirm it, possibly from a short 1970s paper about the majority's will being frustrated by its own fulfilment — where a majority of voters ends up in the minority on a majority of issues; that is the closest thing I've found, but my case doesn't require anyone to lose on most issues, only that the package be one nobody chose, so I suspect it's adjacent rather than identical. If it is genuinely unnamed, please say so plainly, tell me which neighboring concepts come closest and why each falls short, and evaluate the two working names I've been using — 'trade-off laundering' for the decomposition of a single trade-off into separately winnable questions, and 'the unbundling problem' for the aggregate result — including whether either is already taken or misleading.
