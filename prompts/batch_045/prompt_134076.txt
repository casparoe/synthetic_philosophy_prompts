I'm in a small upper-level seminar this term on the wealth and poverty of nations — joint-listed between economics and politics — and the syllabus has been organized as a three-way fight: an institutions account (roughly, that constraints on executive power and secure, broad-access property rights are what separate rich places from poor ones, and that the colonial encounter largely determined who got which), a geography account (the germs-and-latitude, continental-axes, disease-burden line of argument, plus the sheer accident of when a place was pulled into world trade), and a culture account (that what actually persists is norms, trust, and habits of cooperation, carried along family and community lines). I keep leaving section convinced that each camp is quietly answering a different question — one about the *level* of income, one about the *timing* of the industrial transition, one about *persistence* over centuries — and that the word "institutions" is doing enough work in the first account that it could absorb the other two and thereby explain anything. My group's final project is to stage an adversarial collaboration, and my professor, who is allergic to any framing that could not in principle be wrong, has asked for a document both sides could sign rather than a verdict on who is right.

So please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a committed institutions-first theorist and a committed geography-and-luck theorist. I want: (1) a numbered section of roughly eight to twelve claims both could genuinely sign — about the setup as much as the substance: what the units are, what the outcome variable is (income per capita? growth over what window? measured from when?), what the well-known "reversal" — that the places that were densely settled and relatively prosperous around 1500 are disproportionately poor today — does and does not establish, and the methodological point that any deep cause has to act through identifiable behaviour by households, firms, and officials on a human timescale. Please keep these non-vacuous; my professor will cross out anything of the form "both factors matter." (2) A disagreements table with columns for: the crux; the institutions camp's own preferred formulation of its claim; the geography camp's own preferred formulation of its claim; a case, dataset, or argument both agree would bear on the crux; each side's advance prediction about it; and what each camp would count as evidence against itself. The cruxes I suspect matter most are, first, whether the controversy is about levels or about long-run growth rates (a disease burden might plausibly depress a level while institutions set a growth path, and I want to know which parameter is supposed to be doing the explanatory work over five hundred years); second, whether geography acts mostly *through* institutions or has large direct effects; third, whether initial conditions plus path dependence dominate, so that the same shock produces different equilibria depending on when it arrives; and fourth, how much of the dispute is verbal — in particular, whether "institutions" is defined broadly enough to include informal norms and even public-health capacity, which would make the institutions claim unfalsifiable. If part of the dispute is verbal, say which part and why.

I also want you to grade four tests I've been sketching, each as a row in the table or in a short dedicated section, with both camps' predictions written down and an honest verdict on how much each test could really discriminate: (a) the *portable-culture* test — compare the same migrant-origin group's outcomes across destinations with very different rules, and look at second-generation persistence, to separate "culture travels with people" from "host-country rules do the work"; (b) the *transplant* test — cases where formal legal and administrative institutions were imposed or copied wholesale while terrain, latitude, and disease ecology stayed put, asking how fast convergence should be if rules are the binding constraint; (c) the *disease-removal* test — regions where malaria and hookworm burden was effectively eliminated in the twentieth century, asking which camp predicts a one-off level shift and which predicts a change in the growth rate, and how large; (d) the *pre-registered archival* test — both camps write down in advance, before some newly digitized or newly declassified census, tax, or land record is analyzed, what pattern in it would count for or against them, as a guard against post hoc storytelling. Then add a short third section in the voice of a culture-first theorist: exactly which claims in the agreed section she would refuse to sign, and whether her refusal is substantive or a complaint about how "institution" has been defined. Please don't give me citations, bibliographies, or author-and-year name-dropping — I'll assemble the reading list myself, and I want the arguments; if you need to gesture at an empirical literature, describe the study design in your own words instead. Aim for under about 1,200 words.

Finally, I'd like to know how much confidence to place in the document, so please add a short closing note: for each side's formulations, say whether you're confident an actual adherent of that view would accept the wording as fair; flag anywhere you had to reconstruct a position from thin materials; and single out the one claim in the whole statement you're least sure of, with a sentence on why.
