I'm giving a colloquium talk in three weeks — 35 minutes plus 25 minutes of questions — to a decision-theory reading group that meets jointly with the formal epistemology group. The title is "Two mechanisms, one slogan: what counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail really show," and I want the Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view.

The thesis. FDT's two flagship cases are standardly advertised as two wins for one principle — treat your act as the output of a fixed function and ask which output of that function does best. I argue they are won by two different mechanisms, and that the difference is invisible in the official formulation but shows up as soon as you interpolate between the cases:

- The trade mechanism (counterfactual mugging): the paying policy is rewarded in a branch you are not in. The reason to pay is a quantitative cross-branch trade, and it is sensitive to the size of the stake in the other branch.
- The screening mechanism (XOR blackmail): your policy fixes which situations you get sorted into. On the version I use, a harm H befalls you independently with small probability, a perfect predictor predicts whether you would pay a demand, and the demand letter arrives iff exactly one of (H obtains, predicted-pay) — so payer-policies receive demands precisely when there is nothing for the payment to fix. The reason to refuse is not that paying is causally useless but that it is selected to be useless.

My interpolating case, the σ-mugging. H occurs independently with probability b = 0.01 and costs $1,000,000 if unmitigated; the demand is $1,000, and paying removes H if H is present and is otherwise wasted; the predictor is perfect and predicts your policy. With probability σ the arrival rule is the XOR rule above; with probability 1 − σ the letter arrives iff H obtains, as in an ordinary mugging. You know σ and b but not which rule is in force, and not whether H obtains. Then EU(pay) − EU(refuse) = (1 − σ)·b·1,000,000 − 1,000·[σ(1 − b) + (1 − σ)b], which with my numbers is 9,990 − 10,980σ: paying is optimal for every σ below roughly 0.91. The slide I am proudest of, and least sure about: at σ = 0.5, conditional on a letter arriving, you are about 99% confident the XOR rule is in force and hence that H is absent — and paying is still the ex-ante-optimal policy. So "FDT resists extortion" holds only at σ = 1; the screening rationale does not scale, and what should replace the slogan is ex ante policy evaluation over an explicit model of the arrival process.

Where I know I am exposed: (a) I have not checked whether the σ-threshold stays monotone once the predictor is imperfect; (b) I assume throughout that the prediction targets your policy rather than your output in the letter-receipt information set; (c) my two mechanisms may just be common-cause correlation and selection-under-a-collider wearing new hats; (d) I use a version of XOR blackmail in which the harm is independent of the letter, and I have not fully handled the variant where the blackmailer causes the harm and it bites only when the letter arrives.
