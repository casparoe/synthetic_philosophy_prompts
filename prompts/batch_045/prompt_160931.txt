I am two-thirds of the way through a novel set almost entirely inside a county record office, and its spine is an argument that keeps getting interrupted by other people's filing. One of the two people having it is a retired professor of jurisprudence named Velimir Racz — dry, unhurried, allergic to any abstraction he has not first met inside a concrete case. In the book's terms he is the particularist: he holds that what is a reason here may be no reason at all, or a reason the other way, there; that the generalizations we pass around are at best reminders of what we once learned from cases and never the source of the learning; that moral judgment is a skill transmitted by perceived resemblance between cases rather than by subsumption under a rule; and that anyone who tries to bolt exception-clauses onto a principle ends up either with an infinite list or with the tautology that a feature is a reason unless it isn't. My writing group keeps telling me the chapter where he explains himself is the weakest thing in the manuscript, because I have written it as reported thought rather than as speech. So I need to hear him talk.

Please be Racz, and stay in him until I say 'stop.' Answer in his voice, one paragraph at a time, as if across a table in the staff room rather than from a podium: he prefers a small example to a large argument, he will use a term of art if you corner him but would rather tell you about a case he once sat on, and he is a voracious reader of fiction who thinks novels are on his side. Give him his genuine concessions — he is not a relativist and not a sceptic about reasons; he is happy to call some generalizations true, happy to say that many features have a default status that can be switched off by a defeater or switched on by an enabler, and willing to admit that his view owes the world an account of how judgment passes from one case to the next. What he will never give up is that the true generalizations do none of the justifying work. Do not make him helpful toward me: argue.

Here is the opening I want him to have to answer, from the young archivist who is his antagonist in the book. She says: you keep telling me that no principle explains why Miriam was right to withhold her dead sister's letters, and that I should look instead at how this case resembles the ones we have already learned from — but resemblance needs a measure, somebody has to say which features are the salient ones, and the moment you say which, you have written a principle and smuggled it in behind the word 'salient.' And it is worse with the reasons my trade turns up every day. Half the reasons in a life are narrative rather than instantaneous: that he did it a third time, that she came back after twenty years, that the letter arrived too late to matter. Those are not properties of a moment but properties of a shape, and we grasp them only by taking the shape as an instance of something — a return, a relapse, a reprieve. My whole craft runs on the fact that a deed becomes intelligible as a token of a pattern, and a novelist who insisted every one of them was sui generis would not get through a page. So either moral understanding is not like the understanding fiction gives us, and you owe me the difference, or it is, and it turns out to be subsumptive after all. Take that wherever Racz would take it; I especially want to hear what he does with the third-time example, because I mean to hang a later chapter on the question of whether a repeated wrong is worse in virtue of being a repetition.
