The talk is my job-market paper, "Rationality Is Not Free: Costly Nesting, Bounded Reasoning Depth, and the Backward-Induction Outcome," and the argument is roughly this. Take a finite two-player perfect-information game — I use a six-node centipede — and recall the standard epistemic picture: common knowledge of rationality plus common knowledge of the game form pins down the backward-induction profile, so the first mover takes immediately, while in laboratory centipedes people pass for a few nodes and then take. The usual explanations are altruism or social preferences, uncertainty about the opponent's payoffs, or an exogenously imposed bound on reasoning depth from a level-k or cognitive-hierarchy model. My proposal is that the depth of nesting should be endogenous and costly. Define depth inductively: a depth-1 player best-responds to an unmodelled opponent; a depth-k player best-responds to a depth-(k−1) opponent. Now charge δ per level of nesting, either as a payoff cost δ·k subtracted from the outcome or as a probability (1−δ)^k of completing the inference correctly — I argue the two are equivalent up to a rescaling of stakes, which is one of the claims I am least sure about. Each player then chooses a reasoning depth alongside an action plan, and here is what I claim. Result 1, the one I lead with: for every δ greater than zero the symmetric equilibrium depth is finite, so common knowledge of rationality would cost an infinite amount and is therefore not merely unattainable but forbidden by the very rationality it is supposed to idealize — the standard ideal is self-undermining, and the near-certainty literature that relaxes certainty while keeping the infinite hierarchy buys exactly the wrong thing. Result 2: as δ goes to zero the equilibrium depth diverges and play converges to the backward-induction outcome, so my model nests the standard result as a limit and the empirically observed cooperation is explained by the size of δ rather than by kindness or by payoff uncertainty. Result 3: with unequal δ the cheaper reasoner gains only up to a point — beyond a threshold, extra depth is wasted because the opponent has stopped — and because scaling all payoffs by a factor λ raises the optimal depth, the model predicts that cooperation in the centipede should collapse as stakes rise, a prediction I have no evidence for and no way to check before the talk. A friend who works on reasoning in language models pointed out to me that in her systems extra inference steps cost compute and sometimes make the answer worse, which is where the framing came from, and I would like to open with a version of that analogy; tell me whether that opening will cost me the room.

The audience is a joint economics-and-philosophy department and the room will be about forty people, but the questions will come from four or five quarters. There are two senior microeconomic theorists who work on the epistemic foundations of equilibrium and who think the point of epistemic analysis is to characterize equilibrium concepts rather than to model deliberation; they will think I have changed the subject.
