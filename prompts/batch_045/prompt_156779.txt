I am trying to work out what I actually think about the ethics of bringing people into existence, and I would rather begin from my own verdicts on cases than from a theory. Below are the cases and the verdicts I find myself giving, followed by the two pairs of verdicts I cannot reconcile, followed by the principle I have been trying to make work. What I want from you is: a principle, or a short menu of rival candidates, stated precisely enough that I can apply it myself; an indication of which case motivates each clause; its verdicts on several cases I have not listed; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts no candidate can accommodate.

Let me say where I am standing so you know what vocabulary I am reaching for. I have encountered the four-cell comparison associated with Benatar's asymmetry: the presence of pain is bad, the presence of pleasure is good, the absence of pain is good even where there is nobody to enjoy that good, and the absence of pleasure is not bad unless there is somebody for whom it is a deprivation. I find that pattern intuitive in the abstract. What I do not know is whether it is really generating my verdicts, or whether I am dressing some other view in its vocabulary.

My cases.

1. The doomed infant. A couple knows that any child they conceive will be born with a condition causing continuous severe pain and death before age three, with no compensating goods and no capacity in the child to form any view about its own life. They conceive anyway. Verdict: clearly wrong. This is the one case I have no wobble about.

2. The ordinary child. A couple has a child who will have an unremarkable, moderately good life: more pleasure than pain, relationships, projects, an ordinary share of grief and boredom. Verdict: not wrong. I would not call it admirable or required, and I would resist saying the parents did something good for the child. I would say permissible, with no residue.

3. Not conceiving the ordinary child. Verdict: not wrong either, and I also resist the stronger claim that this outcome is better than the outcome in case 2. Permissible, and that is all I want to say.

4. Not conceiving the doomed infant. Verdict: not merely permissible but the better outcome. Here I do want the stronger claim.

5. The saviour sibling. A couple conceives a child in order to use cord blood and, later, bone marrow to treat an existing sick child. The new child's life will, overall, be a decent one, and the harvesting procedures are painful. Verdict: I go back and forth, and I lean toward permissible, because the life will be good on the whole. But one part of me insists that you cannot inflict pain on someone for another person's sake when the alternative available to them was never existing at all.

6. The same painful procedure performed on the existing child, for the sake of the sibling, where the child's own interests are also served by the sibling's survival. Verdict: permissible, and the unease I feel in case 5 largely disappears. The same pain is imposed, for the benefit of the same third party, and my reaction differs. I want this difference explained rather than explained away.

7. The risky child. Two prospective children are available to a couple. The first will certainly have a moderately good life. The second has the same expected net welfare but a five percent chance of a life dominated by suffering. Verdict: choose the first; creating the second is, if not wrong, then close to it, and I feel this even though the expected arithmetic is indifferent. I do not know whether my asymmetry, as I understand it, has any tendency at all here.

8. The non-identity case. A couple will conceive regardless of what I do, but I can affect which child comes to exist, and one of the candidates would have a much better life than the other. Verdict: bring about the better-off child. And if I could instead prevent conception altogether, I would not take that option, though I would not call the parents wrong if they took it.

9a. The affirmed hard life. A child will endure a decade of significant suffering, and the balance of pleasure over pain in their life is, at best, marginal; but at forty they will wholeheartedly and stably affirm that they are glad to exist, and they are not self-deceived or depressed when they say it. Verdict: creating them was permissible. I trust the endorsement.

9b. The unaffirmed pleasant life. A child's life is reliably full of pleasure, clearly net-positive by any hedonic measure, and they will come, from a fully informed and stable position, to judge that they would rather never have existed. Verdict: I do not want to say the parents did something wrong, but I want to say that things went badly for the child, and that this is a misfortune even though the life is pleasant.

The two pairs I cannot reconcile are these. First, verdicts 2 and 3 versus verdict 4: if I accept the asymmetry, then any life containing any pain is at a disadvantage relative to never existing, since the absent pain counts as a good while the absent pleasures count as no loss, and no real life is free of pain. That seems to push me to judge case 2 wrong, and I will not go there. Second, verdicts 9a and 9b: I let the person's own retrospective judgement override the welfare arithmetic in one direction but not in the other, and I cannot say what licenses the asymmetry in my trust.

I also have a scope worry that I cannot settle. If the third cell credits every pain that a merely possible person never feels, it seems to credit me for the pains of all the people I never create, for the rest of time; it seems to make every painless death a benefit to the person who dies; and it seems to make an empty universe the best possible outcome. I reject all three. So the third cell needs a scope restriction. I would like to know what the restriction is and whether the restricted version still delivers verdicts 3 and 4. Relatedly, I do not know whether to read the third cell as a claim about how worlds rank or as a claim about what I may do with respect to a merely possible person, and whether that choice is what decides the fate of verdict 2.

Here is the principle I have been able to assemble, offered to be tested rather than defended. Call it T: you may create a person only if you have no decisive reason to expect either that their life will contain suffering that is not necessary for goods they themselves will enjoy, or that they will, from the inside, withhold endorsement of their own existence. As I read it, the first clause convicts case 1, and it convicts case 5 as well, which sits badly with the verdict I lean toward there; the second clause convicts case 9b, which is stronger than my verdict; and neither clause says anything about risk, about numbers of people, about animals, or about the scope worry. So T is at best a fragment.

What I would like, then, is this. Test T against my cases and either repair it or replace it with the one or two best candidates you can construct, telling me for each which of my verdicts it saves and which it forces me to overturn, and naming the verdict you think I should give up if I have to give one up. Then state what your favoured candidate says about four cases I have not listed: first, the one I keep returning to, which is whether to have a biological child or to adopt an existing one, given that the adopted child's life would be improved and the biological child's life would be good; second, whether to create one child with an excellent life or two children with decent lives, where the two-child option contains more total welfare; third, whether the principle extends to creating sentient animals with net-positive lives for food, and if so with what modification; and fourth, whether it says anything about the choice between a future with people in it and a future with none, supposing the people would face real risks of great suffering. If you think my whole apparatus is confused, say so, and say which of my verdicts survives the collapse.
