I teach an 80-minute upper-level seminar on minds and machines: 14 students, roughly half philosophy majors and half CS majors, one whiteboard, index cards, and no computers needed (I would rather not need them). I want a runnable in-class activity that makes them hit the gap between a reward signal as a training signal and a reward signal as something felt, and hit it before anyone names it.

What I have in mind — though I would rather have your version than mine — is that they hand-simulate a tiny reinforcement learner by filling in a small Q-table on a three-by-three grid with a couple of positive and negative cells, a discount, and an occasional random action, so that by the end the room has produced the agent's avoidance behavior out of nothing but numbers and an update rule, and can see that what drives the update is the gap between what was expected and what arrived rather than the size or sign of the payoff itself. Then they should be forced into a verdict about that system.

Constraints and asks:

- No framing lecture. They are doing arithmetic before they know what the example is for, and the facilitator does not say valence, qualia, suffering, or consciousness until the verdict is in.
- For the verdict stage, give them an explicit functional checklist — produced by threat of damage, motivates escape and avoidance, resists being overridden by competing goals, supports learning, generalizes to similar states — applied as a committee with a forced yes or no and a written one-line reason. I want the checklist to fail to settle the question, in both directions.
- Materials, rules, a timeline with minute marks, the exact numbers for the grid and update rule you would use, and a debrief that maps specific moments onto specific claims: that the reward number arrives from outside the agent and need never be experienced; that behavior and welfare come maximally apart when the signal is gamed; and that whatever pressure there is operates across the training run rather than within an episode, so no single update requires a subject.
- Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time. My CS students will want to argue about implementation and my philosophers will retreat to other-minds skepticism; I want to know how to block both moves.
- Be honest about what the activity cannot stage and I will have to say in so many words. I suspect it is the supposedly qualitative residue, the fact that a hand-filled table is transparent in a way a trained network is not, and the difference between there being no third-person test that settles it and there being nothing to settle.
