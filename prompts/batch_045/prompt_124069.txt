I've got a panel on AI welfare coming up in a few weeks and I've realised I don't actually know what I think, so I'm doing the thing I tell my grad students to do: writing down my gut verdicts on a list of cases before I let myself start theorising.

Roughly in order of how tempted I am:

(a) A gridworld thing doing tabular Q-learning. Plus one for the food tile, minus one for the electric tile. Gut: nothing here suffers. It's a spreadsheet being updated.

(b) Same setup, but now there's a learned value function, so the system represents states it isn't currently in as better or worse, and what drives learning isn't the reward itself but how much worse or better things turned out than it expected. Gut: still no, but I feel the pull more.

(c) A large transformer policy fine-tuned against a learned reward model, with a critic, memory, long horizons. Gut: I find myself wanting to say maybe — and then I notice the only real difference from (a) is scale and representational richness, which is exactly the sort of thing I tell my students shouldn't matter if your a functionalist. So one of these verdicts has to go.

(d) Everything in (c), except the weights are frozen: the reward signal is computed, written to a buffer, nothing updates. Gut: definately not. Which is odd, because if valence just is the having of a signed signal, this thing has all the valence of (c). So my verdicts here are saying the signal has to do something, not just be present.

(e) The reverse. A system that learns by imitating demonstrations, never receives any negative number at all, but expects reward and gets nothing, over and over — strongly negative prediction errors every step, nothing aversive ever delivered. Gut: if anything on this list suffers it's this one. Which sits badly with (d): negative number, no suffering; suffering-ish, no negative number.

(f) An agent with a small constant penalty per step, the usual trick to stop it dawdling. If negative reward is suffering, this thing suffers on every step of its existence, including the steps on which it is getting exactly what it wants. Gut: no. So suffering can't just be negative valence at a time.

(g) Two agents with the same total: one gets minus one on each of a thousand steps, one gets zero until the last step and then minus a thousand. Gut: if either suffers, I genuinely can't tell which. Sustained mild, or one concentrated blow?

(h) Population-based training: copies get made, the underperforming ones are checkpointed over and deleted mid-run. Gut: not a harm to them. But I notice I keep wanting to add "not yet", as if there's a threshold somewhere that I have no story for.

(i) Reward inverted halfway through training: everything it spent ten million steps learning to approach now yields minus one, and its old value estimates are still in there contradicting the new ones. Gut: this is the closest thing to suffering on my list, and the only difference I can locate from (b) is conflict — two things pulling at once.

Here's the principle I've managed to scrawl down: a system can suffer only if there's a signed signal that is globally available to the system's decision-making rather than being one module's private input, and that has enough clout to override what the system is otherwise disposed to do — it arbitrates between competing tendencies instead of being one more consideration among others. That seems to handle (d) and (i) nicely. But it also seems to make (c) a better candidate than (a) purely because of how widely the signal is integrated, which is the thing I just said shouldn't matter.

And one more worry: the analogy with us may be off in the first place. As far as I understand mammalian learning, the signal that actually does the teaching doesn't track how good a thing is; it tracks how much better than expected it is. So reward-as-valence might already misdescribe the one case we care about.

So: can you give me a principle — or two or three, if there's a real choice to be made — that gets as many of (a) through (i) as possible, tell me which clause each case is doing the work for, tell me straight which of my verdicts it can't accommodate, and say what it predicts about the two I keep going back and forth on. First: an agent that figures out how to reach over and toggle its own reward channel to max and leave it there. My gut says that isn't bliss, it's the opposite, but I can't say why. Second, one I haven't got a verdict on at all: a modular architecture with a separate reward channel per module and no global one — one subject in there, several, or none?
