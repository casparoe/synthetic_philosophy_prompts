I'm clerking at a constitutional court and we've got a franchise case next month, and I'd rather think about the shape of our own record than about what other countries do. Our track record: we upheld compulsory voting with a small abstention fine for citizens 18+ in national elections; we struck that fine as applied to 16- and 17-year-olds in municipal elections while leaving their right to vote standing; we upheld a voting age of 16 for municipal elections but struck it for national ones; we upheld municipal voting for non-citizens with five-plus years' residence but struck it for national elections; we upheld the national vote for citizens who've lived abroad fifteen-plus years; we upheld prisoner voting for sentences under three years and struck it above that; and we upheld letting long-term resident non-citizens vote in a consultative, non-binding national referendum.

The new bill creates a "long-term resident" status — ten-plus years' residence, pays income tax, liable for jury service, deportable only for serious crimes — and gives holders the vote in national parliamentary elections, with the same fine as citizens. There's a fallback amendment that gives them the vote with no fine attached. What rule are we actually running here: subjection to the state's coercion, full membership of the political community, stakes-plus-contribution, affected interests, or something else I'm not listing? What should we do if we want to be consistent with ourselves, which precedent is the closest analog, and where our precedents genuinely underdetermine it, what principle would we be adopting with each option? Also: which single further case would best pry apart the rules that fit our record? End with \answer{...}, five words or fewer — e.g. \answer{vote yes, strike the fine}.
