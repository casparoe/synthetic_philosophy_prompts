Hi — I need a dialogue between two philosophers who've both spent a lot of time with Galen Strawson's Basic Argument, the four-step thing where you do what you do because of the way you are, so to be ultimately responsible for what you do you'd have to be ultimately responsible for the way you are, which you can't be (you'd have to be self-created, and the regress of self-choices never gets started), so nobody is ever ultimately morally responsible. One of them thinks that argument is basically sound. The other thinks it has a blind spot, and I want her view to be a position I haven't seen named anywhere, so here's the one I want defended — call it bootstrapped authorship: the claim that responsibility doesn't have to be a property of a first link in a causal chain, or of a link that causes itself, but can be a property of the whole diachronic process, so that an agent counts as responsible when her current motivating reasons are the output of a long run of revisions she made to herself, each one using her own earlier self as the standard, even though no single revision was self-caused and the whole chain still bottoms out in stuff she didn't choose.

Give them a concrete case to argue over instead of abstract hand-waving: an artificial agent whose initial motivational system was designed externally, and which then spent years rewriting its own values over and over, each rewrite using only its then-current values as the criterion, so that it's now something like version 900 of itself. The Strawsonian gets to press the obvious points — that the arbitrariness just gets transmitted down the chain, that each rewrite was done for reasons that agent didn't choose to have, that authorship of the process might just be ownership relabeled — but she needs real answers to what the bootstrapping person says back, not the four steps repeated louder. The bootstrapping person has to deal with whether her view implies that a sufficiently elaborate externally-built self-modifier is responsible too, and either bite that bullet or explain why not.

Two more things I'd like worked in: what each of them says about an agent that genuinely did create itself over time (is that even coherent, and would it be responsible if so?), and whether the conclusion of the argument, if it holds, is supposed to make any difference to resentment and gratitude or whether that's a separate question entirely.

Make it read like two people who have definitely argued about this before rather than a debate script — they should quote each other's earlier lines back, concede at least one thing each that they genuinely find hard, and not settle everything by the end. Casual academic register is fine. No citations, no reading lists, and please don't let either side win just by being more articulate.
