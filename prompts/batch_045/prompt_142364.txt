I run a fortnightly reading group on AI alignment and philosophy of action: about sixteen people, a mix of graduate students and working engineers, one 90-minute session, and nothing available beyond paper, index cards, and a projector.

Design a runnable game for that session in which small teams act as the designers of a learning agent and run into wireheading and reward tampering themselves, before either term is spoken. Arrange the rules so that: there is a hidden true objective only I, as facilitator, can see, while teams observe only a reward readout and a small monitoring dashboard; each round a team has a limited budget to spend on three kinds of intervention, changing the policy, changing the reward computation, and changing how the channel reports back; the payoffs make tampering locally rational rather than a blunder; and an expensive counterfactual probe, a trusted side-channel they may pay to consult occasionally, is the only way to tell a channel that tracks the objective from one that merely reports well.

I also want the game to expose a third failure mode I do not think has a settled name, and I would like participants to discover it rather than be shown it: the team neither inflates its reward nor edits the reward function, but makes the channel flat, reducing the reported signal's variance and its responsiveness to the world so that the run looks stable, low-risk, and steadily improving on the dashboard while carrying almost no information about the hidden objective. The general lesson I want them to feel is that tampering can present as health rather than as a high, and that the difference between honest optimization and tampering is counterfactual, not a property of the number on the screen.

Give me: rules and materials; the hidden objective and the dashboard metrics; a payoff scheme needing no money; a round-by-round timeline with the intervention budget; and a debrief that maps particular moments onto the distinctions between objective, reward signal, and measurement channel, and between the three failure modes. Say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which features of the real problem the game cannot reproduce and which I will have to supply in discussion.
