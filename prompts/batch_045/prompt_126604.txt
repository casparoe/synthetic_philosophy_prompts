Hi — I'm a philosophy professor (ethics and political, mostly) and I've somehow been dragged onto the committee rewriting our campus conduct code, which has turned into a three-hour argument every Tuesday about how much rule-following a community should actually demand. The thing that keeps derailing us is that people are using "strict" for about four different things at once: how many rules there are, how harshly breaches are punished, how much moral stigma attaches to breaking them, and how wide the domain is that rules reach into. I've started separating them in my own notes and it's already paying off, so I want to build an actual instrument out of it: a decision tree / taxonomy for the normative question "how much rule-following should a society demand?", built the way I'd want it built in a seminar — start from the logical space (the branching questions and the answer-combinations they permit), then locate the real named positions inside that space, then tell me which cells are empty and why (incoherent? unattractive? just nobody's home?), and flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely can't separate instead of pretending a question distinguishes them when it doesn't.

Some constraints from me, since otherwise this turns into a Wikipedia summary of the tight/loose cultures literature. First, the three (or four) axes above have to be keepable apart, and I specifically want the tree to handle what I've been calling the enforcement paradox: for some norms — thank-you notes, apologies, spontaneous offers of help, hospitality — the point of the norm is destroyed if compliance is demanded, so "more enforcement" isn't monotonically "more of the good." Any tree where the answer to every node is "demand more / demand less" along one dimension is missing that. Relatedly, I want a distinction between rules whose value depends on near-universal compliance (which side of the road to drive on, double-entry bookkeeping) and rules whose value is basically independent of what anyone else does — and I want the tree to say what each kind of rule is owed. Second, leaves I need present: a convention/coordination view; a fair-play or reciprocity view where my complying is owed to the people who already complied; an expressive or communitarian view where the rules constitute a shared way of life; the trial-and-error/epistemic view where an inherited rulebook is treated as accumulated, not-fully-articulable knowledge and gets a default in its favor; the harm-principle style view that makes looseness the default; something on the anarchist or enforcement-suspicious end; a view that defends tightness as scaffolding for self-control or moral education rather than for order; and a view that treats looseness itself as a public good, because a permissive society is where new ways of living get tried out. Add whichever others the logical space demands. Third, branch questions have to be answerable cold, by someone who has never heard any of these labels, and each should hang on a concrete case rather than a principle — e.g. jaywalking at 3am on an empty street, a norm nobody can give a reason for but whose disappearance would plainly make things worse, a harmless-but-deeply-offensive behavior, a rule whose breach is invisible to everyone except the breaker, masking during a mild flu season, a dying custom, a norm that only functions if it isn't enforced, and a rule that only works if almost everyone keeps it. Aim for something I could hand to a first-year seminar and to my committee: 8–10 branch questions, 6–9 leaves, one sentence of awkward commitment per leaf, plus a short list of the unoccupied combinations with your diagnosis of each.

And please don't hedge your way through it. I want a committed answer: tell me what you think this code should actually look like — which domains tight, which loose, sanctions mild or severe, how much stigma is appropriate, who gets to do the demanding — and tell me which leaf you'd put yourself on, how confident you are, and what single consideration would most likely move you off it. Where your verdict rests on an empirical guess you can't check from where you're sitting (how people actually react to sanctions, whether looseness really does buy innovation, whether inherited norms really encode local knowledge), say so explicitly and say how much of your recommendation is riding on it — I'd rather have a recommendation plus its load-bearing assumptions than a survey that leaves me to pick. Sorry for the length, and thanks — recieving a 40-page code with no principles in it has made me slightly desperate.
