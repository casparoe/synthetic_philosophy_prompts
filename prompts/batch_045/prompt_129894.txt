Hi — I'm a philosophy professor and, somewhat unluckily, I chair my university's research-integrity committee. Five of us, we meet monthly, about twenty cases a year, and our findings run from "go rewrite your methods section" up to dismissal, so the stakes aren't pretend. Nobody else on the committee has read any philosophy, we get roughly forty minutes a case, and I need something they can actually run without me translating.

Here's the mess. Our new and slightly mad code has four verdicts: honest error; wilful blindness (you suspected, and steered around finding out); self-deception; and knowing misconduct. Wilful blindness costs a semester's suspension, self-deception costs only ethics training plus a year of supervision, on the theory that the self-deceived person couldn't have helped it. We keep deadlocking between verdicts two and three. One member, who's read a bit of Sartre, wants to collapse them entirely: you can't both know and not know a thing, so anything in that middle region is either just ignorance (go easy) or just lying (come down hard). I half think he's right and half think he's throwing away a distinction we need.

So: you're a professional philosopher who works on moral psychology and the philosophy of mind. Think properly before you write. Start by brainstorming — on paper, in your head, wherever — about what self-deception would have to be in order to sit between ignorance and lying at all, and about whether the four boxes can be told apart from the outside, given that our evidence is emails, drafts, meeting minutes and one forty-five minute interview. Then give me a decision procedure: the questions in order, what counts as a yes or a no to each, where it stops early, and where it has to stop and hand the question over to us as a values call rather than a factual one. Assume the people running it have forty minutes and no theory.

Then run it on three cases. (a) A PI who signed off on consent forms she definately never read, and who says, believably, that she was certain her postdoc had fixed them. (b) A postdoc who tried forty-odd specifications of the same regression, reported the one that came out significant, and insists to us with apparent sincerity that this is simply what his field does and that the effect is real. (c) A friend of mine — full professor, and I'd rather she wasnt involved — whose paper contains two paragraphs near-verbatim from a review she herself cited, and who says she genuinely remembers writing them.

At least one of those should come out somewhere I won't like; tell me which, and why you didn't bend the procedure to avoid it. And be honest about what the procedure can't do. Prose only please — no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables: write it the way you'd write a long email to a colleague. Take your time.
