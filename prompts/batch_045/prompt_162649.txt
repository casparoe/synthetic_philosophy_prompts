I sit on the grants panel of a small regional arts council. We hand out around forty micro-grants a year (two or three thousand to eight thousand dollars, so real money to the applicants and nothing much to us), two rounds a year, and each of the four panelists reads about twenty applications in a fortnight on top of a day job. We also have to write two sentences of feedback that go back to every applicant, including the ones we turn down, so we can't just say "not innovative enough" and move on. At the moment the meeting is four people arguing vibes and the loudest person usually wins, and I want something better than that which isn't just a spreadhseet of diversity boxes.

I've been reading Tolstoy's book on art — the one where art is the transmission of feeling by infection, where sincerity matters more than craft, where art that only trained people can catch is suspect, and where he ends up throwing out most of what a panel like ours would fund, his own big novels included. I think his conclusions are unusable for us, but the infection/sincerity framing is, I suspect, exactly what we're reaching for when we say "this one moved me" versus "this one is just clever." So: turn it into a procedure four volunteer panelists with no theory background can run in ten to fifteen minutes an application. I want the questions in order, what counts as a passing answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts.

Four places where I want you to add machinery Tolstoy doesn't give me, and to be honest that you're adding it: (1) his test is a first-exposure test and I don't trust first exposure — build in a re-encounter step, panelist meets the work again a few days later in a different format or setting (a recording instead of live, a phone screen instead of the gallery), and give me a rule for what happens when the two exposures disagree. (2) He's scathing about art you need preparation to be infected by, but we fund string quartets and installation work, so I need that turned into a distinction I can actually apply between "needs craft literacy to land" and "needs status initiation to land," with examples of each. (3) His top-tier demand is that real art carries the religious perception of its age, which obviously can't go in a public funding letter; give me the secular translation you'd defend (something about what the work implies about how to live, or about suffering and death, or about what the community treats as sacred) and say plainly what gets lost in translating it. (4) Whatever you take his catalogue of counterfeit art to be — borrowing, realism-as-fidelity, strikingness, amusement — turn it into flags with thresholds, and tell me which flag will misfire hardest on applications like ours.

Then run the finished procedure on three cases from our last round: a technically fearsomely good contemporary composition that all of us admire and none of us enjoys; a participatory neighbourhood project that is unmistakably sincere and genuinely warms you and is, aesthetically, pretty thin; and an immersive, extremely fun installation with a big audience and not much craft to speak of. At least one of the three has to come out the way my panel will hate. Finish by telling me where the procedure genuinely can't be trusted — satire and irony, work that's cold or withholding on purpose, work from traditions none of us are competent in, and whatever else you think breaks it.
