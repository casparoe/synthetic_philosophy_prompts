I'm TA'ing an intro decision theory course this term and we've got a Death in Damascus session coming up — you know the setup: you're in the market in Damascus, Death tells you he'll be coming for you tomorrow, Death is a (near-)perfect predictor of where you'll be, you can either stay put for free or take the ruinously expensive and deeply unpleasant bus to Aleppo. Every year the session dies in exactly the same way, with half the room shouting "obviously flee, you can't *change* where Death is" and the other half shouting "obviously stay, fleeing is just *evidence* that Death is in Aleppo," and nobody learns anything. So I want to hand out something different: a parody of a travel insurance policy, maybe 700–900 words, issued by an invented firm (something like "Underwriters of Damascus & Aleppo, est. 1873") that sells two products side by side — "Evidential Cover" and "Causal Cover" — as numbered clauses with a short FAQ at the end. Deadpan corporate voice throughout: no winking, no hedging, no narrator stepping in to explain the joke.

The jokes have to come from getting the theory right, not from the words "evidence" and "cause" being repeated. I want clauses built on the real machinery: news value versus causal efficacy, plain conditional probabilities versus counterfactual ones, screening off, the symptom-not-cause line (the tickle/lesion style of reply), ratifiability and decision instability — so the Causal Cover client discovers that no pure act is stable for them and has to buy a randomisation rider with a company-certified coin, plus a surcharge if they insist on being told how the coin landed before the prediction is locked in — and the Evidential Cover client discovers that they should in principle pay to be made to stay, or to be kept ignorant, or to be kidnapped. Keep it fair rather than a cheap shot: the Causal Cover section should be just as humiliating as the Evidential one, and the FAQ should include at least one quote from a concerned outside consultant who is himself a causal decision theorist and is not happy about what his own theory implies for the product. Somewhere in the middle, give a worked payout example with explicit numbers (pick your own: a utility for dying, a utility for surviving, a cost for the bus, an accuracy for Death) and make sure the arithmetic on both policies is actually consistent with that theory's recommendation. To keep it from being just a rehash, invent a couple of fresh details for the firm: say, an in-house actuarial predictor it uses to price policies and which has a self-prediction problem of its own, a "Blind Booking" add-on that arranges for you never to hear Death's warning (priced very differently under the two policies), and a clause for clients who try to buy both policies at once.

Two hard constraints: the Evidential Cover clauses must never reason causally and the Causal Cover clauses must never reason evidentially, unless the slip is itself the joke — and no real philosophers get named anywhere, invented ones only (a Professor of Counterfactual Underwriting, a rival actuary, whatever). Aim it at someone who knows the EDT/CDT literature well enough to laugh instead of wince, but write it so a student who has never heard of either theory can follow it and can tell what's being made fun of. End on whatever clause the logic actually forces, even if it's bleak.
