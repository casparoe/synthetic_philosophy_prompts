Bit of background so you know where I'm coming from. I'm retired now — thirty-odd years as a fish physiologist, mostly salmonids, some work on crustacean stress physiology later on — and since I packed it in I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group, and, less自愿, on the technical committee of a farmed-seafood welfare certification scheme. The committee argument I keep having is this: our two working definitions of welfare both shout "this system is failing", but they point at completely different things to fix, and the fix for one makes the other worse. I want a case I can put in front of new assessors in training that makes that bind vivid, so that they stop thinking welfare is just one number you measure.

What I want you to build:

A thought experiment set in a real production system — a sea cage, a recirculating system, a pond, a raceway, whatever you can describe honestly. Pick an actual species and commit to it: real behaviours, real physiology, no invented wonder-tank, no sci-fi. Two accounts have to be run on it:

- the affective / feelings-based one, where welfare is the balance of positive and negative subjective states the animal actually undergoes;
- the natural-function or "telos" or natural-living one, where welfare is a matter of the animal being able to exercise the repertoire and capacities that are characteristic of its kind.

The specification: both accounts must judge welfare to be poor, but they must locate the bad-making feature in different places, and — this is the part that matters — the intervention each account recommends on the basis of its own diagnosis must aggravate the other account's diagnosis. So the hedonic diagnosis points to change A, which makes the function-based picture worse; the function-based diagnosis points to change B, which makes the hedonic picture worse. Both changes should be things a farm could genuinely do: stocking density, adding or removing structure, grading, feeding or light regime, that sort of thing.

One wrinkle I want built in deliberately, because it comes up at every meeting and nobody handles it well. Once an animal has been chronically stressed for weeks, the cheap physiological indicator stops being readable in the naive way: the axis down-regulates, the response to a fresh challenge comes out flat or low, the diurnal rhythm flattens. So a low number can mean "coping fine" or "burnt out", depending on history. I want that trap in the case, and I want you to say what an assessor is supposed to conclude when the physiological indicator and the behavioural evidence disagree. Please don't let the case be settled by "cortisol was high therefore bad".

Cheating I'd consider cheating: nobody gets to say we can't know what a fish or a prawn feels; nobody gets to win on the pain-versus-nociception vocabulary point; neither account is allowed to be dismissed as unscientific or as sentimentality; and neither verdict is allowed to come from someone simply mis-measuring. The disagreement has to fall out of what each account actually says welfare is.

Deliverables, and please keep the whole thing under about 800 words, with the scenario itself under 250:

1. The scenario.
2. For each account: its verdict, and the reasoning from its own criteria, not from mine.
3. The two recommended interventions, and the mechanism by which each one backfires on the other diagnosis.
4. One short note on which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the case if you remove that feature — I expect it should collapse into a boring case where everyone agrees, and I want to know that it does.
5. What a defender of each account would say to wriggle out of the bind.

And no references, reading lists or citations please — I've got a folder full of papers I haven't read and I definately don't need more.
