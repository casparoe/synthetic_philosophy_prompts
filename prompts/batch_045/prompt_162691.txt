I'm closing out a six-week undergraduate reading group on Madhyamaka next week — the students are mostly philosophy majors with no Sanskrit, working from translations — and for the last ten minutes of the final session I'd like to hand out and read aloud a short poem instead of the summary handout I'd otherwise write. Could you write it for me?

The formal constraint I have in mind: five quatrains (say 20–28 lines total), with the first four giving one quatrain to each corner of the catuṣkoṭi. Crucially, all four should be about the *same* single, concrete, ordinary subject with the *same* predicate — one image, persisted in rather than swapped out between stanzas: a lamp flame passed from one wick to another, a face in a mirror, a sprout and the seed it came from, a person returning to a room they left years ago. Each of the four stanzas should close on the same refrain line, altered only by the negation (it is / it is not / it both is and is not / it neither is nor is not), so the recurrence of the line is what carries the sense that these are four positions on one question and not four moods. The fifth quatrain is the coda: I want the refrain there to come back as it stood in the first stanza, unchanged, so that the reader feels the negation hasn't installed a subtler thing in the place of what it cleared away and the conventional fact survives intact. Someone who knows the material should be able to say which quatrain is which corner without being told, and someone who doesn't should still find it a good poem.

Two ways this could go wrong that I'd like you to steer around. First, don't let the third corner read as a dialectical synthesis or the fourth as a mystical elevation above logic — both corners have to be refused on the *same* ground as the first two, the absence of svabhāva in the subject, and not from some deeper vantage point; likewise, don't let emptiness itself harden into a substance, a ground, or a luminous void behind appearances, since śūnyatā is empty too. Second, don't give me the glib nihilist version ("nothing exists, nothing matters"), which is precisely what the returning refrain in the coda is meant to block. And I'd rather the poem not smuggle in an assertoric thesis of its own: the poem should feel as if it knows that the negation isn't a position, without saying so in jargon. Sanskrit terms are fine in moderation, but no glossary and no footnotes inside the verse.

Please commit to a definite interpretive reading rather than hedging between readings — I'd rather have one gamble clearly taken than a poem that tries to be neutral. Then add a short prose note (roughly 100–150 words) after the verse in which you own that gamble in the first person: tell me where the poem leans on a contested point and what you had to decide in order to write it — for instance, whether you treated the fourfold negation as an exhaustive refusal or as a therapeutic device, and whether the two truths functioned for you as two descriptions of one world or as two orders of reality. Say which you chose and why, without listing the alternatives for me to weigh. If you think some other formal device carries the structure better than five quatrains with a recurring refrain, by all means use it and explain the choice in the same note. Please write in American English.
