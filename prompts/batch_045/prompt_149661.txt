I came to philosophy late, after a career in enzyme kinetics — thirty-odd years spent arguing about which step in a pathway is rate-limiting, and about the difference between the route a reaction actually takes and the alternative routes the molecules could in principle have taken. That distinction is doing a good deal of unexamined work in the graduate reading group I now sit in on, and last week it produced an argument I couldn't settle. We were going over Frankfurt-style cases, and a friend — a retired judge — insisted that the literature is covertly answering two different questions at once: what makes an agent answerable for what she did, and what license the rest of us have to blame, punish, or demand an account of her. She may be right, and before I read any further into the debate over the principle of alternative possibilities, I'd like the specification rather than another position paper.

So: set out what an account of the "could have done otherwise" condition on moral responsibility has to deliver in order to count as adequate for a stated purpose — and let the purpose be this one, fixed in advance: an account good enough to underwrite the excuse conditions we actually apply, in ordinary interpersonal blame and in the criminal law's mitigation doctrines, without requiring that we first settle whether determinism is true. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark it non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp — I want to know which of the things partisans demand are genuine desiderata and which are inherited prejudice. The material I want covered, in whatever dependency order you think right rather than mine: the original Frankfurt case and the flicker-of-freedom reply to it; the dilemma about whether the intervener's trigger is deterministic or chancy; plain compulsion and duress, which had better come out differently from the Frankfurt case; omissions and failures to prevent; manipulation and indoctrination cases, where the agent satisfies every local condition on acting freely but came to satisfy it through someone else's design; resultant moral luck, where two agents do the same thing and only the outcome differs; agents with diminished capacity; the difference between the agent's being unable to do otherwise and her not knowing that she was unable; and whether answerability is historical or a matter of the agent's structure at the time of action.

Three cases of my own, which I haven't seen handled and which I want the list forced to decide rather than left at the margin. First, the reason-swap variant: the counterfactual device does not merely guarantee the behavior, it guarantees it by displacing the agent's actual reasons — she signs the petition, but under intervention she would have signed it out of an induced panic rather than from the conviction she in fact acted on. Does what answerability requires concern the behavior's occurring or the sequence of reasons that produced it, and what does your answer cost you elsewhere on the list? Second, the decaying monitor: the device is unreliable in a way the agent does not know about, so her real ability to do otherwise is partial and dwindles — say it falls from near-certain to negligible over the hour before she acts. Does answerability fade continuously with it, is there a threshold, or is the question malformed because ability is not a scalar quantity? Third, the boardroom case: a committee of seven votes four to three to recommend a policy, each member for his own considered reasons, and the chair — who voted with the majority — held a standing authority she would certainly have exercised to impose the identical recommendation had the vote gone the other way. Is the committee answerable, do the individual members' verdicts change, and which of your requirements quietly presuppose a single nervous system?

Then the part I care most about, because it is where I suspect the real result lies: identify which subset of your own requirements you think cannot be jointly satisfied, and explain why. My guess is that something has to give either between the pull of the manipulation cases toward a historical condition and the pull of the Frankfurt cases toward an actual-sequence one, or between demanding robustness against luck and denying that alternative possibilities matter at all — but if the genuine incompatibility is elsewhere, say so. And name the single requirement that does the most exclusionary work, the one that earns its place on the list by knocking out live candidates rather than by being intuitively appealing. Close with a brief scorecard, a paragraph at most each, for the classical principle of alternative possibilities, for actual-sequence or guidance-control style views, and for source incompatibilism — but keep the weight of your answer on the criteria and their motivating cases, since the criteria are what I will actually use when I go back to the literature.
