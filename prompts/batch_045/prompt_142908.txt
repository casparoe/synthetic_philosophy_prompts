I spent three hours after a department meeting last night arguing with a senior colleague about a rule that almost certainly should not exist, and I came away unable to locate where exactly we disagree. The rule requires every PhD student in our department to pass a written translation exam in two languages before being admitted to candidacy. We looked up why it was there: it was introduced so that students could work directly with untranslated archival sources. Almost nobody here does archival work in a second language now; the exam has decayed into a formality that people clear after a fortnight of cramming, and it mostly functions as a scheduling bottleneck. My colleague still refused to vote it down, invoking Chesterton's fence — do not remove a fence until you know why it was put up. When I pointed out that we do know why it was put up, and that the reason has lapsed, he said that this is precisely the case for maximum caution: a rule that has outlived its stated rationale has usually been quietly repurposed, and is now doing something nobody wrote down and nobody will notice until it is gone. Call that *function drift*. I replied that the drift claim is close to unfalsifiable, since the benefits of retention are never observed while the costs of removal always are, and that the same reasoning run in the other direction — toward every rule we never got around to adopting, the unfenced field, the inherited absence — would counsel preserving every absence too, which cannot be right. He said the asymmetry is real because repeal is an action and leaving alone is not. I said that is a distinction about agency, not about evidence. We went around this loop four times.

So I want a number, with its derivation. Take the general claim my colleague is committed to — call it the drift presumption: for a representative sample of inherited institutional rules whose original rationale has demonstrably lapsed, keeping the rule turns out better than removing it over a twenty-year horizon, and it turns out better *because* the rule was performing some function that a competent reforming committee could not have named in advance. Resolve it counterfactually: sample such rules across large institutions, remove half at random, follow both groups for two decades, score outcomes against each institution's own stated aims, and also ask whether the losing side's losses were foreseeable ex ante by people who had studied the rule in good faith. Give me your credence that the retention side wins on both the outcome question and the foreseeability question — or, if you think that conjunction is the wrong thing to put a number on, restate the proposition in the nearest form that does have a truth value and number that, saying why you moved it. My own credence is around 0.35; his is around 0.75.

What I want is the decomposition, not the headline number. Break the question into the sub-questions it actually rides on and give each a rough probability and one line of justification. The ones I would insist on: how often a rule whose rationale has lapsed retains any function at all rather than being genuinely vestigial; conditional on retaining one, how often that function is one a good-faith reformer could not have identified beforehand; how often the retained function is genuinely valuable to the institution as opposed to valuable to some subgroup that happens to hold power (the capture possibility, which the drift story tends to hide); how much of the apparent base rate is an artifact of the evidential asymmetry, since we see what happens when fences come down and never what happens when they stay up; whether the asymmetry between repeal and failure-to-enact can be defended in a way that does not just restate the conclusion; whether the presumption is self-limiting, on the grounds that an institution that never repeals also never learns and so forfeits the very evidence the presumption is supposed to be a proxy for; and how sensitive any of this is to the rate of change in the surrounding environment, since the Burkean picture of an inheritance held in trust across generations was formed in a world that changed more slowly than ours does.
