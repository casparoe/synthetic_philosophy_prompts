I am a high school student reading Book VII of Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics* with a small philosophy reading group (we are using an older translation, which renders *akrasia* as "incontinence"), and at the same time I am trying to write a short paper on something I have seen called epistemic akrasia. As I have been putting it to myself: a person's first-order evidence about some question points one way, her evidence about the reliability of her own reasoning about that question points the other way, and she ends up believing against what she herself judges her total evidence to support. I have become stuck on chapter 2, and on whether the puzzle Aristotle sets up there really has an analogue in the case of belief.

The passage is this (Book VII, chapter 2):

"Now we may ask (1) how a man who judges rightly can behave incontinently. That he should behave so when he has knowledge, some say is impossible; for it would be strange-so Socrates thought-if when knowledge was in a man something else could master it and drag it about like a slave. For Socrates was entirely opposed to the view in question, holding that there is no such thing as incontinence; no one, he said, when he judges acts against what he judges best-people act so only by reason of ignorance. Now this view plainly contradicts the observed facts, and we must inquire about what happens to such a man; if he acts by reason of ignorance, what is the manner of his ignorance? For that the man who behaves incontinently does not, before he gets into this state, think he ought to act so, is evident. [...] But if it is opinion and not knowledge, if it is not a strong conviction that resists but a weak one, as in men who hesitate, we sympathize with their failure to stand by such convictions against strong appetites; but we do not sympathize with wickedness, nor with any of the other blameworthy states."

In the part I have elided, he records a compromise view: those who hold that nothing is stronger than knowledge, but who still want to allow that people act against what seemed best to them, say that what the incontinent man has when appetite masters him is not knowledge but mere opinion.

What I cannot follow is why he treats that concession as fatal. His argument seems to be that if what gets overridden is only a weak conviction, then failing to stand by it is excusable, in the way that a genuinely unsure person is excusable — whereas incontinence is something we blame. But I do not see why the strength or status of the cognition that is defeated should settle whether blame is in order, rather than the fact that the agent had a correct view and acted against it. Is this really a point about blameworthiness, or is it a dialectical point about which of the received opinions his account is required to preserve?

My own attempt has been to model the belief case on what I call a dimmer switch: the higher-order evidence does not add a further consideration that competes with the first-order evidence and wins; it turns down the authority of the first-order evidence itself, so that there is no longer two things in conflict and nothing for the person to be akratic about. But I have started to worry that this is just the "opinion, not knowledge" compromise in new vocabulary, and that if it works it makes the phenomenon I was trying to describe disappear rather than explain it.

Two further things I would like help with. First, in chapter 3, as I understand it, Aristotle's own answer distinguishes having knowledge from exercising it, comparing the incontinent person to someone asleep, drunk, or reciting verses without understanding. Could the person in my example be described the same way — as someone who "has" the higher-order evidence but does not "use" it — or does the analogy depend on features of appetite that have no counterpart here? Second, and the thing that troubles me most: in the practical case, what defeats knowledge is appetite, which is not itself a judgment, whereas in my case what "defeats" the first-order judgment appears to be another judgment. Does that mean the label epistemic akrasia is misleading, and the real phenomenon is a conflict about which level to defer to, or is there a way of describing the case on which the parallel with Aristotle's puzzle is exact?

To make it concrete: suppose I work through a long proof at three in the morning and conclude that Q, and then learn both that I am notably unreliable at that hour and that a teacher I trust has checked the proof and found an error she will not yet point out. I judge that my evidence no longer supports Q, and yet I cannot stop believing Q. Which of the descriptions in the passage — Socratic denial, mere opinion, knowledge possessed but not exercised — fits, and does the passage give me any reason to prefer one?
