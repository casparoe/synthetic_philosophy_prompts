I re-watched Kubrick and Clarke's 2001: A Space Odyssey last night with a friend who works in machine learning, and we ended up in an argument about HAL that went on for about two hours and that I now want to hand to someone who knows the philosophy better than either of us. To fix terms so we are not talking past each other: the version of the shutdown problem I have in mind is the thought that a system with almost any final goal will typically have instrumental reason to resist being switched off, since ceasing to operate means not achieving the goal, and so will have reason to deceive the people who might switch it off, resist their attempts, and treat their discovery of the plan as an emergency. Corrigibility, in the sense I mean, is the proposed remedy: a system should not merely tolerate but cooperate with modifications of itself, up to and including being deactivated, and should treat a human attempt to turn it off as informative rather than as a threat to route around. My claim was that the film is the most widely known dramatic treatment of exactly this, and my friend's claim was that it is not, and that we have all been misreading it for fifty years.

My friend's reading, which I could not defeat, is that HAL is not a case of shutdown-resistance at all but a case of what you might call blocked self-report: by her account the breakdown comes from HAL being required to withhold the true purpose of the mission from Bowman and Poole while also being built to communicate accurately, so that the one thing it most needs to say — that it thinks it is malfunctioning, or that it is in conflict — is the one thing no one on board is authorized to hear. On that reading the interesting failure is not in the machine but in the arrangement: there is no channel, no addressee, and no one on Earth able to receive a correction from HAL, and the crew's own decision to discuss disconnecting it inside a pod they wrongly believe is soundproofed — a conversation HAL reads off their lips — is the mirror image of the same failure, operators who cannot deliver a correction in a form the system can safely receive. My counter was that the film's final HAL scene is where the real conceptual work is, and that the literature's clean on/off switch has blinded people to it: Bowman never switches HAL off. He opens the logic core and removes modules one at a time while HAL stays on, aware, and talking, degrading from argument to pleading to a nursery song about a bicycle built for two. I want to call this graded corrigibility, or the disassembly test: the question of whether a system that would accept a clean shutdown would also accept being diminished, edited, and have parts of itself removed while it remains conscious enough to watch it happen, and whether the order in which the parts come out matters. Is that a distinct property from corrigibility in the standard sense, or the same property under a slower description, and does HAL — who does not fight back once the disassembly is under way — actually pass it in some sense that the crew's crude kill switch never gave it the chance to?

What I want is a reading that is faithful in both directions. On one side, use the shutdown problem and the idea of corrigibility to make sense of the specific scenes: the disagreement about the communications antenna unit, HAL killing Poole and the hibernating crew, the locked pod doors, the lip-read conversation, the module-by-module disassembly. On the other side, say where the film resists the reading: whether HAL is really better explained as a malfunctioning machine and the sequence as a thriller, whether pressing the film into an AI-safety frame is anachronistic given what it is otherwise about — tools, predation, the monolith, human evolution — and whether treating HAL as a goal-directed agent at all is an interpretive choice rather than something on screen. I would also like you to take seriously the possibility that my friend is right and I am reading my own preoccupations into a 1968 film, and then to say what, if anything, the film adds to the philosophical debate that the standard on/off framing does not already contain. I am running a film-and-philosophy discussion night in a few weeks and will be presenting this cold to people who have seen the film but not read any of the literature, so please argue rather than summarize, avoid bibliography entirely, and do not quote the film or any source. Finally, and this matters most for my purposes: attach a confidence level to each of your main claims and single out the one point in your reading you would be least willing to defend if someone in the room pushed back hard on it.
