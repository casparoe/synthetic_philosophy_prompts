Ok so context: a small foundation I advise has maybe $4M to spend over five years and one question it keeps circling back to: whether violence is actually declining. That answer decides whether the next grant cycle goes to violence-reduction work, to measurement-and-indicators work, or somewhere else entirely. What I need from you is a conceptual research agenda — four or five questions a handfull of senior researchers could genuinely move on in five years using argument plus data that already exists (we can't fund large new data collection) — ordered by how much they'd change that funding decision.

Some candidate questions to steal from, replace, or throw out entirely:

- The denominator problem. Per capita vs. absolute is the usual framing, but people today have vastly more interactions, disputes, and transactions per year than people in a village of 300 did, so per-person-year rates may badly understate exposure per social contact. Is there a better unit — per dispute, per enforced norm, per hour of unsupervised contact — and does switching it change the direction of the trend or only its slope?

- The violence possibility frontier: the maximum harm a society could inflict given its current technology, coordination, and energy budget. If actual violence falls while the frontier keeps rising, is that a reduction or violence being stored? Does "stored violence" belong in the accounting at all, and if it does, how would you even put a number on it without new empirical work?

- Displaced or counterfactual violence: a lot of peace is produced by credible threats (deterrence, borders, prisons, sanctions). If the harm is prevented by a standing capacity to do harm rather than by its absence, is that violence reduced or relocated? Same question for substitution, where violence gets replaced by non-violent coercion — reputation systems, credit exclusion, algorithmic gatekeeping.

- Death-weighted vs. suffering-weighted vs. rights-violation-weighted accounting, and whether the trend reverses depending on which you pick.

- Whether there's a fact of the matter at all about "violence" going down, or whether it's a cluster concept with genuinely independent components.

For each question you keep: why it's upstream of the others, how tractable it is for philosophers and theoretically-minded social scientists, how neglected it is, what a good-enough answer would look like at 18 months, and what kind of finding would push it up or down the list.

Two more things. First, name one or two questions that sound central but are actually traps — where five years of work wouldn't change the funding decision either way — and say why they're traps; I suspect the "is there even a fact of the matter" one might be. Second, tell me which single question, if it came out the other way, would flip the whole portfolio, and what result would do it. And please don't give me a survey of the debate or point me at books and papers to read — I want the defended ordering and the reasoning behind it.
