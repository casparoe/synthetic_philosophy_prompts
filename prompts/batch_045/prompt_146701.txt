You are a philosopher of mind who has worked on the unity of consciousness. Please think this through carefully before answering: start by sketching three or four candidate diagnoses of the impasse below, then argue for one. Answer in American English.

The dispute: in a callosotomy patient, is there one subject of consciousness or two? The findings have been in the literature since the 1960s and have not changed much, and neither have the positions. One side says the disconnected right hemisphere understands language, forms intentions, and acts on information the left lacks, so there are two conscious perspectives in one skull. The other says what we actually have is a single reporting agent — the speaking hemisphere — plus a disconnected system that can be cued into skilled responses without thereby being a subject, and that left-hemisphere confabulation has been inflating the data from the beginning. Decades, same experiments, no movement.

My hypothesis is that "consciousness" is doing four different jobs here — unity of a single experience, unity of access, unity of a decision-making agent, unity of a self over time — and that the two sides are quietly answering different ones. But I also suspect a methodological dispute hiding inside the metaphysical one: because nearly all the evidence has to reach us through the speaking hemisphere, the live question may really be what could ever count as evidence for a consciousness that cannot speak.

Here is a case I want your diagnosis to earn its keep on. Suppose surgeons could regrow a functional commissure whose conduction delay could be dialed up and down: below roughly 40 ms nothing changes; between about 50 and 80 ms bilateral tasks reveal each hemisphere working with information the other lacks, while the patient's ordinary behavior stays coordinated and they never report any division of experience; above 100 ms the classic disconnection syndrome returns.

Does that hypothetical result settle anything, and for whom? If it settles nothing, explain why that very fact is diagnostic. What kind of disagreement is this — verbal, substantive but empirically stalled, evidential, or ill-posed — and what would count as progress: a distinction, an experiment, a change of question, or nothing? State how confident you are in your diagnosis and single out the claim in it you are least sure of.
