Hey — I've been reading a popular book about forecasting and decision-making (one of those Kahneman-adjacent ones) and it has a chapter on the "inside view / outside view" thing that I've been recomending to everyone. But I've also been arguing with a friend who does cost estimation for a living, and he says the chapter is basically propaganda for the outside view. I don't know enough to adjudicate, and I'd rather not just trust the book because it's confident.

Here's roughly how the chapter puts it:

"The inside view is the natural way to think about a project: you look at the specifics of your own case, break the work into steps, imagine how it will go, and add up the estimates. The outside view throws all that away. You find a reference class of similar cases, look at the distribution of how those actually turned out, and use that as your estimate — the base rate is the only thing that matters, because your sense of what makes your case special is exactly the thing that makes you overconfident. This is what cures the planning fallacy. It's really just applied Bayesianism: the base rate over outcomes is your prior, the details of your project are your likelihood, and updating one with the other gives you the posterior you should act on. And it's also the epistemically modest stance — instead of trusting your own story, you defer to the aggregated experience of everyone who came before you."

My friend's complaints, as best I can reconstruct them: (a) that the original version says to anchor on the reference class and *then* adjust for what's distinctive about your case, so the "throws all that away" line is a strawman; (b) that the historical outcomes in a lot of these reference classes were themselves produced by planners using the inside view, so the "outside" number is just the inside view's errors averaged together, which he thinks makes the whole move circular, or at least means you'd have to correct the base rate in a direction nobody ever does; and (c) that "pick similar cases" is doing a ton of work, because you can always narrow the class until it tells you what you wanted to hear — I've definately caught myself doing this with my own writing deadlines, I can always find a sub-class where my case looks fine.

What I want from you: go through the chapter's version and tell me what a defender of the inside/outside view distinction would actually accept, what's a fair simplification for a popular book, and what's a real misrepresentation — especially the Bayesian framing and the modesty framing, both of which sounded right to me and might not be. Does it matter that "modest" is being used in a way that has nothing to do with how confident you end up being? And if (b) and (c) are right, does the outside view still have anything to recomend it over just doing the inside view more carefully, or does it collapse into picking the reference class that gives you the answer you like? I'd also like to know what a defender would concede as the genuine cost of the outside view — what you give up — because the chapter makes it sound free, and the one story in it where Kahneman's own team ignored the outside view estimate and blew way past their own plan doesn't really tell me whether the cost is worth paying.
