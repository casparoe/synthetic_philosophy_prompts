I am writing a novel set in a clinic that treats modal regret: people tormented by the thought that some version of them, somewhere, did not make the mistake they made. The clinic's doctrine — which I want the story to take seriously rather than debunk — is that no one is ever in more than one world. The woman in the happier world is not the patient; she is only someone who resembles the patient, to a greater or lesser degree, in respects that shift with what is at stake. Treatment therefore consists in thinning the resemblance: patients surrender a skill, a name, a friendship, until the counterpart's good fortune belongs to a stranger.

Write a short story I could use as a chapter, roughly 1,000 to 1,400 words, narrated by a senior clinician, about a patient who arrives convinced that the counterpart is literally her, and who wants not the procedure but an answer. The patient should be living inside one picture of what it is for a person to have properties across possibilities — the same individual present in two worlds, so that modal claims are about her directly — while the clinician lives inside the other, where everything is resemblance, context, and degree, and where there is sometimes no fact of the matter at all. Neither should lecture. The metaphysics should surface in what they do: in how the resemblance is measured, in what the clinic charges, in what the patient is willing to give up, and in the clinician's final action when the patient asks whether the woman in the happier world is her.

Constraints: no character named for a philosopher, no references to the literature, and no dialogue that states the theory outright. Let me feel which picture is winning through the choices the characters make. And leave genuinely open whether the clinic is a fraud — I have not decided what the novel believes.
