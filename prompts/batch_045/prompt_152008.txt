I want ONE essay question, answerable in about 800 words, that forces them past that summary.

Here's the case I want them to chew on. A country is drafting a constitution, and the framers want to shield a few provisions (say, an independent central bank and judicial tenure) from being swept away in a fit of populist anger. Instead of a flat supermajority they propose what I've started calling an escalating or ratchet entrenchment clause: any repeal motion that fails raises the threshold for the next repeal attempt by five percentage points, and there has to be a two-year gap between attempts. The thought is that repeated failure to repeal is evidence the country genuinely doesn't want repeal, so the provision gets stickier the more it survives.

Ask them whether this device is really a Ulysses contract at all — or something else in Ulysses clothing — and whether the ratchet fixes or quietly worsens the standard objections to precommitment. Things I care about, and hope you fold into the question: the disanalogy between one person binding their own later, Siren-impaired self and one generation binding a partly different population; whether a mechanism that penalises repeated attempts can seperate an impaired reversal from a considered change of mind; and the fact that the clause's own beneficiaries can force repeated votes, lose them on purpose, and ratchet the protection up, so the device can be gamed by exactly the faction it protects.

Give me the question, a model answer of roughly 700–800 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points with the marks each is worth. For each point, one line on how students typically botch it. The A/B split has to turn on specific moves rather than on how much they've read.
