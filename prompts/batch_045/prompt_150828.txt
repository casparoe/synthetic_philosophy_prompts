I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine, and I'm trying to turn an argument I watched unfold into a column about hypocrisy — not the political sort, but the narrower question of whether a hypocrite loses the standing to blame. I couldn't referee the argument myself, and I don't want to signal which side I found persuasive, so I'm handing it over. The case my two friends were fighting about: a media critic we'll call Nadia wrote a column in March attacking a rival outlet for publishing an anonymously sourced leak about a political campaign without independent corroboration, calling it a betrayal of that outlet's own stated standards and saying its editors owed readers an apology. In May, Nadia's own magazine ran an anonymously sourced story about a federal agency's suppression of data, edited by someone other than Nadia, resting on a single document. A reader wrote in that Nadia has no standing to blame anyone for anything until she has fixed her own house. Nadia replied in a follow-up column that standing is a distraction: either publishing the leak was wrong or it wasn't, and a norm does not stop binding because the person invoking it is imperfect.

The exchange then went like this. (1) Dev: the reader is basically right, and Nadia's reply misunderstands blame. Blame isn't a verdict you issue from nowhere; it's a form of moral address in which you implicitly claim the authority to hold someone to a norm, and the address misfires when you don't accept that norm for yourself. So the defect in hypocritical blame isn't that its content is false; it's that the speech act is infelicitous, like a promise made with no intention behind it. (2) Marisol: that conflates hypocrisy with weakness of will. Nadia argued against running the May story in the editorial meeting, lost, and wrote an internal memo recording her objection; she accepts the norm and failed to get her way. On Dev's own test — does she accept the norm? — she has standing, and the reader's letter is an insult with a philosophical vocabulary bolted onto it. (3) Dev: acceptance is the wrong test, and here is the case that shows it. Take a prospective hypocrite: spotless record, sincerely endorses the norm as a general proposition, and announces that she will violate it at the next convenient opportunity. Her hands are clean, she has taken no unfair advantage, she isn't singling anyone out — and yet her blame rings hollow. So no backward-looking story (dirty hands, unequal burden, selective enforcement) can explain the case; what does the work is commitment, and commitment is forward-looking. Applied to Nadia, the question isn't her May record but whether she'd do it again, and since her own outlet did exactly that and she kept her job without public protest, the reader's doubt isn't unreasonable. (4) Marisol: two replies. First, that relocates the whole dispute onto a prediction about Nadia's future conduct, which the reader never established; a standing deficit that depends on a forecast is a credibility discount wearing a more dramatic name. Second, Nadia blames in her capacity as a critic for her magazine, on behalf of readers, not as someone nursing a personal grievance; representative blame is meant to register a community's verdict about a norm violation, and a messenger's record bears on how much weight to give the message, not on whether it can be delivered at all. On Dev's view, any journalist who has ever cut a corner permanently loses the ability to write about standards. (5) Dev: the institutional framing cuts the other way. If the blame is issued in the magazine's name, the magazine's record is the relevant one, and the magazine is the repeat offender — the same editor who approved Nadia's March column approved the May story. Distributing enforcement and violation across different employees of one institution shouldn't launder the problem; call it laundered standing. And there is a genuine unfairness: the March column imposed a reputational cost on another newsroom's editors while Nadia's own shop paid nothing, an advantage she isn't entitled to take. (6) Marisol: on laundering, if we aggregate at the institutional level we should aggregate the audience too — the letter-writing readers who deplore anonymous sourcing in principle and share every anonymously sourced scoop they enjoy are complicit in the very practice they denounce, and if hypocrisy destroyed standing they'd have none left for their complaint about Nadia. On unfairness: Nadia has since criticized her own magazine in print, publicly and at some cost to herself; if even-handed enforcement is what fairness requires, she has met it. What survives in the reader's objection is a complaint about manner — that the March column was prosecutorial and self-righteous, and that self-righteousness is what people actually hear in the word hypocrisy — which is a real complaint, but not one about standing.

What I want from you is a point-by-point adjudication: for each of the six moves, say whether it is right, partly right, or wrong and why; say where each of them is arguing past the other; and say what both are missing. Two things occurred to me afterward that neither of them addressed. First, a demandingness mismatch: the March column demanded an absolute rule against unverified anonymous sourcing, while Nadia's own magazine justified the May story on a case-by-case public-interest balancing test, so arguably she holds others to a stricter standard than the one applied at home — and I don't know whether that kind of mismatch is the heart of hypocrisy or merely one instance of it, or whether it matters that the two stories concerned different subject matter. Second, I don't know whether lacking standing is meant to be binary (the blame is void, or infelicitous) or scalar (the blame is diminished, or merely less credible), and what the difference would imply for how the rival outlet's editors should properly respond — resentment, or an eye-roll and a tu quoque of their own. Please also tell me, for each rating and for your overall verdict, how confident you are, and single out the one claim in your adjudication you're least sure of. Some of this may end up in print under my own byline, so give me a verdict rather than a survey of the options — but flag clearly where a specialist would say the question is genuinely unsettled.
