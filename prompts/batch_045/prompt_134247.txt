I teach a first-year seminar called Chance and Choice — 20 students, one 75-minute slot, a whiteboard, one coin (or a phone if the coin goes missing), index cards, and no budget. I need an activity built around the St Petersburg setup, and I want it to be a game they actually play rather than a paradox I describe to them.

The variant Ive been messing with: payoffs double with every tail — heads on the first flip pays 1 point, one tail then heads pays 2, two tails then heads pays 4, and so on — with a hard cap of 1000 points, because that's the entire prize budget (three chocolate bars for the three biggest balances at the end, everyone starts with the same stake of play money, and they're allowed to trade contract rights with each other). One contract is sold by sealed bid before any flipping, then flipped in public one toss at a time, and between tosses the room re-prices the live contract: everyone writes a bid to buy, the holder writes an ask.

Two things I want them to walk out having felt, and I want the rules to produce them rather than me announcing them afterwards:

- A price can be exactly the right price and still lose money in the single case that actually happens, because nearly all of what makes it right sits in branches that didn't occur. I'd love the moment where someone correctly refuses an offer of 30 for a contract that then pays out 16, and everyone can see both that the refusal was right and that it lost.
- The right price of the live contract moves while nothing about the coin changes. My own numbers: break-even before the first flip is about 6 points, and after each tail it roughly doubles — 6, 11, 20, 36, 64, 111, 190, 317, 506, 756, then a flat 1000 once you're ten tails deep, since from there the cap is guaranteed. Check those and correct me if Ive got them wrong. The pull to think a head is overdue should push the room's bids the wrong way here, and I want that to be something they do, not something I tell them.

Two constraints. First: the usual diminishing-marginal-utility move is off the table. The prize is a chocolate bar, there's nothing to be risk-averse about, and I want them to feel that the puzzle is still there when that escape route is closed. Second: don't let them hear the phrase expected value until the debrief — make them derive the break-even price themselves from the payoff table, in pairs, before the auction opens.

What I want back: materials and setup; the rules, including the trading and the payout scheme; a timeline in minutes; what I write on the board and at what point; a debrief that maps specific moments onto the two ideas above; and an honest section on what will predictably go wrong. Include at least these: the real chance the game ends on flip one and there's nothing to re-price (parallel contracts in small groups? a fixed sequence instead of live flips? running it twice? — pick one and say what it costs); the student who has heard of this before and shouts out the answer in the first five minutes and kills the auction; and the student who bids 400 to show off and distorts every market price after that. Also tell me which part of the idea the game can't reproduce and I'll have to say out loud — I suspect it's the actual infinity, since my cap is what makes the price finite in the first place, but you tell me. Please don't put anything in the debrief you're not confident about; flag it instead of sounding sure.
