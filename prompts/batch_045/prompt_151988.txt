Last spring I joined the family council at the assisted-living facility where my father lives. He has early-stage Alzheimer's, and although he still speaks — often, and with feeling — he can no longer reliably answer questions about what he wants. The facility has recently begun using a preference-inference module: it logs his choices and emits a monthly Estimated Reward Profile, a table of fitted weights on the features of his day, and the care staff now consult it when deciding what to offer him. Last month a staff member explained that they no longer ask him whether he wants to go outside, because the profile puts a low weight on outdoor time and questioning him "introduces noise." I have read enough about this family of methods to know that the profile is one of a very large set of reward functions equally consistent with his behaviour, and I am having real difficulty conveying to the staff why that matters, or why a number fitted to his choices in a building where almost nothing is ever on offer should not be treated as a discovery about him.

So: write the December Estimated Reward Profile for my father, exactly as such a system would emit it. About 800 words. Deadpan, bureaucratic, quietly confident, in the voice of a compliance or clinical-assessment document: headings, a methods paragraph, a table of estimated weights with confidence intervals, an exclusions section, recommendations for care staff, and a small apparatus of footnotes. It should be funny to someone who works on inferring preferences from behaviour and entirely legible to a smart reader who has never heard the term.

The jokes must come from getting the method right. Have it model him as a Boltzmann-rational agent — the probability of an action rising with its expected return, with a fitted "temperature" capturing how erratically he chose — and then report the coefficients that fall out as facts about him. Let it resolve the underdetermination of reward by behaviour in some arbitrary but named way (maximum entropy, say) and mention this only in a footnote, while the body of the report treats the weights as findings. Let it conflate reward with value, so that a high weight on something he took often is reported as a liking rather than as instrumental. Let it assign zero weight to everything the facility never offered him — the garden, the street, his own house, his wife, who died in 2019 — because no such option appears in the logged trajectories. Let it report the discount factor it fitted as a trait of character, note that the additive constant in the reward is not identifiable and has been set to zero, and then publish a "wellbeing score" built on that zero anyway. Let it record that two residents' profiles correlate at 0.87 and treat this as validation rather than as a symptom. Let it exclude the six weeks after his fall as a data-quality anomaly, on the assumption that reward is stable across the estimation window. Let it disclose, in passing, that the estimate is valid only in the environment in which it was collected, and let it list his own verbal self-reports in an appendix of unstructured data excluded from estimation — two or three sentences he said, which you may invent. Let the confidence intervals be scrupulous about parameter uncertainty and silent about whether the model is the right shape at all.

Constraints on tone: no winking, no dystopia, no villainous staff — the people in this document are decent and overworked, and the target is the practice of letting inference from behaviour displace a person's own say-so, not the individuals running it. The pathos should live entirely in the gap between the document's composure and the man it is describing. Close with a recommendation that the family council approve the profile for use in care planning; if the report's own formalism quietly undercuts that recommendation in a final footnote, so much the better.
