I'm a staff-level software engineer and I host a fortnightly ideas slot for my part of the organisation: about a dozen backend and ML engineers, nobody with more philosophy than perhaps one survey course taken years ago, forty-five minutes, a whiteboard and a projector, and no assigned reading because nobody will do it. My turn is coming up and I want to spend it on the underdetermination of theory by evidence. I have read enough to know that the thesis comes in grades that I don't have stable names for: a weak grade, where the evidence available at a given time simply does not yet single out one theory and further data might; a strong grade, where two rivals agree on every observation anyone could ever make, so that no possible evidence could separate them; and a more global grade, where what the evidence bears on is not one hypothesis at a time but a package whose auxiliary assumptions can be given up to shield the hypothesis — the version I have seen called the Duhem–Quine point. Please do not explain the thesis to me. I want a plan for explaining it to them, and I want the plan to be built around our own material rather than around physics or the history of science.

Concretely, I would like the following worked out.

First, the dependency structure: which grade has to be on the board before the next one can mean anything, and which order prevents the strong grade from being heard as a restatement of the weak one. My instinct is that if I open with the weak grade they will all nod and the rest of the session will be lost, because they already believe it in the form "passing tests doesn't prove the absence of bugs."

Second, the one example that carries the whole session. My candidate is a case from our own work: two candidate implementations of the same interface that provably agree on every input anyone could construct and run before the heat death of the universe, and disagree only on inputs of, say, 2^80 bytes or on inputs requiring more memory than the observable universe has particles. Call it the unreachable-input case if you like. Tell me what such an example must have and must lack to carry the strong grade rather than quietly collapsing into the weak one, whether it should be the hook at minute zero or the stress test at minute forty, and how I should handle the fact that we ourselves treat unreachable code as nonexistent — compilers delete it, we don't test it, we don't review it — which is either a decisive practical answer to the strong grade or a sign that our practice begs the question, and I can't tell which.

Third, the misconception map, with the point in the sequence where each one should be headed off rather than merely answered at the end. The ones I expect: the slide from "the evidence doesn't force the theory" to "so any theory is as good as any other, it's all social"; the move of dissolving the problem by appealing to simplicity or other tie-breakers, which I suspect is too quick but can't say why; the colleague who will say that if two theories agree on all possible observations they are the same theory and the disagreement is merely verbal; and the colleague, more common in the ML half of the room, who holds that a theory just is a compressed representation of the data, so that there is nothing over and above the evidence for a rival to disagree about. If there are two or three further misconceptions that predictably form at exactly this level and not in a philosophy seminar, I want those more than the canonical ones.

Fourth, where in the arc the session should stop being setup and say something contestable, and what that claim is; what I should openly refuse to explain at this level and announce as a refusal rather than fudging; and one question I can put to the room at the end such that the answer tells me whether the distinction between the grades has landed, together with what a confident, fluent, and wrong answer to that question would sound like.
