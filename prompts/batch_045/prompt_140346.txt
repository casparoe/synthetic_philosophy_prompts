Hi — I'm a science journalist trying to figure out whether "memes" were ever anything more than a cute metaphor for stuff that spreads, and I keep going in circles. A few weeks ago I was at a dinner after a cultural evolution workshop in Ljubljana and got into it with an anthropologist who said the whole framework collapses because most cultural variants have no lineage you could draw: a recipe you half-remember from your grandmother and half from a video you saw last Tuesday has no single parent. A friend who studies sound trends on short-video apps says the same thing harder — there the things visibly graft onto each other, one clip spliced into another, so whatever "reproduction" is doing it isn't cleanly splitting into two copies. I've started calling that the grafting problem so I have something to call it, and I've got a second worry I half-invented a name for: the vacant-host problem, where a variant thrives while the people carrying it most intensely do measurably worse, which makes the whole "what is it for the variant to be fit" question feel like it has no answer. Meanwhile I keep hearing the opposite complaint, that the framework is fine but empty: people gesture at fidelity, fecundity and longevity as the conditions something has to meet to count as a replicator and then never cash any of it out into anything a fieldworker could go and measure.

So I want the spec, not the scorecard. Please take the role of a philosopher of biology who has actually spent years on this, and do some genuine brainstorming before you commit — I can get the four safe bullet points anywhere and I don't want them. Here's the question: what would a genuinely Darwinian theory of culture have to deliver to be worth building a research programme on, where "worth building on" means it tells an empirical team what to measure and can say in some case that culture is *not* evolving in the relevant sense? You should assume I've read the pop versions and skip them, skip any "as so-and-so argued in 19XX" moves entirely (no citations, no bibliography, nobody can check them here and I want the arguments), and don't lean on the famous lost-technology cases — they're contested enough that they'd just let you dodge the hard part. Give me exactly five numbered items, no intro and no sign-off: four desiderata, each stated with the concrete phenomenon or case that makes it a requirement — ideally something I could point at in a news story — plus a tag for whether it's non-negotiable or just a preference of one camp; and a fifth item naming the pair of desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied and why, since that tension is the bit my editor will actually like. Two or three sentences per item, and be blunt if you think the honest verdict is that the list as a whole can't be met.
