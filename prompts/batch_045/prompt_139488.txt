I teach an undergraduate seminar on scientific method, and I would like to close the unit on theories that appear to outrun their evidence with a screening of Everything Everywhere All at Once (2022). The unit itself is about the methodological dispute over the string landscape and eternal inflation: the objection that a framework rich enough to entail that every possibility is realized somewhere thereby predicts nothing, the reply that such frameworks can still accrue credibility from considerations other than prediction — the persistent failure to construct viable rivals, a record of unanticipated successes, the coherence the whole scheme achieves after the fact — and the counter-objection that these are facts about our community's imagination rather than about the world. I want the film to do genuine argumentative work in that unit rather than to decorate it, so I need a reading that is faithful in both directions and that tells me where the analogy breaks.

Here is where I think the obvious reading goes wrong, and I would like you to correct me if I am wrong. The multiverse of the film is not epistemically remote: verse-jumping is something the characters can perform, witness, and be trained in, and Jobu Tupaki's conviction rests on something close to total empirical access to every branch at once. So the film cannot be dramatizing the difficulty of confirming a theory without evidence; if anything it depicts the far side of confirmation, the situation of someone for whom the plenitude of worlds is not a hypothesis but a lived perceptual fact. What it dramatizes instead, I would argue, is the further thought that predictive collapse and normative collapse are the same collapse: that a theory which accommodates everything also underwrites nothing, and that the bagel is what that looks like from the inside.

Assume that much. I then want three things worked out.

First, the film's resolution. Evelyn ends by affirming kindness in a setting where, ex hypothesi, every cruelty also occurs, somewhere, infinitely often. I can see three ways to construe that: she is tacitly appealing to a measure over branches, weighting the ones reached by kindness as the ones that somehow matter; she is not making an outcome-directed choice at all but a choice of which branch to locate herself in, so that the problem is self-location rather than action; or she is simply denying the inference from plenitude to indifference, refusing to grant typicality any normative force. Which of these does the film's closing movement actually support, and are they even distinct once the metaphysics is granted? If the answer is the third, does the film thereby concede the philosopher's point while changing the subject, and is that a failure or an insight?

Second, the rock world — the branch with two stones, no language, and almost no stakes, which is also where the turn towards kindness is consolidated. I read it as a control case: a world stripped of contingency, in which the plenitude argument has the least to bite on. Say what it shows, and whether it is doing philosophical work or only tonal work.

Third, where the reading fails. The multiverse is also, and perhaps primarily, a vehicle for a family drama about an immigration audit, generational trauma, and the pressure of unlived lives; a philosopher who treats it as a parable about non-empirical confirmation may be reducing it, and the ending may be sentiment rather than argument. I would rather know that than be told the fit is clean. Which scenes would you actually put in front of students, what would you tell them the film gets right that the methodological literature does not, and what — if anything — would it be misleading to claim the film shows?
