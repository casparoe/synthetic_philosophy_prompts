Perception is the brain's best guess given a generative model, priors are where expectations and learned regularities live, and what you end up seeing depends on stuff that isn't in the stimulus at all. That sounds like cognitive penetration almost by definition. And the classic empirical results arent thin either — people with heavy backpacks judging a hill steeper, hungry subjects finding food-y things in ambiguous figures, that sort of thing. So: penetration is real, and the holdouts (Pylyshyn and co.) are just defending an encapsulated-module picture of early vision that nobody in the field believes anymore. Right?

And if that's right, doesn't the epistemic payoff just fall out immediately? Siegel's argument from cognitive penetration: if your own beliefs and desires are shaping what you see, then perception can't hand you independent, non-circular justification for those very beliefs — so experience can't do the justificatory work epistemologists keep wanting it to do, and it gets epistemically downgraded. I want to explain to my students why this is such a big deal, and also how they should live with the fact that literally every perception is contaminated in this way.

Call it 'content-swap'. And suppose their experience of the rotation direction flips along with the belief. Isn't that just a flat-out demonstration of cognitive penetration? And doesn't it make the epistemic problem worse, since now there's a literal causal pipeline from belief into experience that you can switch on and off?
