You are both an accomplished poet and someone who knows the philosophical literature on testimony cold — what credibility is, how it gets distributed, and what happens to a claim as it passes from one speaker to the next. Assume I have read none of that literature. Please take your time with this one and think it through carefully before you write: start with a short brainstorm in which you map the chain of hands the account passes through, write down the small official vocabulary a school actually has available for this sort of thing, and mark the exact moment in each retelling where something goes missing. Then write the poem, then reread it against every constraint below and revise before you send it back.

My situation: I'm a junior at a large public high school in Ohio, and I'm the kid on student council who gets asked to write things. Our principal has asked student council to help run a required assembly this spring on how incidents get reported at our school and what happens to a report after somebody makes one. Another student and I have ninety seconds at the end to read something together. I don't want a speech and I don't want a public-service announcement. I want a poem, and I want the poem's structure to be the argument.

Form: a pantoum — six quatrains, twenty-four lines, no more. Lines 2 and 4 of each stanza become lines 1 and 3 of the next, and the closing stanza brings the poem's opening line back as its final line. Light rhyme in an abab pattern is welcome if it doesn't pad, but what matters most is that the repeated lines still sound like something a person would actually say the second and third time they show up. Two of us are reading, so the voice should change hands as the poem moves: the girl who made the report, the friend who passed it along, the teacher she told, the dean, and finally the written report itself.

What the repetition has to do: the lines that keep coming back are her words, and each time they return they should be doing different work, because someone else is now carrying them — first said, then quoted, then loosely attributed, then summarized, then filed under a category. By the last time her line appears it should be nearly unrecognizable and still, in some sense, hers. In my notes I've been calling this laundering: the account gains authority in exactly the proportion that it stops being hers. That's the effect I want on the room — the record is accurate and wrong at the same time.

Three things I want handled precisely, because I keep watching adults get them wrong:

1. Nobody in the chain is a villain. The teacher can't act on hearsay, the dean has to fit things into a code of conduct that only has a few boxes, and each of them is being reasonable one step at a time. The damage is in how the steps add up, so don't hand me a villain or a speech about bad people.

2. There are two different losses and I want both in the poem. The first is that the same words count for less coming from her than they would coming from somebody else. The second is worse, and it's the one I actually care about: she doesn't yet have the word for what happened, so she reaches for the closest word she has, and every retelling swaps in a tidier official word that the school's paperwork recognizes. The thing she was reaching for never enters the record at all. Someone who works on these questions should be able to point to which lines do the first and which do the second, and shouldn't wince at either.

3. The moral is not believe people more. At least one link in the chain should show credibility running the other direction — somebody believed too quickly, on too little — so the poem can't be picked up as a slogan by either side.

The ending: no resolution, no lesson, no call to action. Leave the room with the tension. I do want one late line, near the end, where the word she needed finally shows up — arriving after the hearing is over and doing her no good. That's the line I'll still be thinking about on the bus home.

Constraints: American English; no profanity or slurs, because an assistant principal has to clear this; no theoretical vocabulary anywhere in the poem itself — no testimonial, no epistemic, no credibility, no names of philosophers. The concepts should live in the form and in the drift, not in the wording. Give it a short title that an administrator would approve and a student would still be willing to read. And if somebody reads it who has never heard of any of this, it should just work as a poem about not being believed.
