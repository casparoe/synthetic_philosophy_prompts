I'm in a small reading group on philosophy of science — six of us, mostly physics grad students plus one mathematician, we meet in the back room of a pub, which probably explains something about how the arguments go. Last session we got stuck on a line that gets tossed around constantly: "all we know is structure." It got used to do two different jobs in about ten minutes, and I suspect it cant do both. Job one: one of the physicists used it to kill a question I actually care about, whether there's anything to ask about what mass or charge or spin is *in itself*, over and above the role it plays in the equations. He said the question is empty, there's nothing there, and the slogan was supposed to be the reason. Job two: five minutes later the same person used it as a way of staying a realist in the face of the fact that basically every past theory turned out wrong — the idea being that even though the entities come and go, the structure survives, so we're not as badly wrong as the history suggests.

So: what are the different precise claims that sentence could be making? I'd like them as a menu, from the trivial to the wild, with a verdict on each — platitude, substantive but defensible, or false — and then, the part I actually want, which reading would do job one, which would do job two, and whether any single reading does both. Please say explicitly if the reading that licenses dismissing my question is a much stronger and weirder claim than the one he needs for the history-of-science point. That's my hunch, and I'd like it stated properly enough that I can repeat it at the next meeting without getting laughed at.

Two things to pin the readings down, because I want more than a canned essay on structural realism. First, the swap: imagine a world exactly like ours in every nomic and relational respect, but where the intrinsic character of every property has been systematically permuted — everything that has what we call mass has what we call charge, and so on — in such a way that nothing observable from inside could ever tell the difference. Is there a fact of the matter about which world we're in, and how does each reading answer? Second, the graft: two theories that agree on every actual and possible relational fact about *our* universe, but that come apart on what would happen if a brand-new relation were added to the world and allowed to interact with the old ones. They agree on all structure here and disagree about structure there, which makes me think every reading owes an answer to "structure of what, and how much of the modal surroundings gets counted." Finally, I've been playing with a precisification of my own — structure is just whatever survives relabeling — and I can't tell whether that's the real content of the slogan, a different claim, or something that collapses the whole thing into a definition and therefore into a platitude. Where does it sit on the menu?
