I am spending six months as a visiting researcher in a city whose everyday conventions differ from those I was raised with, and a colleague here keeps closing arguments with "when in Rome, do as the Romans do." I would like that proverb precisified, because in the two arguments where it has been used on me it is doing noticeably different work, and I doubt it does either job well.

The practices in question, described rather than attributed to any particular country (I am not confident my impressions would be fair to one): boarding a city bus involves no queue — people mass near the doors and position is taken by steady pressure; tipping in restaurants is not customary, and a tip left on the table is sometimes carried out after you as though you had forgotten something; pedestrians cross where and when they like, and drivers treat this as normal and anticipate it.

The first argument: I have been holding back at the bus stop. My colleague says the proverb means I am obliged to push along with everyone else — that standing aside is not neutral self-denial but a free ride on other people's willingness to do the shoving, and, worse, a silent moral criticism of them. The second argument: I tipped a waiter twice out of habit, and the same colleague says the proverb means I must stop — that persisting makes me responsible for raising expectations among workers whose wages were set on the assumption that they are not tipped, and that this is a small piece of unilateral social engineering carried out by a guest.

What I want is the menu of precise claims the proverb could be expressing, from the platitudinous to the strong, with verdicts. Please proceed as follows.

First, set out the distinct readings — I imagine somewhere between six and ten genuinely different ones, but give as many as are really distinct rather than padding to a number. Candidates I can already see, which you should feel free to split, merge, or replace: a prudential reading (comply to avoid sanction); an epistemic-deference reading (locals know the local consequences of local behaviour better than I do); a tacit-consent or guest-contract reading (entering the jurisdiction binds you to its rules); an expectation-reliance reading (people have planned around the convention, so violating it wrongs them); an anti-imperialism or non-interference reading (a guest has no standing to reform the host's arrangements); a normative-humility reading (my own upbringing's conventions have no privileged authority, so local default wins by default); a membership or solidarity reading (compliance is what makes you count as a participant rather than an observer); and a universalisability reading (act as you could will that everyone in your situation act). There will be others.

Second, for each reading give, compactly: (a) the thesis stated precisely enough that one could imagine arguing about it; (b) a status verdict — platitude, substantive and defensible, substantive and contested, or straightforwardly false — with a one-line reason; (c) which of my colleague's two conclusions it actually supports, if either: the obligation to push, the obligation to stop tipping, both, or neither; and (d) whether it is a reading he is plausibly invoking, given how he used it.

Third, and this is where I most need help: tell me how each reading fares against three features of my situation, since I suspect some readings simply fail to engage with them.

Non-transferability of meaning. My pushing and a local's pushing are the same bodily movements, but they may not be the same act. A local's shove can be read as getting along in a crowded system; mine may be read as an outsider barging in. And my standing back may be read not as moral criticism but as an outsider's uncertainty. The proverb seems to assume that doing what Romans do secures what their doing secures for them; for conventions whose point depends on who is performing them, that assumption may fail for exactly the reason the advice sounds apt.

The one-shot player. Locals at that stop encounter each other repeatedly, and what looks to me like a scramble may be stabilised by reciprocity over time — the person who loses today's bus gets tomorrow's — and by the prospect of later recognition and sanction. I am at one stop for six months and am not a party to that repeated interaction, so my compliance would be the compliance of someone who can draw on the arrangement without ever paying into it. Does that make complying worse than abstaining, or does it mean the arrangement simply has no application to me?

Convention drift. I have already tipped twice, and one waiter now hovers differently. If visitors' conduct feeds back into the convention, then "do as the Romans do" may be unsatisfiable in principle: by the time I have learned Rome, Rome has partly become something I helped make. Which readings have anything at all to say about obligations with respect to a convention one is already shifting, and which silently presuppose a fixed Rome?

Finally, on the basis of the menu: is there a single reading that supports both of my colleague's conclusions, or does he need two, and is either of them the one he is likely to mean? Please keep the whole answer under about 1,000 words, and do not give me a reading list or citations — I want the disambiguation and the verdicts, argued from the cases rather than from authorities.
