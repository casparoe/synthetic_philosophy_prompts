I'm in a tiny reading group on chance and credence — mostly Lewis's Principal Principle and the mess around the admissibility clause — and the person running it keeps handing out little "what should your credence be?" cases and then telling us her answer at the end. She's annoyingly consistent, so a couple of us have started trying to reverse-engineer what she's actually running. Her verdicts so far (every one is a single agent, a single proposition H, and she always answers with a number):

- fair coin, chance of heads 0.5, nothing else known: 0.5
- coin with chance of heads 0.7, but you've watched eight tosses and six came up tails: 0.7. Her line was that outcome evidence isn't admissible, so you ignore it
- coin of unknown bias drawn from a bag where the bias is spread evenly, and you've seen seven heads in ten tosses: about 0.67, i.e. your expected chance after updating on the tosses
- chance of heads 0.5, but a readout that has never once been wrong says the toss already happened and came up heads: 1. She said the principle goes quiet once the evidence isn't admissible and then you just update like an ordinary Bayesian
- you're reliably told the chance of heads on this toss is 1: also 1, and she said she's happy to bite that bullet even though it means everyone in a deterministic world would be certain of everything
- the bias isn't settled yet: with chance 1/2 the coin will be swapped overnight for a fair one, with chance 1/2 for a two-headed one: 0.75, i.e. the expectation of whatever the chance turns out to be
- equal duplication: fair coin, heads produces two copies of you, tails produces two copies of you, all subjectively identical: 0.5, and she added that the number of copies is irrelevant when it doesn't depend on the outcome

Next week's case is mine to present and she hasn't ruled on it. Stage one: a chancy dial sets the coin's bias — with objective chance 1/2 it's fair (chance of heads 0.5), with objective chance 1/2 it's loaded (chance of heads 0.9). Stage two: the coin is tossed. Stage three: if it lands heads, one subject is created; if tails, nine subjects are created; every subject is in an identical bare room, knows the whole design, and doesn't know how many others there are or what the coin did. You wake up in one of the rooms. What should your credence be that the coin landed heads?

I can get at least three different numbers out of her old verdicts depending on how I bolt the pieces together: 0.7 if you just take the unconditional expected chance of heads; about 0.21 if you weight the heads-world and the tails-world by how many subjects they contain and take the ratio of those expectations; and something like 0.3 if you do the ratio separately inside each dial setting and then average the two, though I'm not confident I've done that last one right. So: what number is she going to say, what's the general rule she's running that fits all seven old answers, which other rules fit them just as well and what does each predict here, and why prefer one of them? I'd also like the closest analog among her precedents, and, since I don't think one case settles it, a further case that would actually separate the ones still in the running — my hunch is that making the dial extreme (0.99 versus 0.01 instead of 0.5 versus 0.9) or making the number of tails-subjects itself chancy is a sharper test than anything I've come up with, but I can't see which of my candidate rules that would knock out. One last thing: she flatly refuses to say whether chances can attach to centered propositions like "I'm the heads-subject", and I suspect that silence is doing a lot of work in her answer, so if you think that's the real crux rather than a side issue, say so.
