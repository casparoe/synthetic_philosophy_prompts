I work as the staff researcher for a technology review committee at a regional parcel logistics company. The committee has seven members — four of them warehouse workers, one a part-time industrial economist, none of them readers of political economy — and it meets roughly eight times a year to review automation proposals that the company is contractually obliged to disclose to us before it signs anything. We do not decide. We write a recommendation of at most two pages, the local's membership votes on it, and the vote is binding, which in practice means a recommendation has to survive being read aloud in five minutes and argued about by people who are tired.

Over the last three years we have opposed some proposals outright, negotiated over the terms of others, and let others pass in silence, and we have no way of saying which of those was right. What I want is a decision procedure we can actually run on a proposal, grounded in Marx's account of machinery and the labor process — the chapters on machinery and modern industry in the first volume of *Capital*, and the notebooks published as the *Grundrisse*, if the material there is genuinely load-bearing rather than decorative.

The procedure needs several things I have not been able to supply myself.

First, it has to separate three questions we constantly conflate: what the technology does to the labor process (deskilling, intensification, the fragmentation of what the collective worker knows how to do, who sets the pace); what it does to our leverage, including the effects that run through workers who are not in our bargaining unit or who may be hired in this labor market later; and whether opposing the technology, as opposed to bargaining over its terms, is the right use of strike capacity we can spend only once or twice a year.

Second, it has to classify the thing being proposed, and the classification has to do real work in the procedure rather than being a label. I suggest three kinds, but correct me if the taxonomy is wrong: a tool that replaces human effort; fixed capital that absorbs a skill and then sets the tempo to which the living worker must move; and software that does not replace anyone but measures, routes, and disciplines the work that remains. Our hardest recent cases are all of the third kind, and I am not confident that Marx's machinery analysis reaches them in the same way — if it does not, the procedure should say so at that step rather than forcing the fit.

Third, it must be runnable on the documents we can actually obtain: vendor throughput claims, staffing projections by job classification, OSHA injury logs, the current collective agreement, and the wage schedule. We cannot get the company's internal rate-of-return model, so for every step that wants a number, the procedure has to say what to do when the number is unavailable and what our confidence should be when we are guessing.

Fourth, it must mark where it stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to the membership because the question is one of values rather than facts. My members will not accept a flowchart that pretends to settle whether solidarity with workers at a non-union supplier is worth our own dues money.

Please also state plainly, early, whether Marx's own argument commits us to not treating machinery as such as the object of our opposition. I suspect it does — that fighting the instrument while leaving untouched the relation that sets it to work is, on his account, a strategic error and not merely a tactical one — and if that is right, the procedure should say what replaces machine-breaking for us, concretely, in a bargaining round.

Then run it on three proposals. One: autonomous case-picking robots that would remove roughly forty percent of picker hours, offered with two years of no-layoff guarantee and retraining into maintenance roles. Two: a routing and pacing software upgrade with no job losses, a twenty percent increase in the required rate, and continuous performance monitoring with algorithmic discipline. Three: automating heavy parcel sorting — the single most injurious job in the building, and also the entry point into the unit and the job around which much of this local's identity was formed, which a majority of my committee already wants to fight. I want the third one run honestly. If the procedure clears a proposal that my members have already decided to oppose, I would rather have that on paper now than discover it in the meeting.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do. I am particularly interested in whether it has anything to say about whether productivity gains at a firm facing competitive pressure can ever be converted into shorter hours rather than fewer jobs, whether any of this survives a change in ownership form — a customer-cooperative or an employee-owned firm in the same market — and what happens to the analysis when every firm in the sector adopts the same technology within two years and the question stops being local.
