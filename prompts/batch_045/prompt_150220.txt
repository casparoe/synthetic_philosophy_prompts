I work as a software engineer and have been reading about the regress problem with a small reading group — four engineers and a biologist, none of us with any formal philosophy training. The presentations I've found are either too brisk to argue with or too bound to terminology I can't anchor to anything. My own instinct is to reach for the things I know: dependency graphs, build systems, type checkers, recursion. So the problem as I keep hearing it is: a belief counts as justified only if something else can be offered in its support; that support itself needs support; and then you either reach something that needs no support, go in a circle, or never stop. I don't want another survey of the three views. I want an instrument — something that sorts a person by what they say about concrete cases and only then puts a name on the result.

Please write me a decision tree (equivalently, a short sorting quiz with a scoring key) for the question of where justificatory support can legitimately stop. My requirements, in rough order of importance:

1. Start from a commitment my whole group would accept before diverging — something in the vicinity of "a belief is justified only if there is something to be said for it that is not simply the belief restated". If you think that framing begs a question that matters, propose a better entry node and say what the original one smuggled in.

2. Eight to twelve branch nodes. Each node is a single question, phrased so that someone who has never encountered the words "foundationalism", "coherentism" or "infinitism" can answer it, and so that it does not obviously telegraph which leaf it leads to. Where you can, draw the cases from build and debugging practice rather than from philosophy: a pair of internal documents each of which certifies the other; a CI pipeline in which every check's trustworthiness appears to depend on another check; a compiler that compiles itself; a test that passes locally and fails in the pipeline; a recursive procedure with no base case that nonetheless runs because it is evaluated lazily; a cached or memoised result whose derivation no one now remembers; a dashboard and a log that disagree. Use at least those, and add cases of your own where a genuine distinction needs one.

3. The leaves must include: strong foundationalism (the stopping points are indefeasible or incorrigible); modest foundationalism (they are defeasible but not themselves inferentially supported); coherentism in two flavours — one requiring only mutual support within the system of beliefs, one adding the constraint that some beliefs be appropriately responsive to what arrives from outside that system; infinitism (an unending chain is fine); the suspension leaf (the requirement cannot be satisfied at all, so nothing is justified); and the local-practice leaf (justification-talk is internal to a practice, and the regress stops where that practice's stopping points are, without those points having the special status the foundationalist wants for them). Label a leaf "unnamed" where you think nobody actually defends it.

4. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from the list of names. Give me the questions, the combinations of answers they permit, and only then locate the named positions within that space. Where a permitted combination is unoccupied, mark it and diagnose why: incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Where your tree cannot separate two leaves, say so explicitly rather than contriving a distinction, and tell me what further question would separate them.

5. Three distinctions I care about, each of which should get at least one node: (a) whether what must terminate is the structure of support itself, or only the subject's access to it — that is, whether an endless chain could justify a finite mind if the next link is always available on demand without ever being held all at once; (b) whether the support relation must be acyclic, or whether a cycle can justify provided the system as a whole is in some global good standing; (c) whether "stopping" means "no reason is needed here" or "a reason, but of a different kind".

6. My own half-formed way of seeing it, which I would like you to use where it helps and correct where it misleads: I keep wanting to model foundationalism as an inductively defined structure (everything built up from base cases), infinitism as a coinductive one (a stream with no base case, well-behaved as far as you ever go), and coherentism as dropping well-foundedness in favour of a global fixed-point condition. Make room for this framing where it genuinely locates a view, but tell me plainly which of the positions it distorts — I would rather have the distortion flagged than have my analogy flattered.

7. The scoring key should map answer patterns onto leaves, and should also handle patterns that land on no clean leaf. Please flag any place where a respondent's answer would likely be driven by an ambiguity in your own question rather than by a real difference of view; I would rather know that a node is unreliable than score it confidently.

8. Constraints: no node may use the name of a position, and no node may turn on a term of art a non-philosopher could not answer — define any such term inside the question if it is unavoidable. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations, and no attribution of views to named authors; I want the positions, not the literature. Please write the whole thing in British English.

What I want out of this is twofold: to find out which of these views I have actually been holding without knowing its name, and to hand the quiz to my group and have it produce a spread of positions rather than another inconclusive argument.
