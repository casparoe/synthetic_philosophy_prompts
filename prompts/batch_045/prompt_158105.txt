I team-teach a seminar on induction and simplicity — half philosophy grad students, half CS students who've had information theory but no philosophy — and I keep running into the same move from the CS side. My friend in the machine learning group (hes giving a guest lecture in two weeks, which is why I'm prepping this) puts it roughly like this: Kolmogorov complexity is just Occam's razor made mathematically precise. Simplicity is shortness of the shortest program that outputs your data, the prior that weights a hypothesis by two to the minus its description length is the razor with a number bolted onto it, and Solomonoff's predictor is what you get when you actually obey that norm all the way down. He then uses it to carry three things I'm not sure it can carry. One: the old vagueness about what simplicity even means — is it number of entities, or of laws, or of free parameters, or of ad hoc adjustments — is dissolved, because simplicity is compressibility and there's a number. Two: the razor gets upgraded from a heuristic or a pragmatic virtue to something provably correct, since the universal prior converges on the truth and dominates every computable predictor in the sense that its expected total error over any computable data source is bounded by a constant, so philosophers who fuss about projectibility and grue-style gerrymandered predicates are worrying about a pseudo-problem the formalism has already routed around. Three, the practical one: therefore simplicity can be measured with an actual compressor — description-length model selection, or just gzipping the rival theories' prediction files — and a big language model trained to predict tokens is a crude approximation of Solomonoff induction, so scaling up compression is a genuine route to inductive knowledge.

Two wrinkles I want you to deal with, because I think that's where the thing breaks and I havent seen them handled. First: the invariance theorem only pins down Kolmogorov complexity up to an additive constant that depends on the choice of universal machine, and that constant is unbounded across machines. The razor does its work precisely in the regime where we have short-ish data — the first few thousand emeralds, the first ten thousand data points — which is before the constant washes out. So in the regime that actually matters, whether hypothesis A or hypothesis B is the simpler one can flip with the reference machine you happened to fix in advance. Doesn't that mean the analogy quietly imports an absolute ordering of simplicity that the formalism itself doesn't deliver? Second, a student of mine proposed a zip-file test: encode two rival theories together with their predictions, run them through gzip, and read off which is simpler. But the verdict seems to depend on conventions — what primitives your description language has, whether you're allowed a lookup table, whether the rival gets to introduce a gerrymandered predicate — and the thing scientists argue about when they argue about simplicity includes unification, independent testability, and not being ad hoc, which don't look like they map onto bit counts at all.

So give me the audit. What corresponds to what — I want the table: on the razor side, the hypothesis, the entities, fit to the data, the ad hoc adjustment, the judge, the future instances; on the formalism side, whatever plays those roles, including the ones that have no counterpart. Then seperate the disanalogies that are load-bearing for the three conclusions above from the ones that are merely academic — I don't think the fact that Kolmogorov complexity is uncomputable is decisive on its own, since a normative ideal can be uncomputable and still be the right ideal, but tell me if I'm wrong about that. For each of the three conclusions give me a verdict — survives, survives in an emaciated form, or collapses — with the reason, and don't sit on the fence; I need to know where to press in the Q&A. Is there a better analogy available, one that maybe captures the fact that the razor picks a winner while Solomonoff's prior spreads credence across an enormous hypothesis space and lets the data reallocate it? And here's my own suspicion, which you should feel free to demolish: that the third conclusion is leaning on more than it admits, because the dominance result is an expected-error bound weighted by the prior itself rather than a guarantee about the environment you happen to be in. Last thing: a short note on which part of your answer you'd retract first if someone who actually works on this walked into the room.
