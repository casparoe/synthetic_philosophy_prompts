So I've somehow ended up as one of two editors-in-chief at a mid-sized philosophy journal, and I need something usable out of a pair of puzzles I normally only teach.

The flow: we get maybe forty errata and matters-arising notes a year — someone writes in saying a published paper got something wrong, or that its central argument rests on a claim that's false. For each one I have to decide, inside about ten days, between doing nothing, publishing the note, asking the original author for a reply first, issuing an expression of concern, or (rarely) retracting. I read maybe half of them myself. The rest get triaged by a rotating associate editor and by our managing editor, who is excellent and has no philosophy training whatsoever. Every decision eventually needs both me and the co-editor to sign off, and it has to be explainable to the original author in a single paragraph. Our publisher has boilerplate wording for retractions and expressions of concern that I basically can't edit, and there's a small amount of legal exposure, so I'd rather not say anything I can't stand behind.

What I want is a decision procedure my team can actually run on one of these, built out of the epistemology of the lottery case and the preface case. The thing I keep running into is this. For each individual claim in a note, I'm comfortably above whatever bar we use for we accept this — call it the Lockean idea that you count as believing p once your credence clears some fixed threshold short of 1 — and yet I know perfectly well that across the few hundred claims we act on in a year, several are wrong. And then the flipside: a note that only has force if three of its claims are all true is in much worse shape than any one of the three taken alone, and I don't think my team is tracking that at all. And then there's the signature problem: the boilerplate wants me to affirm that the editors have verified the correction, and I can't affirm the conjunction even when I'd happily affirm every conjunct separately.

Please don't give me a literature review or names and dates — I've read the stuff, and what I need is something I can hand to a managing editor on a Monday morning. Concretely:

- The questions in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and the points where the procedure just stops with a no.
- It has to work in coarse confidence language, because my managing editor will not give me a number, and it has to say what to do when the information simply isn't there: the author doesn't reply inside the window, or the disputed claim is interpretive and can't be checked against anything.
- It has to distinguish three risk shapes, because I think they want different responses and I don't trust myself to keep them straight: risk spread flat and roughly independent across a set, so there's no way to point at where the error is; risk clustered in a part I can name, like a formal appendix or a historical section, so the second-order belief becomes actionable; and risk where several suspect claims all hang off one shared step, so their failures aren't independent at all and testing the claims one by one is the wrong move. My guess is that two of those are the shapes the two paradoxes are really about and the third is the one that bites in practice, but tell me if I've got it backwards.
- Asymmetric stakes, built in rather than bolted on: publishing a correction that turns out to be wrong does more damage than sitting on one that's right, and a retraction is close to irreversible in a way a published note isn't.
- Explicit markers for where the question is one of values and has to come to me or the co-editor instead of being settled by the procedure. How much we're willing to cost a first-time author, say.
- And a straight account of what the procedure can't do. I already know the cheap move is to print a blanket caveat on everything; I want to know when that's genuinely the right output and when it's just noise that makes us look careful without being careful.

Then run it on three cases, please.

1. A note arguing that a well-known paper's central argument fails. The argument only fails if all three of the note's sub-claims hold; each one looks individually likely; none is checkable, because it's interpretive rather than factual.
2. A claim that a figure in a paper by a first-time author is mislabeled. In principle checkable, but the author hasn't replied inside the window and probably won't.
3. Twelve separate corrections from the same prolific author against one paper's formal appendix, each individually plausible, in a paper I was the handling editor on and have some mild interest in turning out to be fine. This is the one where I expect the procedure to tell me something I don't want to hear, so don't soften it.

Last thing: if the procedure ends up having to treat the lottery shape and the preface shape the same way, say so and say why. I've always assumed the preface is the soft case, since you know your own error rate, and the lottery is the one with teeth — but if that distinction collapses the moment you try to operationalise it, that's definately worth knowing.
