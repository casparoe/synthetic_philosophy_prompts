Draft the joint statement that a year-long adversarial collaboration could produce between two philosophers of historiography who have spent that year talking past each other about the claim that history is written by the victors.

One of them — the archival objectivist — holds that the surviving documentary and material record constrains admissible narrative tightly enough that a victor's advantage is mostly one of emphasis, diffusion, and prestige rather than of fact; that professional source criticism, foreign and subaltern archives, and physical evidence have repeatedly overthrown official victor narratives; and that the slogan quietly runs together a causal story about which sources survived with a reason for doubting what they say.

The other — the power-and-narrative constructivist — holds that victors shape not only which documents survive but the categories in terms of which any document gets read: the periodization, the boundaries of the event, what counts as a side, who is treated as an agent and who as backdrop. On this view source criticism conducted inside those categories cannot fully correct the bias, and the currency of an account tracks institutional power more than evidential support.

What I want is the document, not a map of the debate. Give me: a numbered section of claims both could actually sign, including about what the thesis is claiming in the first place, about how much of the surviving record is victor-produced, and about whether historiography has in fact corrected official accounts; then a table of the residual disagreements, each row phrased in the wording its own side would accept and with the other side's formulation beside it; then the cruxes, and for each crux the test, case, or body of evidence both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

On the cruxes, here is where I suspect they lie — keep, revise, or replace them as you see fit. One: the losers-litigation asymmetry. Defeated parties often produce more and better-circulated exculpatory memoirs, and stories of defeat travel better than stories of consolidation, so in some corpora the bias may run against the victors; both parties should say what pattern in the citation and translation record would confirm which. Two: the self-critical victor. When an official historiography publishes material damaging to its own side, does that count against the thesis, and if not, has the thesis been qualified into unfalsifiability? Three: how much of the dispute is verbal, turning on a slide from a causal claim about why we hold the beliefs we do to an epistemic claim about what we are entitled to believe.

Close with a short note on how confident you are that a genuine adherent of each camp would sign each section, and single out the one formulation you are least sure is free of slant.
