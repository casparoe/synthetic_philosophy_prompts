The exam question was:

(a) Kant's Formula of Universal Law says roughly: act only on a maxim that you could at the same time will to become a universal law. His Formula of the Law of Nature says the same sort of thing but adds that the maxim is to hold as if, through your will, it were to become a law of nature. What work is that extra bit doing, and is it a second, separate test? Then: distinguish a contradiction in conception from a contradiction in the will, and say which kind of duty each is supposed to ground. (8 points)

(b) Here's a test that isn't in the literature, so you have to think it through yourself. Call it the Double-Negation Test (DNT). For any action you're considering in circumstances C, let M be your maxim for doing it in C, and let M⁻ be the maxim of never doing it when you're in C. DNT says: the action is permissible iff you can will M as a universal law AND you can will M⁻ as a universal law. Is DNT equivalent to the Formula of Universal Law, is it stricter, or is it weaker? Argue for your answer and use at least one of Kant's four examples (suicide, the false promise, neglected talents, refusing to help) to show how the difference plays out. And is DNT a better test than Kant's? (12 points)

The student's answer:

(a) A maxim for Kant isn't just the bare action, it's the agent's principle of volition — circumstance, end and action taken together. That matters, because the universal law formula isn't asking "what if everybody did that thing"; it's asking whether the agent's own principle can be willed to hold for everyone. Two people can perform the same outward action and their maxims can come out differently, which is also why the test isn't a disguised consequentialist filter: the failure isn't in the aggregate outcome, it's in whether the willing is consistent.

The law of nature version adds the bit about the maxim becoming, by your will, a law of nature. I take it that clause is doing two things. First, universality and necessity: laws of nature hold without exception, so it blocks reading universalization as most people, most of the time. Second, it gives you a world to picture, and you need a world to picture before any contradiction can show up. It's the same test in a metaphor, not a second and stricter test: nothing is added to what's required, only the imagery.

The two ways a maxim can fail are different. Contradiction in conception: you can't even so much as think the universalized version, because the practice the action depends on dissolves and so the maxim cancels itself. The false promise is the case — universal deceit makes promising impossible, so a maxim of promising falsely could not be a universal law. Contradiction in will: the world is conceivable fine, but a rational agent could not will it, because willing it would mean giving up something that reason necessarily wills. Neglected talents and refusing to help are those cases. The first kind grounds perfect, narrow duties, with no latitude; the second grounds imperfect, wide duties, where you get some latitude about how and how much.

Really, though, all of this is the golden rule with the theology stripped out and a consistency constraint bolted on: treat others only as you would be willing to be treated in return.

(b) DNT is equivalent to the Formula of Universal Law, just run twice. FUL takes a maxim and asks whether it can be willed as universal; DNT asks that question of M and then again of M⁻, so nothing new is demanded of M itself. It can't be stricter than FUL, since M⁻ is only another maxim going through the very same test.

Check it on the man who won't help. His maxim — when others are in need and helping would cost me little, I won't — fails by contradiction in will: I can't will a world in which no one ever helps, since I may need help myself. His M⁻ — when others are in need and helping costs little, I will — is willable, and it's what the imperfect duty of beneficence recommends anyway. So DNT says refusing is impermissible and helping is permissible, exactly what FUL says. Same verdicts, no difference in strictness.

If anything DNT is the better test, because FUL can only tell you that the maxim you actually formed fails. DNT also makes you check the option you didn't take up, so it catches people who pass by accident, merely because they never considered acting otherwise.
