A popular-science-ish book on economics I grabbed at an airport summarizes Friedman's 1950s essay on positive economics like this: 'Friedman's claim is that a theory's assumptions do not matter. The only thing that matters is whether its predictions come out right. He argued this with his example of the expert billiards player, who takes his shots as if he had solved Newton's equations of motion, and with firms that price as if they were maximizing profits even though no manager has ever worked out a marginal cost curve. He then went further and said unrealistic assumptions are actually a good sign: the more significant a theory is, the more wildly unrealistic its assumptions will be, because a truly general theory has to abstract away from all the messy detail. Critics who complain that a model's assumptions are false have simply made a logical blunder — false premises can perfectly well entail true conclusions, so a model built on false assumptions can still predict beautifully. So the only real test of a model is out-of-sample predictive success. Asking whether its assumptions are realistic is psychology, not science.'

I mostly buy this, but the 'unrealistic assumptions are a virtue' part sounds stronger than what I remember, and a friend says you have to seperate assumptions that are just simplifying shortcuts from assumptions that have to be roughly true for the theory to apply at all. Can you go through the summary and tell me which parts Friedman actually held, which are overstatements or conflations of two claims he kept apart, and what both a defender and a careful critic would object to? Also, does the final 'only test is prediction' claim actually follow from anything true in the earlier sentences — and what would it commit me to if I applied it to a model that assumes voters are perfect Bayesians, or to an epidemic model that assumes everyone mixes uniformly with everyone else?
