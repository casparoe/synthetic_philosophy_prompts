ok so I'm trying to write up my position on the norms of assertion for a seminar paper and I'd like someone to check whether the package actually hangs together before I commit to it in print. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) I defend a knowledge norm of assertion: you're permitted to assert p only if you know p, and violating that isn't just impoliteness or unhelpfulness, it's the characteristic defect of the speech act itself. Part of why I like it is that it explains why we criticise careless assertions in completely ordinary low-stakes conversation, where nothing much hangs on anything.

(2) I want this to fall out of something more general, namely that knowledge is the norm of practical reasoning: you may treat p as a reason for acting only if you know p. I like the unification — asserting is something you do, so the norm of assertion shouldn't be a special rule that only applies to talking — and I also like that it explains the bank cases: the person who acts on Friday-afternoon evidence is criticisable because they didn't know, not merely because acting was imprudent.

(3) I'm also drawn to pragmatic encroachment: whether you count as knowing p partly depends on what's at stake for the person concerned. The bank cases feel to me like they show something about knowledge itself, not merely about the rationality of acting.

(4) I'm a fallibilist: knowledge doesnt require certainty or evidence that entails p, so flatly asserting that the train leaves at nine on the basis of a memory or a timetable is fine even though I might be wrong. I don't think flat assertion owes anyone a hedge, and I don't think this causes trouble for (1).

(5) I want to keep the norm of assertion narrow. When someone asserts something true and known but deliberately useless or misleading — 'is he a good candidate?' 'he owns a tuxedo' — I think there's genuinely something wrong with what they did, but I want to say it's a violation of a separate norm of cooperation, not of the norm of assertion. I lean on this move a lot.

(6) I'm attached to one case verdict: I don't think whether I'm allowed to assert something should depend on who I'm talking to. If I know the bank is open I can say so to the person with a trivial errand and to the person whose mortgage payment depends on it; if I don't know, I can't say it to either. I really don't want assertion to be audience-relative. The version of this that bugs me most is one utterance to a room of two hundred people whose stakes are all over the place — it seems crazy that a single sentence is permitted relative to half the room and not permitted relative to the other half.

(7) I also don't think assertion needs to be useful: telling a stranger the time when they had no need of it isn't defective in any way I care about, even though nothing came of it.

Is this a consistent package? I'd like an audit rather than a lecture: go through the pairs and tell me which are actually in tension and which only look like it, ranked by how bad the tension is, and for each real one what the cheapest fix is and what it costs me. I don't know where the problem is — if there is one, I'd rather find out now then in the seminar. I'm happy to give up any of these if I have to, I just want to know which. And tell me if there's a single revision that would clean up more than one of them at once, and what I'd have to swallow for it.
