I want to skip the case everyone reaches for (the planet whose orbit was already sitting in the tables before the new theory showed up) because I think there's a harder version. Here's mine.

A research station has logged nightly bird-counts at a dozen sites since the 1970s: tens of thousands of numbers, sitting in a public database, plenty of people have looked at them. In 2019 a biologist proposes T: the birds navigate by a magnetic cue that tracks the solar cycle, and T says the counts should show an eleven-year sawtooth. Someone goes to the database, runs it, and there it is. Nobody had ever thought to look for an eleven-year pattern.

My opening: the numbers were known, so P(E)=1, so on your account T gets zero confirmatiom from the one thing everyone agrees is the reason to take T seriously. And I think it gets worse, because there are only three ways out and I don't like any of them for you. (1) Let the evidence proposition be phrased in the theory's own new vocabulary — there's an eleven-year sawtooth in these counts — a claim nobody had any credence in whatsoever before T came along. But then a theory can manufacture its own confirmation: hand me any old dataset and I'll define a new quantity, the squiggle-index, rigged so the data come out at 7.3, then point at the data and say T predicted 7.3. If P wasn't 1 purely because the phrase didn't exist yet, I just got confirmed for free, by stipulation. (2) Insist the evidence has to be the old proposition in the old vocabulary, i.e. the raw numbers. Then T gets no credit for the sawtooth, and the only difference between the sawtooth and the squiggle-index is how impressed we are — which is psychology, not probability. (3) Say it's about what the actual agents happened to have noticed, so P wasn't 1 for them. Then the same numbers confirm T for the grad student who heard about it yesterday and don't confirm it for the guy who typed them in back in 1978, and confirmation has turned into a fact about who happens to be in the room. Pick one and defend it.
