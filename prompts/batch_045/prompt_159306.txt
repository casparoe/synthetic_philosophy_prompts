I'm putting together a one-day workshop session for my grad seminar on philosophy of mind this spring, and I've got two collegues coming who have been sniping at each other about whether the brain literally computes for something like a decade. One of them is a realist about neural computation: she thinks there is a fact of the matter about which computation a given neural circuit performs, that this fact is part of what makes cognitive-scientific explanation work, and that the brain is, in the relevant sense, a computer. The other is an ascriptivist: he thinks calling a physical system a computer is always relative to a mapping we choose from physical states onto mathematical ones, that indefinitely many incompatible computations can be truly ascribed to the same bit of tissue, and that when a computational model earns its keep in neuroscience it earns it by letting us predict and intervene, not by being what the tissue is running. I don't want another ninety minutes of them talking past each other, so I want the document they should have written together.

Write the joint statement of their adversarial collaboration. Structure it as a numbered section of agreed claims — things both can sign, phrased so they aren't vacuous — followed by a disagreements table, then a short closing section on which parts of their dispute you take to be merely verbal and why.

What I want in it, plus a few guesses of mine that I'd rather you argued with than just accepted:

Agreed claims, numbered. Both should be able to sign things like: the same physical system's behaviour can be captured by many different formal models at once, only some of which are useful; the credentials of a computational model in neuroscience come from prediction and intervention, not from intuition about what the tissue is really doing; and whatever the right story turns out to be, it has to be consistent with the brain being a messy, wet, partly analogue, noise-ridden physical object rather than a tidy symbol manipulator.

The disagreements table: one row per crux, with a column for the computationalist's own formulation of her view on that crux (wording she would accept), a column for the ascriptivist's (wording he would), a column for the case or argument both would agree bears on the crux, and a column with each side's prediction about that case, writen down in advance.

Cruxes I suspect are in there — re-cut them, drop them, or add better ones:

1. Whether computation is something a system does intrinsically or something we ascribe. My test case: a model fits a circuit's spiking behaviour under every intervention we can currently run, and then it turns out the model's internal variables correspond to nothing in the tissue. Does the fit alone settle that the circuit performs that computation?

2. Grain and the analogue worry: whether there is always some level of description at which a continuous, timing-sensitive neural process counts as computing something, or whether some real neural work depends on fine-grained physical detail that no implementation-independent description preserves. Test case: a circuit whose behaviour collapses if you discretize time any more coarsely than a fraction of a millisecond.

3. Explanatory surplus: whether saying that a circuit computes such-and-such explains anything that saying its dynamics are thus-and-so plus here is the mechanism does not. Test case: does the computational description buy you successful transfer of an intervention to a different individual, or to a different species?

4. The verbal bit: how much of the fight is just about which thing gets to be called computation, and what is left over once that is fixed.

Keep it under a couple of thousand words and skip footnotes and citations entirely — I need something I can hand out, not a literature review. Assume a first-year grad student who has never read any of this has to follow it.
