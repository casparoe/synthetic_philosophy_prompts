I'm a senior in high school, and I've been asked to give three or four minutes of remarks at the naturalization ceremony my town holds in the old courthouse next month, where about thirty people will take the oath and become citizens. I'm speaking on behalf of the students at my school, who were invited because our civics class does a unit with the court. The room will be: the thirty people taking the oath and their families, some of whom have been waiting years for this day; the judge; the mayor; two or three men and women from the local veterans' post; some of my classmates and their parents; and my AP United States History teacher, who will be listening to every word and who will absolutely talk to me about it on Monday.

Here is why I'm stuck. We spent a week on American exceptionalism, and my teacher sorted it into three claims that he says everyone slides between without noticing. The first is the creedal one: that the country is constituted not by a people or a place but by assent to a set of propositions, so that anyone can in principle join it by subscribing to them, and that this makes it different in kind from most nations. The second is the comparative one: that the country is freer, or more mobile, or more prosperous than others, and that this is an empirical claim you could actually check and could actually lose. The third is that the rules which bind other countries don't bind this one, so it gets to lead or act alone — the version where being special means being exempt, and where the word exceptionalism turns into something like exemptionalism.

The first of those is the one I think is true, and it's the one the ceremony is literally doing: thirty people are joining a political community by saying they hold certain things to be true. That's why I want to give the speech at all. But my teacher's objection bites in a way I can't shake. If membership really is assent to a proposition, then the long stretch of our history in which people like my mother's family — they came from Korea in the 1970s — could not have naturalized at all isn't a sad footnote; it's a contradiction of the very thing the ceremony celebrates. And he also says the creedal story is partly a myth we told afterward. I think he overstates that. I also think I can't just ignore him, and I can't turn a happy civic ritual into a lecture about it.

So please write the remarks. Three to four minutes when read aloud, in a voice that sounds like a seventeen-year-old who means it and not like a graduation card. They need to say something true about why this particular ceremony matters, and to be genuinely glad, without sliding into the comparative version or the exempt version, without scolding the room, without using the people taking the oath as props for my own discomfort, without implying the door was always open, and without explaining America to people who just passed the civics test and know more of it than I do.

After the speech, add a short note addressed to me, not to the room: tell me where the argument I've given you is weakest, whether my three-way split smuggles in something it shouldn't — whether the creedal version quietly depends on the empirical one being true — and whether the creedal account really is as clean a way of making the ceremony meaningful as I think it is. And if you think what I've asked for can't be done well — if a four-minute celebration genuinely cannot carry that much honesty at once, or if my distinction collapses the moment it's used in front of people — say so plainly and tell me what I should say instead, rather than handing me something smooth.
