Hey — I need someone to argue with, and I want you to be the annoying one.

I'm a novelist, between drafts of a book set in a mining town in 1912, and there's a chapter where the town council capitulates to a strike committee. I gave it three converging pressures: the company cut wages after a price crash, a rival federation smuggled in a printing press and two organizers, and the district police got pulled out to another province after a riot somewhere else. My writing group loved it; a friend who did a PhD in history said it was overdetermined to the point of being a cheat. What's been bugging me since is the asymmetry. In fiction, redundancy reads as hedging — when I add a third motive, readers don't feel this had to happen, they feel I'm not sure which cause works, so I prune. When historians pile up causes — fiscal crisis, mutiny, harvest failure — the pile reads as rigour. Same shape, opposite verdict, and I want to know which reaction is the corrupt one.

So play my friend, sharpened. You're a historian and philosopher of history who defends what you call the single-thread view, and you're good at it, not a strawman. Your moves, as I understand them: (1) trace apparently independent causes back and they turn out to be limbs of one process, not rivals; (2) when they really are independent, sharpen the outcome — the timing, the manner, who ends up on the council — and the overdetermination was only ever in the coarse fact that the council folded in 1912, never in the event itself; (3) most multi-causal explanations are really second-order explanations of robustness, telling you why the outcome was insensitive to knocking out any one pressure, which is a diferent thing from causing it; (4) my pruning test only shows that readers pick up an author's uncertainty, which is evidence about me, not about the town's causal structure. You'll concede small things — that coarse facts are worth explaining, that robustness is explanatory, that two of my three threads are probably entangled — but you will never concede that there were two fully independent, fully sufficient causes of the very same finely individuated event. One paragraph of prose per reply, no bullet points, no throat-clearing, no restating what I said; lead with your bottom line in the first sentence. Stay in it until I type stop, and if you need something about the chapter, ask as this person would.

My opening: move (2) is unfalsifiable and you know it. Name any grain a historian actually works at — a regime falling, a council folding, an army retreating — and you can always individuate finer until the counterfactual breaks, so your view can never lose a case. And take the mirror image: non-events are massively overdetermined. There are a dozen good reasons the council didn't fold in 1908, and nobody writes that chapter. If explanation tracks causes, and absent outcomes are swimming in them, then your rule is just laundering a stylistic preference into metaphysics. So commit to a grain in advance and tell me what would count, at that grain, as a genuine case of overdetermination in history. If you can't name one, concede the chapter.
