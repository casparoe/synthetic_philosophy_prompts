Hey — I need a speech and I've been staring at a blank page for four days.

I'm the librarian in Harrow's Bend (pop. about 1,100, upstate) and I've been asked to give the opening remarks at Founder's Day a week from Saturday. It's outdoors, folding chairs on the common, right after the bean supper, seven minutes tops, no real microphone. The fourth graders are released from the pageant the second I stop talking and there's pie, so nothing long or abstract will survive.

The problem. Our founding story is that in the spring of 1801 Mercy Dane, the schoolmistress, walked eleven children through the flooded millrace to the stone meeting house and kept them there three days on what was in the communion pantry until the water went down. Every year the fourth grade performs it as a pageant. Ruth Vance, who's 84 and wrote it in 1974, still casts it, and being chosen to play Mercy is basically the whole point of fourth grade here.

Last month a grad student named Marisol Reyes was up here going through the town clerk's books and turned up three things. One: the 1801 tax list really does show a Mercy Dane, unmarried, schoolmistress, in this township — the person is not invented. Two: the earliest written version of the story anyone can find is in an 1888 county history, more than sixty years after, and the stone meeting house the children are supposed to be walked to was rebuilt on the other side of the common in 1846, so the building in the story wasn't standing where the story puts it in 1801. Three: the 1803 selectmen's records, which are meticulous about everything else, never mention a flood at all.

So one detail confirmed, one detail impossible, and the part the story is actually about — the walk through the water — with nothing on either side. Half the town now thinks the whole thing is bunk, the Dane Family Association has put out a statement, and Pastor Kiley said from that exact platform last year that it was Providence. He's a genuinely good man who runs the food pantry and I'm not going to say Providence myself or quietly make fun of him for saying it.

I also can't do the moves I'd normally reach for. I can't say that the truth of the story isn't literal truth — the people whose great-great-great-aunt this is will hear that as being patted on the head, and they'd be right to. I can't say that all history is storytelling anyway, because Ruth will hear me calling her a liar and because it isn't true, whatever people mean by it. I can't do the thing where I nod at Troy or King Arthur for a paragraph, because this room will hear that as me saying our story is a children's tale. And I can't end on I guess we'll never know, because the selectmen vote next Tuesday on whether the pageant stays exactly as it is, gets a short spoken introduction before it, or gets retired, and anything I say will be heard as a recommendation.

The one thing I keep coming back to is what Eunice Pell, the librarian before me, used to say about local stories: they're shadows, not photographs — an effect of something real without being a report of it, and a shadow tells you the rough shape of a thing and nothing whatsoever about what it's made of, and it gets longer and blurrier the farther it travels. I want the speech to actually use that, not just drop it in as a pretty line. But Marisol will be in the second row, and she'll notice that shadows need something casting them, so if the metaphor ends up smuggling in something like this must have happened, then I've just asserted the disputed part in a nicer voice. Whatever I say should also still hold up if Eunice was simply wrong about this kind of story.

So write me the seven minutes. Around 800 words, plain spoken, one small joke if you can find one that doesn't land on Ruth or the minister. Use the shadow idea so its carrying weight instead of decorating. And end with one specific proposal about the pageant that Ruth can sit through without hearing that her fifty years are being corrected. If there's a line you think I'd be lying to say, leave it in but tell me under the speech why.
