I run a small reading group — six of us, no specialists — and last month we spent two evenings on the "Grand Inquisitor" chapter of The Brothers Karamazov, the passage where Ivan tells Alyosha his poem about Christ coming back to Seville in the middle of the Inquisition and being arrested by the old cardinal, who argues that Christ was wrong to refuse the three temptations in the wilderness, because almost nobody can bear the gift of freedom, and that the church has spent centuries quietly correcting his work by handing people bread, mystery, and authority instead. We got stuck in the same place both nights: my brother thinks the Inquisitor is simply a villain and that settles it, whereas I think the uncomfortable possibility is that his empirical claim is true — that most people really would be better off under his arrangement — and I can't say what is lost that outweighs that. So I gave up on the chapter and instead wrote down my actual gut verdicts on concrete cases where someone's freedom is traded for their happiness, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. A hospital that withholds a terminal prognosis from a patient who, at intake, signed a form saying "only tell me what I ask for": fine — but if that same patient asks directly and is refused, not fine, which makes my verdict hinge on something more specific than consent. A city that puts barriers on a well-known jumping spot and quietly stops publishing its name and location: fine, and I feel no unease at all, although the option really is gone. A parent who filters what a fourteen-year-old sees online: fine; the same filtering for a twenty-two-year-old who is objectively worse off without it: not fine. A government that suppresses a true, actionable method for synthesizing a pathogen: fine; the same government suppressing a true fact that merely embarrasses it: not fine. A partner who never mentions a one-night stand from years ago, genuinely regretted, where disclosure would only cause pain: I say fine, but I go back and forth and feel slightly sick about it. A cult that supplies total belonging and purpose while gradually dismantling its members' capacity to doubt the leader: not fine, even though its members report the deepest happiness of their lives — and this is the pair I can't reconcile with the bridge barriers, since both remove options, both leave people better off, and I judge them oppositely. A feed algorithm that curates what adults see so as to keep them calm and content: not fine, and this is the second pair I can't reconcile, since the users clicked "agree" and the intervention resembles the one I approve of for the fourteen-year-old. And the Inquisitor's own bargain: not fine — moreover I want to say the happiness he buys doesn't even count as happiness, though I can't say why without an account of what it is for a desire to be one's own.

The rule I half-invented on the walk home is this: a trade of freedom for happiness is permissible when the arrangement can be stated to the person without ceasing to work, and when the person keeps, or later regains, the standing to reject it. I already see it break on the pathogen case, where telling is exactly what destroys the arrangement and I still approve — which makes me suspect I need two principles, one for cases where the person constrained is also the beneficiary and another for cases where third parties are at stake, and I want to know where exactly the seam falls. I'd also like you to test that rule against a case not on my list: an assistant that quietly filters options so well that you never learn anything was withheld, versus a lock you can set once on yourself, irreversible after a thirty-day wait, which I find obviously permissible even though it is the most complete removal of future freedom of the lot. And please tell me honestly which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and would have me overturn, and what it predicts for the case I keep returning to — a state that guarantees income, housing, and a shared civic ritual, on condition that no one publicly criticizes the arrangement. Argue it out from the cases rather than surveying what anyone has written on the topic.
