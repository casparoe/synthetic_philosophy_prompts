Hi — I host a tiny linguistics-adjacent podcast (around 300 listeners, mostly my old department plus some friends from grad school), and next week my guest is a psycholinguist who's just published a book defending what she calls "obligatory-category relativism". Rough version, as I take it: the Whorfian effects that actually replicate only show up where the grammar *forces* you to code something on essentially every utterance — evidentials, aspect, grammatical gender, honorifics — and not where a language merely lacks a word (the whole "Eskimo words for snow" genre of example is exactly what she says is irrelevant). And the mechanism isn't that your concepts get reshaped; it's that mandatory coding builds an encoding habit, and the habit survives as a retrieval cue, so effects show up in memory and in tasks where you have to hold something online, not in what you can perceive or reason about in the moment. Her flagship new claim, and the reason I booked her, is about evidentiality: obligatory evidential marking does not give speakers a richer concept of evidence at all, it just attaches a mandatory source-tag to the memory, so speakers of an evidential language do better on delayed source monitoring (did you see this, or were you told it?) but are no better at weighing testimony, discounting hearsay, or resisting bullshit generally.

Here's my problem: every time I've heard her on other shows she gets the same four easy questions — the Hopi-and-time thing, the snow-lexicon thing, the colour studies, the "so you think language determines thought?" thing — and she fields them in her sleep, largely with the move "that's the strong version, nobody defends that anymore".
