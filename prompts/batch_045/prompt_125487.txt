I am revising a dissertation chapter on evolutionary debunking arguments, and I have written the argument out in what I took to be its cleanest form. Written out cleanly, it seems to establish something I do not believe, and I cannot locate the mistake. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

Call a genealogical explanation *bypassing* when it explains why we hold a belief without invoking the fact, if any, that makes that belief true.

(1) Genealogy. For each of our central moral beliefs there is a well-confirmed genealogical story, stated wholly in fitness terms, about why we hold it: partial concern for kin because of inclusive fitness; resentment of non-reciprocators because of reciprocal altruism; horror at certain bodily acts because the disgust system was co-opted from pathogen avoidance; and, more generally, moralized concern for the welfare of group members because coalitional competition favored it. These stories are bypassing. None of them mentions a moral fact.

(2) Tracking. A belief is justified only if we would not have held it, or would have held it with less confidence, had it been false.

(3) Independence. The moral realist is committed to moral facts holding independently of what anyone believes, desires, or would converge on under ideal reflection. If so, the genealogies in (1) are modally robust: for any moral belief B that we hold, had the moral facts been different in a way that made B false, the pressures described in (1) would have been the same, and we would have held B anyway.

(4) So by (2) and (3) our moral beliefs are unjustified. A realist who concedes this retains, at most, a metaphysical thesis with no epistemology attached, which is dialectically inert.

(5) Generality. Nothing in (1)–(4) turns on the specifically moral character of the content. Take the norms constitutive of theoretical reasoning: modus ponens, enumerative induction, abduction, the evidential force of coherence and simplicity. Each has a bypassing genealogical story in fitness terms: organisms that drew fast, cheap inferences from noisy local cues out-reproduced organisms that deliberated expensively, and selection had no interest in whether the cheap inferences were valid. Each is independent in the sense of (3): facts about what there is reason to believe hold independently of what creatures like us take there to be reason to believe. So by exactly the reasoning of (4) these norms are unjustified as well. With small adjustments the same reasoning reaches perceptual belief, since a bypassing story in terms of fitness-relevant mid-level features is available for why we experience a world of colored, solid, middle-sized objects rather than the structure physics describes.

(6) But (5) is not merely skeptical; it is self-undermining in a way that (4) is not. The inference from (1) and (3) to (4) is itself an inference to the best explanation, from the bypassing genealogy to the conclusion that moral facts are explanatorily idle. That inference employs the abductive norms that (5) declares unjustified. So if (5) is true, the debunking argument can be sound only on the assumption that it cannot be rationally accepted.

(7) Therefore at least one of (1), (2), (3), or the step from (4) to (5) is false.

My problem is that I want to keep all of them. I am confident of (1): the genealogies are good science. I am fairly confident of (4) as a conclusion about morality; I think moral realism is in genuine trouble, and I do not want to surrender that because the argument generalizes inconveniently. And I definitely do not want (5): I do not think the argument gives us any reason to distrust abduction, and if it did it would take itself down with it. So the culprit must be (2), (3), or the generalization to (5). But every time I try to say precisely what is wrong with one of them, the fix I reach either leaves the debunking argument too weak to threaten moral realism, or re-imports the same problem one level up, at the level of epistemic norms.

Two further things I have noticed and cannot settle. First, when I try to test (3), it seems to depend on how the belief is individuated. Selection plausibly explains why we have *some* norm of reciprocity, but it is much less clear that it explains why we hold the quite specific judgments we actually hold about, say, which payoff structures generate obligations of fair division, or which distributions count as exploitative. Does the argument need the genealogy to explain the belief at the fine grain, and can it get that? Second, the standard escape — a third-factor or bridging account on which the fitness pressures and the moral facts are correlated because both are explained by something further — seems available only if I may help myself to first-order moral claims in constructing the bridge, since the obvious candidates (welfare, the demands of practical reason, the structure of cooperation) are themselves contested moral claims. But if I am allowed that, the realist appears to get the same permission, and the argument starts to look question-begging rather than neutral.
