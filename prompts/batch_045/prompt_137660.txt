**Question 4 (20 marks).**

(a) The "regress argument" about justification begins from the thought that a belief B is justified for a subject S only if S has a reason for it, that this reason is itself something whose justification is in question, and so on. Assume this generates a chain of reasons: B is supported by R1, R1 by R2, and so forth. Standardly, three positive views are distinguished: foundationalism (the chain terminates in beliefs justified without support from other beliefs), coherentism (the chain has no termini but instead involves mutual support within a body of beliefs), and infinitism (the chain is endless and non-repeating). In your own words, state what each of these three views claims about the structure of the reasons that make a belief justified, and say what each denies about the other two. (8 marks)

(b) A familiar objection to infinitism is the "finite minds" objection: a subject can entertain or complete only finitely many steps of reasoning, so an infinite chain of reasons can never be gone through, and a chain that is never gone through justifies nothing. Infinitists often reply by distinguishing *having* an infinite stock of reasons from *traversing* it. Now consider a variant view — call it generative infinitism — on which a belief B is justified for S if and only if S possesses a rule (an effective procedure) which, given any reason offered for B, yields a further and distinct reason for that reason, and which never yields a reason that already occurs earlier in the chain, so that the chain can be extended without end. Discuss: does generative infinitism escape the finite minds objection in a way that ordinary infinitism does not? And is generative infinitism genuinely a distinct position, or does it collapse into coherentism, or into foundationalism? (12 marks)

**Student's answer.**

(a) The regress argument starts from the assumption that a belief is justified only if the believer has a reason for it, and that a reason is itself a belief which also has to be justified. This generates a chain: B needs R1, R1 needs R2, and so on. There are three ways the regress can be handled, plus scepticism, which says that since none of the three works, no belief is justified at all.

Foundationalism says the chain has to stop somewhere. There is a class of basic beliefs that are justified without being supported by any other beliefs — perceptual beliefs, memory beliefs, perhaps simple a priori truths — and every other belief inherits its justification from them, directly or indirectly. What foundationalism denies is that justification could consist in a chain with no beginning; an infinite chain would never get started, so nothing would ever be justified. Some foundationalists also hold that basic beliefs must be indubitable, though more recent versions require only that they be justified by something other than further belief, such as experience.

Coherentism says that there are no basic beliefs: a belief is justified just in case it is consistent with the rest of the subject's beliefs, and the more of the subject's other beliefs it is consistent with, the better justified it is. Coherentism therefore allows chains that loop back on themselves: B is supported by C, C by D, and D in turn by B. What it denies is that there has to be any starting point; what matters is the fit of the whole set.

Infinitism says that the chain neither stops nor loops: a belief is justified if and only if there is an endless, non-repeating series of reasons available to the subject, each lending support to the one before it. Infinitists insist that the relevant question is whether the subject *has* a further reason, not whether she has actually worked through every step, so the mere fact that we are finite does not settle the matter against them.

(b) The finite minds objection says that since we can only ever complete finitely many steps, an infinite chain of reasons can never be finished, and an unfinished chain justifies nothing. The infinitist reply is that this confuses completing the chain with having the reasons: I can be justified in believing something even if I have not thought of every reason in the series, provided that whenever I offer a reason, a further reason is available to me on demand. This reply seems to me to be correct, and generative infinitism makes it work better, because it says exactly what it is for such reasons to be available: you possess a rule or procedure that produces the next reason from the last.

The advantage of the generative version is that a finite rule can specify an infinite series. That is all that the "..." in a sequence such as a, a+1, a+2, ... really comes to. So if I possess the rule, I possess the entire infinite series of reasons at once, and that means that I complete the chain in a single step. The finite minds objection is really only the complaint that we never finish; but if the whole series is already in hand, there is nothing left to finish, so the objection does not apply.

Is the view distinct from coherentism? I do not think so. Coherentism also holds that no belief gets its justification from a privileged starting point, and that the justification of any one belief depends on the subject's other beliefs. The only difference an infinitist can point to is that her chains are non-circular whereas coherentist ones loop back. But an infinite chain never arrives at a terminus either, so from the point of view of any actual believer the two views demand exactly the same thing: that the subject have more reasons available the more she is challenged. Generative infinitism is therefore coherentism with extra notation. It is not foundationalist, obviously, because there is no basic belief — the rule is not a belief at all.
