I've just come out of a long, genuinely unresolved argument with a friend about lexical threshold negative utilitarianism — the version of suffering-focused ethics on which there is some intensity-and-duration threshold such that any single episode of suffering above it lexically dominates any finite quantity of milder suffering, pleasure, or relief, so that the task of ethics is essentially the prevention of the worst states rather than the maximization of net well-being. I kept pressing a cluster of objections she couldn't dispatch at once. First, the view needs a single hedonic scale in order to locate the threshold at all — you have to say how many mild headaches it takes to matter as much as one torture — yet that is precisely what the worry about the commensurability of pleasure and pain denies, so the view seems to presuppose the comparability whose denial motivated threshold machinery in the first place. Second, everything turns on where the line sits, and the line is a cliff: the difference between 3.99 and 4.01 units flips the ranking of options that differ arbitrarily little, and any non-zero probability of crossing it lexically dominates every certain benefit, which threatens to forbid every action whatsoever. Third, and this is the wrinkle we invented while arguing, the exchange rate may not be constant: she wanted to say that a million mild headaches are not a million times worse than one, a kind of bulk discount on mild pains, and I pointed out that if the rate is scale-dependent then the threshold cannot be a number at all. Fourth, whether an episode crosses the threshold depends on how experiences are carved into individuals — one week of moderate agony versus seven days of moderate agony — and nothing in the view settles the carving.

What I want from you is a parody of an institutional document that makes a reader feel all of this without anyone ever naming it. Seed: the Clinical Ethics Committee of Thackeray General Hospital, which three years ago inherited a bequest conditional on adopting "an ethically rigorous decision procedure" and hired a consultant who installed a lexical threshold version of the view; the committee consists of a hedonic psychologist who supplies the units, a compliance officer, a rotating lay member, and its former chair, Dr. Ada Okonjo, a classical threshold-free negative utilitarian who thinks the whole point was to minimize the total quantity of suffering and who resigned over the threshold. Write the committee's internal memorandum, 750–900 words, in deadpan bureaucratic register, formatted as numbered clauses with two short appendices (Appendix A: the definition of the committee's unit of suffering and the calibration of the threshold; Appendix B: one worked example), announcing its revised protocol on the administration of local anesthetics and elective surgery, and closing with a clause on the distribution of the memorandum itself. The satire has to come from the view's actual structure, not from invented silliness: the clause on experience-carving should be how the committee repermits the surgery its own rule has just banned; the appendix should quietly reveal that the threshold was set at whatever value reproduces the committee's pre-theoretic verdict in the torture-versus-very-many-mild-headaches case; the compliance officer should introduce a second, lower "triviality cut" below which suffering is lexically weightless, and the committee should then authorize substituting indefinite regimens of just-sub-cut discomfort for genuine analgesia; and the probability clause should force a regress the committee resolves by definitional fiat rather than by argument. Include at least one quote from Dr. Okonjo registering her dissent in her own vocabulary — her complaint should be the one a real threshold-free suffering minimizer would make, not a generic "you can't put a number on it." Nobody in the document should ever say the policy is absurd, and there should be no winking at the reader: the irony should be legible entirely from the fact that the clauses follow the view faithfully. A reader who knows the literature should come away having felt the commensurability problem, the arbitrariness and discontinuity at the threshold, the paralysis from tiny probabilities, the scale-dependence of the exchange rate, and the dependence on how experiences are individuated — while a reader who doesn't should simply enjoy a hospital slowly banning its own anesthetic.
