I got into an argument about tort law and now I can't work out how this whole package of doctrines is supposed to hang together. In most common-law jurisdictions (England, most US states, Australia), if a doctor negligently fails to warn parents about a serious genetic risk, the parents can sue for wrongful birth and recover the extra costs of raising a disabled child. The child, the one actually living with the condition, generally can't sue for wrongful life; California, New Jersey, Maine and Washington let the child sue but only for provable economic losses and not for pain and suffering, and Germany's constitutional court struck the action down as incompatible with human dignity while leaving the parents' claim for the child's upkeep intact. Meanwhile the same courts cheerfully award damages in wrongful death for the life the dead person lost.

So what does this presuppose? It smells like the procreation asymmetry — a reason not to bring a suffering person into existence, no matching reason to bring a happy one in — but I can't tell whether the package really needs that, or whether it only needs some weaker zero-line claim: that a life above a certain threshold simply cannot be an injury to the person living it, and that there is nobody there for nonexistence to be compared with anyway. And the wrongful-death half seems to presuppose that life is a good, which sits oddly with refusing to treat a terrible life as a harm.

The bit I want pressed on is a reform a friend proposed: call it wrongful life lite. The child may recover, but only the shortfall between their condition and a minimally decent life, capped at zero — nothing for the gap between their life and a healthy one, and nothing at all for a life that is merely drab. Does that actually encode the asymmetry in a damages rule, or does it smuggle in something the asymmetry doesn't supply, like a determinate worth-living threshold or a non-comparative account of harm? Is the asymmetry even the right thing to reach for, given that it is about reasons to create while tort is about compensating someone who now exists? And could someone who rejects the asymmetry outright — a total utilitarian, say — still defend barring the child's claim on deterrence or administrability grounds, so that the practice is underdetermined by the philosophy? Reconstruct the implicit view, tell me what strictly has to be presupposed versus what merely fits well, and say what a court that took a symmetric view would have to start doing differently.
