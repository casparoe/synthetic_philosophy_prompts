My friend Dani is the most consistant person I know on duties of rescue, and I want to find out whether her view actually holds together or whether it's just a list of intuitions with a story attached. She's the hardline Singer-style sort: shallow pond, muddy clothes, distance doesn't matter, nobody's special. Here's her track record, roughly as she'd put it:

- Kid drowning in a shallow pond, you ruin a $200 suit getting her out: "obviously you get in, there's nothing to talk about."
- 10% of your income to a charity that hands out malaria bednets: "that's not sainthood, that's just what you owe."
- Your daily $6 coffee: she'll say you should give the money instead, but every time I push her on whether buying it is actually wrong she goes quiet and changes the subject.
- Donating a kidney to a stranger: "amazing, not required."
- Everyone in the office is chipping in $20 for a colleague's surgery and everyone does chip in: "yeah, obviously."
- No duty to vote in a national election even when the other side is much worse, because one vote makes no difference.
- Jumping into a fast river for a drowning kid at maybe a 1-in-20 risk to her own life: yes. At 50/50: no.
- Someone else is already wading in and looks like they'll manage: she says you can keep walking.
- She'd skip a friend's birthday dinner to help a stranger in trouble. She would not skip her sister's wedding.
- She gives to the bednet charity and not to a local outfit she thinks is ineffective, "because if it doesn't work, what's the point."

As far as I can tell she has never once been asked about a case where she was the only person who could help and a lot of other people who could have helped were just standing there doing nothing. So:

(1) Same shallow pond. Two hundred people watching, none of them will move. Going in alone costs you about $2,000, something like a fifth of your income for the year, plus a job interview you can't reschedule for the career you actually want. If they'd coordinated it would have cost each of them ten dollars.
(2) The pond is on your walk to work every morning for the next thirty years. A different kid each time. Every single rescue costs you a pair of shoes and ten minutes and nothing else.
(3) You can save the kid: 95% you both get out fine, 5% you drown and your two small kids lose their only parent.

What does she say about each of those, and what's the rule she's running? More usefully: give me the other rules that fit all her old verdicts just as well, show me where exactly they come apart on these three, and then give me one further case I could put to her that would settle which one she means. I care way more about the rule than about her answers.
