Two members of the epistemology reading group I sit in on spent most of last week's session arguing about a case one of them had invented, and at the end they asked me to settle it. I could not, and I still cannot, so I am passing the question on with the argument reported as faithfully as I can manage.

Both of them accept the same starting point. A Gettier case is one in which a belief is true and justified in the ordinary internal sense but intuitively fails to be knowledge, because the truth and the justification come apart. Two of the diagnoses they were fighting over are the requirement that the subject's route to the belief not run through any false lemma, and the modal requirement that the belief be safe: in nearby cases in which the subject forms the belief in the same way, the belief is still true. A stricter modal requirement, which came up later, is sensitivity: if the proposition were false, the subject would not have believed it.

The case. A distribution depot receives pallets in matched pairs. Under a standing contract, every pallet from one supplier is shadowed by an identical pallet from a second, same product code and same unit count, and the inventory system links the two as siblings. The handheld scanners have an indexing fault: on reading a pallet's barcode they return the record of that pallet's sibling rather than its own. Because siblings are identical as a matter of contract, the record returned is always accurate about the contents, and the fault has gone unnoticed for months. Nadia scans a pallet, the terminal shows product code 8821 with a count of 60, and she comes to believe that the pallet in front of her holds 60 units of 8821. It does. Her evidence is exactly what it would have been in an ordinary faultless scan, she has never heard of the pairing arrangement, and she has no reason to suspect anything. Three metres away there is an audit terminal that reads pallet contents directly and has never been wrong; she was trained to use it whenever a scan matters, and she skipped it because she was behind schedule.

The exchange, as close to verbatim as I can get it:

1. Ines: This is a Gettier case, so no knowledge. The route from her evidence to her conclusion goes through a false lemma, namely that the record she is reading is the record of the pallet she scanned. Take that lemma away and her evidence tells her nothing whatever about this pallet. So the no-false-lemma diagnosis gives the right verdict here, and the modal conditions are at best rough proxies for it.

2. Marcus: There is no false lemma. Every proposition she actually relies on is true: the terminal has been reliable, the record it returns is a genuine record and is accurate, and the pallet does contain 60 units of 8821. And notice that even the bridge you want to accuse her of using is not false either, since siblings really are identical by contract. The oddity is in the causal chain, not in the propositions. A condition that has to posit lemmas the subject never entertained is not tracking anything in her reasoning.

3. Ines: A basing requirement does not ask whether the subject explicitly entertains the lemma; it asks whether her evidence supports her conclusion by way of truths. And a bridge she has never heard of cannot be what makes her belief knowledgeable, because that just deletes the subject from the account and leaves bare reliability. Here is the sharper test: had this pallet been mis-picked, the sibling record would still have read 8821 with a count of 60, and she would have believed exactly what she believes now. Her evidence does not discriminate between the pallet's being as it is and its being otherwise.

4. Marcus: That is the sensitivity counterfactual, and sensitivity is the wrong test. It asks what she would believe in a distant case rather than whether her actual procedure is truth-conducive here. Her belief is safe: run the same scan in any nearby case and the contractual guarantee makes the returned record match the pallet in front of her. Safety was introduced precisely to handle environmental-luck cases of the fake-barn kind, and it returns the verdict that she knows.

5. Ines: The safety verdict is bought by the very feature that makes the case Gettiered. The record is accurate for a reason that has nothing to do with the pallet she is looking at. A test that returns knowledge exactly when the truth arrives from somewhere else has stopped doing the work it was introduced to do. And safety holds only because I built the guarantee to be robust: relax the contract slightly and the verdict flips, which shows the condition was never tracking anything epistemic in the first place.

6. Marcus: That is a demand for evidential fit dressed up as an intuition, and it is a substantive theory, not a datum. In any case the luck complaint fails on its own terms. She was not stuck with the defective route. An accurate check was three metres away and she had been trained to use it. Somebody with a guaranteed route to the truth available is not in the position the subjects of the standard cases are in.

7. Ines: Being able to have known is not knowing. Knowledge requires that the belief rest on the sound route, not merely that a sound route was within reach; otherwise anyone with the answer in an unopened envelope knows it. And the skipped terminal is not inert. A competent employee who has a reliable check available and bypasses it for reasons of schedule is arguably not justified at all, which would make the case not a Gettier case but a plain failure of justification.

8. Marcus: Fine. But then look at what we have just done. The no-false-lemma condition, safety, sensitivity and the basing requirement each deliver a different verdict on one case, and each sounds compelling when stated on its own. That is evidence that there is no fourth condition to be found. Knowledge has no informative necessary and sufficient analysis; it names a cluster, and the post-Gettier industry has been hunting a unity that was never there, across what are really several distinct species of epistemic luck.

9. Ines: A case engineered so that the tests disagree is evidence about the tests, not about the thing being analysed. Split verdicts from split cases show only that each proposed condition is too simple; they do not show that the project is bankrupt. Besides, your confidence in the intuition rests on people who already accept a modal theory of knowledge, and you have not checked whether the intuition survives contact with anyone who does not.

What I would like from you is a move-by-move verdict rather than a decision about who won. For each of the nine moves, say whether it is right, wrong, or only partly right, and in virtue of what. Where a move rests on a confusion, name the confusion; where it rests on a substantive theoretical commitment the speaker has not defended, say which one and how much of the argument hangs on it. Then, separately, give your own view of the case: what is the correct verdict about Nadia, if there is one, and what are both of them leaving out. I am particularly unsure whether this is a Gettier case at all rather than a case of knowledge through an oddly structured but locally reliable procedure, and I do not trust my own reaction either way. Please engage the moves as they were actually made rather than restating the standard positions they belong to.
