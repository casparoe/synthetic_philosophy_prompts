I run a short teaching session, twice a year, for doctors in their first years of practice who are attached to a clinic that sees people recovering — or not recovering — from viral illness, and I want to close the session with a poem instead of a slide of bullet-pointed takeaways. Every piece of material I have tried before has failed in one of two opposite ways. Either it makes the clinician a villain, so that everyone in the room can locate the problem in one person's bad manners and go home reassured; or it makes the patient eloquent and triumphant, which suggests that the whole difficulty was that nobody had bothered to listen yet. Neither is the thing I actually want them to see, and I would like you to write against both failures.

The distinction I am trying to teach is between two ways a person can be wronged specifically in her capacity as someone who knows things. In the first, she is not believed: the credibility she is given falls short of what her evidence supports, and it falls short because of who she is rather than because of what she said. In the second, nothing is wrong with the hearing — the clinician may be attentive, kind, and genuinely trying — but the stock of shared concepts the two of them have to work with contains no word for what has happened to her, so her account gets filed under the nearest available heading and comes back to her changed, as something about her mood, her fitness, or her attitude. The two run together in practice, and the second is the harder one to get junior staff to notice, because nothing in it looks like anyone's fault. There is also, I think, a third thing that the versions I have tried keep missing: that after enough of this the speaker begins to distrust her own memory and her own judgment, and that this erosion is part of the injury rather than a side-effect of it.

So: a sestina — six stanzas of six lines, unrhymed, with the standard rotation of end-words (each stanza taking the previous stanza's in the order 6, 1, 5, 2, 4, 3), followed by a three-line envoy carrying all six words — spoken in the voice of a patient at that clinic, in the middle of a consultation or in the letter she writes afterwards. Choose the six end-words from the vocabulary a clinician would actually write in the notes: let at least one name a mental state, at least one a measurable bodily function, and at least one a way of closing a case off. Keep the lines near ten syllables so it speaks aloud in about two minutes. No jargon anywhere in the poem — the vocabulary of the theory must not appear in it — and it must not diagnose a named disease. The rotation should be doing the argument: each return of a word, in a new position and a new context, should show a different way that word distorts what she is trying to say, and by the fifth and sixth stanzas the words should feel worn out rather than clever. Give her at least one small thing she is right about that the reader can check — a number, a date, something that happened in front of someone else — so that the gap between her credibility and her evidence is visible rather than taken on trust. Somewhere in the middle, let the reader feel her beginning to doubt her own account, not because she has been contradicted but because she has been patiently and sympathetically misunderstood. And in the envoy, either produce, inside the three lines and among the six words, a phrase that the six of them could not have produced — a name for what has been happening to her — or deliberately break the rotation and tell me afterwards, in a sentence, why you think handing her that name would be a false comfort. I would rather it not end in vindication: the poem will be read aloud to a room of people who, having heard it, now have a concept they did not have before, and I want the ending to leave them holding it rather than to release them.
