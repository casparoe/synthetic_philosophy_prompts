I got into an argument at a dinner party recently about whether you can ever be worse off for knowing something, and I have not been able to get my own verdicts to line up. One friend, an economist, said the question is settled: for an expected-utility maximizer facing a fixed decision problem, free information is never worth less than nothing, because you can always ignore what you learn and do exactly what you would have done anyway, and by the same style of argument a signal is never worth less than any noisy garbling of it. I think that argument is right as far as it goes, but I keep producing cases where I would pay to avoid learning something, and I want to know whether the principle can be repaired or whether some of my verdicts have to go. My tentative formulation, which I suspect is too crude, is this: learning is good just when it lets my action depend on the world in a way it could not before, and bad just when it forces my action to depend on the world in a way I would rather it did not.

Here are the verdicts I am fairly confident about. I would pay for a market survey before deciding whether to launch a product, and I would pay more the nearer I am to indifference between launching and not; the same survey is worth exactly nothing if it is so noisy that no result could move me, and worth nothing if I already know what it will say. I would pay a little to learn who wrote a certain anonymous seventeenth-century pamphlet, even though I will do nothing differently once I know. I would pay a real sum not to be told which of my students wrote which anonymous exam, even though knowing would let me write them more useful comments, because once I know I cannot grade blind. I would take, for free, a sealed envelope containing that mapping which I alone could open and would probably leave shut, and I think that is clearly better than never being offered the envelope. I would decline a health-risk test whose result is automatically entered into a file my insurer can read, and I would want the very same test if the result sat in a file only I control. In a negotiation, I would pay to read the other side's cost sheet if they never learn I have seen it, and I would pay not to be handed it in front of them. And a friend who runs a two-person clinic says a fifty-variable forecast would make her team worse than a one-page summary, because her team can act on about three things a quarter and reading the rest eats the attention the three would have had; she would take the summary even if the full forecast were free and strictly more informative. Finally, I would pay for a cheap pilot whose only use is telling me whether the expensive study is worth commissioning at all, even though the pilot says nothing directly about the launch decision. The Blackwell-style claim and the clinic case are the pair I cannot reconcile, and the pamphlet case sits badly with the claim that information is worthless when it cannot change your action.

What I want from you is the principle, not a survey of positions and not references. State one or two candidate principles precisely enough that I could apply them myself, say which case motivates each clause, and tell me which of my verdicts each candidate accommodates and which it forces me to overturn, including whether the crude formulation above survives in any form. Then give me its verdicts on three cases I have not listed: a physician deciding whether to tell a patient the exact numerical risk of a side effect when the patient will have the operation either way and has said they would rather not know; a juror who has accidentally seen material the judge will later instruct the jury to disregard; and me, halfway through a novel, deciding whether to read a detailed plot summary before finishing it. If the honest answer is that no single principle fits and that the disagreement between my verdicts is real rather than apparent, say so and tell me which verdict you think is the mistake.
