I'm in this undergraduate reading group on animal minds and moral status, and my roommate Dev — a CS major who mostly argues from first principles because he hasn't actually read anything — has built an argument he's extremely pleased with, and I definately can't find the exit. Here it is in his steps:

(1) Sentience, understood as the capacity for valenced experience (the sort of thing that makes a pain bad *for* the subject it happens to), is not an on/off property. It comes in degrees, and the underlying functional/neural stuff it tracks — feedback sensitivity, integration of information across the system, behavioral flexibility, call the whole bundle an "integration score" — forms a continuum with a thermostat at one end (a thermostat has negative feedback but nothing it's like to be one) and an adult human at the other.

(2) Now consider what he calls the dimmer case: an engineered organism whose integration score can be turned up and down continuously with a dial, which you watch as it's dialed from human-level toward zero. There's no point on the dial where anything qualitative happens, no moment at which you'd want to say "ok, now it stops mattering." So there is no non-arbitrary threshold at which moral status switches on: pick any cutoff you like and there's a creature sitting an arbitrarily small distance below it that you'd have to write off as mattering not at all, which is unacceptable.

(3) So moral status has no thresholds at all — it's a scalar that varies continuously with degree of sentience, and every creature gets some number, call it its unit-status, somewhere between 0 and 1.

(4) If moral status is a scalar quantity of that kind, then it's interpersonally comparable and additive across individuals: the moral status of a population is just the sum of its members' unit-statuses, the way the disvalue of a bunch of separate pains is the sum of their disvalues.

(5) Insects are somewhere above zero: they have nociception, they learn to avoid shocks, in some setups they'll work for analgesic-ish stuff — so each insect gets some small positive ε.

(6) There are absurdly many insects. Total insect status therefore dwarfs total human status.

(7) So we should be redirecting basically the whole research and charity budget into insect welfare, and if it's a straight choice between one human child and a hectare of marsh full of mosquito-ish things at ε apiece, the marsh wins.

He also has a second version he springs when I push back on (2): (8) if there really is no principled cutoff, then there's no principled difference between a creature at 0.0001 and a thermostat at 0, so either unplugging a thermostat carries a nonzero moral cost too, or thresholds are back and (2) is false — the "no sharp line" thought supposedly eats itself either way.

I'd like to keep (1) and (5) if at all possible; I'd much rather give up something in the middle. When I described this to the TA she muttered something about "scalar welfare status versus threshold-gated protections" and said that was the whole answer, but it felt like a dodge and I didn't know how to press her. So: which step is actually doing the damage, why does it look fine when you read it fast, and what do I end up paying for rejecting it — and is (8) a separate problem or just (2) wearing a hat?
