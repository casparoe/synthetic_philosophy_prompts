I'm 71, retired a few years back from 35 years in experimental condensed matter (mostly magnetism, nothing philosophical about it), and since then I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the state university and doing volunteer work, which unfortunately includes being on the bylaws committee of our county orchid society — I grow orchids badly, and apparently that qualifies me to rewrite the rulebook. Through the reading group I got interested in rules that govern their own amendment (someone described a game to me, Nomic I think it's called, where changing the rules is itself a legal move), and our little club rulebook turns out to be a miniature version of the same mess. Our meta-rule M says: any rule in this document, including M itself, may be amended at a regular meeting by a two-thirds vote of the members present, provided at least half the membership is present. At the last meeting two things happened. First, someone moved to amend M so that amendments to M itself need three-fourths; it passed with 68%. Second, someone moved to count emailed proxies as "present," which changes the denominator in every two-thirds calculation; that passed with 71%. Now people want to undo the tightening, and the cry goes up: "you can't use a rule to change that rule," and someone else chimed in with "a rule can't be the judge of its own amendment." Both were said as if that settled it, but as far as I can tell neither one settles anything, and its been bugging me for a week.

What I'd like: take that slogan ("you can't use a rule to change that rule") and lay out the distinct precise things it could be claiming, from the trivial to the radical, and for each say whether it's trivially true, substantive and defensible, or just plain false, and whether it would actually support blocking either of the two amendments — the tightening of M or the redefintion of "present." I have my own rough taxonomy I've been playing with and you're welcome to use it or throw it out: (1) first-order amendments, which change what members may do; (2) self-amendments, which change the amendment rule itself, like the tightening; and (3) what I call frame amendments, which change who counts as a voter or what counts as a vote, like the proxy thing, leaving M's text alone but moving its numbers around underneath it. I also suspect there's an asymmetry — tightening a threshold under the old laxer threshold seems fine, while loosening a threshold under a stricter one seems to fail — and I can't tell whether that's a real principle or an artifact of how I set the case up.

And there's a case I made up that nobody in the reading group will take seriously: an amendment with a self-validating clause, saying it takes effect if adopted by a simple majority and that its own adoption is deemed to have satisfied whatever higher threshold was previously in force. Does the slogan say anything clear about that one? Please answer as exactly seven numbered items, each one or two sentences, no introduction and no closing paragraph: one reading of the slogan per item, each with a verdict (trivial / substantive but contested / false) and a note on which of my club's amendments, if any, that reading would actually block. Don't cite anything or point me at articles — I don't have journal access and I'm not writing this up for anybody, I just want the menu of claims so I can bring it to the next meeting and watch people squirm.
