Here is my opening move. Suppose you and I are epistemic twins: we have the same evidence, the same starting assumptions and the same reasoning habits; we each know this about the pair of us; we each know that we each know it; and so on for as many levels as you want. Then whatever conclusion I reach about this dilemma, you will reach too, and we both know that. So my cooperating would be strong evidence that you cooperate, and my defecting strong evidence that you defect — and since I would much rather we both cooperated than that we both defected, that symmetry seems to hand me a reason to cooperate which has nothing to do with influencing your choice, or with any causal link between our acts, or with any failure of common knowledge. On top of that: as far as I can make out, anonymous strangers in actual one-shot dilemmas do cooperate a substantial share of the time, which suggests the mutual-defection argument is leaving out something about real people rather than telling us what they ought to do. Tell me where that goes wrong.
