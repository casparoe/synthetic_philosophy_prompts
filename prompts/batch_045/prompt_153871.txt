I teach an advanced seminar on the epistemology of forecasting transformative technologies, and I am assembling a problem set on takeoff speeds. What I need is a constructed scenario rather than a survey of the literature. The dialectic my students arrive already knowing runs in one direction: the dramatic forecast comes from the inside view, where one looks at the mechanism — compounding returns, systems helping to build better systems, a curve that goes nearly vertical — while the deflationary forecast comes from the outside view, where one looks at how long earlier technologies actually took to diffuse, at regulation, at capital-stock turnover, at the long gap between a demonstration in a lab and a technology fielded at scale, and concludes that even steep underlying growth gets smeared into a gentle ramp. I want a case engineered to run the other way, and I want it to be usable with students who have read nothing on this beyond a couple of survey articles.

Specifications. Build a scenario in which a forecaster reasoning from the inside — from the actual mechanism by which the relevant systems improve, their bottlenecks, their feedback loops, the thing that has to go right for improvement to compound — arrives at a slow, continuous takeoff, while a forecaster reasoning from the outside — from a reference class of earlier technologies and their base rates — arrives at a fast, near-discontinuous one. Constraints: keep it realistic and non-exotic, with no appeal to anything beyond current engineering, nothing stipulated to be superintelligent, a starting system no more capable than a competent human team, and extrapolation only modestly beyond present trends in compute and data; make the two forecasters share every empirical belief, including the same underlying capability growth curve and the same facts about institutions and the economy, so that the divergence is generated by the two methods rather than smuggled in as a disagreement about the facts; make the reference class one that both forecasters would antecedently have chosen rather than a gerrymandered one, since a colleague in economics insists that any case in which the outside view supplies the alarming forecast is ipso facto rigged, and I want a case that survives him; and give the case the property that adding further inside-view detail monotonically deflates the forecast, so that the more carefully one models the mechanism the slower the takeoff looks, rather than the reverse.

One further wrinkle, which I would like built into the scenario rather than bolted on afterwards. I have been using a three-way distinction in the seminar: a capability takeoff, which is how fast the systems' raw abilities rise; a deployment takeoff, which is how fast systems with those abilities are actually fielded at scale; and what I have been calling the decision-relevant takeoff, which is the speed at which the set of options open to any human agent who might want to steer the outcome shrinks. I want the case to pull these apart, but I want the divergence between the two forecasts not to be an artifact of the two forecasters quietly tracking different ones of the three. What I need back is: the scenario in a paragraph or two; then, for each forecaster, an explicit run-through of how the forecast is generated and what shape it outputs; then an honest diagnosis of which single structural feature of the case is doing the work, together with a demonstration that deleting that feature flips the case back into the familiar direction in which the inside view is the dramatic one and the outside view the deflationary one. Since the point of the exercise is that students identify the load-bearing feature themselves, please also say what would count as cheating here — my own view is that stipulating the verdicts, or concealing an empirical disagreement between the forecasters inside the choice of reference class, would both be cheating, but tell me if there is a subtler way to cheat at this particular specification. Finally, give me the best reply available to an outside-viewer who wants to resist the case, and your assessment of whether that reply is any good.
