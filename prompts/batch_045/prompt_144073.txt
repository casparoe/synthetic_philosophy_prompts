im in a 300-level seminar on corruption, globalization, and ethics right now and we spent last class on this contrast between US and UK anti-bribery rules that i cannot stop thinking about. Under the US Foreign Corrupt Practices Act, there is an exception for small grease or facilitation payments to foreign officials to speed up routine governmental action—like getting paperwork processed, permits issued, or shipments cleared—where the official is already required to do the action and the payer is not trying to change the outcome, only the timing. The idea is that these are different from bribes to obtain or retain business or to get an official to exercise discretion in your favor. By contrast, the UK Bribery Act 2010 has no facilitation payments exception. Its core offense covers giving or offering a financial or other advantage in exchange for the improper performance of a relevant function or activity, and the usual compliance reading is that even a small payment to a clerk to jump the queue or to get them to do a job they are supposed to do anyway is technically bribery, even if prosecutors might not bother with tiny cases. On top of that, many US multinationals have internal compliance policies that ban all grease payments anyway, even though US law would allow them, and their UK subsidiaries are covered by the UK regime. So the same small payment to a low-level official to stamp a form can be permitted under one legal regime, criminal under another, and against internal policy under both.

What philosophical commitments about the concept of corruption does this whole setup presuppose? i dont need a bibliography or reading list, and please dont just say it depends on which theory of corruption you like—i want the implicit theory reconstructed and its weak points marked.

First, reconstruct the implicit theory behind the US grease-payments exception. Does it strictly presuppose that corruption is about distorting an official's discretion or judgment, so that only outcomes and decisions can be corrupted, while timing and queueing are not real goods that an official distributes? Or could a rival view—say, one that thinks the problem is unfairness in who gets served first, or the appearance of partiality—also explain why we allow small timing payments but ban big outcome-changing payments? Tell me what the exception strictly needs to assume versus what it merely fits well with.

Second, the US rule leans on a distinction between discretionary acts and ministerial or non-discretionary acts, meaning acts where the official has a choice versus acts they are just mechanically supposed to do. Is that distinction stable here? If an official can decide whose file goes first, then the payment changes something they do have control over—your place in the queue. Does the practice undermine its own rationale, or can it bite the bullet and say only substantive outcomes matter?

Third, what view of the official's role and of improper performance does the UK approach presuppose? Does it presuppose that any payment to turn a public duty into a paid priority service is improper because it corrupts the official-citizen relationship or the ideal of impartial administration, regardless of the substantive outcome? Is it strictly committed to that, or is it also compatible with a more pragmatic slippery-slope or enforceability rationale? Where is the UK practice philosophically underdetermined, so that two or three different theories would all say ban the grease payment?

Fourth, both regimes seem to work with a pretty transactional model: a payment is either a bribe that buys a discrete official act, or maybe a permitted speed payment. But the domain of our course is clientelism and gift-giving, and in lots of contexts a small gift or payment to an official is not a one-off purchase at all—it is part of an ongoing relationship of mutual obligation, a way of building a bond and expecting future help or loyalty. Does the Western legal distinction implicitly presuppose an arm's-length, impersonal, transactional model of exchange and of bureaucracy, and quietly rule out clientelist or relational conceptions of the gift? If we took a relational or clientelist view seriously—where the point of the gift is the relationship rather than the immediate stamped form—what would a corruption rule that respected that have to look like? Would it have to permit some ongoing gift relations and forbid others, and what exactly would change in the compliance policy?

Fifth, the corporate practice of banning grease payments even where US law allows them: what does that presuppose? Does the ban strictly presuppose a slippery-slope or expressive theory, where small payments normalize corruption or express that the company sees officials as purchasable? Or could several different theories—say, protecting institutional culture, avoiding unfair advantage for big firms, or plain risk management—all license the same ban? Which parts of the actual practice are overdetermined, so that multiple theories say the same thing, and which would distinguish between them? And if we replaced the presupposed view with, say, a pure impartial-queuing view, or a welfare-consequentialist view where small payments to underpaid clerks might actually unclog bureaucracies and help people, what would the rule have to become—would some grease payments be required rather than banned?
