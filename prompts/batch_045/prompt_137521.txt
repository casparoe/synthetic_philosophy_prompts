I hold a view about Bongard problems that I would like stress-tested. Recall the format: a dozen small panels of abstract shapes, six on the left and six on the right, where every left-hand figure satisfies some rule and every right-hand figure violates it, and the solver's job is to say what the rule is. My view is that success at such a problem is evidence that a solver has induced a concept rather than merely matched a pattern, and better evidence than ordinary few-shot image classification provides, for two reasons: the sample is too small for shallow image statistics to separate the two classes by accident, and the right-hand box fixes a contrast class, so what has to be recovered is not just any separator but the one under which the negative figures are the natural complement of the positive ones.

Tell me what would count against this, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and distinguish the defeaters a defender of the view should concede as genuine from those she may reasonably dismiss as resting on a rival assumption.

Three worries of my own, which I would like you to evaluate rather than merely concede. First, underdetermination: for any twelve figures there are infinitely many rules that separate them, so having the concept cannot mean having found the unique separator; if it instead means having found the rule a human finds, the test may be measuring a shared perceptual and cultural vocabulary rather than concept induction. Second, my probe test: take a problem the solver gets right, add a thirteenth figure on which the human rule and a shallow rival rule disagree, and see how the solver sorts it — is a wrong answer there decisive, or does it only show that the solver had a different but equally admissible rule? Third, my generation test: give the solver a rule in words and ask it to produce the twelve figures, or ask it to build a minimal pair — two sets of figures as similar as possible while instantiating different rules. Does an asymmetry between classifying and generating tell us anything about concepts, or only about the gap between recognition and production? Of the three, I would be most moved by the underdetermination point if it can be stated without collapsing into general skepticism about rule-following.

Last, assess honestly whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability: whenever a solver succeeds one can always say it was pattern matching all along. If it has, say whether that is a defect here or an unavoidable feature of claims about what a system really represents. Say which of your defeaters you take to be merely possible and which you suspect are already actual, but do not pause over citations.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning. No preamble and no restating of my question.
