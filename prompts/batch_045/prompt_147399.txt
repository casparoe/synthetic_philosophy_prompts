I'm giving a short talk to our uni philosophy society at the end of the month on whether the future is open, and I've been reading one of those chunky popular books about time to get my bearings. The chapter on Aristotle says roughly this:

"Aristotle's worry, in chapter 9 of On Interpretation, was this. Suppose we say today that there will be a sea battle tomorrow. If that sentence is true today, then tomorrow's battle is unavoidable — nothing anyone does can make it not happen, and deliberation is pointless. Aristotle's way out was to deny that statements about future contingents are either true or false: they get a third, indeterminate value. He therefore rejected the law of the excluded middle for them. Two thousand years later Łukasiewicz built the first many-valued logic precisely to formalise this, giving future contingents the value ½ — and in his system 'either there will be a sea battle tomorrow or there won't' also comes out as ½ rather than as true, which is exactly what Aristotle had argued. The moral is that an open future isn't compatible with classical logic: you have to weaken logic itself before you can take seriously the thought that tomorrow is genuinely unsettled rather than just unknown to us."

Something's nagging at me — my instinct is that the book keeps sliding between "it's true now" and "it has to happen", and that the bit about excluded middle is doing more work than it admits — but I don't know the literature well enough to say where it goes wrong, and I don't want to stand up and repeat something a specialist would wince at.
