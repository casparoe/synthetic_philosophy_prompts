A pop-philosophy blog post I keep seeing cited in rationalist-adjacent threads summarizes the situationist critique of virtue ethics roughly like this:

"Social psychology has refuted virtue ethics. Milgram's shocks, the prison experiment, the seminary students who stepped over a slumped stranger because they were in a hurry: in each case behavior tracked features of the situation, not the agent's character. Psychologists call the illusion to the contrary the fundamental attribution error, and it has been quantified — cross-situational consistency in trait-relevant behavior comes out around 0.2 to 0.3, i.e. essentially zero, which means there are no character traits. Nobody is honest; someone is at most honest-in-the-office or honest-in-fine-weather. Virtue ethics, which is built on the idea of a person who reliably does the right thing for the right reasons across situations, therefore has no subject matter. And the standard reply — that virtue ethics is normative, about how we ought to be rather than how we are — misses the point, since a normative theory has to be action-guiding, and 'be honest' cannot guide anyone if honesty is not something one can be."

I am fairly confident the steps from "consistency is low" to "there are no traits" to "virtue ethics is refuted" are doing more work than they can bear, but I cannot say where, and I do not trust my sense of which parts are fair simplifications and which are distortions. Please go through it claim by claim: what would someone who actually defends the situationist critique accept as stated, what would such a person distance herself from, what would a virtue ethicist call a misrepresentation, and does the conclusion survive once the contested steps are stated more carefully?

Two things in particular. First, is there a real option in the vicinity of what I will call the aggregation escape: virtues defined not over all logically possible situations but over the distribution of situations a given agent actually encounters, weighted by how often she encounters them, with aggregation across occasions within that distribution recovering enough stability to make virtue attributions true of some people? If that is more than wordplay, what is the strongest situationist reply to it, and does the kind of evidence the post gestures at settle the question or leave it open? Second, can the critique be read as an empirically conditioned challenge to one premise — that the virtues are in fact widely possessed — rather than as a refutation of the normative claims, such that a virtue ethicist could grant the psychology and retreat to the claim that the virtues are rare, and if so how much practical usefulness is lost by that retreat?
