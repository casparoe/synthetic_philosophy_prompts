Someone in my typography seminar produced the following argument, and I cannot find the break in it, though I am sure there is one.

(1) List every visual feature a glyph can have: stroke weight, contrast, axis, aperture, terminal treatment, whether a bowl is open or closed, single-storey or double-storey, and so on.
(2) For any feature F on that list, there are two inscriptions that differ in F and that any competent reader of Latin script reads, without hesitation, as the same lowercase a. A single-storey a and a double-storey a share almost nothing and are both plainly a.
(3) If a feature can vary between two inscriptions while the letter stays the same, that feature belongs to style, not to the letter's identity.
(4) So every feature belongs to style, and the letter a has no visual identity of its own: what makes a mark an a is only the role it occupies in a system of contrasts with the other glyphs of the script. This is supported by cases where a bare vertical stroke is an l in one typeface and a capital I in another — the company a shape keeps settles which letter it is.
(5) Call a typeface Slip if it is produced by taking an ordinary typeface, giving each letter the shape the original gives to the letter two places later in the alphabet, and shearing the whole set uniformly by five degrees. Slip preserves every distinction the original preserves, and readers trained on it for a week read it at normal speed.
(6) In Slip, the glyph sitting in the a slot plays exactly the relational role that a plays, so by (4) it is a perfectly good a — on that view there is no further sense in which the ordinary a is the real one and Slip's is a stand-in for it.
(7) I cannot accept that. There is a difference between a shape's being an a and its merely occupying the a position in a notational scheme; a scheme that assigned the ordinary o shape to a and the ordinary a shape to q would be getting something wrong, not merely doing something differently.
(8) So (4) is false and the letter a has a visual identity after all — yet (2) and (3) look airtight, and nobody has ever produced a feature that all and only a's share.

Which step do you reject, and why did it look sound? I would most like to keep (2) and (7). If your answer is that letter identity is a matter of degree, of family resemblance, or of a cluster of features, tell me what that costs when I try to explain why Slip's glyph is a stand-in for a rather than an a — and whether that costs more than simply giving up (7).
