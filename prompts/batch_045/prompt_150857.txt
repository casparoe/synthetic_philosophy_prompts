I have no academic background. I left school at eighteen, worked up from a helpdesk job, and now run incident response at a mid-sized hosting company. The vocabulary I actually think in is the postmortem vocabulary: trigger, contributing factor, root cause, blast radius, defence in depth, single point of failure, near miss, retry storm, failover that did not fire, time to detect, time to mitigate, and above all the counterfactual I ask at every review — if we had fixed this one thing and changed nothing else, would we still have had an incident? I also think in probabilities rather than certainties: fixing this alone would have kept us up maybe sixty per cent of the time.

I got into an argument with a friend who reads a lot of history. She said that overdetermination — several things each of which would have been enough on its own — is a genuine problem for causal explanation in history, and that historians argue about it in a way that has no answer. I said this is just Tuesday: it is an incident with multiple contributing factors, you list them all, you fix the cheap ones, and the philosophical puzzle is a fuss about nothing. She said no, the historical case is different in kind, and I could not say exactly why. So I want the translation done properly rather than won by assertion.

Please translate the following into my vocabulary, one item at a time. I have written them in plain English as I understand them from general-audience books; I do not need names, authors or citations, and please do not supply any.

1. Symmetric overdetermination: two independent things present together, either of which would have produced the outcome by itself. My work version: an expired certificate took down one service, while a client retry storm was independently enough to knock over the shared queue, and both happened.
2. The backup that never fired: a second cause standing by which would have produced the same outcome if the first had not, but did not act. My version: a failover that was healthy and would have covered the failure had we routed to it.
3. The claim that most causes are neither necessary nor sufficient on their own, but matter as a non-redundant part of a larger bundle which as a whole was enough but not required. This is the one I can least see the shape of in my own terms, because in a postmortem a contributing factor is allowed to be small.
4. Causes versus background conditions, and the observation that which things get demoted to background depends on what was normal or expected at the time.
5. Contrastive explanation: that we always explain why this happened rather than that, so which cause is picked out depends on which contrast is in view and on what the explainer cares about.
6. Explaining a human action by the reasons the agent took themselves to have, rather than by what brought it about.

Use this only as the shape of a case, not as something to adjudicate: as it is usually told, the assassination in Sarajevo in 1914, alongside the alliance system, the mobilisation timetables and the arms race — several things such that you cannot simply delete one and say the war would not have come.

For each item give me three things: what translates cleanly, what translates only if I distort it or smuggle in an assumption my vocabulary does not actually have, and what cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all. Be honest about the second category in particular — I would rather be told that you had to cheat than be given a tidy mapping.

Then the diagnosis, which is the part I care about. For whatever does not survive: is it a blind spot in postmortem thinking, or a sign that the historians' worry is less sharp than it sounds? My suspicion is that at least some of it is the second — that my practice already dissolves the puzzle by treating a full list of contributing factors as a to-do list rather than an explanation — and my friend's suspicion is the opposite, that postmortems work only because a team has the authority to act and a forward-looking remit, so the hard part of the historical question never arises for us. Take a view.

Finally: tell me where your own translation is most likely to be doing violence to the original, which single item you are least confident you have rendered faithfully, and what argument or consideration would move you to a different verdict on the residue. I would rather have your uncertainty located than have the answer sound finished.
