I am writing a paper on the structure of populist appeals to "the will of the people," and I want to get one nineteenth-century passage exactly right before I use it as my stalking horse. My own working taxonomy has three readings of the phrase, and I have given them provisional names so that I can ask about them precisely:

- the *aggregative* reading: the will of the people is the output of a procedure (a vote, a parliament, a plebiscite) applied to the wills of individuals;
- the *ascriptive* reading, which I call the **attribution loop**: a group counts as "the people" and its policy counts as "the people's will" partly because it is successfully *treated* as such — named, acclaimed, deferred to, not successfully contested — so that the will is constituted by the practice of claiming it rather than merely registered by it;
- the *idealized* reading: the will of the people is what the people would will under specified conditions (full information, deliberation, absence of manipulation).

Here is the passage I want help with, from John Stuart Mill, *On Liberty* (1859), chapter I, where he is describing what he takes to be the characteristic doctrine of an earlier generation of European liberalism:

> What was now wanted was, that the rulers should be identified with the people; that their interest and will should be the interest and will of the nation. The nation did not need to be protected against its own will. There was no fear of its tyrannizing over itself. Let the rulers be effectually responsible to it, promptly removable by it, and it could afford to trust them with power of which it could itself dictate the use to be made. Their power was but the nation's own power, concentrated, and in a form convenient for exercise. This mode of thought, or rather perhaps of feeling, was common among the last generation of European liberalism, in the Continental section of which it still apparently predominates.

Please work from the passage itself and from the distinctions above; assume no library or internet access, and do not spend words on where to read more.

1. Reconstruct the reasoning as charitably as you can. In particular: is the "identification" of rulers with people meant as a *causal* claim (elections and removability make the rulers' will track the nation's antecedent will) or a *constitutive* one (whatever responsible, removable rulers end up willing just *is* the nation's will)? Which reading does the sentence "Their power was but the nation's own power" support, and what in the wording tells against the other?

2. The claim "There was no fear of its tyrannizing over itself" looks self-evident and is not. Say exactly what suppressed premise it needs. I have been calling this the **no-residue condition**: that there be no person or subgroup over whom the power is exercised who is not also, in the same sense, among those exercising it. State the condition carefully, show how the inference from "the power is the nation's own" to "no one is tyrannized" is supposed to go, and say precisely where it breaks. Then test it on this case: a state in which 62 per cent support the government, and the government uses concentrated power coercively against the remaining 38 per cent. On the view Mill is describing, are the 38 per cent tyrannized by themselves?

3. Consider the temporal requirement I call **synchrony**: the phrase "power of which it could itself dictate the use to be made" seems to require that the nation's will exist *at* the moment of exercise, not only at the moment of election. Does the passage commit the view to synchrony? And does the pairing of "effectually responsible" with "promptly removable" suggest that Mill thinks the mechanism is doing something stronger than registering an antecedent will — i.e., is the passage evidence for my attribution-loop reading?

4. Mill calls this "a mode of thought, or rather perhaps of feeling." What is he doing with that hedge? If he is right that it is not really an argument but an affect, what does that imply for my taxonomy — in particular, does it make the ascriptive reading the most faithful reconstruction of what such a "feeling" actually asserts?

Finally: tell me which of my three readings the passage genuinely supports, which it is neutral between, and what the strongest version of the passage would be if someone wanted to resist my use of it as a stalking horse. Write roughly 700–900 words, in numbered sections matching my questions, and add a short closing note flagging any point where you are going beyond what the text strictly supports.
