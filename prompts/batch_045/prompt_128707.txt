I'm TAing a first-year seminar with a title like Chance and Choice — half probability, half philosophy — and I've got one 80-minute slot next week on Bertrand's paradox and the principle of indifference. About 22 students, mostly not maths people, in a room with movable chairs and a projector but no laptops, and essentially no budget: straws, coins, string, rulers, printed circles, that kind of thing. They'll already have seen the standard setup in the reading — draw a random chord of a circle, ask how often it's longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and note that it comes out as 1/3 or 1/2 or 1/4 depending on whether you randomise the endpoints, the radius, or the midpoint. What I don't want is to stand up and re-explain that. I want them to walk into it.

Here's where I'm stuck, and it's the part I actually want your help with. My half-baked plan was to split them into three groups, hand each group a different physical procedure for generating chords, have them produce a few dozen each, and then let them fight about why the frequencies came apart. But I want the session to land somewhere sharper than "the description was incomplete, lol". The idea I keep circling is a second round: give every group a sealed set of chords generated by a procedure they're not told, and make them bet on — or at least rank — which procedure produced them. That converts "random chord is underdetermined" into "here's data, go recover the missing parameter". I like it, but I genuinely can't tell whether it teaches the intended lesson or quietly changes the subject, turning a puzzle about what indifference can license a priori into an ordinary bit of statistical inference about a hidden mechanism. If it does change the subject I'd rather know now and build the debrief around that fact. Related worry: someone will ask why we don't just toss straws at a big circle on the floor and count, and I think the answer involves translation invariance pinning things to 1/2, but I'm not confident I could defend that on my feet, and I'm not sure it finishes off the objector either, because whoever throws the straw still has to be indifferent about something — where its midpoint lands, what angle it comes in at, how the floor is divided up.

So: design me the actual session. The rules and materials, the timeline with rough minutes, who does what when, and a debrief that maps the moments where they get confused onto the philosophical moves — including where the principle of indifference still has a job left after all three procedures are on the table, and where it's been exposed as doing nothing. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (people are terrible at randomising by hand, they'll aim the straws, someone will miscount the ones that miss the circle entirely and I don't trust myself to say on the spot whether that even matters) and how to handle each. And be honest about which feature of the paradox the game can't reproduce and I'll just have to say out loud at the end. One last thing: write it as continuous prose please, no headings or bullet points or bold or tables, the way you'd explain it to me in an email.
