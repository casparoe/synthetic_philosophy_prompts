I work on the team that owns ranking for a large social platform, and I have been asked to help write the internal doctrine that decides when we may suppress, down-rank, or label a piece of content. I wanted something I could defend to a skeptical colleague without appealing to taste, so I tried to derive it from Mill's harm principle: roughly, that the only warrant for coercing someone against their will is prevention of harm to others, that a person's own good is not a sufficient warrant, and that mere offense or dislike is not harm. What I got instead dissolves our entire moderation function, and I cannot locate the broken step.

The argument, set out:

(1) Coercion is warranted only to prevent harm to others, where harm is a wrongful setback to someone's interests — not offense, and not a setback the person brought on themselves by acting voluntarily.

(2) Call a harm consent-laundered when every link in the causal chain that produces it is a voluntary act by someone other than the person who ends up worse off.

(3) Nearly every downstream consequence of a post is produced by other users' voluntary acts — clicking, sharing, quoting, imitating, organizing, showing up — so the harms that flow from posts are consent-laundered.

(4) A consent-laundered harm is one the sufferer's upstream agents accepted, so it is not a wrongful setback and does not count under (1).

(5) Therefore no post can be suppressed on harm grounds. Whatever harm flows from it flows through consent-laundered chains, and (1) leaves nothing left to count.

Two things make (5) unacceptable to me. First, we can measure: in an A/B test, ranking a given class of post slightly higher produces a statistically reliable increase in the number of people who end up worse off by our own incident criteria, holding the rest of the system fixed. That looks like harm caused by us, by the only standard of causation I trust. Second, there are cases the doctrine should plainly reach — a coordinated brigade aimed at a private individual, non-consensual imagery, a campaign that gets someone fired — and in each of them the person harmed did nothing to bring it about.

I would rather keep (1) than keep (5), and I would rather keep the counterexamples than give up (1). So something earlier has to go, and I have three candidate diagnoses with no way to choose between them. That (3) is false, because some of these chains contain links nobody chose. That (4) is false, because the consent doing the laundering belongs to the person acting while the claim being waived belongs to the person harmed, and in the aggregate those are not the same set of people. That the step from (2) and (3) to (4) commits a composition error, since the aggregated effect of individually voluntary acts — a cascade that crosses a threshold, a norm that hardens once enough people conform — is not itself something anyone consented to.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I also want to know whether my measurement point does any work at all, or whether it only changes what we know and not what we owe: if the counterfactual in our experiment is a different ranking rather than no ranking at all, and that alternative ranking has victims of its own, have I measured harm, or only redistributed it?
