I write a monthly column on science and ideas, and a device I lean on heavily is the counterfactual: what would have happened if the grant had gone to the other lab, if the observation had been missed that morning, if the launch had slipped a year. My copy desk has exactly one rule about these sentences — do not write as though someone who never existed could have had experiences — and it gets applied unevenly, so my drafts come back rewritten in ways that are sometimes better and sometimes, I think, worse. I would like a procedure I can actually run on a sentence before I file it: a short sequence of tests, in plain prose with no formalism, for deciding how to refer to an individual inside a scenario that did not happen. Assume five minutes, one page, a writer with no philosophy training, and copy editors who will not read anything longer. For each step give me what would count as a pass, what would count as a fail, and what the rewrite is when a sentence fails.

The conceptual material I am working from is the contrast between transworld identity and counterpart theory, and I need the procedure to be faithful to the counterpart-theoretic side without dragging the metaphysics into the column. In particular I have been toying with a distinction I have not seen drawn cleanly and which I want the procedure to adjudicate: the counterpart of a person picked by causal origin (same parents, same gamete, same early history) versus the counterpart picked by narrative role (the person occupying the same slot in the story I am telling — the discoverer, the director, the dissenter). My suspicion is that journalists almost always want the narrative counterpart while the copy desk's rule implicitly demands the origin counterpart, and that these come apart exactly in the cases I care about. So: when does each one govern, and when they conflict, which wins, or is that genuinely undecidable? I also want the procedure to handle three things I keep getting wrong: cases where there is no unique closest counterpart, so that a name should be replaced by existential phrasing; cases where I chain counterfactuals and silently assume the counterpart relation is transitive and symmetric when it need not be; and the point that what counts as closest is fixed by the explanatory purpose of the sentence rather than by overall similarity, which I think is where most of my disputes with the desk really live.

Please run the finished procedure on three cases. First, a discovery that in fact was made by one group and, on the standard historical judgment, would have been made by a different group within about five years — how should I write the sentence so that I do not imply the same people made it twice? Second, a biography piece where I want to write that a subject would have had a certain career even if her parents had never met, or that a historical catastrophe robbed the world of millions of children who would have grown up to do particular things: I expect the procedure to tell me something here that will spoil a paragraph I like, and I want to see the argument for spoiling it rather than a flat prohibition. Third, a counterfactual about an office rather than a person — if the committee had chosen differently, the agency would have acted otherwise — where the individual and the role pull in different directions. Finally, tell me honestly what the procedure cannot do: which sentences it has to hand back to me as an editorial judgment, where it will misfire on deadline, and where the metaphysics simply does not settle the question of how a sentence will land with a reader.
