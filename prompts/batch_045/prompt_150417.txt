**Question 3**

(a) Set out the argument from hallucination against naïve realism as sharply as you can, isolating the premise that does the real work.

(b) Explain how a disjunctivist about perception responds to that argument, and say what she concedes.

(c) Consider the following pair of cases.

*Mediated Vera.* Vera's optic nerves have been destroyed. A camera mounted on her head drives an array implanted in her visual cortex, calibrated so that the resulting visual experience is, by her own report and by every behavioural and discriminative measure available to us, exactly as it was before the injury when she looked at a well-lit red cube on a plain table. In the test condition a real red cube is placed on the table in front of her, at exactly the position where she seems to see one; the camera is working normally; and her experience is in fact produced, via camera and array, by that very cube.

*Screened Vera.* Same subject, same device, same calibration, and an experience subjectively and behaviourally indistinguishable from the first. But an opaque screen has been slid between Vera and the table, and the array is driven by a recording made the previous day of the same cube in the same position. Vera cannot tell which condition she is in, and no introspective or behavioural test we can devise distinguishes them.

Are Mediated Vera and Screened Vera in the same kind of state? Does your answer to (b) settle the matter? If not, what further question does the pair raise that the standard argument from hallucination does not?

(d) Is the disjunctivist's position best understood as a claim about the kind of state the subject is in, or as a claim about what the subject is entitled to believe? Why does the distinction matter?

**Student's answer**

(a) The argument from illusion — or, as it is often put, the argument from hallucination, which is the same argument with a different example — runs as follows. A subject's experience of a pink rat when no rat is present can be subjectively indistinguishable from her experience when she really sees a pink rat. Since she cannot tell the two apart from the inside, the two experiences must be the same kind of state, and whatever account we give of one we must give of the other. But in the hallucinatory case there is no mind-independent object for the subject to be related to; so perception cannot consist in a relation to a mind-independent object either. The premise doing all the work is therefore the indistinguishability premise. This is the phenomenal principle in its most plausible form: if it visually appears to a subject that something is F, then there is something of which she is aware that is F; and in the hallucinatory case that something cannot be the rat. Naïve realism is refuted.

(b) The disjunctivist denies the inference. On her view, the fundamental kind of state a subject is in differs between the good case and the bad case: in perceiving, the subject stands in a relation to the object itself, and the object is a constituent of her situation; in hallucinating, she does not. The disjunction "S either perceives o or hallucinates o" is not to be cashed in as a single common kind that both disjuncts share. Importantly, the disjunctivist can and should concede what the indistinguishability premise says: she can grant that the hallucinator's experience has phenomenal character, that it is for the subject just like perceiving, and that no introspective test will reveal which case she is in. What she denies is that any of this forces a common kind. Her opponent needs the further claim that subjective indistinguishability entails sameness of kind, and it is that claim which is doing the work in the argument — not indistinguishability itself.

(c) Applied to the cases, the disjunctivist must treat Mediated Vera and Screened Vera alike: in neither case does Vera stand in the relation to the cube that naïve realism requires, since in neither case does the cube act on her in the way ordinary objects act on perceivers. So the disjunctivist must classify both as hallucination. But this is exactly why the pair is damaging. The disjunctivist wants the difference between the two conditions to be a difference in the kind of state Vera is in; yet, by her own admission, Vera cannot register the difference in any way, and the only thing that distinguishes the cases is an extrinsic matter of causal history. A difference the subject can never be in a position to detect is not a difference in the kind of experience she is having. So the view does not collapse into naïve realism, as is often said, but into the causal theory of perception — with the additional and less plausible claim that what matters is the provenance of the experience rather than what it is like for the subject. The pair of cases thus does no real work beyond the standard argument; it merely dramatises it.

(d) The disjunctivist's claim is best read as an epistemic one. What she really wants is that a perceiving subject's experience gives her a reason for believing that there is a cube in front of her which a hallucinating subject's experience does not give — a warrant that is factive, one that could not be had in the bad case. This is why she insists on the difference of kind. For if perception and hallucination were the same kind of state, then, since a hallucinating subject's experience gives her no justification whatsoever for believing there is a cube before her, perception would give her no justification either, and perception could never ground knowledge of the environment. The distinction between the metaphysical and the epistemic reading matters because on the metaphysical reading the view is a bare classificatory claim with no consequences for anything we care about, whereas on the epistemic reading it explains what perception is *for*.
