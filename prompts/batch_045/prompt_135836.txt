I would like help disambiguating a slogan I keep meeting in arguments about psychiatry: "mental illness is a myth" — the sentence Szasz made famous.

Where I met it, and what work it was being made to do. A close friend was recently given a diagnosis of bipolar II disorder. His family urged a trial of a mood stabiliser. He refused, and defended the refusal with the slogan, roughly as follows: psychiatry has found no disease in him, only a judgement that the way he lives is disapproved of; since nothing in him is diseased, there is nothing there for medicine to treat; and a drug offered in those circumstances is not a treatment at all but a way of managing him for other people's convenience. In the same conversation a second person, who works at a peer-run crisis service, used the very same sentence to a different end: to argue that nobody should ever be detained and treated against their will, since a diagnosis is a moral verdict in medical vocabulary and the state has no business enforcing moral verdicts by force.

So the slogan was asked to carry two conclusions: first, that there is nothing here for medicine to fix; second, that compulsory treatment is never legitimate. My guess is that it carries one or the other only on certain readings, and neither on others.

What I want is a menu. Please set out the distinct precise theses the sentence could be expressing, ordered roughly from the nearly trivial to the very strong. The distinctions I can see opening up but cannot get straight include: whether the claim is about the words (talk of illness here is metaphorical, stretched, a category mistake) or about the things (there is no such condition, no disease entity, no bodily abnormality, nothing that happens to a person rather than being done by them); whether it is a claim about the disunity or constructedness of current diagnostic categories rather than about the absence of any underlying pathology; whether it rests on a particular requirement on the concept of disease — a lesion, a discoverable bodily abnormality, a statistical dysfunction, something happening to a subject rather than something a subject does — and which of those requirements is doing the real work; whether it is best read as an epistemic claim about the evidential standing of psychiatric judgements, a semantic claim about what they mean, an evaluative claim that they are disguised moral judgements, or a political claim about what they are used for; and whether "myth" means simply false, or means something more like a story that is not literally true but does work in the world, in which case calling mental illness a myth might be compatible with its being useful or even indispensable. There are probably readings I have not separated out.

For each reading I would like: (a) the thesis stated as precisely as you can; (b) what else it commits its holder to, including its verdicts on the three cases below, since a reading that sounds moderate in the abstract often turns out drastic in application; (c) a short verdict on the reading itself — near-trivial, substantive and defensible, substantive but contested, or straightforwardly false, with the reason; and (d) which, if either, of the two conclusions above it supports. I am particularly interested in readings that support one conclusion and not the other, and in whether any single reading supports both.

The cases I want each reading run against:

1. A constructed case. Suppose a condition — call it aldreth syndrome — in which previously well people develop, over a few weeks, complete loss of the capacity for pleasure, profound slowing of thought and movement, and crushing fatigue; suppose it is caused by an identified antibody binding to a particular receptor in the brain, visible on a scan, and resolved by removing the antibody; and suppose patients uniformly describe the change as something that happened to them rather than something they did, wanted, or chose, and that they seek help for it. This looks like the sort of thing any defender of the slogan would have to concede is a disease. What I want to know is what follows from that concession: does the slogan become a claim about only some of the conditions currently gathered under psychiatric labels; does it survive as a claim about the rest; and is it a claim of the form "no abnormality has yet been found", which any future discovery could overturn, or one that no discovery could overturn?

2. A case of social mismatch. A teacher becomes miserable, anxious and sleepless while working under a headmaster who bullies her; the misery lifts entirely when she moves schools. A clinic diagnoses a depressive episode and prescribes an antidepressant. My instinct is that the slogan is at its strongest here, but I cannot tell whether it says that she is not ill, that she is ill but not in the way the diagnosis implies, that the diagnosis is a moral or political judgement in medical dress, or something else again.

3. A case in which the person wants treatment. A man is tormented by voices that insult and comment on him, asks for help, and finds that an antipsychotic silences them; he says the drug gave him his life back. Nothing was coerced and he agrees with everything he has been told about the diagnosis. If the slogan is true on some reading, what is true of him? Which readings imply that he is being deceived, that his consent makes no difference, or that no medical description applies to him?

Finally, please say which reading you take to be the one actually at work in each of the two uses I described, and whether either use requires a reading stronger than its speaker would be willing to defend if it were stated plainly.

Two constraints. Please argue from the cases and from the logic of the claims rather than from the literature: I am not asking what anyone wrote, and a survey of positions and names would be of no use to me. And please keep the readings genuinely distinct — I can already produce the two or three that everyone reaches for first; I am interested in the ones that get lost between them.
