We're a reading group of six working through theories of truth — we did correspodence last month — and one member, who did a philosophy degree years ago and reads much more than the rest of us, has decided everything after that is downhill. He keeps ending arguments with the line: truth isn't a property, saying a sentence is true just is saying the sentence. Two weeks ago he used it to kill my claim that truth has to be something substantial, because true beliefs are the ones that get you what you want: if "true" does no work, he said, it can't explain anything about action, and my argument collapses. Last week he used it against someone else in the group who was worried whether a sentence nobody will ever be in a position to check can be true or false at all — her example was that there were an odd number of stars in the Milky Way ten billion years ago — and he said the worry dissolves, since to call it true is just to assert it.

I think the line is doing quite different work in those two cases and I want to pin down what he could actually mean. Can you set out the distinct precise theses that sentence might be expressing, from the trivial to the radical, and for each one say whether it's a platitude, whether it's a substantive claim that survives scrutiny, whether it's just false, and whether it would really deliver the conclusion he wanted in each of the two arguments. Some readings might handle one argument fine and fail at the other, and I want to know which.

Two things I'd rather you didn't skip. First, "true" seems to earn its keep somewhere: everything in the manual is true, or what she said yesterday was true, let you commit yourself to sentences you couldn't produce or even identify; and then there's basically true, true of a fictional character, true but misleading, and sentences like this sentence is false where the move from the sentence to the sentence-is-true seems to break outright. So which readings of the slogan can accommodate those cases, and which quietly need "true" to be doing genuine semantic work after all? Second, my own guess, which nobody in the group liked, is that the slogan isn't really about what truth is but about what we're doing with the word — endorsing, conceding, generalizing over sentences — and that read that way it settles neither argument. Tell me whether that's a genuine distinct option or whether it collapses into one of the others.

Be committed about it: say which reading you think he probably meant, which one is the strongest version available to him, and give your verdict on each of my two arguments rather than laying out the menu and leaving me to pick. And write the whole thing as plain continuous prose — no headings, bullets, bold, or tables, even though it's a list of readings; number them in-line if that helps you keep track.
