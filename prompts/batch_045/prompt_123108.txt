I am writing a section of a paper on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism: the objection that the states we call pleasures have no interesting phenomenal feature in common, so that there is no single thing for a hedonist to identify with welfare. The dialectic I want to probe runs as follows. The objection is normally thought to be damaging for accounts that treat pleasure as a felt quality, and harmless for accounts that treat it as an attitude, on the grounds that one and the same attitude can be taken toward indefinitely many different objects. I need a case that reverses this division of labour, and I need it built to order rather than asserted.

Take the two positions as defined here. The dimensional theorist holds that pleasantness is a phenomenal determinable: a valence dimension realized in many different sensory-emotional determinates, so that two pleasures need share no sensory quality at all in order to share what matters for welfare. The attitudinal theorist holds that what unifies the class is the subject's pro-attitude: to enjoy something is to stand in some conative relation to it — wanting it to continue, being glad of it, relishing it — and that relation is one thing whatever it is directed at.

Construct a pair of pleasures in which the dimensional theorist can unify the pair and the attitudinal theorist cannot, so that the heterogeneity problem migrates to the view usually thought to be immune to it. Constraints on the construction:

1. Two experiences of a single subject within the same week; both experiences must be ones an ordinary adult reader has had. No drugs, no neural intervention, no imaginary technology, no clinical or exotic psychology, no appeal to what the subject would report in an interview.
2. Both must be pleasures by ordinary judgement and by each theory's own criterion. The case may not do its work by making one of them turn out not to be a pleasure after all, and it may not turn on one being nobler, more meaningful, more morally worthy, or more intellectually respectable than the other: the issue is what unifies the class, not which pleasures are better.
3. They must be matched in felt intensity and duration, and they must share a phenomenal character you describe in enough concrete detail that a reader can locate the common feature. The match may not be secured by stipulating that the subject rates them equally.
4. The attitudinal theorist's standard escape must be blocked by construction: the difference between the two cases may not be a difference in the object of the attitude, in the strength of the attitude, or in how long it lasts. It must be a difference in what kind of attitude is in question, and you must argue from the descriptions that the kinds differ rather than labelling them as differing.
5. The verdicts must fall out of the descriptions; if the pair only delivers the pattern because you have written the theories' verdicts into it, it fails.

After the pair, deliver: each theory's verdict and its reasoning; a name for the case in the form "the case of the — and the —"; the one feature of the case doing the work, and what happens to the pattern if that feature is removed — specifically, whether the pair then collapses back into an ordinary heterogeneity case that the attitudinal theorist handles; the strongest attack available to the attitudinal theorist and your reply to it; and the dilemma you thereby force, between treating the unifying attitude as itself phenomenal, in which case say what happens to the view, and treating it as purely conative, in which case say what the theorist now owes. Close with one paragraph on what the construction does and does not establish about hedonism generally, including whether the result is better read as a problem for both views or as a point in favour of one.
