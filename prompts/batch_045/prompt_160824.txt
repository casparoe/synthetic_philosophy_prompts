I've been trying to settle what I actually think about perception by recording verdicts on cases rather than by picking a camp, and I want to know whether there's a principle that captures them. Please skip the survey of who has defended which view — I'd rather have the principle and its costs than a map of the dialectic. Tabooing the word "perception" hasn't helped me.

Case one: an ordinary mug on my desk, good light, nothing tricky. Verdict: perception, and I know there's a mug there.

Case two: a straight stick half-submerged in water, looking bent. Verdict: still a perception of the real stick, and yet the experience misrepresents it — I don't come to know the stick is bent. I also want to say the stick looks bent to me in a way that isn't just a belief I could argue myself out of.

Case three: a hyperrealistic silicone apple on a pedestal, which I take for a real apple. Verdict: a genuine perception of a real object, the apple-shaped lump. I know there's an apple-shaped thing there; I don't know there's an apple.

Case four: the same mug, seen in a mirror. Verdict: perception of the mug, and knowledge. So reflection defeats nothing, and any principle requiring unmediated contact with the object is already wrong.

Case five: a drug-induced hallucination of a mug, no mug present, no special setup. Verdict: not a perception, and no knowledge of any mug — and yet I'm strongly inclined to say this experience shares its intrinsic character with case one. That inclination is exactly what gives the standard argument from hallucination its pull on me, and I don't want to stipulate it away.

Case six: two subjects, no mug anywhere, but their hallucinated mugs are produced by a process that keeps the two streams coordinated from their different positions, so that each behaves, as they move around, precisely as one public mug would. Verdict: still not perception, still no knowledge. But I hold this verdict with much less confidence than the one in case five, and the pull toward saying there is something public they are both in contact with is real.

Case seven: a screen in front of me fed by a camera pointed at a real mug. From where I sit — and I don't know about the camera — it is subjectively just like case six. Verdict: perception of the mug, and I know there's a mug there.

Case eight: I hallucinate a mug at a spot behind an opaque screen, and unknown to me there is a mug at exactly that spot. Verdict: I go back and forth. The claim "there's a mug there" is true and I would be right, but nothing about that mug produced my state.

The pair I can't reconcile is six and seven. From the subject's point of view they are the same case: same experience, same evidential position, same capacity to act on it. I say no knowledge in the first and knowledge in the second. Either knowledge and justification fail to supervene on anything internal to the subject, in which case I don't understand what "same evidence, same justification" was ever supposed to mean, or one of the two verdicts has to go, and I can't tell which. Cases one and five press the same tension from the metaphysical side.

My tentative principle: an experience is a perception of an object only if that object is in the causal-explanatory loop that produces the experience — had the object not been there, or not been arranged as it is, the experience would not have had the course and content it had. This handles six and seven the way I want and rules out eight. But it says nothing about whether five shares its character with one, it needs a "partly in, partly out" clause for two, and I suspect it breaks somewhere near seven.

So: state the best principle you can, with the case that motivates each clause; tell me whether I need one principle or two — one for what kind of event the experience is, one for what it justifies — and where the two come apart; tell me which of my verdicts the principle forces me to give up; and tell me what it predicts for two cases I haven't listed. First: a mixed scene where everything is real except one projected object that isn't there at all — is the total experience one event of one kind, and if not, what are its parts? Second: a blind subject whose visual cortex is stimulated by an implant fed by a camera, who has trained with it for years and navigates reliably by it — perception or not, knowledge or not?
