I have been asked to give a seventy-minute guest lecture on collective intentionality and joint action to a master's-level seminar in multi-agent systems. The students are not philosophers. They have seen Nash and correlated equilibrium, belief hierarchies, common knowledge in the Aumann style, and POMDPs; they routinely model a group as a tuple of policies plus an epistemic state, and they will arrive using "intention" as a loose synonym for an agent's committed policy. I do not need the concept explained to me, and I do not need a survey of positions, names, dates, or anything to read. I need a plan for the explanation.

The destination is this: nothing built out of individual intentions, individual beliefs about others' intentions, and common knowledge of all of that is yet a case of two or more agents doing something together; something further has to be added — either mutual responsiveness to one another as co-agents, or a commitment that is irreducibly plural — and once it is added, it constrains what a joint action can be done for and what counts as one. Plan the route to that claim, not a defence of it. The class will generate the reductionist reply on their own within ten minutes; tell me where to let them have it and where to press on it.

Specifically, I want:

1. The components in the order they must be introduced, with the dependency stated: which step cannot land before which, and why the order cannot be permuted. Their formalism is simultaneously my best hook and my biggest obstacle, so I want to know where to lean on it and where it has to be explicitly set aside.

2. The single example that carries the whole session, what it must contain, and what it must not contain. Please do not build it on the cases that get recycled in every presentation of this topic — two people painting a house together, two people going for a walk together, the rowing crew, the orchestra. My students will half-recognise them and stop thinking.

3. Two cases of my own, which I want placed somewhere in the sequence — and tell me frankly if either is doing different work than I think:

   (a) Two people hold a heavy fire door shut, one to keep a dog in the hallway, the other to keep a crowd out of the hall. Neither cares at all about the other's end. Each would say, accurately, that they are holding the door. I want this to separate joint action from shared purpose.

   (b) Two colleagues carry a filing cabinet down a stairwell. Each is helping only because each believes the other needs it; each believes the other is going along grudgingly; each would let go if they knew the other did not really want the cabinet moved. The intentions interlock, each is continuously responsive to the other's movements, and both are badly wrong about the other. I want this to separate interlocking plans plus responsiveness from mutual recognition of one another as co-agents.

4. A check for understanding at the end that distinguishes someone who has grasped the distinction from someone who can only repeat the slogan, and that can be answered without having read anything.

5. What I should openly refuse to settle in seventy minutes, and say out loud that I am refusing.

Please deliver the plan as a Markdown table, one row per step (aim for six to nine rows), with exactly these columns: Step | Minutes | Move (one or two sentences: what I put on the board, or what I ask them) | What they can do by the end of it | Depends on (earlier step numbers) | Misconception created or defused here. Mark in the Move column where each of my two cases enters.

After the table, give one fenced JSON object with exactly these keys: "carrying_example" (string, the scenario in under eighty words); "example_must_have" (array of three or four strings); "example_must_lack" (array of two or three strings); "rejected_examples" (array of objects with "example" and "reason"); "case_verdicts" (array of two objects, one per case of mine, each with "case", "does_the_work_I_think" as a boolean, and "note"); "check_for_understanding" (string); "what_a_good_answer_sounds_like" (string); "refusals" (array of strings); "biggest_risk" (string, the single way this session most likely fails).
