Each spring I teach a graduate seminar on infinity, modality, and the limits of classical reasoning, and the unit on supertasks is the one part of the course that never quite works. Students arrive able to state the standard moves about Thomson's lamp and to repeat the names attached to them, but they cannot tell you which claim is doing the work, and when I change a detail of the case they have nothing to say. This year I want to replace my survey handout with a classification instrument instead: a decision tree that takes a student from a handful of claims about concrete cases to a named position, so that what gets sorted is what they are willing to say about the cases rather than which label they have decided to wear.

Please build that tree for me.

The root case is the familiar one: a lamp, initially off, is switched at 11:30, again at 11:45, again at 11:52:30, and so on, with each subsequent switch half as long after the previous one as the previous was after its predecessor, so that infinitely many switchings happen strictly before midnight. Everything before midnight is unproblematic; the question is what state the lamp is in at midnight. Every node of the tree should be phrased as a claim a student could accept or reject without knowing which position it leads to — no node may contain the name of a view, a school, or an author, and none may simply ask whether supertasks are possible as an abstract matter.

Some constraints on the structure. I want at most eight branch nodes and between six and nine leaves. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from the list of names: lay out first the independent choice points and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the actual positions in that space, and tell me which combinations are unoccupied and why — incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Where two leaves cannot really be pulled apart by any of your questions, flag the pair explicitly and say what kind of further case would be needed to separate them; I would rather have three flagged collisions than a tree that pretends to a sharpness it does not have.

The choice points I have in mind, roughly: whether the toggling schedule can be completed at all, and whether an answer that blocks this lamp also blocks every other completed infinite sequence of actions in finite time; whether the lamp's state at midnight has to be settled by the pre-midnight history together with the laws, or may be a further fact added to the story; whether the state at midnight must be the limit of the sequence of earlier states, and what is supposed to follow when that sequence has no limit; whether the thing around at midnight still is a lamp, and whether "on" and "off" are jointly exhaustive and exclusive predicates of it at an instant; and whether anything after midnight can constrain the answer — a further switching, or a later observation of the lamp, or the lamp's subsequent causal upshot.

I also want three case variants woven into the nodes, because these are the places where my students' answers come apart from their stated allegiances. First, an audit case: at 12:01 you ask, of the lamp, whether it was on at midnight. Someone who denies that there is a determinate state at midnight now owes an account of that question — is it answerable but unknowable, unanswerable, or not a genuine question at all — and the three answers pull in different directions. Second, a closed-loop variant: suppose the switch is not worked by an external agent on a schedule but is triggered by a comparator that flips whenever the filament temperature crosses a threshold, so that the lamp's trajectory is the solution of a continuous dynamics with the toggling as its limit behaviour. Does the "no fact of the matter" answer for the original case carry over here, and if not, what did the original answer depend on? Third, an urn cross-check: at each stage a large urn receives ten numbered balls and loses one, with infinitely many stages before midnight, and every ball that is ever added is eventually removed — so the urn is determinately empty at midnight, even though the number of balls in it grows without bound right up to the limit. If a student accepts that argument for the urn, what exactly entitles them to resist the parallel style of reasoning about the lamp, or to accept it and land somewhere they did not expect?

For each leaf, give the name of the position, or mark it "unnamed" if nobody defends it, and one sentence on the most awkward thing it is committed to — the consequence a holder would least like to have pointed out in seminar. The leaves must include: the view that this schedule cannot be completed and no question about midnight arises; the view that the description is itself contradictory and no such lamp is so much as possible; the view that the supertask is performed but the final state is not fixed by anything in the story and must simply be stipulated; the view that the final state is fixed once the actual mechanism is specified, so that the bare logical question is ill-posed; at least one view that reaches a determinate on or off by some principled rule about the togglings rather than by stipulation; and one view on which the lamp is neither determinately on nor determinately off at midnight, whether because it has ceased to be a lamp, because the predicates do not apply at that point, or because the state admits of degrees.

Close with three questions the tree cannot ask, but which I should put to the class anyway, together with a sentence each on why they resist being turned into branch nodes.
