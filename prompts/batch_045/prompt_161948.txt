I'm assembling a short packet for a graduate seminar I help teach on long-term prioritization and existential risk, and I want one item in it to be a piece of in-universe satire rather than another position paper. What I have in mind is a leaked internal memorandum — roughly 900 to 1,000 words, in the house style of a large private foundation that funds work aimed at improving the very long-run future — written about three months after an internal working group provisionally accepted the simulation hypothesis. The argument the group accepted is the familiar trilemma: either civilizations at something like our stage almost always die out before they could run vast numbers of high-fidelity simulations of their own ancestors, or the ones that reach that capacity almost never choose to run them, or else creatures in our epistemic situation are almost certainly inside one. Assume throughout that the foundation's whole method is expected-value comparison across interventions, and that its defining vice, the thing its critics call fanaticism, is the pattern where a one-in-a-million chance of an astronomically large payoff swamps every ordinary near-term item on the list. The memo should be written as a real internal document — an 'Epistemic status' line at the top, an Executive Summary, numbered recommendations with reallocations between its existing program areas, a section on cruxes, an inside-view/outside-view paragraph, a request for two new hires, a dissenting footnote from an internal faction that holds the hypothesis makes no practical difference, and a closingsection on what would change the group's mind — and it should treat its own conclusion seriously enough to actually revise its portfolio, its discount rate, and its headline estimate of how many future lives are at stake.

The comedy has to come out of the reasoning rather than out of the premise, and the reasoning has to be fair to what the hypothesis really does to this style of prioritization. Three cruxes in particular should be handled accurately and without strawmanning. First, whether the welfare of simulated minds matters at all: if it does not, then everything the foundation cares about downstream is zeroed out regardless of what it does; if it does, then the overwhelming majority of the value it recognizes lies in simulations it cannot causally reach, so the fraction of the future it can affect is negligible either way. Second, something you might call the horizon problem: a simulation has a runtime, a reference-class thought about where in that runtime you should expect to find yourself suggests the median remaining runtime may be short, and if simulations can contain simulations then the shut-down hazard rates at every level above you compound, so the far future collapses from billions of years to something merely long, and the astronomically large numbers that drive the foundation's rankings deflate. Third, the granularity complaint: the world renders fine-grained detail at cosmological distances and at small physical scales that no ancestor simulation would have any reason to compute, which is evidence against the naive version of the hypothesis and therefore against the usual move of treating us as typical simulated beings. I want a specialist in this area to read the memo and think the jokes are aimed at real joints, and I want a smart reader who has never read any of this literature to be able to follow it anyway, with terms defined in passing in the foundation's own voice — for instance a personnel note telling staff who joined to work on a billion-year future what the revised figure is, or a hiring line for a horizon analyst whose funding is 'secure by our standards, which is now not saying much.'

The turn I care most about is that the memo should fail at its own assignment, honestly. It was commissioned to decide whether the simulation hypothesis dampens fanaticism, and the interesting answer is that the damping does not survive contact with itself: even after all the discounting, the one lever left is that events inside the simulation might influence the simulators — by making the run worth continuing, or by signalling outward to whatever reality hosts it — and that lever is precisely a small probability of affecting stakes located outside the simulation, which is large enough in expectation to dominate every near-term intervention all over again. The memo should notice this in its own vocabulary, cost it out, fund a small pilot line item for it anyway, and not resolve it. So the piece should end on a modest, deadpan proposal that a reader recoils from — something like a pilot programme to make human history more legible and more gripping to an external audience, budgeted at a few hundred thousand dollars — with no winking, no admission that this is absurd, and no convenient escape into the thought that the hypothesis makes no practical difference after all unless that conclusion is earned line by line. Don't name, quote, or caricature any real person, organization, or publication; invent the foundation and its staff.
