I'm in a reading group on astrobiology and philosophy of biology and we've spent about a year on the "what is life" question, and its completely stalled. Everyone in the room knows the same cases — viruses, prions, a mule, a sterile worker ant, fire, self-replicating ribozymes in a beaker, a computer virus that mutates — and everyone has stopped moving. Roughly three camps: (1) we need a working definition because we're building detectors and biosignature instruments and someone has to decide what to point them at, so the definition should be operational and we should stop pretending it's deep; (2) there's no sharp boundary, life is a cluster / family-resemblance sort of thing and any definition will gerrymander at the edges; (3) the whole search is a mistake — "life" is a word we inherited from long before biology, and what we actually care about is a bunch of capacities (metabolism, evolution, autonomy, whatever) that come apart from each other, so we should retire the noun and keep the list.

My theory, which I want tested rather than confirmed: those aren't three answers to one question, they're three different jobs wearing one word — instrument design, taxonomy and natural kinds, and a practical "what are we obliged to care about or protect" question — and each camp is quietly optimizing for its own job and then acts surprised when its definition looks silly to the others.

But I'm not sure. Maybe there's a real substantive disagreement underneath, e.g. about whether there's any natural kind there at all, or about whether replication or self-maintenance is the load-bearing feature. I made up a test that I think pressures this: take a hundred-step continuum from a self-copying molecule inside a lipid bubble up to a modern cell, and ask each proposed definition where it cuts. The cuts dont cluster at all — they're smeared across the whole range. Is that evidence the question is ill-posed, or just evidence that my continuum is a bad instrument?

Three things I'd like you to actually engage rather than nod at. The sample-of-one worry: everything alive that we have shares a single ancestor, so we can't tell which features are essential to life and which are just what that ancestor happened to use — is that a permanent methodological limit, or a practical one that a cell built bottom-up from scratch, or a second genesis, would dissolve? The lone hermit: a self-maintaining system with metabolism, self-repair and responsiveness, sitting alone on a cold moon, no population, no reproduction, no evolution, ever. Half my group says obviously alive, half says obviously not. What is that split tracking? And the swap case: a cell functionally indistinguishable from an ordinary bacterium but running on different chemistry — extra base pairs, non-standard building blocks, different solvent. Everybody calls it alive, which suggests we're tracking organization rather than chemistry, and that seems to undercut every chemistry-first definition at once.

Give me the diagnosis with reasons: is this verbal, substantive, several questions sharing one name, bedrock intuitions nobody can argue for, or a hidden empirical question doing all the work? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, a synthetic cell, a second genesis, a change of question — or should we expect none and why? Please dont just list the competing definitions of life; I've read them all and that's the thing that isn't helping.
