I run a small reading group — six graduate students in history, two philosophers, and a retired diplomat — and we spent last term working through the grand theories of history: Polybius on the rotation of constitutional forms, Ibn Khaldun on group solidarity and the lifespan of dynasties, Spengler on cultures as organisms with a fixed span of growth and ageing, Toynbee on challenge and response and the part played by creative minorities. The term ended in a frustration I would like to fix, namely that cyclical versus linear turned out to be a nearly useless axis: nobody in the room believed in pure recurrence, nobody believed in a straight line, and yet they plainly disagreed with each other about something and none of them could say precisely what. So for the last session I want to hand out a decision tree that sorts a person by the structure of what they believe rather than by the label they would choose.

Please build it from the logical space first: give me the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, and only then locate the named views in that space, noting which cells are unoccupied and why — incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Start from a node everyone will grant, such as that some large-scale political orders have risen and collapsed, or that over long stretches both population and the stock of stored technique have increased, then use roughly eight to ten further nodes, each a yes-or-no or three-way question phrased so that an intelligent reader who has never opened any of these authors can answer it. No node may name a theorist, a school, or a century, and none may ask whether history is cyclical or linear or whether things are getting better. The distinctions I want used, though you may add or substitute where you think the space genuinely cuts: whether what recurs is a state or a process, that is a real return versus a spiral that passes through a similar-looking posture at a different height; whether the engine is a stock that accumulates and is then spent, such as solidarity, discipline, civic virtue or legitimating belief, or a cost that rises with scale and complexity while the returns to coordination fall, or largely exogenous shocks; what the unit is, a civilisation, a dynasty, a regime form, or the species; whether the pattern is meant to be lawlike enough to project forward, and what its holder would count as a disconfirming sequence; what, if anything, survives a collapse, so that a view can be cyclical about politics and cumulative about technique at the same time; whether the account has a terminal condition, exhaustion, endless turning, some post-historical stasis, or genuine openness; and two cases I want forced into the tree whether or not you think they belong. The first is what one member calls the closure problem: if the whole planet is now one interdependent system with no outside and no successor people waiting at the frontier, the usual cyclical machinery has nothing left to hand the torch to, and I want to know whether that refutes those views, is a special case for them, or just means the present system is the last of its kind. The second is his further claim, which the rest of us argued about for two hours, that a successor need not be a people at all, since something non-human could inherit our instruments and our coordination problems without inheriting our politics, and that this deserves either its own leaf or an explicit exclusion. Please also give me a node on periodisation, because another member insists that any pattern you find is an artifact of where you start and stop the clock, and I cannot tell whether that is a real position or a way of declining to answer.

The leaves should include, at minimum, Polybian constitutional rotation, the Ibn Khaldunian solidarity cycle, Spenglerian morphology, Toynbee's challenge-and-response scheme, at least one uncomplicated progress or stage view, at least one cost-of-complexity view, at least one spiral view, at least one compartmentalised view of the cyclical-in-politics-but-cumulative-in-technique sort, and the deflationary view that history has no shape and only our narratives impose one; add further leaves if the space requires them, and mark any of them as unnamed if nobody actually defends it. Each leaf should carry one sentence about its most awkward commitment. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, and say either what further question would prise them apart or why no such question exists. One page, plain text, drawn as a tree I can photocopy, with no footnotes and no citations, since the group has not read these authors and will not read them before we meet.
