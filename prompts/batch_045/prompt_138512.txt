My father died last spring, and since then I've been sitting regularly with an insight-meditation group that works in a broadly Theravāda idiom. Anattā has stopped being an intellectual curiosity for me and become something I'm trying to actually live with, and I've been asked to give the group a short talk laying out where I've landed. Before I commit to it in front of forty people, I'd like to know whether the package is coherent. Here it is:

(1) A person is nothing over and above the five aggregates and the causal relations among them. There is no owner of experience and no witness behind the aggregates; what we call a person is a causally continuous psychophysical stream. Any question about identity that isn't settled by those causal facts has no further determinate answer.

(2) At the same time, ordinary identity talk is perfectly in order for everyday, legal, and moral purposes. I don't think the analysis in (1) should change how we speak, or how we assign responsibility and praise and blame. I'm not proposing any revision of first-personal language.

(3) Identity is not what matters in survival. What matters is the holding, to a sufficient degree, of psychological continuity and connectedness, whatever its cause. Where those relations hold, that is as good as ordinary survival, and the further question of whether it is strictly the same person adds nothing.

(4) The branching case: where both hemispheres are transplanted into two different bodies, I'm fairly settled that the question of which of the two is me has no answer, that neither is a mere replacement, and that the prospect is about as good as ordinary survival rather than a way of dying.

(5) The non-branching replication case: where the original is destroyed and an exact functional replica built elsewhere, so that there is exactly one continuer, my gut says that this is death for the original and I would not step into the machine. I'll admit this verdict is doing a lot of work in how I think about my own dying, which may be why I hold it.

(6) The long-replacement case: an eighty-year-old shares almost no memories, traits, or projects with the twenty-year-old whose stream it is, and I have no hesitation at all in saying they are the same person. The thinness of the psychological overlap doesn't seem to me to threaten that.

(7) Where the view has actually helped me is with fear of death. I've become less afraid on the grounds that there is no subject who dies, only a stream that stops, and that the stopping of a stream is not something that befalls anyone. Put starkly, I've been telling myself that my death is not bad for me — not merely less bad than I used to assume — because there is no me for it to be bad for.

What I want is an audit rather than a lecture. Which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which only look as though they do? Rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and for each one say what specifically would have to give and what giving it would cost me — I don't know where the problem is, and I'd rather you didn't assume I've flagged the commitment I'm most attached to. I'd also like to know whether any pair is consistent only given some further claim I haven't stated, and whether any of my case verdicts in (4) through (6) is one that the principle in (3) cannot actually deliver.

One last thing: I've set the rebirth and karma side of the tradition aside, not because I reject it but because I can't see how to state it without smuggling back in what (1) denies. If you think the rest of the set only hangs together because I left that out, say so, and say what it would take to put it back in.
