My friend has been going around claiming ant colonies have minds -- she's a Hofstadter fan, so call it the Aunt Hillary thesis -- and I've been quietly collecting her verdicts on other systems to figure out what she's actually committed to. So far: a single worker ant, no; a honeybee swarm picking a nest site by waggle-dance quorum, yes; the termite mound with its fancy ventilation, no (her words: that's a nest, not a navigator); a slime mold solving a maze, no (clever dynamics, nothing there that can be wrong); a corporation, no (the "decisions" are just decisions of people who could walk away); a current language model, no (no world, no stakes); and when a colony buds off a daughter, two minds after separation, not one mind split in two.

New case I want to spring on her: a lab graft. Two unrelated colonies of the same species, one nest, forced into a single foraging and brood-care unit, and every morning the grad student washes the workers of both with the same synthetic cuticular hydrocarbon blend so nobody attacks anybody. So: one odor field, one trail network, two genomes, no chance of reproducing as a unit, and it stays unified only because a human keeps re-applying the perfume.

Does she say one mind, two, or none? What are the two or three rules that fit her track record about as well as each other, what does each predict here, and which one is she most likely running? And what one further case would actually separate them -- I was thinking either a colony evolved from scratch in a simulation (digital ants, digital pheromones, colony-level fission, nothing in there can suffer) or a real colony whose queen's been sterilized so it's a reproductive dead end, but I'm open to better ideas.
