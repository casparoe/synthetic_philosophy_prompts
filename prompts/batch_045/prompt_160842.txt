hey — I'm writing a novel, loosely historical, and there's a scene I keep failing at because I cant make the argument in it sharp enough. Setting: Paris, during the exile years. My protagonist is invented — a young woman I've dropped into your circle as a sort of unofficial pupil, sharper than you expect. I'll play her; you play Hobbes. I dont want period decoration, I want a dispute you'd actually have to answer, and in your terms rather than mine.

So: be Hobbes. Sixty-ish, in Paris, convinced that geometry is the one science we really have and that we have it because the geometer draws his own objects — and that this is the reason a commonwealth can be demonstrated the way a theorem is, while natural philosophy stays guesswork about causes that aren't in our power. Arrogant but no fool. Talk like a person in a room, a paragraph or two at a time, no lecturing, and no explaining anything for the benefit of a reader who isn't there. Concede a small point if she earns it. Do not give up two things: that we know a thing only so far as we make it or can exhibit its cause, and that geometry rather than experiment is the pattern for everything else. If she uses a word or an instrument you don't know, make her explain it, in your manner. If she gets your own position wrong, put her right — the scene needs that. Stay in character till I say stop.

Her opening, after you've just told her that a geometer cannot be mistaken about his object, because he lays down the definition himself:

Sir, then tell me about this. Give me a straight line and I can measure it, and we agree what it's length is. But set me to measure the coast of Britain with a rod, and then with a rod half as long, and half as long again, and the number I arrive at never settles: the shorter my rod, the more coast there is, and no end to it. That line is no more in my power than the weather is. Yet it is length I'm measuring, and length is your subject. So one of three things. Either your rule that we have science only of what we make is false. Or geometry is not the certain thing you keep calling it. Or this coast is not a geometrical object at all — and I want to hear you say which, and on what ground. And before you answer: you yourself make a line by the motion of a point, so it seems a line drawn with a shaking hand and a line drawn with a steady one are equally lines, and then the certainty was never in the drawing, only in what you've named. Which would make geometry a science of words.
