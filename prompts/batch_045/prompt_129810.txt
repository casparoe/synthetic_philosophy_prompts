Quick one, and please keep in mind my expertise more or less stops around 1958. I'm redoing my second-year Minds and Machines seminar and I want to lead with von Neumann's The Computer and the Brain, because as I've always read it it's the earliest clean statement of the brain-is-a-computer view: neurons fire all-or-none, so each one is basically a logic gate, ten billion of them wired together just is a stored-program machine in wetware, and you can even do his back-of-the-envelope bit with the timings — pulses are milisecond-scale, but you recognize a face in a few tenths of a second, so there's only room for on the order of a hundred sequential steps (I'd have to go back and check the exact figure), which is why the horsepower has to come from doing an enormous number of things side by side. He even makes a fuss about the brain doing arithmetic with only a couple of significant digits where our machines carry many more, but I've always filed that as a detail about implementation rather than about the architecture.

The bit I can never make tidy is the ending. He suddenly insists that the brain's real language — the primary one, with mathematics at best a secondary language built on top of it — is not the language of logic and arithmetic we use to describe our own machines, and that we have essentially no idea what that primary code is. Every time I teach it I end up saying something woolly like "so von Neumann thinks there's something about the mind that resists formalization," which either makes him a mysterian or makes him a forerunner of the Penrose/Gödel crowd, and both of those feel badly wrong. Its not as if he was ever anything but a mechanist: this is the man who worked out how you get reliable behavior out of unreliable components, statistical coding and redundancy and all that, which is exactly the kind of trick you'd expect the brain to need if it's just a noisy parallel machine.

So what do I actually say to a room of twenty-year-olds? I've been toying with two framings and I can't decide. One: he's an honorary connectionist avant la lettre — the point of all the language stuff is that at the neural level there is no symbolic code, no syntax over representational items, only statistical dynamics, and that is precisely why our mathematical vocabulary doesn't fit the thing. Two: he's only making a dated, historical point, that neuroscience in the mid-fifties just wasn't mathematized yet, so once the right equations come along the tension dissolves on its own. Which of those is closer to what he's actually doing, or is there a third reading I'm missing? I'd rather not stand up in front of them and say he changed his mind halfway through an unfinished book, but right now that's the only story in which the first half and the last chapter aren't fighting each other.
