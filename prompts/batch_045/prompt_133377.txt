I am a backend engineer with roughly a decade of experience, most of it on teams that implement and maintain publicly published protocol specifications. My colleagues and I spend a good deal of our time arguing about interpretation: whether an implementation should do what the text of a specification literally says or what its authors were plainly trying to achieve; how much weight the design discussion, the changelog, and the reference implementation should carry when the text is ambiguous; and what to do when the text is not merely ambiguous but wrong. I know the vocabulary of specifications, versioning, deprecation, bug-for-bug compatibility, undefined behaviour, and reliance on accidental implementation details well enough that you need not explain any of it back to me. I have no training in law or philosophy.

I would like the debate about statutory interpretation — textualism against purposivism, together with the apparatus surrounding them: the reasonable reader, ordinary meaning as against terms of art, the canons of construction, the absurdity exception, legislative history, scrivener's errors — restated as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use: a specification and its released version, the reference implementation, commit messages and design discussions, deprecation and backwards compatibility, the position that the code is the specification, semantic versioning, and the difference between a consumer of an interface and its maintainer.

Please test the translation against two cases of my own construction, telling me what each camp does with each and what that becomes in my terms.

Case A, a drafting failure: a statute's penalty provision cross-refers to the offence described in subsection (c), but a renumbering late in the drafting process moved that offence to subsection (e), so that the cross-reference now picks up a subsection describing conduct everyone agrees was meant to carry a minor penalty, and a literal reading attaches a fifteen-year mandatory minimum to it. In my world this is a dangling cross-reference in a specification whose intended target is unmistakable from the commit history and the reference implementation — except that some implementers have followed the broken pointer for years and built on it.

Case B, unforeseen technology: a consumer-protection provision of my own devising, which reads: "No creditor shall accelerate a loan unless written notice has been sent by first-class mail to the debtor's last known address." A lender sends the notice by authenticated email to an address the debtor uses daily; the debtor concedes receiving it the same day. The text is violated and its evident purpose is satisfied, and the debtor's argument is that compliance with the specified form is itself part of what the statute was for. In my world this is a specification requirement that survives because it was the outcome of a negotiation between parties with different interests rather than because anyone thought it optimal.
