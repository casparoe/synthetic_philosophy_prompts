For the past year I've been responsible for scheduling unannounced safety inspections at the plant where I work, and because I wanted the scheme to be fair rather than merely arbitrary, I made the mechanism public: the inspection falls on exactly one of Monday to Friday, the day is drawn from a distribution I publish in advance, and I announce in advance that on the morning of the inspection nobody's confidence that today is the day will be above some fixed threshold. A colleague sent me the literature on the surprise exam paradox and said what I was attempting had been shown to be impossible.

Working it out myself, I get a narrower result: on the last day that still carries positive probability, the conditional probability of an inspection that morning is one, and setting that day's probability to zero only moves the argument back a day, so no publicly known draw over a fixed finite window can guarantee my announcement. And the argument needs only that final step, not the full backward induction, and it evaporates if I drop the fixed finite window: with a constant daily probability the morning-of confidence stays flat below any threshold above it, and the inspection still happens eventually, with probability one.

Reading into the literature to see whether any of this settles anything, I found what looks like a decades-long stalemate. One camp reads surprise epistemically and locates the fallacy in the induction; another holds that a self-referential announcement like mine is void or that its truth is something the addressees cannot believe; a third holds that it is straightforwardly true and that the induction simply fails. My hypothesis is that these are not rival answers to a single question: the mechanism question, whether a public procedure can satisfy such an announcement, seems to me to have a clean negative answer of the kind sketched above, while the epistemic question, whether one can know or rationally believe a proposition whose truth appears to depend on one's not knowing it, is where the genuine dispute lives, and it bottoms out in differing intuitions about the reach of self-referential knowledge claims that neither side can argue for.

Is that the right way to carve it, or is one camp already answering the other's question, and does a result about mechanisms even bear on the epistemic version? I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, verbal, a clash over what counts as a solution, or a real unresolved question about self-reference, and given that diagnosis an honest account of what would count as progress, or of why none should be expected.
