I'm a retired geophysicist -- forty years of seismic inversion, which is a fancy way of saying I got paid to squeeze signal out of noisy data -- and since I packed it in I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the local library. We got onto Pascal's mugging last month and I came away unsatisfied, so I've been chewing on a fix of my own, and I want your honest number on whether it works.

Quick restatement so we're on the same page: a stranger offers you a deal -- hand over five dollars or he'll use his magic powers to create, or torture, or fail to save, some astronomically large number of people. The number is big enough that even a preposterously tiny credence in his story makes the expected value of paying come out positive. None of the answers I've seen seem right to me: "cap the utility function" feels like bolting a constant onto the theory purely to kill the counterexample, and "just ignore very small probabilities" never says where the line goes or why it lands where it does.

Here's what I've been playing with, call it leverage accounting. The mugger's real advantage isn't the size of the number, it's the gap between the size of the number and the cost of naming it. Ten-to-the-forty lives takes a hundred and thirty-odd bits just to write down; three-arrows-up-three takes maybe fifty, if you've already got up-arrow notation. Now suppose you weight a hypothesis by two-to-the-minus-its-description-length, the way a Solomonoff-style prior does. Then a hypothesis that promises V and costs K bits to state contributes something like V over 2-to-the-K to the expected value. If V has to be spelled out digit by digit, K is about log V and the contribution is order one -- somewhere between a hundredth and a hundred units of value, not a googol -- and paying five dollars stops being forced. The mugger only wins if he can name an astronomical V in a handful of bits, which is exactly what the up-arrow numbers let him do. So my claim is that the real target is that gap -- the leverage, if you want a word for it -- and that any solution worth having has to close it, rather than capping utility or throwing away small probabilities.

The proposition I want your credence on: leverage accounting, or something in that neighborhood, is a better response to Pascal's mugging than bounding the utility function or refusing to act on tiny probabilities -- where "better" means it kills the mugging without being bolted on just to kill the mugging, and without also killing the low-probability-high-stakes reasoning we don't want to lose (spending on existential risk, arguing about whether some experiment is safe, buying the damned seatbelt).

Give me a number, and decompose it. The terms I'd want, at minimum:

- that a fix is needed at all: that straight expected-value maximization with an unbounded utility function really does tell you to pay, and that this is a genuine problem rather than a feature;
- that simplicity-weighted, description-length priors are the right thing to be using in the first place, independent of whether they help here;
- that the move actually defuses the ordinary case, where the payoff is a number a physicist could name -- 10^40, the number of Planck volumes in the observable universe, whatever you like;
- that it also defuses the ugly case, where the payoff is hyper-compressible -- 3↑↑↑3 and friends. This is the one I think is weakest;
- that it doesn't overreach, i.e. that it leaves ordinary small-probability-big-stakes reasoning more or less intact;
- and separately, how much credence you put on the real work being done by something else altogether -- the thought that for every hypothesis on which paying helps there's a comparably simple one on which paying hurts, so the terms roughly cancel, or whatever else you think belongs.

Put a number and one line of justification on each, show me how they combine into your headline figure, and then tell me which single term you're least confident about and what specific consideration would move it most.

For calibration: I'm at about 0.4 that this is the right place to look, mostly because I havn't been able to see how leverage accounting handles the up-arrow case. A friend in the group is at about 0.8, on the grounds that any sane epistemics has to penalize hypothesis complexity anyway and the mugging is just where that shows up. Tell me where you think our disagreement actually lives -- which sub-question are we most likely answering differently?

And please don't give me a reading list or tell me who to go look up. I'm not going to chase references, and I'd definately rather have your reasoning than someone elses.
