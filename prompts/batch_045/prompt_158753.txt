Please adopt the role of a professional epistemologist who has published on the semantics and pragmatics of knowledge attributions, and think the problem through carefully before writing — I'd rather have three half-worked sketches and an honest diagnosis than one polished case that secretly cheats. I'm assembling a handout for a graduate seminar on the semantics of "knows," and I've run into a design problem. The standard bank-style pairs of cases (low stakes versus high stakes, evidence held fixed) strike me as evidentially degenerate: in virtually every ordinary version, the subject whose practical stakes are high is also being evaluated by an attributor who is himself in a demanding context, usually because he is deliberating about the very same practical question. So the data are equally well predicted by attributor-contextualism (the truth of a knowledge attribution varies with how demanding the standards are in the attributor's context — Lewis and DeRose are the usual names attached to this) and by subject-sensitive pragmatic encroachment (whether a subject knows depends on what is at stake for that subject — the interest-relative invariantism usually associated with Stanley). The point of the handout is to break that tie, so what I need is a pair of cases in which the two variables come apart. That purpose is what fixes what counts as cheating here: I can't use a scenario that stipulates the verdicts by having characters say "she knows" or "she doesn't really know," or one that bakes the outcome in by describing her epistemic position in loaded terms. The verdicts have to fall out of the two theories applied to a scenario described in practically and epistemically neutral language.

What I want, concretely: two short and realistic cases — a paragraph each, ordinary life, no lotteries, no skeptical scenarios, no fake barns, no science fiction — such that (i) the subject's evidence and the reliability of the method by which she formed her belief are held fixed across the two; (ii) in Case A the stakes for the subject are high while the attributor's conversational context is lax (he asks merely out of idle curiosity, or for some purpose for which rough-and-ready standards are good enough), so that encroachment says she does not know while contextualism says she does; and (iii) in Case B the stakes for the subject are trivial while the attributor's context is demanding (he must risk something significant on the truth of her belief, though she herself has nothing riding on it), so that the two theories swap verdicts. Two further constraints, both meant to block easy escapes: the subject must herself be aware of her own stakes in each case, since I don't want the puzzle dissolved by an unbeknownst-to-her maneuver; and the attributor's heightened standard must not be a matter of how much evidence he demands she have gathered, but of how well her actual belief must track the truth, since otherwise both theories collapse into a dispute about justification. After each case, walk through the verdict each theory gives and the reason for it, name the single structural feature doing the work, and show what happens if that feature is removed — my expectation is that the pair then collapses back into an ordinary bank-style pair, but demonstrate it. If after brainstorming you conclude that a clean double dissociation can't be built because some third variable is always doing the work, say so and explain which variable and why, rather than forcing a case.

Close with a section on how a defender of whichever view comes out worse would attack your cases — for instance by arguing that stakes for the subject only ever matter by being salient in the attributor's context, or that apparent shifts in attributor standards are really facts about assertion, implicature, or what counts as a useful contribution to a conversation rather than about the truth conditions of knowledge claims — and say whether you find the attack persuasive. Throughout, please separate what is settled from what is contested: mark which claims are definitional or orthodoxy (each view's core commitment, the shape of the standard bank data), which are genuinely disputed among people who work on this (whether pragmatic encroachment is true at all, whether contextualism is best read as a semantic or a pragmatic thesis, whether the two views are really rivals rather than descriptions of the same phenomenon under different descriptions), and where your own judgment is doing the work. Don't present a contested view as the consensus, but don't hedge so comprehensively that I can't tell what you actually think.
