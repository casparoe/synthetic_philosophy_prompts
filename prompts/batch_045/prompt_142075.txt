I sit on a university committee that is drafting a policy on "cognitive prosthetics" — the wearables and cloud services that researchers here use to record, store, and cue back material they would otherwise have to carry in their heads. Two cases prompted the exercise. The first is a lab-wide annotation ledger: about a dozen people have added to it continuously for roughly ten years, it is the only record of why certain protocols were changed, no individual member can reconstruct more than a fragment of it unaided, and it is now being migrated off a departmental server. One member has objected that this is not a question of shared property at all but of erasing part of her memory. The second is a device that several people here wear, which captures conversation and replays relevant fragments at moments it selects itself; you cannot query it deliberately, you usually cannot tell when it has misfired, and yet long-term users report that without it they feel as though their recall has been cut in half. The accommodations office has also asked us for a definition, because students have begun asking whether being made to surrender such a device before an exam is like being made to surrender their glasses, or like being forbidden a calculator.

A colleague on the committee who has read some of the philosophical literature says that everything here turns on the extended mind thesis, and he has offered an argument I cannot quite fault. If the thesis is right, he says, then what makes something part of your mind is the functional role it plays rather than whether it sits inside your skull; but then there is no principled line to be drawn between the notebook that the amnesiac in the famous example uses to find his way around the city and my phone, my search engine, the ledger, and the library catalogue, so the thesis is either a trivial remark about how much we lean on tools or an absurd one about where our minds are located. And if we take the absurd branch seriously, as he thinks intellectual honesty requires, then deleting the ledger or confiscating a recording device is not really a dispute about property, academic integrity, or accommodation: it is damage to someone's cognition, and it ought to be regulated the way we regulate damage to someone's brain. I am the one who has to write the memo that either adopts this argument or explains why it fails, and I would rather not build a policy on a bad inference.

My own suspicion is that the slippage lies in the move from "this resource partly constitutes a cognitive process" to "this resource is part of a person, and damaging it is damaging them" — but I cannot say exactly where that step goes wrong, partly because I am not sure the first claim is the interesting one, and partly because I cannot tell whether the standard remedies people offer (the checklist of reliability, trust, easy accessibility, and automatic endorsement; the insistence that being causally coupled to something is not the same as being partly constituted by it) are real constraints on the thesis or merely ad hoc manoeuvres for avoiding conclusions the authors find embarrassing. Please reply in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no preamble and no closing sentence: identify what is confused in the argument as I have reconstructed it, or, if you think it is sound after all, say which premise carries the weight and how I should defend it in the memo.
