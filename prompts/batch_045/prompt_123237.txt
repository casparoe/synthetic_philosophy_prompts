I have now taught the truth unit of my graduate seminar on classical American pragmatism four times, and every year the discussion arrives at the same place and stops. I would like to know what kind of impasse this is before I teach it a fifth time.

The dialectic is always the same. As I read them: Peirce identifies truth with the opinion that inquiry would settle on if it were pushed far enough — the belief that investigation, indefinitely extended, is fated to reach and that nothing further would dislodge. James ties truth instead to the practical difference that holding a belief makes: on his account, what makes a belief true is that acting on it is what pays for the person who holds it. The Peirceans in the room then say James has licensed wishful thinking, since a soothing falsehood can be more useful than a hard fact. The Jamesians reply that Peirce has offered a truth no one could ever recognize, apply, or be guided by, and that a constraint with no bearing on any actual deliberator is not a notion of truth at all. The Peirceans answer that James has confused the test of truth with its nature. At that point each side has said what it believes, neither finds the other's objection new, and everyone goes home.

My own hypothesis — which I offer as a hypothesis — is that at least three disagreements are being run together under one name: a difference in subject matter (Peirce assessing a practice of inquiry in the long run, James assessing an individual belief in a concrete deliberative situation); a substantive disagreement about whether truth must be independent of what any inquirer would ever accept; and a background modal commitment — that the world is such that inquiry would converge — which Peirce needs and James does not. I also suspect the stock examples in the literature (religious belief, perceptual error, claims about the deep past) are too easy: each lets both accounts produce the same verdict in different vocabularies, which may be why nothing is ever decided.

To force the issue I have started using three harder cases. First, a proposition about a region of spacetime from which no signal can ever reach us, or about what happened in a room whose every trace was destroyed an hour later: something no possible investigation could settle, where it still seems there is a fact of the matter. Second, a depositor wondering whether her bank is solvent, where the only way to check is to withdraw her money and general withdrawal destroys the solvency she was asking about, so that the investigation moves the very truth value it was investigating. Third, an inquiry that simply stops — the inquirers die out or lose interest — so that the last opinion reached is not the limit of anything but merely last.

Please diagnose the standoff: is it verbal, substantive, a collision of different questions under one heading, a hidden empirical or modal commitment doing the work, or an ill-posed question wearing a well-posed one's clothes? Tell me which, if any, of my three cases actually discriminates between the two accounts — that is, which one forces a Peircean and a Jamesian into conflicting verdicts rather than into two descriptions of the same verdict — and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a formal result about ideal-limit operators, a principled separation of the nature of truth from its tests, an argument that one of the two theories is really a theory of warranted assertibility, or something I have not thought of.

I want a committed answer rather than a survey: defend one diagnosis, rank the three cases by the diagnostic work they do, and be explicit about which part of your diagnosis you take to be least secure and which of my framing assumptions you think is a mistake. If you conclude that the disagreement is genuine, substantive, and unlikely ever to be settled, argue for that as a diagnosis instead of leaving it as a shrug.
