Here's my opening. Two astronomers, Ravi and Ines, work on the same survey. In 2003 the telescope logs an odd dip in a star's light curve; both of them look at the number that week. Ines keeps careful notebooks and still remembers it. Ravi files it and forgets it completely — by 2019 he couldn't tell you the dip was ever there. In 2019 both of them independently arrive at the same new theory T, and both work out that T entials the dip. Same evidence gathered over the same years, same theory, same derivation. But on the counterfactual fix you presumably like — take the evidence back out of the belief corpus, see how much T gains against what's left — only Ravi gets a real confirmation boost, and how big it is depends on how thoroughly he's forgotten. Call it the laundering objection: on your account, losing the archive is a confirmation-generating technology. Two people in identical evidential situations get different support for the same theory because of a fact about one of thems memory hygiene, which is about as psychological as confirmation can get. And don't grab the 'what you really learn is the conditional, not the evidence' move without telling me why it doesn't rescue you: Ines sees the entailment just as clearly as Ravi does, so if the conditional is what's learned, they should get the same bump, and we're right back to forgetting doing all the work. So — where exactly does the laundering objection go wrong?
