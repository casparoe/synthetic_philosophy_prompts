hey — assume the role of a professional philosopher who works on measurement and social epistemology (or, if that's easier, an econometrician who has thought way too much about Goodhart's law). please think this through carefully: brainstorm two or three candidate scenarios first, tell me in a couple of lines why you're dropping each of the runners-up, and only then commit to the one you're going to build. show your work on the design, not just the finished product.

why I'm asking: I recently joined the board of a small housing nonprofit, and we just landed our first outcomes-based grant, which means from next quarter a chunk of our funding tracks a number on a funder dashboard. every board meeting now ends with somebody saying "well, Goodhart's law" as a conversation-stopper — and what they mean by it is "people cheat when you pay them by the numbers." I think that's the shallow version and I want to convince them the problem is structural, not moral. so I need a case where nobody cheats.

build me a thought experiment to these specs:

1. one paragraph, realistic, ordinary institution — a clinic, a school, a city agency, a QA team, a housing org like mine. no sci-fi, no superintelligent optimizer. if there's software doing the optimizing, it can be no cleverer than a shrewd human analyst.

2. the no-vice condition: nobody in the story lies, cheats, misreports, or knowingly games anything. every person is acting in good faith and genuinely trying to improve the real target. if your case needs someone to fudge a number, the case has failed.

3. the proxy has to be the genuinely best available measure of the target at the start, and it has to be causally downstream of the target, not a mere correlate — so the easy escape route of "the correlation was always spurious" is closed off.

4. the dashboard never lies: the proxy is measured correctly for the whole story and the reported number keeps improving or at least stays flat. the metric and the target come apart, but there's no point at which anyone is misreporting the metric.

5. yet sustained good-faith optimization of the proxy makes the actual target worse.

6. the trajectory has to be non-monotonic: the target really does improve first — say for a year or two — and degrades only later. I want the reader to feel how seductive the dashboard is before it turns. say explicitly what changes between phase one and phase two.

7. it can't be a case where the fix is obviously "just also measure X." there should be a moment where a thoughtful reader thinks "well obviously you should track X as well," and the scenario needs a concrete reason X isn't available, isn't comparable over time, or would itself get absorbed into the optimization.

8. the failure should be collective/structural: no single individual in the story could have averted it by working harder, being more honest, or blowing the whistle.

(I have a half-baked version of this involving a school reading-comprehension test — please don't just hand that one back to me. if you end up anywhere near it, it has to clear an extra bar: tell me what makes your version not the standard one.)

then, after the scenario:

- name the mechanism that's doing the work, and separate at least three candidates: the extremal / regression-to-the-tail version (pushing to the top of a distribution selects increasingly on noise and on causes other than the target), the causal version (acting on the proxy breaks or reverses the path that made it informative), and the version where the relationship itself moves because the people being measured changed how they behave once the measurement mattered. tell me which one your case is, which one you deliberately built it not to be, and why that exclusion is what makes it bite. also say honestly where the boundary between those labels gets blurry here and which of the usual names you think actually fits.

- collapse test: remove or dial down the one feature you claim is doing the work, and show the case degenerating into something boring — either no Goodhart at all, or exactly the cheating story my board already believes.

- steelman the skeptic: give the strongest version of "this isn't a deep problem, it's just a badly chosen proxy that better auditing would have caught," and then say why it doesn't defuse your case.

- finally, two sentences on what I should actually do differently given that turning down the grant isn't an option.

keep the whole thing under about 900 words, and please don't pad it with citations or reading suggestions — I want your own construction, not a literature survey.
