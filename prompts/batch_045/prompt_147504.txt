I've just spent a long evening arguing with a friend about the Clifford–James exchange, and I've since written up my side of it as a draft that I'm considering developing into a paper for a generalist philosophy journal. She is a hospital physician, professionally steeped in evidence-based medicine, and her position was blunt: James's essay is a licence for motivated reasoning dressed up as a sermon, and everything worth keeping in it is already captured by the ordinary duty to proportion belief to the evidence and then to act, when one must act, on the balance of risks. I think she is wrong, but I could not say why in a way that satisfied either of us. The draft below is my attempt, and I would be grateful for comments on it.

Doxastic Credit

1. The shared frame. Both parties to the old dispute accept a frame I want to reject. There is a proposition P, a body of evidence E available now, and a question about whether it is permissible to believe P given E. Clifford says no whenever E is insufficient, and insists that the verdict is settled by the provenance of the belief rather than by its content or its outcome: the question of right and wrong has to do with the origin of the belief — in his words, "not what it was, but how he got it; not whether it turned out to be true or false, but whether he had a right to believe on such evidence as was before him." James replies, in effect, that for a restricted class of options — what he calls a genuine option, i.e. one "of the forced, living and momentous kind" — abstention is not the neutral posture it appears to be, since "he who refuses to embrace a unique opportunity loses the prize as surely as if he tried and failed." My friend reads this as a consequentialist intrusion into a properly evidentialist norm. My draft tries to show that the intrusion is already there in the cases themselves, and that once we see why, we can state a norm that neither side has stated.

2. From beliefs to exposure. The first move is to change the unit of evaluation. Belief is not directly voluntary, but the management of one's evidential situation is. Clifford's own case supports this: the shipowner is not indicted for choosing to believe the vessel sound. He is indicted for arranging things — declining the overhaul, soothing his doubts, dismissing "ungenerous suspicions" — so that the discomforting evidence would not reach him. The wrong sits one step upstream of the belief, in what I shall call doxastic exposure: the set of choices by which an agent arranges what she will be in a position to come to believe. Read this way, James's cases are structurally identical. In his example of the forced offer of regard — "either love me or hate me" — what is chosen is not a belief but an entry into a situation in which a certain belief becomes available to you and to the other person. So the ethics of belief is a special case of an ethics of exposure, and the interesting norms attach to what we do to our own future evidence.

3. Fixed-point propositions. Now the class that I think breaks the deadlock. Call a proposition evidentially self-dependent, or a fixed point, when the evidence for it at a time depends on the distribution of belief in it at that time: that the strike will hold, that the currency will keep its value, that the overture of trust will be reciprocated, that a norm will be upheld here, that the venture will find backers. For such propositions no individual can satisfy Clifford's rule, because the evidence sufficient for my believing P is partly constituted by other people's believing P. If each of us defers to the evidence first, the fixed point the community lands on is the pessimistic one: the evidence never arrives for anyone, and not because anyone has reasoned badly. There is a second-order version as well — propositions about whether others will honour the rule itself — which is why the problem cannot be solved by adding a clause about coordination once it is noticed.

4. Why the Cliffordian cannot simply absorb this. The natural reply, and the one my friend groped toward, is that Clifford's rule should be conditionalised: believe on insufficient evidence only when doing so is part of the pattern that makes the evidence. I think that reply concedes the substance. A conditionalised rule justifies the belief by its downstream effects, which is precisely what Clifford's origin-focused verdict forbids; a belief with a defective provenance cannot be rehabilitated by what it later produces. So the Cliffordian can accommodate fixed-point propositions only by ceasing to be a Cliffordian about them. If I am right, the dispute is not really about whether consequences can ever matter; it is about how large the fixed-point class is.

5. The temporal dilemma, and doxastic credit. For fixed-point propositions the belief is at best retrospectively vindicated, and that generates a dilemma. Either warrant must be truth-indicative at the time of believing, in which case vindication arrives too late and the belief was unjustified exactly when it mattered; or warrant can be discharged after the fact, in which case we owe an account of how. I take the second horn, and propose to cash it in as a norm rather than as a prediction. Call it doxastic credit: adopting a belief on insufficient evidence is permissible only if (i) the proposition is a fixed-point proposition, or the option is genuine in James's sense; (ii) the agent undertakes, at the time of adopting the belief, to discharge the evidential deficit — a plan of inquiry, a disposition to revise, and an acceptance of the costs of being wrong; and (iii) the belief does not itself reduce the agent's future evidential exposure. Condition (iii) is what separates this from wishful thinking and is why the shipowner gets no credit: his trust in Providence was a device for not looking.

6. Two wrinkles I cannot settle. First, the permission is indexed to what I call evidential leverage — how much the agent's believing contributes to producing the evidence — and not to how much she wants the proposition to be true. That is my answer to the charge of motivated reasoning, but it inherits a measurement problem. Second, credit is self-liquidating: once the deficit is discharged, the belief no longer needs the credit that made it permissible, so the permission is conditional on something that removes the need for it. I do not know whether that is a defect or the point.

What I would like from you: comments on the argument, not a rewrite of it, and no reading suggestions — I can assemble those myself, and I would rather spend the space on the objections. I am most interested in four things. (a) Does the fixed-point argument actually damage Clifford's rule, or does it only identify a class of cases that a conditionalised Cliffordian can absorb without conceding anything of substance? (b) Does doxastic credit collapse into one of the two positions it is meant to sit between, or into some more familiar view under a new name? (c) Is the exposure reframing stable, or does it regress — are the choices that shape my exposure themselves so belief-dependent that I have merely relocated the voluntariness problem one step back? (d) Is anything here genuinely new, or have I restated a known point less elegantly? Please also tell me how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
