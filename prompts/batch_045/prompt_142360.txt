Here's roughly how it went (I'm collapsing a lot of interruptions):

1. Rina: Before Darwin, the argument from design looked unanswerable — look how perfectly the eye fits seeing, only a mind could produce that. Darwin showed the same fit can be produced by a completely mindless process, differential survival over lots of variation. No comprehender needed anywhere in the loop. Turing did the same thing for reasoning: we assumed you had to understand arithmetic to do arithmetic, and he showed that a device shuffling symbols by dumb rules can in principle do any of it. She said Dennett calls this a strange inversion of reasoning — the competence is real but the comprehension never has to enter the story. So: when you look at the model producing a working merge sort and feel that there can't be any real understanding there, you're making the sameinference Darwin and Turing killed.

2. Kwame: The inversion works for organisms and says nothing about artifacts. Organisms have the special property that nobody understood them into existence, and that's the whole point of Darwin's argument — it removes the need for a prior comprehender. But the model was built by engineers who understand code and language perfectly well, on an objective they chose. Its competence is borrowed, downstream of real comprehension. So the inversion gives you no reason at all to think there's anything home grown in there.

3. Rina: Training isn't design. Nobody writes the weights, and nobody specified anything close to the behavior we actually see. The engineers write an objective and an architecture and then run a search process that looks a lot more like selection than like a blueprint. No one at the lab can tell you why it fixes this bug and not that one. So if comprehension is what's supposed to explain the competence, there's no comprehension anywhere in the loop that covers the specific competence — the engineers' understanding doesn't reach it and the training process has none. The inversion applies one level up: design without the designer's understanding.

4. Kwame: Even granting that, the inversion is a negative move, not a positive one. What Darwin and Turing show is that you don't need to posit comprehension to explain where the competence came from. That's a claim about what you're allowed to leave out of an explanation. It says nothing about whether the thing that has the competence also has comprehension, and it can't be used as evidence that it does. The frog's fly-catching has, what's the term, a free-floating rationale — the frog doesn't need to know why it does it — but the frog still sees flies. So at best the inversion tells you to stop treating your gut reaction as decisive; it doesn't tell you the model understands.

5. Rina: Right, but it cuts both ways, which is her actual point. It also kills the inference from the mechanism is mechanical to therefore no understanding, because that's the exact inference the inversion was supposed to destroy. After Darwin and Turing the burden is symmetric: you need some positive grounds for saying the thing that can do the task lacks something, not just a story about how it's all statistics underneath.

6. Kwame: Here's the asymmetry he thinks actually does the work — call it the stakes test. For an evolved creature, competence is anchored: there is something for whom it matters whether the competence is correct, namely the creature, which gets eaten if its fly detection fails. The loop is closed in the world, continuously, and the competence has to pay for itself. For the model nothing is at stake. It's optimized to predict what people who do have stakes wrote next. So its apparent aboutness is derivative — it's downstream of a whole population of real comprehenders whose traces are in the data. Organisms are the original and the model is a kind of echo of them.

7. Rina: The stakes test proves too much, and also the stakes are just on a longer leash. Longer leash: when the model is actually working, the loop does close — the code compiles or it doesn't, our test suite passes or it doesn't, and we're the ones who care. Its behavior gets selected by something in the world, the way a bacterium's does, without the bacterium knowing anything. Proves too much: a shut-in who reads everything and never acts, or any reasoning system we deliberately keep in a sandbox, would fail the test, and we wouldn't want to conclude from that alone. And evolution doesn't need anything to be at stake in any deep sense, just differential success over variants — if you want someone it matters to in the picture, that's a property of the process, not a property of whatever understanding the individual has.
