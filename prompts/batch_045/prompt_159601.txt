I'm sitting in on a law-and-politics reading group this term, and last session two members ended up talking past each other over an analogy that the whole campaign-finance argument seems to run on. Marisol, who has worked on state disclosure-and-caps legislation, keeps saying that money is not speech but a megaphone: the dollars are not the message, they are the amplification, and we regulate amplification all the time (noise ordinances, decibel caps, limits on how many leaflets you may push through a door, time limits at a podium), so a ceiling on spending restricts the volume of political speech rather than speech itself and should be as unremarkable as a volume limit. Dev, who thinks the doctrine is roughly right, says the distinction collapses: every act of speaking at scale requires purchased inputs — paper, presses, airtime, staff, postage, a hall — so singling out money as the one input the state may cap is just singling out speech, and if a ban on buying airtime would burden speech then a cap on buying it burdens speech too; on his view that is why the cases from the 1976 decision striking down expenditure limits through the 2010 decision on corporate independent expenditures treat spending as protected, and why caps should face the most demanding scrutiny rather than being waved through as mere volume control.

I raised three things at the end and both of them waved me off, so I would like them audited properly rather than dismissed. First: a megaphone only makes its owner louder, whereas money can make other people quieter — preemptively buying up a district's available ad inventory, funding litigation or regulatory complaints aimed at a speaker, hiring a firm to flood a public-comment process — so the megaphone picture may understate what money is for, and if so it is not clear which side that helps. Second: money is fungible, transferable and aggregable in a way that convictions are not; I can hand you my money so that you speak, but not my sincerity, and the size of the amplification tracks my wealth rather than how much I care, so on the identity version of the claim the quantity of one's speech becomes an alienable commodity and the volume knob comes unhooked from the speaker's intensity. Third: the exchange rate between dollars and impressions varies by market and drifts as advertising gets cheaper, so a fixed dollar cap is not really a decibel limit — it is a limit that silently loosens every year — which suggests that what is actually up for regulation is the conversion rate between wealth and audibility, not volume. I also want to know what either picture says about lobbying, where the money mostly pays for people: drafting model legislation, supplying a time-poor legislator with research and language, maintaining a presence — that looks less like broadcasting a message and more like subsidizing an official's staff, i.e. buying labour rather than speaking. And I want to know whose speech an independent expenditure is supposed to be, given that the donor did not write the message and the candidate did not choose the volume: is that hiring an agent to speak for you, donating paper to a pamphleteer, buying access, or none of these?

What I would like from you is an audit rather than a verdict. Give me the correspondence table explicitly — on the speech side the speaker, the message, the audience, the medium, the volume, the answer; on the spending side whatever plays each role, including where nothing does. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the work the analogy is being made to do from the ones that are merely decorative, and say for each side whether its conclusion survives once its own disanalogies are on the table: does Marisol get to say that caps are ordinary volume limits, or does the fungibility and silence-buying problem push her toward a different rationale entirely; does Dev get to say that any limit on spending is a limit on speech, given that page limits, word limits and time limits on speech are routine and given that the counterspeech reply he relies on assumes answering is cheap in a way money makes false? Say what each analogy quietly imports — a neutral baseline volume that regulation restores, on one side; an input-output package in which quantity of speech is immune to quantitative regulation, on the other — and whether the real load is being carried by an unstated premise about democratic equality instead. Finally, if there is a better analogy available, name it and state its own breaking points as well: money as a ration coupon for attention, as a printing press, as a licence to broadcast, as a proxy speaker, or something else.
