I should say at the outset that I have no academic background in this. I left school at eighteen and have spent about two years reading on my own — mostly popular books, and the parts of the papers I can follow — about how much of what we think we know about moral judgment comes from a very thin slice of humanity. The acronym I keep meeting is WEIRD: Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic. The complaint is that when the same questions get put to people in small-scale societies, the answers sometimes come out differently, and that the differences track things like whether people herd animals on common land or farm separate plots, whether they sell their labour or not, whether they answer to distant officials. I found that convincing, and I have been trying to work out how convincing it actually is. Here is the argument I have ended up with.

(1) Whatever else can be said in support of a moral judgment, the support eventually stops at judgments that strike someone as correct. There is no further court of appeal that is not itself someone's sense of what is obvious.

(2) If the fact that a judgment strikes you as obvious is explained by causes that have nothing to do with whether the judgment is true, then that fact stops counting as evidence for it.

(3) The cross-cultural work shows that which moral judgments strike people as obvious varies with features of their social and economic life — subsistence, markets, distance from authority — that are not plausibly tracking any moral truth.

(4) So the defeater in (2) applies to everyone's moral intuitions, including mine and including those of whoever presses this argument, and therefore to every moral judgment that rests on them.

(5) When an outsider's judgment conflicts with a community's settled judgment about how that community should arrange its own life, both sides' evidence has been defeated in the same way, so neither can claim to be in the right.

(6) A decision still has to be made. Where two sides are equally without moral warrant, the tie should be broken by who will live with the consequences, and that is the community, not the outsider.

(7) Therefore an outsider should defer to a community's settled judgment about its own internal arrangements, however strongly they disagree, and should not act against it.

I cannot accept (7), and I will stipulate the whole case so that nothing turns on any fact about the world.

There is a valley of about forty villages with no road in. Every household sends its youngest daughters, from about nine, to work in households a day or two away; they regard this as a plain duty, and the arrangement is what keeps the households solvent. The costs — ill health, no schooling, the narrowing of a life — fall almost entirely on the daughters. The benefits accrue to the households and to the elders who speak for the valley. The only evidence anyone has about what the daughters want is what they say when asked in front of their families, and they say they accept it. An outside health service runs a clinic in the valley. It can do one thing and nothing else: tell any girl who comes in, privately, that there is a hostel elsewhere that will take her, and help her reach it if she asks. The elders call this corrupting the daughters' sense of duty and have asked the clinic not to do it. I am willing to grant that the elders are right that the arrangement cannot survive if the daughters stop believing it is a duty, and that this is a genuine cost to them. It still seems to me wrong to defer.

So something in my argument is broken, and I would like to know what. Please do not just tell me the conclusion is wrong; I know that. I want the step, why that step looked sound enough to get past me, and what it costs to give it up.

Three further things. First, I have noticed that (7) is itself a moral judgment, so if (4) is true then (7) has no warrant either — but that observation only leaves me with nothing to think, so it is not the answer I am looking for on its own. Second, of the premises, the ones I would most hate to surrender are (1) and (3), because those are what convinced me in the first place; I would also rather not lose (6), since without it I do not see how the problem ever becomes a practical one. Third, I am unsure what to do with this detail: if the same daughters, asked privately by a nurse rather than in front of their families, sometimes say something quite different, does that detail do legitimate work, or am I only smuggling my answer into the case? I would like to know which, and why.
