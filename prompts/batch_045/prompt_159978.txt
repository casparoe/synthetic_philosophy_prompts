I’m taking an upper-level seminar on law and political philosophy, and after a week comparing how different jurisdictions regulate adult sex work I’ve been arguing with friends from Sweden and Germany; we keep talking past each other, and I realized I don’t actually know what implicit view each legal framework is committed to. I can’t look anything up and I don’t want you to either, so please work from the rough descriptions below and do not give me citations, bibliographies, or a reading list — I’m after the conceptual reconstruction, not sources.

As I understand them, the regimes are these. The Nordic or Swedish model, first enacted in Sweden in 1999, criminalizes the purchase of sexual services and also keeps in place criminal liability for third parties who organize or profit from other people’s prostitution, such as pimps and brothel operators, while deliberately not criminalizing the person who sells her own sexual services; the official rationale is that prostitution is a form of violence against women and a manifestation of gender inequality, so the seller is treated as the exploited or victimized party and the buyer as the wrongdoer, and the point of the law is to shrink demand. By contrast, the legalization or regulation model associated with the Netherlands and Germany treats sex work as a sector of the legitimate economy: brothels and sex businesses can be licensed and operate openly, sex workers are in principle laborers with contracts, taxes, and occupational rights, and the state regulates it like other risky work through health and safety rules, registration, and, in Germany’s later federal law, obligations that include registration and mandatory condom use. Finally, New Zealand’s decriminalization model, under the Prostitution Reform Act 2003, repealed the old crimes of soliciting, brothel-keeping, and living on the earnings of prostitution and replaced them with ordinary civil and health-and-safety regulation; the Act decriminalizes prostitution while stating that it does not express moral approval of prostitution or of its use, gives sex workers a right to refuse particular clients or services, allows ordinary contract law to govern disputes with clients, and is framed around safeguarding rights, welfare, occupational health and safety, and public health.

Here is what puzzles me, and what I’d like you to reconstruct. On the one hand, the Nordic model treats the seller as victimized enough that she is not punished, but also treats her consent as insufficient to license the buyer’s act, and it criminalizes the manager even when he mainly supplies security or accounting; on the other hand, the German and New Zealand models treat her consent and labor as real enough to regulate, tax, and enforce contracts, with New Zealand even applying consumer-law remedies to clients. To sharpen the contrast, I have been using a test I call the reverse-asymmetry probe: imagine a well-paid, unionized, openly advertising sex worker who reports no pimp or trafficker and says she genuinely wants the job; the Nordic model still makes her client a criminal, while the other models treat her simply as a worker. What philosophical commitments does each practice presuppose about consent, exploitation, agency, and the proper role of the criminal law? In particular, what view of sexual versus transactional consent — or of paid sex as inherently degrading versus merely risky — does the Nordic asymmetry only make sense under, and does it secretly presuppose that sex work is categorically unlike other labor? Please reconstruct the implicit theory of each regime, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say whether its parts are consistent, for instance in how it treats sellers, buyers, and third parties; also indicate where the same surface policy is philosophically underdetermined, so that more than one theory could license it. If the Nordic model were rewritten on the labor view implicit in New Zealand or Germany, what exactly would have to change: the criminalization of clients, the criminalization of third parties, or the official disclaimer about not endorsing prostitution? Please give me a committed response rather than a survey: commit to a single reconstruction you think is most defensible for each regime, take a definite position on which model is most internally coherent and which presupposition is weakest, and give your reasons rather than just canvassing options. Be explicit about how confident you are in each interpretive claim and where you are extrapolating beyond my descriptions, and say which apparent tension dissolves on inspection and which is real. Finally, please write your answer in American English.
