My friend (she's a wildlife vet tech, I'm a backend engineer, so neither of us has any real philosophy background) has been getting into animal suffering stuff and turns out to have pretty firm opinions about wild-animal cases, so I've started keeping a list of her verdicts because I can't tell whether there's one coherent rule underneath or whether she's just going by vibes. Her calls so far: oral rabies vaccine baited out for foxes and coyotes, where the outbreak traced back to unvaccinated dogs people brought in — she's all for it. Fencing plus underpasses under a highway so deer stop getting hit — for it. A wildlife hospital that patches up individual injured animals one at a time, window-strike hawks and orphaned fawns and so on, which does literally nothing at the population level — she's for it, and she volunteers there on weekends. A city that wants to shrink a herd of extremely fat, well-fed suburban deer, either with immunocontraception darts or by hiring sharpshooters — she's against both, which surprised me because I'd of thought she'd at least like the non-lethal version. A proposal to turn a decommissioned strip mine into a big rewilded reserve — against it, with some line about how you'd be manufacturing a whole pile of lives that will mostly go badly. And basically always, for every case, she'd rather fund more research than act yet. She's also said she has no idea whether insects matter at all and doesn't want to touch anything insect-related in either direction, if that's a clue about anything.

Here's the case I actually want to put to her, and I'd rather work out what she should say than argue with her about it. There's a island nobody has really touched — no introduced predators, no human-caused mess to clean up — with a native mouse species that booms and busts: the population explodes, then crashes, and during the crash a large fraction of them spend a week or two slowly starving or freezing to death. One team wants to release a self-spreading fertility-control agent, some gene-drive-ish thing that biases inheritance so litters get smaller generation over generation, which over about ten years would cut the population by maybe 70%. No mouse alive now is made worse off, and the mice who do exist have lives roughly as good as before; there are just far fewer of them each generation, and the line might eventually wink out on that island entirely. A second team wants to do what's standard for clearing rodents off islands — poison bait spread over a few days — which by everyone's numbers prevents more starvation down the line but obviously also kills every mouse currently alive. Oh, and the mice are the main food for a native owl, so fewer mice probably means fewer owls too.

I don't so much care which of the two she picks as I do about this: what's the rule she seems to be running, what other rules fit all her earlier calls about as well, what each of those rules says about the fertility-control option and the poison option (including the "neither yet, fund a study first" answer), which of her old verdicts is the closest analog to this one, and if her track record genuinely doesn't settle it, what's the single follow-up case I should put to her that would actually split the remaining options apart?
