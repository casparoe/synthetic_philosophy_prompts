I'm in a reading group — four grad students, a postdoc, we meet in a pub and get through about ten pages a week if we're lucky — and we've been slogging through social construction stuff: race, gender, money, that whole cluster. I keep tripping over a pattern that I can't find a name for, and it's annoying me because I want to use it in a short paper and I don't want to reinvent something that already has a literature. Here's the shape of it, in three sketches. (1) A city's crime data says neighbourhood X is bad, so more patrols go to X; more patrols mean more low-level offences actually get *noticed* and written down; next quarter's data says X is worse than ever, so more patrols go to X. (2) A hospital flags certain patients as high-risk; flagged patients get more monitoring and more tests, so more things get found and recorded about them; the flag looks brilliantly predictive at review time and its thresholds get re-tuned using exactly that data. (3) Money: a bank gets a whiff of illiquidity, counterparties pull their funding, and the bank becomes illiquid — the panic that was supposedly a reaction to the fragility is what produced it. What I think is common to them: some institution sorts things into a kind, the sorting changes how *third parties* behave, that changed behaviour generates the data that gets cited as evidence that the sort was tracking something real, the criteria get re-estimated from that data, and the kind drifts to fit the effects of its own application. And then it settles. Once it's settled, if you go poking at it — run a programme to test whether the kind is really out there, or deliberately stop patrolling X to see what happens — the machinery re-estimates and you more or less end up back where you started. So the kind is at once empirically robust and entirely made, and the robustness is precisely what makes it look like it was discovered rather than built.

The thing is, I don't think this is just one of the obvious nearby things, but I can't say exactly why. My flatmate says self-fulfilling prophecy, and I think that's too thin: a self-fulfilling prophecy is a belief that makes itself true once, whereas this is a standing machine with a re-estimation rule that keeps making it true and keeps hiding that it's doing so. My economist friend says endogeneity, or reverse causality, and that feels both right and useless — it's a complaint about a regression, not a claim about a kind. The thing I've been pointed at most often is Hacking: something about looping effects and making up people. My reason for setting it aside is that in his cases, as I understand them second-hand, the loop runs *through the people classified* — they get labelled, they come to understand themselves under the label, they change, and the kind has to be revised to catch up. My cases don't need that at all. The residents, the patients, the depositors may never know they've been sorted; the loop runs through other people's behaviour and through which events end up in the dataset. But I genuinely don't know whether that's a real difference or whether the self-conception bit is inessential to Hacking and I've just read a bad summary. There's also a feature I can't decide is essential: in all three of my cases the criteria get re-described in a vocabulary that mentions no institution at all — a rate, a risk score, a measured frequency — and that's what makes the kind look like a natural one that social construction talk is somehow second-class about. Is that laundering part of the phenomenon, or a separate thing that happens to travel with it?

So: is there an established concept for this, one that isn't just the general neighbourhood of social construction? And whichever way that goes, I'd rather have the carving than the label. Specifically: (a) if there are several nearby concepts, which one fits best and what exactly does each of the others leave out; (b) is the loop-running-through-the-evidence-base rather than through the classified people's self-understanding a real distinguishing mark, or a red herring; (c) here's a case I can't sort — suppose the flagging changes *only* what gets recorded and not what actually happens, so the underlying rate is identical and yet every number still moves in the confirming direction. Same phenomenon, or a different one that deserves its own name, and if you had to pick one as the more important target for a paper, which; (d) does it matter that everybody involved can know about the loop and it still runs — the policing case has been publicly described for years and the numbers keep being used anyway; (e) what do you call the runaway version, where the loop doesn't settle but oscillates or escalates — degenerate case of the same thing, or its own animal? And if the honest answer is that there's no established term, then give me one: coin it, define it tightly enough that I can tell in/out, and give me a couple of clear positive cases and a couple of near-misses that a careless reader would lump in. Please don't just gesture at a literature or hand me names to go look up — I can't tell whether the gesture is right until somebody does the distinctions, and the distinctions are the part I actually need.
