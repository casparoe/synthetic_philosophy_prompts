**The answer I have been handed**

> The argument is not sound, though its premise is true. The equivalence results — that the Turing-computable partial functions, the general recursive functions and the λ-definable functions all coincide — really are theorems. But a theorem stating that three formal classes have the same membership shows at most that they have the same membership. Whether any of them captures effective calculability is a further question that no purely mathematical result settles: if all three formalisms happened to leave out the same intuitively computable function, they would still coincide. So the convergence of formalisms gives the thesis no deductive support. It is not nothing, though: that three quite independently motivated attempts to explicate the same informal notion landed on the same class is surprising, and surprising coincidence is the sort of thing that confirms a hypothesis.
>
> Nor is convergence the only resource. It is a theorem that the Turing-computable partial functions are exactly the functions obtainable from a small stock of initial functions by composition, primitive recursion and unbounded search — equivalently, that they form the smallest class of partial functions containing those initial functions and closed under those three operations. Now any function that is effectively calculable must be obtainable by combining steps of just these kinds: that is what it is to compute something. Hence the class of effectively calculable functions is by definition the smallest class closed under the operations that generate computations, and the Turing-computable functions are provably that class. So the thesis is not a hypothesis awaiting confirmation at all, but a corollary of the closure theorem, and this is the consideration that answers part (c): we have a proof, not merely evidence.
>
> It helps to say what the thesis is not. The mathematical thesis concerns effective calculability — what can be got out by following a procedure — and is silent about what physical systems can do. The physical Church–Turing thesis is a distinct and stronger claim: that no physical system computes a function that is not Turing-computable. The two can come apart, and it is worth being clear about how. Quantum computation already puts pressure on the physical thesis: if, as is widely expected, there are problems solvable in polynomial time by a quantum computer but not by any classical one, then the natural strengthening of the physical thesis — that no physical device can outpace a universal Turing machine by more than a polynomial factor — is false, even though the plain physical thesis and the mathematical thesis both survive it.
>
> Where the two stand or fall together is part (b), and here I think a common line is mistaken. It is often said that a physically realised hypercomputer would refute only the physical thesis and leave the mathematical one untouched. Consider a device — call it an O-drive — that takes the code of an arbitrary Turing machine together with an input, and reliably reports within one second whether that machine halts on that input. The halting function is not Turing-computable. So if O-drives are physically possible, some function that is not Turing-computable is computed by a physical system, and the physical thesis is false. But it cannot be that the mathematical thesis survives this: the mathematical thesis says simply that whatever is computable is Turing-computable, and an O-drive computes. We could use the device in place of a computation, and a notion of computability on which using it does not count is one on which the thesis is true only by stipulation. So the falsity of the physical thesis would indeed entail the falsity of the mathematical one, and the common line is a way of protecting the thesis from any possible counterexample.
>
> The upshot is that the thesis is best understood as an explication in something like Carnap's sense: a proposal to identify a vague pre-theoretic notion with a precise formal one. Explications cannot be proved, since what is being proposed is the identification itself, and they cannot be straightforwardly falsified either, since there is no observation that settles whether some function is effectively calculable. But they can be inadequate, and the thesis has survived eighty-odd years of attempts to show it inadequate. That is why it now functions as a licence to move between informal talk of algorithms and formal talk of machines, and why we are entitled to rely on it in proofs.
