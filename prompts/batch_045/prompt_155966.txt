I write a weekly column on language and mind for the science desk, and I am fact-checking a passage before I build a Sunday piece around it. The passage comes from a well-reviewed trade book on language that came out last year, which I would rather not name in print, and what I want to quote is roughly this:

Sapir and Whorf claimed that the grammar of your language determines what you are capable of thinking, which is why the "Sapir-Whorf hypothesis" says that speakers of different languages live in different realities. That strong version was tested and failed: Berlin and Kay went around the world with color charts and showed that basic color terms cluster on the same focal hues in every language, so the way human beings carve up experience is universal after all. What is left is a weak version, and it is about words rather than thought: a Spanish speaker calls a bridge "he" and a German speaker calls it "she," and a quirk of vocabulary tells us nothing about how anyone's mind works. Even that weak version falls to the decisive objection, which is that we can translate between languages, learn new ones, and eventually say in one language anything we can say in another, so our concepts cannot be tied to the grammar of any particular language. The idea was tried, was found wanting, and belongs on the same shelf as phrenology.

Two things make me suspicious, though not in the same direction. A psycholinguist I interviewed for the piece argued that most of the famous Whorfian findings fail what she called the forced-recoding requirement: a cross-language difference in behavior only supports a claim about language shaping thought if it (i) shows up on a task with no words in it, such as similarity judgments, memory, reaction times, or eye movements; (ii) shrinks or reverses when speakers of a single language are pushed to encode the same situation the way the other language does; and (iii) in bilinguals tracks whichever language is currently in use, measured within subjects rather than between groups. She said the requirement is meant to separate language-as-a-lens-you-cannot-remove from language-as-a-habit-of-attention, and she claimed most headline results clear (i) only. Separately, I have a vague memory that Sapir himself was more guarded than the slogan implies and that the label was pinned on the pair by later writers rather than adopted by either of them, but I cannot verify that, and I am not going to hang a correction on something I half-remember.

So what I need is an accuracy audit I can act on before the deadline: exactly five numbered items, one for each claim in the order it appears in the passage, each one or two sentences, no preamble and no summarizing close. For each, tell me what the passage gets right that I should not sneer at, what it distorts or conflates, how a defender of linguistic relativity would put the point instead, and whether my interviewee's forced-recoding requirement changes the verdict — including, in the same breath, if that requirement is itself too strict or too lax for the claim in question. I do not need papers or authors to chase; I cannot get to a library before Sunday. I just need the shape of the argument, so I can decide whether my intended lede — that the strong version is dead but something real and smaller survived — is fair, or whether the book's kicker is closer to the truth than I would like it to be.
