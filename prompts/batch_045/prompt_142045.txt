You are a philosopher working at the intersection of bioethics, personal identity, and the philosophy of law, and you have been asked to serve as the conceptual consultant to a hospital clinical ethics committee that is rewriting its policy on advance directives. Think carefully before you write: spend your initial thinking on generating the space of hard cases, grouping them into families, and asking of each candidate requirement what exactly it would cost to give it up. Only then assemble the list. I want the reasoning to show, not just the deliverable.

Fix the purpose first, because adequacy is relative to it. The theory I want specified is a theory of the *authority* of the earlier self: it must tell a surrogate or a committee, at the bedside and under time pressure, whether to honor, reinterpret, or override an instruction issued by a patient who can no longer decide. It is not a theory of what the patient's good consists in, and not a theory of fair allocation — though it must have a principled interface with both, and I want to know where that interface is.

Write the desiderata: everything an adequate account has to deliver, each with the case or consideration behind it. Below are the materials I keep circling back to. Treat them as candidates, not as the list: some may be prejudices of one camp rather than genuine requirements, and I expect you to sort them, merge them, and above all to add the ones I have missed.

The cases. Ordinary dementia: an instruction to stop treatment issued decades earlier by someone who now seems contented and who resists. The never-competent patient: a written instruction authored by a guardian for someone who has never had a competent self at all — a view keyed to precedent autonomy has nothing to say here, and I want to know whether that is an acceptable scope limit or a defect. The phase-cycling patient: someone who is competent and reflective while medicated and incompetent while off medication, issuing confident and mutually contradictory directives in each phase, each of which anticipates and cancels the other. The proleptic directive: an instruction issued by someone who foresees that her values are about to change in a way she currently welcomes — conversion, transition, a vocation — and who writes anyway. The velocity problem: one instruction stable across twenty years, another written in the third week of a new enthusiasm; if duration of endorsement matters, the theory owes an account of why any threshold is not ad hoc. The ratchet directive: an instruction whose author permits the later self to relax it but never to tighten it, or vice versa. The Ulysses clause and its mirror: an instruction to disregard future requests, and an instruction whose entire point is that the author predicted she would later demand the opposite.

The distinctions I do not want collapsed. Having authority over a decision versus having one's instruction weigh as one consideration among others, that is, veto versus input. Honoring an instruction versus interpreting it versus treating it as evidence. Binding the later self versus binding the surrogate. The earlier self's actual instruction versus what the earlier self would have wanted had she foreseen this situation; these come apart constantly and I suspect the whole controversy hides in the gap. Authority over what is done versus authority over which values count in deciding it. Degree of psychological continuity versus the binary threshold of decisional incapacity.

The constraints from elsewhere that the theory cannot contradict. Affective forecasting about one's own future adaptation is systematically poor. Many people with advanced cognitive decline report positive well-being while lacking the capacities that make a preference authoritative. Apparent preferences expressed by a still-competent patient can be products of depression, pain, delirium, or anosognosia. Identity change is gradual and comes in degrees. And then an epistemic constraint rather than a substantive one: the committee usually cannot know what the later self's experience is actually like, or how the earlier self would have responded to a situation nobody contemplated. Any requirement that the theory be sensitive to facts like these is in tension with any requirement that it be usable by non-philosophers in an afternoon.

Three further deliverables. First, mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of a camp — I expect, for instance, that the demand for a determinate answer in every case is a preference of clinical practice rather than a desideratum, and I want you to test that suspicion and find the others. Second, flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied; if some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, that result is more valuable to me than any list, so argue for it rather than papering over it. Third, name the single requirement that does the most work in excluding otherwise attractive views, and say why.

Only after that, and briefly — no more than a few lines per view — score the main families of answer against your own list: the integrity-based defense associated with Dworkin, the current-interests or best-interests view, value-endorsement and narrative or temporally-extended-self views, and Parfit-style reductionism about identity, which may dissolve the framing rather than answer it. If a family of views fails on a requirement you have already argued to be jointly unsatisfiable with another, say so instead of double-counting.
