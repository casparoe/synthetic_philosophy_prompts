I'm 71, retired about six years ago from a career in isotope geochemistry — lake sediments, paleoclimate, a lot of mud cores, not exactly seminar material — and a few of us from the old department have kept meeting on Tuesdays at Miriam's place. We drifted from popular science into archaeology and now we're stuck on this collapse business: the end of the Bronze Age in the eastern Mediterranean, the Maya, Easter Island. Our argument never moves. Miriam says "collapse" is just a value judgement that archaeologists smuggle in, because what actually disappears at these transitions is the stuff archaeologists happen to like — palaces, bureaucracy, written record-keeping, monumental sculpture, long-distance trade — while ordinary people mostly carry on farming under a different arrangement. I think that's too fast, but I can't stand the popular "they committed ecocide and failed" version either; its moralistic and, honestly, bad systems thinking. My own half-thought is that collapse could be a perfectly respectable descriptive notion while "failure" is the word doing all the moralizing — but tell me if that's just cowardice on my part.

Here's the bit I actually want you to engage with rather than define around. In my old field we'd say a lake can flip abruptly from clear water to turbid with the same nutrient input going in, and we call that a regime shift, not a failure, because the lake is still a lake. So I tried to make collapse precise the same way: not depopulation and not suffering, but a measurable, persistent drop in the number of independent variables a society tracks and acts on — how many distinct administrative offices show up in its records, how many specialized crafts are being practiced, how wide its exchange network is, whether anyone is keeping written accounts at all. Call it dimensional collapse: the society falls from a high-dimensional organizational state to a much lower-dimensional one and stays there for generations, with the same people, the same land, the same climate. Two jobs for you. First, is the criterion cheating? If a two-hundred-person farming village was never high-dimensional to begin with, my account seems to imply it can never collapse, which looks a lot like a preference for complexity dressed up as measurement. Second, settle our group's fight: does collapse name a real kind of event worth keeping — one you could in principle detect in a contemporary society, and say what you'd measure — or should the word be retired in favour of plain descriptions of transformation? Please don't finish on "it depends what we mean by collapse"; pick a meaning, defend it, and say why the other side loses.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, no introduction and no summing-up paragraph at the end. I don't mind if they run: what collapse is, the strongest objection to treating it as real, your reply to that, what your account says about at least two of my three cases, and your verdict. I read enough numbered reports in my career, but it does keep a person honest.
