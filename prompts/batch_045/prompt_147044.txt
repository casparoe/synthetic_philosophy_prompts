My roommate joined the campus apologetics society this year and ever since someone showed him Hilbert's Hotel he won't stop repeating the same line: "a hotel that is completely full can still take more guests — that's absurd, so an actually infinite collection of real things can't exist." He uses it in that argument about the universe having a beginning, and when I push him he also starts in on how you can subtract equal amounts from equal amounts and get different remainders, which feels to me like a second argument wearing the same clothes. My philosophy of maths TA says basically the opposite: that nothing in the hotel story is even slightly paradoxcial, that it's little more than one definition of "infinite" dressed up as a puzzle, and that my roommate is smuggling in habits of thought he learned from finite hotels. I've got a response paper due Monday (~800 words) and I want to know what that one sentence could actually be claiming before I write anything.

So: can you lay out, as a menu I can work through, the different precise theses "a completely full hotel can still take more guests" might be expressing — and for each say whether it's trivial, whether it's substantive and defensible, whether it's straightforwardly false, and (the bit I actually care about) which readings would get my roommate his conclusion about concrete actual infinities and which would only be claims about sets, concepts, or what our words mean? The ones I can half-see: "full" as every room occupied vs "full" as no rearrangement could free a room; "can" as there being a bijection/reassignment vs "can" as achievable by some actual process; the "matchable with a proper part" characterization vs "not the same size as any {1,...,n}" (I assumed those were equivalent but the TA mumbled something about needing a weak choice principle, which I didn't follow); "same size" as cardinality vs as natural density or measure, where the evens are exactly half of the naturals; "absurd" as logically contradictory vs merely counterintuitive; and whether any of it is really about whether the totality can be "collected" or completed at all.

Then here's a wrinkle I invented and can't tell what it shows. Call it Hilbert's Walk-Up: same hotel, all rooms full, but guests move only by walking (one room per minute), no room may hold more than one guest at any instant, and — the bit that does the work — only finitely many guests may be in the corridor at once, or equivalently no two guests may move at exactly the same instant. My attempt at an argument that the new guest definately cannot be accommodated: whoever is to move into room 2 needs room 2 empty first, which needs its occupant to have already left, which needs room 3 empty, and so on with no end and therefore no first mover, so any successful shift-up requires either infinitely many people in transit simultaneously or an infinite chain of moves with no earliest move. Does that argument actually hold up, and does it generalise (say, to fitting infinitely many new arrivals)? If it does, which reading of the slogan does it support — does it show that the usual "shift everyone up one room" story is quietly helping itself to infinite parallelism or infinite coordination, and is that an objection to actual infinities themselves or just to hotels with a manager who can snap his fingers? Also, is the strict/ultra-finitist complaint (the people who apparently don't even accept arbitrarily large finite numbers) the same complaint as my roommate's or a different one, and should I bring Cantor's "some infinities are bigger than others" into this at all, or is that irrelevant to whether concrete infinities are possible?
