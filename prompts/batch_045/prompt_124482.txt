I sit on a fair number of hiring committees, funding panels, and internal strategy reviews, and over the last few years I have kept running into the same shape of thing. Three instances, from the smallest to the largest.

In a faculty search, the first two people to speak about a candidate say mildly positive things. The third, not wanting to be heard as the one person with reservations, says something a shade warmer. By the eighth speaker the candidate is being described in language that nobody in that room would ever write in a confidential note. When the confidential individual rankings are collected afterwards, the committee's aggregate assessment of the candidate is what it was before the discussion started — it has not shifted measurably. What moved is only the register in which an unchanged view was expressed.

In a professional body responding to a misconduct allegation, the first statements express concern. Within a week the statements are condemnations; within a month members are being asked why they have not spoken. Several of those members have told me privately that their reading of the evidence has not changed since the first week, and that they think the later statements are performance. The archived record, however, shows a community that travelled from concern to fury.

In internal reviews, each layer of management restates a proposal upward in stronger terms than it received it, because a faithful, uninflected restatement reads as lukewarm. By the top, it has become a commitment that no one below the top actually holds.

The structure I extract: nobody is changing sides and nobody's private conviction is moving; what moves is the intensity of the public display, and it moves only upward. Each step is locally explicable without any change in belief — once the last speaker has set the local threshold for what sincere assent sounds like, repeating the previous speaker's formulation is now heard as hesitation, so the next speaker must clear a higher bar, and clearing it raises the bar again. The escalation is made of material that costs almost nothing to produce, but it is not retractable: the earlier, milder formulation is now unavailable to the person who made it, and de-escalation reads as defection.

I have tried to fit this to concepts I know from reading about herding informally, and each seems to me to miss. In the standard observational-learning story, the failure is that people discard their own private signal and imitate, so that later actions carry no information; here no private signal about the matter at hand is being discarded, and the participants' problem is not which way to go but how loudly to go the way they were already going. Group polarization has the wrong direction of causation: there the private opinions themselves drift outward, whereas here they appear not to. Pluralistic ignorance requires that people be wrong about what others privately believe; my sense is that these people are often right that the display is exaggerated, including about themselves, and escalate anyway.

So: is there an established concept for this — the endogenous ratchet in the intensity of assent, driven by the need to clear a bar that one's own and others' previous compliance has raised — or is it a special case of something, or does it have no name? I am not fishing for a label to pin on it; I want the carving. If the nearest concepts are several, tell me exactly where each of them fits and where it fails. In particular I would like the following distinctions drawn: escalation that is defensive (avoid being read as the dissenter) versus competitive (seeking credit for being the most committed); displays that are cheap versus displays with real costs attached, since I suspect the ratchet behaves differently in the two cases; what sets the ceiling, given that the register of available expressions is finite and that hyperbole should in principle inflate itself into worthlessness; and how to tell, from the outside, this phenomenon from a genuine cascade in which conviction really does spread. One further thing I have noticed and cannot place: the record these episodes leave behind gets read later by people who were not present — hiring files, minutes, press archives — as straightforward evidence that the group strongly believed X, and it then feeds further herding by those readers. That downstream effect may be a separate phenomenon, or it may be the point of the whole thing; I would like your view on which.
