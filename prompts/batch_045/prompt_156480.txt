I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background (well, one undergrad ethics class I mostly slept through), and somehow I ended up as the "technical person" on the clinical ethics committee at one of the hospitals my company builds decision-support tools for. I keep finding that my gut verdicts on individual cases are very confident but don't add up to anything I can actually write down, and I'd like the rule. I even tried writing it as a spec — if X, then permissible — and it collapsed every time I tested it against the next case.

Here's where I land. Unplugging the ventilator on a patient who'll never breathe on their own again, because the family asks us to: fine, obviously. Never starting the vent in the first place on that same patient: also fine, and I'll say out loud that it's the same decision, even though the ICU attendings treat withholding as completely different from withdrawing and much easier to sleep after — I think they're just wrong about that. Stopping a feeding tube: also fine, but it makes me uneasy in a way the vent doesn't, and I can't tell whether that's a real distinction or just squeamishness about food and water. Taking a young, awake patient with a high spinal cord injury off the vent because he's decided he doesn't want to live like that: fine, and somehow clearer than the first case even though he isn't dying of anything. Morphine for someone in end-stage lung disease who's gasping for air, at a dose where everyone in the room knows it'll shorten things by a few hours: fine. The same drug at a much bigger dose, where the whole point is to end it: not fine. I know those two look inconsistent — same drug, same route, same patient, same physiological cause of death, only the reason is different — and my friend mutters something about "double effect," but I don't want a tour of the literature, I want the principle.

The pair that really breaks me: the guy who sneaks into the bathroom to hold his cousin's head under the water for the inheritance is a murderer, and the guy who came prepared to do exactly that, finds the kid already face-down, and just stands there and watches is also a murderer, close enough that I don't care about the difference. But then why isn't unplugging the vent the same thing? My best attempt at a principle: it's OK when what kills them is whatever was already wrong with them — their lungs, their heart — and not OK when the death comes from something I introduce into the situation. That doesn't handle the morphine pair (the drug kills them in both), and I'm not sure what it says about a nurse accidentally knocking a dialysis machine off and me deciding not to restart it. And next month we've got two cases that aren't in anybody's binder. One: during a surge, an allocation model ranks patients and recommends moving the last ventilator from a patient with poor projected benefit to one with better projected benefit; the attending's entire action is one click, accept recommendation, and a respiratory therapist does the actual disconnect per protocol. That feels fine to me if the ranking process is fair, but it definately feels worse than never having allocated the vent to the first patient in the first place, which is supposed to be the same thing. Two: a closed-loop sedation controller that titrates propofol to a comfort target, where the team's only real choices are let it run or switch it off — if the patient dies sooner because the controller ran, who did what, and does the principle say anything? So: can you give me a principle, or two competing candidates, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it, tell me honestly which of my verdicts it can't save and which one I should probably give up, and tell me what it predicts for those last two cases?
