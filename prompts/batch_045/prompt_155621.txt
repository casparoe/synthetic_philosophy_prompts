I volunteer on my county's rare-bird records committee — we decide which sightings go into the annual report — and I have no formal training in anything mathematical; I taught myself Bayesian reasoning from a blog about medical screening tests and I keep all of it in a spreadsheet. Last winter we argued for two evenings over a single submission: a voicemail from an experienced but not unimpeachable observer, left in one breath, saying she thought she could see the pale wing-panel on a gull at the reservoir and also thought the tail-band looked right, but was unsure about both, plus one badly blurred photograph attached to the same message. I wanted to handle it the way I handle all our soft evidence: the rule I was taught — I've seen it called Jeffrey conditionalization — says that when an experience moves your confidence in a partition {E, ¬E} to new values P′(E) and P′(¬E) without making you certain of either, you set your new confidence in any hypothesis H to P(H|E)·P′(E) + P(H|¬E)·P′(¬E), while leaving the conditional confidences P(H|E) and P(H|¬E) exactly where they were, a condition I've seen called rigidity. My friend's objection, which I think is wrong but can't refute, is that any strangeness here must come from rigidity failing somewhere: once you let the first update change how E bears on H, of course the order matters, and a clean application of the rule would commute. So I want a case that settles it.

What I need from you is a worked scenario — please make it an ordinary one, a bird record will do or anything else at that scale: no perfect detectors, no demons, no exotic physics, no numbers a person couldn't actually arrive at. Specifications: one hypothesis H (say, that the bird really was the rare species rather than the common lookalike, with a prior somewhere between 0.2 and 0.6, since a record that is already near-certain isn't the interesting case) and two binary propositions E and F that the evidence bears on only uncertainly; two soft updates with stated target confidences, each between 0.05 and 0.95; all four cells of the joint distribution over E and F strictly positive, and every intermediate distribution a genuine probability distribution summing to one. The two pieces of news must arrive bundled together — one voicemail, one message with two attachments, one paragraph making both claims — so that "do them in the order they arrived" is not available to me. Each update must be a scrupulous application of the rule above, and yet the final probability of H must differ by at least twenty percentage points depending on which update is performed first; if you cannot reach twenty without pushing some number to an extreme, say so plainly and give me the largest gap you can honestly get. If you can arrange it, I'd also like the second update to be one that pushes its own proposition's probability up when applied first and down when applied second — my guess is that this is what makes the case feel perverse, and it strikes me as realistic: the same blurry photo looks like mild confirmation before you have taken the wing-panel claim on board, and like a partial retraction once you have. I have started calling these piggyback updates, because each one drags the other proposition's probability along with it through the correlation between them, and calling the size of that side-effect the drag.

Then please demonstrate rather than assert. Give me the prior joint distribution over the four cells; run both orders step by step and show me the numbers I would need in order to check you — the probability of the other proposition after the first update, the two conditional probabilities of H that the second update uses, and the two final answers; and check rigidity explicitly at each step by reporting P(H|E) and P(H|F) before and after each update, telling me which of those changes, if any, actually violates the rule as I stated it and which are merely the rule propagating, since my friend will pounce on any of them and I need to know which ones he is entitled to. After that, tell me honestly which feature of the case is doing the work. My own hypothesis is that the gap is driven by how far the second update's target sits from the dragged-along value — the bigger the drag, the bigger the gap — but I would rather you test that by varying one number at a time and telling me what actually happens than confirm me. Then remove the feature and show the case collapse: make E and F probabilistically independent of each other while keeping the way H depends on them as close as you can, and show whether the two orders then agree, since I have half-convinced myself that independence is sufficient for the rule to commute — check me on that too, and give me the counterexample if I am wrong. Finally, answer the objection I expect from the committee: someone will say the right move is to stop doing two updates and do a single update on the joint partition {E∧F, E∧¬F, ¬E∧F, ¬E∧¬F}. Tell me what I would have to know in order to do that, whether the two experiences in your scenario supply it, and — if I add the smallest extra assumption that makes the joint update well-defined — which of the two orders it lands on, or neither. And then the part I actually care about, because I have to go back to this committee: if the gap is real and the rule does not pick an order, is that a defect in the rule, or a sign that my two reports simply did not specify enough for any rule to return a unique answer, and what should the record say?
