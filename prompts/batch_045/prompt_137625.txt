Hey — I'm a PhD student in philosophy (I work on stuff adjacent to phil math) and at our reading group last week two of the postdocs got into it over Skolem's paradox and were still going when we moved to the bar. They asked me to referee and I honestly couldnt tell who was winning. Rough reconstruction:

Nadia (1): Forget the word "paradox." The downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem is a plain meta-theoretic result: if ZFC is consistent it has a countable model M. Inside M there's an object M calls the power set of the naturals, and M satisfies "that set is uncountable" — M even satisfies Cantor's theorem. But M itself is countable, so from outside there's a bijection between the naturals and that very set. And the theorem applies to any consistent first-order axiomatization you like, so no first-order theory pins down uncountability. The conclusion should be that "uncountable" always means uncountable-in-M: there's no absolute notion of countability, and your P(ω) is just one more model-relative object.

Theo (2): That last step is a scope slip. The bijection isn't in M — that's what "outside" means. Cantor's theorem holds in M: nothing in M surjects the naturals onto M's version of P(ω). What M lacks is subsets, which is why the enumeration can exist out here. So the result shows only that first-order axiomatizations don't determine which subsets exist. Nobody ever claimed ZFC was categorical. Non-categoricity isn't relativism about size.

Nadia (3): "The subsets M lacks" is doing all the work, and it's exactly what I'm disputing. To say a model is missing subsets you need a background universe with its own power set of the naturals — and whatever fixes yours is either another first-order theory (which I'll Skolemize in turn) or an informal notion you can't state without circularity, since the intended interpretation of *power set* is the very thing in question. And it's symmetric: from inside M, your extra subsets are as absent as my bijection is from your vantage point. You don't get to call M "wrong" unless you've already won.

Theo (4): I'm not claiming categoricity, I'm claiming an asymmetry. Hand me any countable list of subsets of the naturals and I can diagonalize past it, and I can see that move as repeatable without end — a resource no countable model has about its own reals. And I don't need a completed set of all subsets to say it: I can say "take any subsets you like, there's one not among them," which is plural talk, not set talk. So what's open-ended is how far the hierarchy goes: the universe is indefinitely extensible. That's inexhaustibility, not indeterminacy. Also note you need the naturals pinned down for your own argument — "M is countable" needs a real enumeration — so you can't relativize everything either.

Nadia (5): Two things. First, your plural version is my indeterminacy with a friendly name: if there's no determinate totality of subsets of the naturals, then "how many reals are there" has no determinate answer, and CH isn't true-or-false-but-unknown, it's relative to a universe — and the reals are exactly where realism was supposed to pay off. (I got there with pure Skolem, no forcing or independence results.) It's like simultaneity: frame-relative, stated from inside a frame, and still a real discovery. Second, your argument that we pin down N uses second-order resources to rule out nonstandard numbers — the resource under dispute. And even granting you determinate N, determinate N plus indeterminate P(N) still gets me everything I wanted about the continuum.

Theo (6): Then your thesis eats itself. "Countability is relative to a model" is either said from somewhere — in which case you're borrowing the absolute background you're attacking — or it's only true-in-your-M, in which case it says nothing I have to answer. Relativity claims don't get to free-float; you have to state them, and stating them costs you absolutism somewhere.

Here's the bit nobody said out loud, which I've been turning over since: in the countable-model case the model isn't a world where the reals are countable — it's a world with too *few* reals. The enumeration exists only outside, and it exists because the model is missing subsets, not because it contains a surjection. So maybe they aren't disagreeing about the size of one fixed object at all, but about which object is the power set of the naturals. Does that asymmetry (omission versus commission) actually break the symmetry Nadia needs in (3), or does she just absorb it — "missing" is only missing relative to a background you helped yourself to — and we're back to the regress?
