I'm a retired field biologist and I've gotten into this cooperation stuff as a hobby — its for a short talk I'm giving to my old department in the spring, twenty minutes, mostly people who've heard of tit-for-tat and nothing past it. So I want one made-up example I can walk them through, built to my spec rather than borrowed, because the standard ones all lean on somebody making a mistake and I've come to think that's a bit of a dodge.

Setup to start from. A group of somewhere between eight and forty people — a real human group, nothing sci-fi: a fishing cooperative, a volunteer fire crew, a shared workshop, whatever you like. Every round each person is paired with someone from the group, both choose cooperate or defect, and the payoffs are a genuine prisoner's dilemma: temptation beats reward beats penalty beats sucker, and two rewards beat temptation plus sucker. Pairings are redrawn at random each round and you never get the same partner twice running, so nobody can do anything with "I'll see you again." Nobody knows which round is the last. Assume people aren't doing backward induction on that — they're following a rule, noticing who's doing well, and copying them; that's the evolutionary flavor I'm after. The only thing that carries across rounds is what the others know about you.

Two rules to pit against each other. Rule A, the tally: everyone carries a mark, good or bad, that goes bad whenever they defect and goes back to good once they cooperate again; people cooperate with good-marked partners and defect against bad-marked ones. Rule B, the excuse: same thing, except that defecting against somebody who is currently marked bad costs you nothing — justified punishment is free.

What I want is a group where Rule A lets cooperation decay down to something near universal defection while Rule B holds it at a high level. And here's the constraint I actually care about: the decay under Rule A must not come from anyone making a mistake. No mis-implemenetation, no misperception, no mutation, nobody misreading a mark, no trembling hands. Observation can be as good as you like — perfect and fully public for all I care. The decay has to fall out of the structure itself: who is marked bad, and what happens to a person who defects against them.

Deliverables, please:

- The scenario as a single paragraph of ordinary prose, with the payoff numbers written out, since I want them on a slide.
- The walkthrough: for each rule, what a cooperator does, what happens once a defector is present, and where the group ends up. Trace the first several rounds explicitly — I want to watch it fall out of the rules, not have you announce the verdict.
- Name the one feature of the case doing the work, and say what happens to both rules if that feature is removed — if nobody ever starts out bad, say, or if marks are private rather than public.
- How somebody who likes Rule A would attack your example: what tweak to the tally rescues it, and whether that tweak is cheating or a genuine fix.
- A robustness note: does this hold across a decent range of payoffs, group sizes and starting compositions, or only on a knife edge? If it's knife-edge, say so plainly; I'd rather know before I'm standing at the podium.

Two last things. I can't check references and wouldn't recognise most of the names, so don't give me any — no reading lists, no "see so-and-so 1992," no bibliography. And be clear about what's settled versus what people who actually work on this argue about: my guess is that some of this is more contested than it'll sound in your answer, and I don't want to present a simulation result as if it were a law of nature. Flag anything you're extrapolating, and flag anything where you're not sure the case really does what I've asked for.
