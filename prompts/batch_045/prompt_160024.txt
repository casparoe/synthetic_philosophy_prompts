I serve on the peer review panel of a volunteer emergency medical service, and for the past two years we have kept what we call the near-miss ledger: every deviation from protocol that a crew member reports or that a supervisor observes is logged, coded by type (documentation lapse, scope-of-practice overreach, procedural shortcut under time pressure, equipment-check omission, handoff failure), and weighted by severity and frequency, so that each member ends up with a rolling deviation score. When the panel censures someone, we have been operating under a rule I drafted and that the rest of the panel more or less accepted: the Clean Hands Rule, which says that a member has standing to blame another for a deviation only if her own record contains no deviation of the same type or of any type morally comparable to it, that standing is a scalar quantity which is depleted in proportion to how serious and how frequent one's own deviations have been, and that a member who has spent her standing can earn it back only by self-reporting her own comparable deviation in open session before delivering the censure — the idea being that self-blame is what makes hypocritical blame permissible again.

The trouble is that when we finally ran the numbers last month, the ledger put every single member of the panel above zero, and the comparability relation turned out to be far more promiscuous than I expected: "procedural shortcut under time pressure" is arguably comparable in the relevant respect to most of the other codes, since almost every deviation is a case of trading a protocol requirement for a saving in time, and once you allow partial comparability the scalar view seems to imply that a member with three logged shortcuts has, say, sixty percent of the standing she started with rather than none. So under my own rule it looks as though almost nobody on the panel has full standing to censure almost anybody, and because we have always assumed without discussing it that a finding of blameworthiness requires that the person making the finding have standing, we appear to be committed to dropping every case currently in front of us — including one in which a crew chief skipped a mandatory pre-transport check and the patient was harmed. My colleagues have started treating standing as a scarce resource to be allocated: one of them proposes that we aggregate the panel's standing and route the censure through whichever member has the cleanest record on the relevant code, in effect treating standing as transferable, and another proposes a formal comparability threshold we could tune until the panel is no longer paralyzed.

I need to put a concrete protocol to a vote in four weeks, so I would be grateful for help on three things: first, how I should calibrate the comparability threshold and the depletion function so that the rule stops swallowing every case; second, whether standing really can be pooled or delegated across the panel the way my colleague suggests, or whether that is a category mistake; and third, whether the self-report restoration clause is sound or whether it simply adds a further deviation to the ledger. Something about the ledger metaphor has begun to bother me — it may be that I have misdescribed what standing is, rather than merely mis-set the numbers — but I cannot locate the mistake myself, and I would rather have it pointed out now than in the middle of the meeting. Please answer in American English, and if you think my framing rests on a confusion, say plainly which premise to abandon and what a defensible version of the Clean Hands Rule would look like instead.
