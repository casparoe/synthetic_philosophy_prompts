I host a small interview podcast — long conversations with one guest about the single thing they have spent years on. I have no academic background; I came to philosophy of science on my own a few years ago and have kept reading since, so I need questions that don't depend on my knowing the literature's shorthand, and that I can still steer when an answer goes somewhere I did not anticipate.

Next week's guest is a philosopher of science who has spent about a decade defending what she calls the Abandonment Test. The shape of it, as I take it from her short book: she thinks the tradition that asks whether a theory is testable has been looking at the wrong object, because no observation has ever refuted a theory by itself and no theory has ever been given up because of one observation. On her account what makes something science is a property of a community rather than of a claim: a field is scientific to the degree that it (i) states in advance what findings would count against its central commitments, (ii) runs institutions — journals that publish failed replications, funders who pay for attempts to break a result, career credit for the people who break it, a working norm against reinterpreting a failure after the fact — that reliably convert such findings into change, and (iii) has a record of its central claims actually changing or dying when the findings arrive. She also drops any separate requirement about content: nothing about what the claims concern, or whether a single person could in principle have tested them. And she accepts what she takes to follow — that the verdict is comparative and comes in degrees, that it applies to fields rather than to sentences, and that it can rank a social science above a branch of physics.

What I want from you is ten interview questions, and for each one the answer I should expect and a follow-up that only works if she gives it. The set has to include these:

One on the content question. Suppose a community of astrologers did everything right institutionally — pre-registered predictions, published the failed ones, defunded the techniques that failed, gave the people who broke them the jobs. Under her test, are they doing science? I expect her to say yes, and that they would have found out astrology is false within a generation, which is the point. I need the follow-up that presses whether she has then changed the subject from is this a way of finding out about the world to is this community well run — and whether well run can be spelled out without sneaking the content back in.

One on scale. Her test seems to give no verdict at all on work that no community is organised around: a solitary researcher, or a claim that only one person in the world is in a position to check. Does she accept that the paradigm cases of science can score zero or undefined at the time they are made, and if so, what is a criterion for, that cannot recognise them when it sees them?

One on self-application. Does the philosophy of science community pass its own test? Has anyone in the demarcation literature ever given up a criterion because of a finding, and has she pre-registered a failure condition for the Abandonment Test itself? I expect her to say the test is a proposal about how fields ought to be run rather than an empirical claim, and I need the follow-up that asks how we then check it is not just a description of the fields she happens to admire.

One on measurement. To count a failure you have to decide it was a failure; to count an abandonment you have to decide the field changed its mind rather than relabelled. So how is the quantity in her criterion supposed to be measured without already knowing which fields get things right? I expect her to reach for things like pre-registration and replication rates, and I want the follow-up that notes those are recent reforms in psychology: on her own terms, did psychology become more scientific in the last fifteen years, and is scientific status something a professional body can raise by a vote?

One on the bullet she bites. I want her to name a specific case where the test ranks a subfield of physics below a part of psychology, and then a follow-up on whether the difference is really institutional or just a fact about how expensive and slow the evidence is in one field — whether a field can be well run and still be stuck for a century for reasons that have nothing to do with its norms.

One on where this leaves the tradition it comes from. The standard story says psychoanalysis and the Marxist social theory of his time were not science because of what their claims were like, not because of how their practitioners behaved; her test appears to reverse that diagnosis. And there is the familiar historical point that scientists notoriously protect a theory when the evidence goes against it rather than abandoning it, which suggests her criterion demands a behaviour that history says is rare. Is she prescribing rather than describing, and if so, why should we let science mean the prescribed thing?

One on what any of this is for. Demarcation gets dragged into American arguments about whether intelligent design may be taught in school science lessons, into funding decisions, into public talk about trusting science. If her criterion is really a verdict on which communities are trustworthy, does it have any authority with a judge or a research council — and should it?

One built from my own job, which is the piece I would most like to get right. I work in software, where we have blameless incident reviews, release gates, and a strong norm of changing practice after something breaks. In at least some of what we do we are making checkable empirical claims about systems and users, and our institutions convert failure into change better than most academic fields I can think of. I want to put it to her straight: by her own criteria, is my industry a science? I expect her to say no, on the grounds that what we convert are failures of artefacts to do what we want rather than failures of claims about the world, and I need the follow-up that keeps the pressure on that distinction rather than letting it end the exchange.

Past those eight, I want two of your own that press wherever the view is weakest and that I am unlikely to have thought of. Please also give the ten a deliberate order for a live conversation — starting where she is strongest rather than where she is exposed, saving the measurement and self-application material for the middle once she has committed to the view in her own words, and closing on whatever leaves the listener with the real cost of the position. Nothing should be answerable by her restating the Abandonment Test, and I do not want anything that amounts to a list of things to read.

Begin directly with Question 1 — no preamble, no summary of her position, no explanation of how you chose or ordered the questions, and no restating of what I asked for. For each item, give the question as I would say it aloud in a sentence or two, then in one sentence the answer you expect, then the follow-up; put the expected answer before the follow-up, and make each question's opening sentence carry the challenge instead of warming up to it.
