I am preparing the question sheet for a ninety-minute recorded interview — no live audience, released as a single edited episode — with Dr. Miriam Fenn, whose recent book argues that epistemic bubbles and echo chambers are two distinct pathologies rather than two points on a continuum of closed-mindedness. Her formulation, in brief: a bubble is a structure of omission, in which a member simply fails to encounter certain sources, claims, or counterevidence, so that the natural remedy is exposure to what was missing; a chamber is a structure of active discrediting, in which the member's trust in outside sources has been undermined in advance of any particular claim, so that supplying the missing material does not correct the problem and may deepen it, and the remedy is the repair of trust rather than the transmission of content. She also maintains that the two types are idealizations that real communities instantiate only in mixtures, and she has a reliable habit of answering hard cases with the observation that they are mixtures rather than counterexamples.

Assume a listener who has read the book's opening chapter: I do not want any question that lets her restate the distinction or walk through her own stock examples. Please draft eight to ten questions, each with a single follow-up written against the answer you expect her to give, ordered so that the episode moves from mechanism to hard cases to remedy. Press on the following, and add anything of comparable sharpness that I have missed.

First, the "accurate chamber": a tenants' association that systematically distrusts an official housing agency because that agency's data really has been doctored, and whose internal culture of ridicule toward agency sources is structurally indistinguishable from a paradigm chamber, yet whose beliefs are true and well-supported. Ask whether this is a chamber at all. Anticipate that she says it is not a malfunction, or not the kind that matters; the follow-up should press her on whether the discrediting mechanism is ever sufficient for the diagnosis on its own, and if not, on whether her account can identify what is wrong with a chamber without appealing to the falsity of what the chamber discredits.

Second, the mixed case in which both mechanisms are present and pull in different directions: a specialized research subfield where omission does most of the filtering — jargon, paywalled venues, citation networks, hiring practices — but where there is also an in-group norm, professionally rewarded, of mocking outside critics. Ask which diagnosis governs and which remedy applies first. Anticipate the mixtures reply; the follow-up should force an assignment of explanatory priority and ask what observable difference the assignment makes, so that the answer cannot remain classificatory.

Third, the topically bounded chamber: a community whose discrediting is confined to one domain, while its members are open, curious, and well-informed about everything else. Ask whether chamberhood attaches to persons, to communities, or to domains of claims. Anticipate that she allows local chambers; the follow-up should ask how a local chamber differs from a global bubble plus one locally corrupted topic, and what that implies about how many chambers a single person can occupy at once.

Fourth, the escape problem, which I want handled in two steps. The mechanism destroys the credibility of exactly the sources a member would need in order to leave, insiders who defect are discredited in turn as having been corrupted, and even a member's own memories of outside contact can be re-described as contamination. Ask what the minimal exit route is; anticipate an appeal to the member's own first-order evidence or to internal anomalies that the chamber cannot explain; the follow-up should test whether any such route survives once discrediting is extended to defectors and to first-person recollection, and should force her to say whether escape is impossible in principle, merely costly, or possible only for reasons external to her model.

Fifth, detection. Ask what pattern of behavioral traces — sharing, quoting, argument patterns, source diversity over time — would discriminate a chamber from a bubble in a community whose substantive beliefs you do not antecedently accept or reject. Anticipate something like engagement with out-group material as the signature, since a chamber must cite what it rebuts while a bubble merely never sees it; the follow-up should raise the confound that outrage-driven communities quote out-group material constantly without any corruption of trust, and should demand a signature that distinguishes hostile engagement from discrediting proper.

Sixth, the self-awareness question: if members are explicitly taught what a chamber is and told that their community has this structure, does the mechanism dissolve? Anticipate a qualified yes on the grounds that discrediting depends on a certain invisibility, followed by the observation that sophisticated chambers pre-empt the diagnosis itself by discrediting the vocabulary used to describe them. The follow-up should ask whether this recursive absorption makes the model unfalsifiable in practice, and should require her to specify a finding — behavioral, historical, or experimental — that would count against it.

Seventh, the burden of remedy, which I expect to be the most uncomfortable part of the conversation. She allows, I take it, that chambers can be epistemically protective for groups with good reason to distrust a hostile mainstream. If so, the remedy is a demand that outside institutions become trustworthy rather than a demand that members open themselves up. Ask her to commit, explicitly, on whom the cost of trust repair falls, on whether the marginalized community is ever required to extend trust it has no reason to extend, and on whether her remedy is therefore an epistemic prescription at all or a political one wearing epistemic clothing.

Close with one question about what her account rules out: an intervention, a diagnosis, or a policy that someone attracted to her framework would want but that the framework cannot license.

Two constraints on the format. Keep every question self-contained, since the episode will be edited and individual exchanges may be cut. Omit anything biographical, anything about the state of the literature, and anything answerable from the book's own examples.

On commitment: do not hedge by surveying options. For each question, predict her reply specifically and give your reason for the prediction; where a case admits of both diagnoses, commit to one and defend it; where you think a question of yours exposes a genuine defect in the account rather than an under-described corner, say so plainly, identify which one, and rank the set by expected yield so that I know what to cut if we run long. If one of the pressure points above is misconceived — if, for instance, a case I have described does not in fact discriminate between the two diagnoses — say that instead of writing me a question you expect to misfire, and supply a replacement.
