I write on science and ideas for a national newspaper, and I am putting together a long read — around two thousand words, for an educated general readership — built around Ursula K. Le Guin's 1973 story The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas. I keep meeting the story as shorthand in arguments about complicity: people invoke it when they want to claim that ordinary consumers cause harms in supply chains they never touch, or that individual emitters cause climate damages that would arrive anyway. What strikes me is that every one of those arguments leans on a single unexamined metaphysical claim, namely that a person can cause an outcome merely by not doing something. I would like the piece to take that claim seriously, and I would like you to read the story through it.

The literal question I want answered is whether the citizens of Omelas are causes of the child's misery, given that what each of them does is fail to act. Please take the two standard ways of handling that seriously in turn: the complaint that an absence is not the sort of item that can stand in a causal relation at all, having no location, no duration, no mechanism and nothing to push against; and the counterfactual treatment, which swaps mechanism for dependence and says that had this citizen gone down and brought the child up, the misery would have ended, at the cost of everything else the city enjoys. Tell me which of the two the story's own terms favour and what each of them costs. Then notice the shape of the case: each citizen is individually such that, had they acted, the outcome would have been different, so on the dependence view everyone is an actual cause, while the outcome arrives regardless of what any one of them does. Is that a defect in the dependence view or merely a feature of it, and does the story push you towards a notion of actual causation that does work beyond dependence?

I have also been playing with two variations, and I would value your judgement on them, since they seem to me to separate the theories in a way the story itself does not. In the first, change the bargain in one respect only: suppose the city requires that some child or other be kept in misery, and that any citizen who freed this child would simply see another installed in its place, so that no individual's failure makes a counterfactual difference to whether a child suffers at all. Does the dependence view now let everyone off, and does the view that absences cannot be causes do any better? Does the gap between my variant and Le Guin's actual story show that causation by omission is hostage to how the counterfactuals happen to be arranged, or is that simply what causation is? In the second variation, attend to the timing: the young people are not told until they are old enough, and the visit is the moment at which they acquire both knowledge and, presumably, an opportunity. Does an omission begin only when the agent could have known what was at stake? If so, the causal relation appears to switch on with a change in someone's beliefs rather than in the world, which is a strange result, and the story starts to look less like a moral fable than like a claim about the conditions under which failures can be causes. Does ignorance excuse causation as well as blame, and what is the story doing by making the visit the hinge of the whole thing?

Finally, there are the ones who walk away. They do not rescue the child, nothing about the outcome changes, and on one description they are omissive causes like anyone else; on another, something has genuinely altered, since they no longer consent and no longer participate, and perhaps the right account of the citizens' role all along was not omission but participation in a practice that reproduces the misery — in which case leaving changes their causal standing while leaving the outcome untouched. I want your view on which is correct, and on whether the story is best read as showing that the causal question and the practical question come apart: establishing that I cause a harm by my inaction may be the least useful thing I can learn, because it does not tell me whether to stay and fight or to walk. Be equally plain about where the lens fails. Le Guin withholds everything a causal theorist needs — no mechanism, no institution, no account of how the bargain operates, and a narrator who professes she cannot suit every reader — so the dependence is stipulated rather than explained. Is the case too thin to do metaphysical work, or does its thinness reveal that our judgements about omissions rest on features no theory has yet isolated? And does the story, by making the reader's own verdict part of the exercise, dramatise something the argumentative literature misses — for instance that attributions of causation by omission are doing expressive or normative work which a metaphysics of absences cannot underwrite?

I need roughly a thousand to twelve hundred words of continuous prose: no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italic emphasis, no tables — plain running text, as you would write in an email, because I shall be lifting sentences straight into a draft. Please do not quote the story; I have it in front of me and will do the quoting myself, so describe the scenes rather than reproduce them. Do not trouble yourself with citations, named positions or reading lists, as I can supply the apparatus, and keep the vocabulary within reach of a reader who has never studied metaphysics, glossing any technical term in a clause as you go.
