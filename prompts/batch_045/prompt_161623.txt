I teach an upper-level seminar in political philosophy and am preparing a two-page handout for it: a decision tree that walks a student from ordinary judgments about cases to a named position in the contemporary debate about negative, positive, and republican liberty. Please approach this as a political philosopher who has taught this literature many times; work out the logical space first, and discard any candidate splitting question that merely restates a label (nothing like "do you think domination matters?").

Every branch node must be a concrete case the student can answer without knowing where it leads: the slave whose master never interferes; the unlocked door the agent mistakenly believes is locked; the citizen bound by a law she voted against in a well-ordered democracy; the employee legally free to resign who would starve if she did; the person who retains an option only so long as another's goodwill lasts. Ten to twelve leaves.

Two constraints matter to me. First, do not let the non-interfering-master case do all the work: I want students to see that some negative-liberty views can already count that agent as unfree once the right modal profile is built in, so give the tree its own node for how robust an option must be to count as freedom — surviving actual interference, surviving counterfactual interference, surviving another's arbitrary power, surviving the agent's own weakness of will. Second, the republican corner must split at least two ways: unfreedom as exposure to arbitrary power, and unfreedom as failure of civic standing or non-subjection; say plainly whether these two come apart on any case you can construct, and if they do not, say that instead of inventing a difference.

Required leaves: pure negative liberty, an opportunity-weighted negative view, non-domination republicanism, civic-status republicanism, positive liberty as self-mastery, positive liberty as autonomy. Add whatever else the space permits, and mark any combination nobody occupies as unoccupied.

Please give me: "Splitting questions" (~150 words) — the axes in the order they must be asked, with a clause on what each one excludes; "The tree" (~350 words) — a plain-text diagram from root to leaves; "Leaf notes" (~200 words) — one sentence per leaf on its most awkward commitment; "Unoccupied regions and collisions" (~150 words) — empty combinations with a line on why they are empty, and any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate.
