I run a monthly discussion group at my local public library: usually twelve to sixteen adults, mostly retired or between jobs, none of them with any training in philosophy or history, and I have a ninety-minute room booking, no reliable Wi-Fi, no screen, and no budget beyond paper, index cards, envelopes and pens. I have no academic background myself — I work in logistics and have been reading about judgment, decision-making and narrative history on my own for a couple of years, mostly popular books — so please spell out any term of art you use and don't assume I know the experimental literature behind it.

What I want is one session, a game or structured activity rather than a talk, that makes the group feel three things I keep conflating when I read about them. The first is the sense that one had known it all along: once you know how something turned out, you cannot reconstruct what you thought beforehand, and the past reorganizes itself so that the outcome looks as though it was always going to arrive. The second is the storytelling effect: a chain of events that hangs together feels probable because it hangs together, even though every extra link added to the chain can only lower the probability of the whole, and even though the same dossier of facts will support a smooth story in either direction. The third, which I have mainly noticed in myself, is the quiet relabeling: facts that before the outcome looked ambiguous or pointed the other way get recast afterwards as warning signs or as further confirmation, and nobody notices the recasting happening. I would like participants to leave with the sense that a past they had taken to be settled was genuinely open at the time, and that its openness is invisible from inside a finished story.

Please design this around a single historical episode with a cut-off date — a genuine decision point at which more than one outcome was live. The group can pick the episode in advance, but please also propose one or two yourself and say why they would work. Constraints that matter to me:

- Nobody should hear either term, or the name of any bias, until the debrief. The effects have to come out of what participants do, not out of anything I say first.
- I want a sealed-forecast stage whose written record can be produced later, so that people are confronted with their own earlier numbers rather than with my assertion that their judgments moved.
- I would like part of the room charged with building the strongest case for the outcome that did not happen, and I would like the scoring or the structure to reward narrative coherence first and then to reveal that coherence has been trading against probability, so that the most convincing story in the room turns out, on its own numbers, to be among the least likely ones. If that trade-off cannot be built into the rules, say so and give me the nearest thing that can.
- The debrief should map particular moments in the session onto the particular effects, should name what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I especially worry about the one or two people who already know the episode well, and about someone deciding that the counterfactual exercise was a trick played on them — and should tell me how to handle each.

Please be frank about two further things. First, which parts of these effects the activity genuinely cannot generate and which I will therefore have to state outright in the debrief: I would rather know where the game does the work and where I do. Second, where you are guessing: if you are unsure that non-specialist adults will actually behave as the design requires, or unsure of a historical detail you have relied on, say so and show me how to build the session so that it does not collapse if the guess is wrong. And if the three things I have described are not really three things, or if one of them is not a real phenomenon, tell me that plainly instead of designing carefully around a mistake — I would rather run a shorter session about something real.
