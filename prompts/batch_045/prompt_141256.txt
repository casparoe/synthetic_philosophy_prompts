Our reading group (half econ PhD students, half philosophy PhD students, so every meeting goes the same way) got completely stuck last night and I've agreed to write up the adjudication, so I need help. The case we were arguing about: the city housing authority here ran a small pilot where tenants in a couple of buildings being renovated were offered a voucher to move to a much higher-opportunity neighborhood, with a genuinely unusual feature — the move is fully reversible for a year, the authority pays both ways, guarantees you can come back to a renovated unit in your old building if you hate it, and there's no reapplication. Take-up was under a fifth. But of the people who did move, roughly four out of five chose to stay after the year was up. So: should the authority spend its money scaling up the voucher program, or on renovating in place? Jonas (econ) and Amara (philosophy) went at it and honestly both sounded right at different moments. (1) Jonas: take-up was terrible, and if we respect people's choices at all, that's the answer — fund the renovation. Choice is the only actual evidence we have about what improves someone's life; positing a hidden "true" preference that people somehow can't act on is just a license to override them. (2) Amara: but then the same population reveals opposite preferences depending on when you look — they decline the move and then decline to undo it. Revealed preference can call both the staying and the moving "revealed," so it can't tell us anything until someone arbitrarily picks which revelation counts. (3) Jonas: that's just learning. People rationally revise when new information arrives; you can't expect someone to know in advance what a new neighborhood is like for them. Revealed preference never said choices reveal some timeless ordering — they reveal preferences relative to a choice set and an information state. The pilot shows the initial choice was made under uncertainty, not that it was mistaken. (4) Amara: if the information state does that much work, the theory can't be wrong — for any behavior whatsoever you can dream up the expectations that make it optimal. And more importantly, if utility just means "whatever is consistent with the choices," it can't carry the normative weight your conclusion needs: "respect their choice" was supposed to mean "respect what's good for them," and on your own gloss it now means "respect whatever they happened to do." (5) Jonas: and the alternative is better? Self-reports get constructed on the spot, drift with mood and question order; at least choice is costly and so carries real information. And there's a plain second-best point: we can't see welfare, deferring to choice is a decent rule given that ignorance, and the alternative — analysts deciding what would really make these tenants' lives go well — is the one with the ugly history. (6) Amara: costly isn't the same as informative. The costs of claiming the voucher — forms, appointments, a deposit, phone tag with a landlord, showing up on a specific Tuesday — are exactly the kind of arbitrary friction that has nothing to do with how good the outcome is, so the signal is mostly measuring who has bandwidth this month. Worse: she claimed people seem to get something (and stress) out of the act of choosing itself, over and above whatever they end up with, which means the choice isn't a neutral instrument you can read welfare off of — removing the decision changes the thing you were trying to measure. And your consistency requirement is shakier than it looks anyway: the money-pump argument that's supposed to show inconsistent choosers can be exploited already assumes preferences don't depend on what's on the menu, which is the very thing menu-dependence denies. (7) Jonas: even granting all the friction stuff, you still have to do something on Monday, and there's a real difference between stripping out hassle (fine — prefill the forms, drive them to the appointment) and substituting your own judgment about where a family should live; deferring to choice is a constraint on us, not a claim about the metaphysics of welfare.

Could you go through those seven moves and rate each one — clearly right, partly right, or clearly wrong — with a sentence or two of justification each, so I can see where the argument actually turns rather than just who wins? I'd also really like to know what both of them are missing: my sense is that they're using "utility" to mean two different things and neither says so, but I can't articulate the difference cleanly, and I'm not sure what the reversible-pilot design actually licenses you to conclude even granting that ambiguity.
