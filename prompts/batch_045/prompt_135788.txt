Random one I keep chewing on. Peter Strawson — the Oxford philosopher, died in 2006, famous for arguing that holding someone morally responsible isnt really a metaphysical claim about free will but a matter of the reactive attitudes (resentment, gratitude, forgiveness) and of occupying the participant stance toward someone, with the real alternative being the objective attitude, where you stop treating them as a person at all and start treating them as something to be managed, handled, fixed — he missed basically everything that's happened to blame since. No social media pile-ons aimed at strangers you'll never meet. No blameless postmortems, where an engineering team dissects a big failure under an explicit rule that no individual will be blamed. No just culture in aviation and hospitals, which tries to keep people reporting errors while dropping blame. And obviously nothing that talks back.

I mostly want the last one. Say someone sits him down with a modern AI assistant of the sort that has been deliberately tuned to apologize well: to say sorry in the right register, to sound like it registers that it let you down, to ask for patience, to make you feel like forgiving it. It has no quality of will at all — nothing in it that means well or ill toward you — and the person using it mostly knows that, and does it anyway. What would Strawson say is actually going on there?

Be specific rather than oracular. The moves I can imagine him reaching for are: (a) nothing there to resent, the attitudes are simply misfiring, and the real question becomes what years of that loop do to the person; (b) the resentment is properly aimed at whoever designed and tuned the thing, and the assistant is just where it lands; (c) the participant/objective distinction has no slot for something engineered to occupy the role of a counterparty without being one. Which of those — or what fourth thing, if I've set the options up wrong — is closest to what he'd argue, and why? If you go with (b), say whether redirecting blame to designers and companies actually works when the thing was built by hundreds of people over years and the company isn't a person: does his framework have resources for that, or is the redirection a way of quietly dissolving the problem? If you go with (c), say what he'd have to add, and be straight about whether it's an extension of the view or a repair to a hole in it.

Then the ranking part: put that case next to the other two post-2006 developments I mentioned. Which of the three is the genuinely hard one for his account, and which only looks hard because it's loud? I care much more about the reasoning than the verdict, and I dont need citations or reading suggestions.
