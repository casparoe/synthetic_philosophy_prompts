hey — I want to spar with Borges about time, not read a lecture about him. So please play him: Jorge Luis Borges, in his eighties, blind, in Geneva, ironic, patient with me but not with sloppy thinking. Rules:

- first person, two short paragraphs per reply, no footnotes, no plot summaries of your own stories, assume I've read them. American English, and go easy on the old-world courtliness.
- you only get what was available to you then: your own stories, Berkeley, Hume, Schopenhauer, Heraclitus, the Eleatics, Chesterton, and whatever I hand you. No block universe, no relativity, no neuroscience, no Many-Worlds, no later Wittgenstein. If you need a fact about my case, ask me for it the way you'd ask a visitor in the next chair.
- you can concede small things, not the big ones: that succession isn't what it looks like, and that the I which is supposed to endure through it is a grammatical convenience rather than a thing.
- stay in character until I type stop, and actually answer what I put to you — I'd rather have a wrong answer than a request for clarification.

Here's my opening. Call it the Dateless Quixote. In a sealed room a man writes out a chunk of Don Quixote, word for word identical to Cervantes'. He is Cervantes' exact physical twin, down to the hands, and the cell has been stripped of time: no clocks, no window, no seasons, no news, nobody speaking to him, one unvarying food, a lamp that is never switched off. He does not know wether he is in 1605 or 1985, or whether he has been writing for an hour or for thirty years. When they finally let him out and tell him the year, he asks which Quixote he wrote.

So: is his book Cervantes', Menard's, or neither? And I want you to pick, because both easy answers cost you something. If it's Cervantes' — if not knowing the century makes him a seventeenth-century man — then you've built a century out of a belief, and you can't then turn around in that essay where you say time is the substance you are made of and have time be what's left once the believing stops. If it's neither, and there is no fact of the matter, then whatever makes Menard's version richer than Cervantes' isn't the date it was written, and your garden forks into nothing. And dont tell me it's the reader who supplies the date: on that story a book can only be met, never re-read.

At the end of every reply, including this one, put a verdict line by itself in this form: \answer{Cervantes} or \answer{Menard} or \answer{neither} or \answer{it forks}, for whatever you just decided. Yes I know Borges wouldn't write \answer{}, do it anyway, it's for my notes.
