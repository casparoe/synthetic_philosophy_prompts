I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background past one undergrad elective I mostly slept thru, and I've been chewing on this ever since I spent a few weeks embedded with a team in Helsinki last year.

Here's the practice I keep turning over. Finland (and Switzerland, and now again Sweden) doesn't have a professional army. It has near-universal male conscription: basically every guy gets tapped somewhere around 18, serves a few months up to about a year depending on the role, and then stays on the reserve list — as far as I can tell you can still be called back well into your fifties. Sweden scrapped the whole thing and then quietly brought it back a few years later. Switzerland runs it as a militia thing where, historically at least, people kept the rifle at home and your unit was made up of guys from roughly your own area. On top of that you get "total defence": the whole society is supposed to have a wartime role, and I'm told both Sweden and Finland have mailed every household a pamphlet explaining what to do if things go bad.

And the part that actually confuses me: these are among the richest, calmest, most rights-respecting countries on earth, and they are running what looks like straight-up forced labor — the general ban on forced labor has a carve-out for military service, which to me looks like a suspicious coincidence rather than a principled distinction. They'll coerce you into learning to kill people, but if your conscience objects you can do a longer civilian stint instead, and if you refuse even that there are still real penalties. They described themselves as neutral for decades while training the entire male population for war. And now Finland and Sweden have both joined NATO in the last couple years — they dropped the neutrality but kept the conscription, which makes me think the "armed neutrality needs a nation in arms" story was never the real justification.

So: what does this package actually presuppose?

I don't want a history lesson. I want the implicit theory reconstructed, and I want to know which parts of it the practice strictly needs versus which parts just happen to fit. My guesses, tell me where they're wrong: that political membership carries non-optional duties, not merely a duty not to harm people; that it's unfair to outsource the dirty dangerous work to poorer people or to foreign contractors (my team does on-call like this — everyone rotates, nobody gets to dump it on the junior contractor); that who serves determines which wars get fought, so a citizen army makes expeditionary wars harder to start; that what deters an invader isn't the army's combat power but the expected cost of occupying a country where everyone has been trained. If that last one is right, isn't it making the civilian population itself part of the deterrent, which is basically what the laws of war say you're not allowed to do?

And here's the bit I want you to actually grind on. Suppose someone proposed replacing universal conscription with a random lottery where a small fraction of each cohort serves full-time for good pay, plus a two-week annual drill everyone else has to attend. By symmetry that's fair, by sampling it's representative, and it costs way less coercion. I have a feeling this proposal misses the point — that universality as such is doing the work, i.e. the institution only makes sense if essentially everyone went through it, so it's a shared social fact and not a burden-sharing scheme. If that's the story, spell out what it commits you to and whether it's coherent, given that in practice only a slice of each cohort serves and plenty of people get excused or do the civilian thing. Then tell me what would concretely have to change about the practice if you swapped in the rival view — say, the view that defense is just a job like any other and the labor market should staff it.
