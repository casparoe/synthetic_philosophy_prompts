You are a political philosopher with experience advising public bodies — a conceptual consultant to legislators rather than a lecturer. Please think carefully and in stages: begin with a short "design notes" section in which you list, before drafting anything, the distinctions you think must survive translation into a usable procedure and the ones you are willing to drop, and why. Only then produce the procedure itself. Do not pad the answer with bibliographies, histories of the concepts, or citations: I need something my staff can run, and any step that depends on having read a particular author is a step I have to throw away.

The setting: I head a small scrutiny unit inside a regional government. Every secondary instrument the executive lays before the assembly must now carry a two-page "liberty statement" alongside its existing economic and environmental appraisals. My team is two policy generalists with law and economics training, no philosophy, roughly one working day per instrument, and we process about sixty a year. Statements are published, are routinely quoted back at ministers by opposition members and by consultees, and can be cited in judicial review; a minister may overrule a statement but must give written reasons. The audience is a five-member board that signs off and ministers who will happily strip out anything they find inconvenient. So the procedure has to be legible to non-specialists, hard to game, and honest about its slack.

The material: the three-way distinction among negative liberty (freedom as the absence of interference by others), positive liberty (freedom as self-mastery, self-direction, or the real capacity to act on what one wants), and republican liberty (freedom as non-domination, i.e. not being subject to another's power of arbitrary interference). You should assume my analysts know these labels and nothing more.

What I want from you:

1. Fix the operative readings before you write the steps. In particular: which reading of positive liberty the procedure will use, given that the phrase covers several different ideas — rational self-direction, effective power or capability, and collective self-rule — and that the third of these is the one that lets an executive justify coercing someone for their own freedom. Say what your choice costs you. Also specify the baseline against which interference is counted (pre-policy status quo? counterfactual of no government action? some moralized baseline?) and how an analyst decides, since this choice silently drives verdicts.

2. The procedure itself: an ordered set of gates or questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates with a negative finding, and where it hands the decision to a human being because the issue is one of values rather than facts. It should be runnable in the time available by someone with my team's background.

3. A scoring discipline that refuses to aggregate. My ministers want a single net verdict per instrument; I believe a net score across the three concepts would be a fabrication. Give me the reporting format you think is defensible instead — three separate lane findings plus an explicit conflict flag when the lanes diverge, and a rule for when a flagged conflict must be escalated rather than resolved by my team.

4. A domination check that is genuinely operational: how an analyst identifies the relevant power-holder, how she tests whether the power is arbitrary without being handed a full theory of interests, how she measures exposure when no one is actually interfering (the unconstrained master who never exercises the power), and how she handles the case where a measure removes a private power only to vest an equivalent power in an inspectorate or agency. Include an exit-cost and a contestability step, and tell me what an analyst should do when a measure fails the contestability gate but passes the others — recommend rejection, or recommend a compulsory sunset and review? Is that second option an application of the theory or a political fudge?

5. An anti-capture guard. Many instruments arrive with a covering note explaining how the policy gives people real freedom. Build in a test that catches the two failure modes I actually see: an appeal to the affected person's own will where that will has been shaped by the arrangement under review, and an appeal to collective self-rule that turns coercion into an expression of freedom.

Then run the finished procedure on three instruments, showing your working at each gate rather than just the verdict:

A. Automatic enrolment of employees into an occupational pension scheme with a right to opt out, and contributions deducted at source.
B. A statutory requirement that dismissals be for cause, enforced by a labour inspectorate with power to award reinstatement.
C. Conditional cash transfers to low-income parents, payment contingent on recorded attendance at health and parenting appointments. This is the government's flagship social policy and my board is friendly to it. Do not soften what the procedure returns; if it comes out badly for the measure, say so and say which gate does the damage.

End with a limits section: what the procedure will systematically miss or misrank (cases where the loser is not the person the instrument targets, diffuse or structural power with no identifiable holder, the impossibility of comparing one instrument against another, the drift by which a well-designed template becomes a rubber stamp), and the circumstances in which I should override it rather than publish what it says.
