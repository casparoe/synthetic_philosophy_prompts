I'm in a reading group that's doing Hume's essay on the standard of taste next week — me, two philosophy grad students, and my flatmate who does psychometrics and keeps asking what the dependent variable is even supposed to be here. The thing we keep going in circles on is the mismatch between his opening move (beauty is no quality in things themselves, it exists only in the mind that contemplates them — I'm paraphrasing badly from memory, so don't hold me to the wording) and the rest of the essay, where he more or less tells you which works really are better, on the authority of critics picked out for delicacy of taste, practice, comparison, freedom from prejudice and good sense. Half of us thinks the second half quietly takes back the first.

To stop us spinning I tried to turn it into a bet, and my flatmate invented a study to pin it down — call it the Meridian study, and just take the results as given. 300 critics from 11 countries, all with 10+ years professional experience, screened for delicacy with a fine-grained perceptual discrimination task, no financial stake in any of the works. Three sets of 60 objects: Ming-dynasty porcelain, twelve-tone serial music, and procedurally generated images nobody had seen before. Everyone got 60 hours of exposure spread over six months, and half were randomised into an art-historical briefing. Results: rank agreement was high for the porcelain (around 0.7), essentially chance for the serial music, and for the generated images it started at chance and climbed to roughly 0.25 with exposure. In a second, balanced condition each object was shown with either a prestigious or an obscure attribution; mean ratings moved about 0.4 standard deviations with attribution, but the ordering was largely preserved (around 0.8 between conditions). Two-year followup: individual verdicts on the porcelain were stable, verdicts on the images drifted. And the delicacy screening score predicted agreement on the porcelain but not on the other two sets.

So here's the proposition I want a number on: that there is a substantial domain of objects over which Humean ideal critics would converge, and that this convergence is explained by features of the objects themselves rather than by shared training, prestige cues, or cultural coordination. Give me your credence in that, decomposed — I don't want "it depends what you mean by objective," I want the ambiguity split into terms with numbers on them. My suggestion for the split: whether convergence of this kind is genuine rather than an artifact of how the judges were selected and measured; whether convergence, where it happens, tracks response-independent features of the objects rather than a shared learned schema; whether the vetting loop is fatal (my flatmate's point: if you identify ideal critics partly by the durability and agreement of their verdicts, agreement among them can't be independent evidence for anything); how much it matters that the convergence is domain-restricted — porcelain yes, serial music no — rather than general; and whether "objective" as Hume needs it is even the sort of thing that convergence plus verdict-independence would establish. Use your own split if mine is wrong. For each term: a rough number and one line of why. Then combine them, tell me which term you're least confident in, which sub-question the final number is most sensitive to, and what single further result — from a study you'd actually want run — would move it furthest in either direction.

Give me your own number before you take the Meridian results into account too, if you can seperate the two, and then tell me where you think our three numbers actually diverge — which term we're implicitly disagreeing about, not just which of us is closest to you.
