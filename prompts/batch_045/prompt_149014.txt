My mom's had a cochlear implant for about eight years and last spring she got into a trial for a retinal prosthesis, so she jokes she's already a few percent cyborg, and the whole mind uploading debate has stopped feeling like pure sci-fi to me. I got into an argument about it at a barbecue last weekend with a friend who works at a neural interfaces startup, and I want to write it up properly before I lose the thread. His position was that the usual copy objection is the only serious one, and that it entirely dissolves if you do the replacement gradually: swap one neuron at a time for a silicon unit that does exactly the same input-output job, wait a week, swap another, and so on. There's never a moment where there are two of you, so there's no branching and nothing for a theory of personal identity to have to adjudicate, and no single swap can be the one where you stop existing, because the difference between the brain before and after any given swap is smaller than the difference between the brain you have now and the one you had last tuesday after two glasses of wine. So, he says, whether the finished upload is you is settled by construction, and the people still arguing about it are just fighting over the word you.

The bit I added, which I think is what actually kills the last surviving objection, is that you should do the swaps under general anaesthesia, and do them as a small randomly scattered fraction of the whole brain per session rather than region by region, so no functional subunit is ever offline at once. Then there is no experience happening at the moment of the swap at all, so nothing can fade out mid-procedure the way that fading qualia argument is supposed to worry about — there's nobody home to notice — and instead you just check afterwards: she wakes up, reports completely normal visual experiences, remembers the conversation from before the surgery, passes the same battery of tests, and you do it again next month. If after two hundred sessions the reports and the behaviour are indistinguishable from baseline, I don't see what's left to dispute, and it seems to me the only way to resist that inference is to say that verbal reports aren't evidence about experience at all, which would wreck psychology and neurology generally.

I want to structure the piece around exactly which objection survives this setup, so: is the duplication worry really the only one, and does the gradual version genuinely handle it, or is there something in the neighbourhood I'm not seeing? And relatedly, is there a definately coherent version of the story where the procedure goes fine by every external measure and yet the person doesn't survive it, and if there is, how would anyone ever tell the difference?
