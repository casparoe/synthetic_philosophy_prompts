I am convening a small interdisciplinary workshop on the ethical significance of sentience at the fundamental level, and I want participants to complete a self-placement instrument individually before the first session, so that the discussion can begin from where their answers diverge rather than from which labels they prefer. I therefore need a decision tree, not an essay.

Please build it in the following way.

Start from the logical space, not from a list of names. Work out which independent questions separate the possible views, which combinations of answers those questions permit, and only then locate the actual positions — panpsychism in its micro-subject form, cosmopsychism, panprotopsychism, Russellian monism, illusionism or other strong denials, threshold or complexity views about valence, and the "emergency" view on which suffering at the fundamental level dominates all other moral concern — in the resulting space. Where a coherent combination has no defenders, mark the leaf as unoccupied and diagnose briefly whether that is because it is incoherent, because it is merely unattractive, or because it has simply not been explored. Where two named positions cannot be separated by any question in your tree, say so explicitly rather than inventing a distinction that does not do any work.

Every branch node must be phrased as a concrete claim about a concrete case, not as an abstract question that contains the answer's label. A participant should be able to answer each node without knowing which view it leads to, and without having to take a stand on whether words like "sentience" or "suffering" properly apply below the level of organisms — several participants will deny that they do, and the instrument has to sort them too.

I want the tree to include nodes on at least these dimensions: whether the intrinsic nature of fundamental physical entities could be phenomenal at all; what the bearer of a fundamental-level experience is supposed to be (a single entity, a system, the cosmos as a whole, or nothing); whether a maximally simple state could be aversive, or whether negative valence requires some structure or complexity; what the quantity of experience is supposed to track, if anything — the number of entities, the number of physical events or interactions, the degree of complexity, or none of these; whether the total amount of fundamental-level experience is something any agent's action could alter, given that the laws of physics may fix it; and whether moral value aggregates across very many very small experiences or instead depends on intensity within a single one.

Two positions I keep encountering in discussion and want represented as leaves, whether or not they have names. The first holds that even if fundamental experience is real and aversive, the total is fixed by conservation or by the dynamics, so no intervention can change it and the worry is practically idle. The second holds that the total tracks something we routinely change — running a computation, heating a room, rearranging matter — so ordinary actions acquire costs or benefits that no one has begun to account for. If either already has a settled name in the literature, give it and distinguish it from its nearest neighbours; if not, name it yourself and say how it differs from the adjacent leaves.

Give me eight to ten nodes, eleven to fourteen leaves, one sentence per leaf on that position's most awkward commitment, two stress cases your tree handles badly together with an explanation of why, and a key mapping answer patterns onto leaves. No bibliography, no citations, and no reading suggestions.
