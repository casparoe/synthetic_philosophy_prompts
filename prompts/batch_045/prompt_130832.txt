I am trying to work out, by trusting my gut on a series of concrete cases and then asking what principle the pattern answers to, when my individual act counts as contributing to a collective harm in the way that matters morally — that is, when the fact that my act makes no real difference to the outcome defeats the reason against it, and when it does not.

My tentative principle is expected-difference-making: the reason against my performing an act, when I have one, is a function of the difference that my performing it makes in expectation to the total harm, so that where the expected difference is zero the no-difference objection succeeds outright, and where it is very small my reason is correspondingly very small. I set out below the cases that make me doubt it. In each I am one contributor among many to a harm that is produced by the aggregation of contributions like mine.

The continuous case. I drive sixty miles alone to visit a friend instead of taking the train. My trip adds a few kilograms of carbon dioxide to the atmosphere; the effect on any person is real but lost in the noise. My verdict: I have a genuine reason to take the train, but a weak one, and I would not reproach myself for driving.

The fixed-contract case. A supermarket sells me a chicken breast. It happens to buy poultry under a twelve-month take-or-pay contract that fixes the number of birds it must accept and pay for, so that nothing I do at the till can alter that quantity, even probabilistically, during the life of the contract. Suppose I know this for certain. My verdict: I still have a reason not to buy it, and not a merely sentimental one. Expected-difference-making says I have none at all.

The necessary-contribution case. Ten neighbours must sign a petition for the council to approve a development that will destroy a wetland; nine have signed and the tenth signature is the only one outstanding. My verdict: signing is clearly wrong, and clearly more wrong than anything in the first two cases. I include this only as a control.

The threshold I am unlikely to reach. The same petition, but it needs ten thousand signatures and nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-eight are already in. From what I know of the neighbourhood I would put the chance that two more people sign before the deadline at well over ninety per cent. My verdict: a strong reason not to sign, much stronger than a few per cent of the harm to the wetland, and much stronger than any expected-value calculation I can construct seems able to deliver.

The trigger with an override. The supplier reorders in lots of ten thousand birds whenever the counter passes a multiple of ten thousand, and the store's app shows me that the counter stands at nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine. If I buy, ten thousand more birds are raised and slaughtered. If I do not buy, the customer behind me buys the same breast tomorrow and the same ten thousand birds are raised and slaughtered a few days later; I am as confident of this as I am of anything about the future. So the only thing my purchase changes is the timing. My verdict: I still should not buy it, and this moves me more strongly than the fixed-contract case does, even though in neither case do I change how many birds die. I cannot see why, and I would like the principle either to explain the asymmetry or to tell me to drop one of the two verdicts.

The good that will happen anyway. A neighbour's surgery is being funded online; the goal is fifty thousand dollars, the total stands at forty-nine thousand nine hundred and eighty, and I happen to know that a local church has pledged in writing to cover whatever shortfall remains at midnight. My verdict: my twenty dollars is pointless, and I would feel faintly ridiculous giving it, as though I were pretending to matter. This is the mirror image of the contract and trigger cases: the outcome is fixed either way and my contribution is causally inert. Yet here the inertia seems to dissolve the reason, while there it does not. I want to know whether that asymmetry can be defended or whether one half of it has to go.

The attributed share. My country's annual emissions are enormous and cause serious harm; I am one of some eight million residents and my own footprint is about average. My verdict: I bear responsibility for my own tonnes and not for an eight-millionth share of the national total, even though it is the national total that does the harm and even though allocating shares would be far easier to administer. I am not confident this verdict is coherent.

The recorded and the unrecorded purchase. The same chicken breast again, except that the store's loyalty scheme feeds my purchase into a demand-forecasting model with a two-week lag, so that buying does slightly raise expected future orders, while paying cash leaves no trace and changes nothing downstream. My verdict: I feel exactly the same about the two. If that is right it seems awkward for expected-difference-making, which must distinguish them, but even more awkward for any view that grounds my reason in what my act expresses or signals, which distinguishes them more sharply still.

What I would like from you is a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, meeting four conditions. First, state it precisely enough that I could apply it to a case you have not seen. Second, motivate each clause by the case that forces it on me, so that I can see what the principle would lose if the clause were dropped. Third, give me its verdicts on three cases I have not listed: the case in which I sell my car to a buyer who will drive it exactly as much as I would have; the case of a co-operative of two hundred members voting on whether to switch to a supplier whose practices are harmful, where the motion carries on a hundred and one votes, where I expect about a hundred and fifteen members to vote yes, and where my own yes is mildly in my financial interest; and the realistic version of the chicken case, in which I cannot discover whether the supply chain works like the fixed contract, like the trigger, like the forecasting model, or continuously, and could not find out even with effort. Say whether the principle gives me the same guidance under that ignorance as it would if I knew the mechanism, and whether it should. Fourth, tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and would require me to abandon.

If no single principle satisfies all four conditions, give me the two or three best candidates, say which verdict each sacrifices, and tell me which sacrifice you take to be cheapest and why. My preference throughout is for a principle I could actually apply while standing in an aisle deciding what to buy, over one that is elegant but tells me to go and find out how the supplier's ordering system works.
