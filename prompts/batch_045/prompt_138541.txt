I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm finishing a piece — scheduled for the issue after next, and already past my first deadline — on the way popular cosmology borrows the vocabulary of metaphysics. The specific move I keep hitting, in trade books and podcast interviews alike, is the claim that multiverse physics has more or less vindicated David Lewis's modal realism: every way the world could be is a way some universe in the multiverse already is, the word 'actual' turns out to be a merely indexical label for the branch or bubble we happen to inhabit (the way 'here' and 'now' pick out a place and a time without marking them as metaphysically special), and so modality has been naturalized — possibility becomes existential quantification over concrete things, and the long contest between modal realism and actualism gets settled by observation rather than by argument. One cosmologist I interviewed put it by saying that metaphysics was simply the last science to get its own telescope.

My difficulty is that the analogy is doing a great deal of quiet work and I can't tell how much of it is load-bearing. The multiverses actually on offer seem to come in at least two shapes: branching ones, in which all the histories share a common ancestor and the same fundamental laws and differ only in how things turn out, and landscape ones, in which different regions realize different effective laws or constants within one overarching framework and, in most versions, one spacetime. Lewis's pluriverse is neither: its worlds are spatiotemporally and causally isolated from one another, they are not jointly governed by any law, and the point of the machinery is to underwrite the full breadth of metaphysical possibility, including possibilities no physics would countenance. So what I want from you is an audit rather than a verdict.

Please lay the correspondence out explicitly — what, on this comparison, plays the role of a world, of a possibility, of necessity, of actuality, of the accessibility relation, and of counterparts — and then say where the mapping breaks, and which of those breaks matter for the particular use the analogy is being put to. I'd also like the disanalogies sorted into the ones that are fatal to that use and the ones that are harmless: I suspect it matters a great deal that any physical multiverse can deliver at most nomological possibility, and that the indexical treatment of 'actual' may establish only that actuality is not detectable from inside a world, which is not the same thing as there being no metaphysical difference; but I am much less sure about what the analogy smuggles in elsewhere, and I'd like your read on whether it quietly assumes that all these universes are concrete in one and the same sense, that they could in principle be enumerated, that cross-universe existence claims are all of a piece, or that once you grant one kind of plurality the old parsimony objection to Lewis falls silent. If it helps to make this concrete, run the mapping against three things people actually want modality for — that there might have been nothing concrete at all, that the fundamental laws themselves might have been different, and that I might have been a different person — since the piece has to be fair to the actualists I've spoken to and I need to know which of those the comparison can carry. Then tell me what I should actually print: is the honest conclusion that physics has confirmed modal realism, that the two projects are simply unrelated, or that the analogy establishes something narrower but real — and if there is a better comparison for what the multiverse really is to modality, I would rather use that, provided I can state it in one sentence my editor will let through. A written reply of roughly seven hundred to nine hundred words, with the formalism kept out of it, would suit me best.
