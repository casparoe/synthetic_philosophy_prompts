My friend and I spent most of last night on Zeno, and he has a version I cannot answer and also cannot accept. He insists it needs no infinite sums and no assumption about space at all, only that time is dense.

(1) A happening that occupies an interval of time has proper initial parts that are themselves happenings occupying intervals: if the candle burns for an hour, its burning through the first half hour is one of its parts.
(2) Every proper initial part has a proper initial part that is completed strictly before it.
(3) So a happening is completed only if some proper initial part of it is completed first.
(4) So there must be a first part, one completed before all the others.
(5) By (2), there is none.
(6) So nothing that takes time is ever completed.
(7) The elapsing of an hour takes time; so no hour ever elapses — and, for that matter, he never finished stating the argument and I never finished hearing it.

He thinks the standard replies miss it. Convergent infinite sums are irrelevant, because he never claims the parts add up to an infinite duration; the sting is only that there is no first one. And going discrete does not help: if the first step of a journey takes time, then it has parts and the argument restarts; if it takes no time, then it is over in an instant, and nothing moves at an instant.

I would rather keep (1) and (2), and I cannot see how time could fail to be dense, so (5) is not where I want to cut. I also raised a case he brushed off: an interval with no earliest instant but only a lower limit, open at the start — that hour seems to me plainly to elapse, and he said it is not a happening at all but a mere aggregate of them, which felt like a dodge.

Which step do you reject, and why did it look innocent? What does each escape cost — denying that extended happenings have temporal parts, denying that they need a first one, denying that time is dense, or denying that the passing of time is itself something that takes time? And once the argument is repaired, is anything left that is specifically about motion, or was the paradox never about space in the first place?
