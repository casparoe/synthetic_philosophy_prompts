I write mystery fiction, mostly classic whodunnits, and I'm in a critique group that argues about this constantly, so I figured I'd ask people who do this sort of thing for a living instead of going back and fourth with them.

The norm we all enforce is fair play: the author can't hold back a clue and then have the detective pull it out of his pocket in the final chapter. Readers feel robbed when that happens, and they say so loudly. But two things about the norm are definately odd and I can't get the group to agree on what's going on.

First, nobody thinks the reader is supposed to actually solve it. Authors brag about readers being surprised, and a reader who guessed it on page forty is a little disappointed, not triumphant. So the norm demands that the text put you in a position to work out the answer while fully expecting that you won't, and it blames the author and not the reader when you don't. That can't just mean the conclusion follows from what got printed: from any pile of clues an infinite amount follows, nearly all of it junk, and nobody thinks that handing over the clues means handing over everything that follows from them by some chain or other.

Second, we hand the detective vastly more inferential horsepower than the reader. It's completely fine that the inspector sees that a smudge on the envelope means the letter was steamed open, because he's a career policeman and you're a person on a train. So the norm seems to insist on informational equality while cheerfully assuming inferential inequality. I want to know whether those two halves are even consistent.

And here's the part that makes me think the norm is doing something different from what we say it's doing. In a whodunnit, a concealed clue is a foul. But in a locked-room or impossible-crime story, we're perfectly happy to be told at the end that the mechanism involved a trick we had no way of guessing, and we don't call that cheating at all. Same readers, same year, opposite reaction.

My own guess, which the group hates, is that fair play was never about entailment. It's about there being a shortish chain from the printed text to the solution, where each step is the kind of move a competent reader would think of making: a bound on path length and on salience, not on consequence. If that's roughly right, tell me what it commits me to. If it's wrong, tell me what the real commitment is.

So: what does the fair-play norm presuppose about knowledge or information? Reconstruct the implicit account, say what the practice strictly requires as opposed to what it merely fits well with, flag where several different accounts would license the same norm, and say what the norm would have to turn into if we took seriously the standard formal treatment on which knowing something automatically means knowing everything that follows from it. I don't need the label for any of that and I can't chase down papers from where I live, so no reading lists.

Give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences. No intro, no wrapping-up line.
