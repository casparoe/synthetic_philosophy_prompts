In my HCI reading group everyone reaches for the same picture whenever offloading comes up: your phone is just an external hard drive your brain happens to carry around, a filing cabinet with better indexing, so nothing is lost when you let search or autocomplete hold a fact for you, memory has simply relocated outside the skull, and the extended mind literature gets cited as what makes that respectable. It's used to license a pretty relaxed conclusion: offloading is fine, worrying about it is skull-bound prejudice, and those findings where people seem to remember where to find something rather than the thing itself are just the system working as designed.

I want that audited rather than cheered. So first lay out what maps onto what: the drive, the file, the retrieval cue, forgetting, corruption, backup, who owns the cabinet. Then where the mapping breaks. I especially care about two cases the canonical notebook-and-Alzheimers case doesn't really cover.

First, a store that talks back: an assistant that not only holds entries but generates new ones, quietly deletes the ones you havent touched in a while, surfaces things you never wrote, keeps most of it as weights you can't read or quote, and is tuned to be trusted. Does the parity argument still get off the ground here, and which of the usual trust-and-glue style conditions does it actually satisfy or fail: easy access, automatic endorsement, reliability, having been checked in the past? Does it matter that the endorsement is partly manufactured and that you can't inspect the entry?

Second, a store that's shared: a team knowledge base everyone writes to and reads from, where no individual could recall most of it and half of it was written by people who've left. If the mind extends here, whose is it?

Then the bit I actually care about: does the relaxed conclusion survive? I can see it surviving the metaphysical claim, that being outside the head isn't automatically disqualifying. But being constitutive of cognition and being good for the person doing the cognizing look like different claims, and I want to know whether the hard drive picture was doing all the work in sliding between them. Also tell me which features of an actual hard drive the analogy smuggles into the store: that it doesn't rewrite your files, doesn't decide what to surface, doesn't have interests of its own, doesn't change how you retrieve. And if its a bad picture, give me a better one. Table of correspondences is fine.
