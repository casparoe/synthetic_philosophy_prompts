I am a graduate TA in a first-year survey of modern moral philosophy, and I have one fifty-minute section in which to do justice to Adam Smith's machinery of sympathy and of the impartial spectator. The students will have read Part I of The Theory of Moral Sentiments (the material on sympathy, on the concord of sentiments, and on the degrees of passion that spectators can go along with) and the opening of Part III, where the spectator turns inward; they will not have read Part II on merit and demerit, nor Part V on custom and fashion. They have read no Kant. Their only prior contact with Smith is the invisible hand and the claim that we get our dinner from the self-interest of the butcher and the brewer. I have the theory reasonably well in hand, so please do not explain Smith to me: what I want is a plan for teaching him, and I want it in 600 words or fewer — a tight spine with reasons attached is worth more to me than a full lesson script.

What I am after is the dependency structure: what must be in place before what, and why. My provisional spine is to present sympathy first as a mechanism rather than a virtue — the claim that I come to feel with you by imagining myself into your situation, not by catching an emotion from you — then to generate propriety out of the mismatch cases, the passions a spectator cannot enter into, rather than out of any rule, and only then to move the spectator inside the agent and treat conscience as the verdict of that inner tribunal. Two placement questions I cannot settle alone. First: where should the spectator's two verdicts be split apart — whether the passion is proportionate to its cause, versus whether the passion is owed to the person it is aimed at, the propriety side and the merit side, the second of which is not in their reading? Second: where in the hour should I make the move from what the people around me actually approve to what an impartial spectator would approve, given that the students will supply that move on their own and get it wrong?

I have been turning over two further things. One is a pair of readings of the inner spectator that I want adjudicated as a teaching decision: the surrogate reading, on which he is simply my representation of the people who actually watch me and whose approval I need, and the fixed-point reading, on which he is the standpoint you arrive at by repeatedly correcting for the partiality, ignorance and interestedness of actual spectators. The second reading seems to me to be what makes the account moral rather than sociological, but I suspect that opening with it will make the students think I have smuggled in a universal rational agent from somewhere else. Should one of them anchor the session and the other be demoted, and if so which, and where does the demoted one belong? The other is the calibration worry: if the inner spectator is assembled out of internalised actual spectators, and those spectators are as partial as I am, then what does the correction push off from? I can either spring this at the end as my diagnostic, or defuse it in passing, and I cannot tell which does less damage.

I also need one example to carry the whole fifty minutes, and I would like you to say what it must contain and what it must not. It has to let the students feel the difference between failing to enter into someone's passion and judging that their passion is aimed at the wrong person, and it has to leave room for the thought that the bystanders' actual reaction is not itself the verdict. I assume it should not involve death or bereavement, since that will make them hear sympathy as pity and miss that Smith's mechanism ranges over any passion whatever; I assume too that its outcome must not be settled by a rule they already accept. What other properties must it have, and what must it avoid?

The misconceptions I expect, roughly in the order in which I expect them, are these: sympathy as compassion or as altruism; the inner spectator as conscience internalised from social approval, the reading that quietly turns the whole account into sociology; "impartial" as meaning abstract or universal in the way a law is; and, late, the relativist objection that if the standard is what spectators approve, no entire society can be wrong. Tell me where each must be headed off and, where you can, what has to be established before the correction will take. Tell me as well what I should openly decline to cover in fifty minutes and say out loud that I am declining — I would rather name the omission than botch it. Close with a single question I can put to the room at the end that would show me they have the machinery rather than the vocabulary.
