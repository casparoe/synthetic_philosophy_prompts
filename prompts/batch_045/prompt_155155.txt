I work as a backend engineer on a live-streaming platform, and a few months ago an informal reading group I belong to (nobody in it has any philosophy training) worked through Rousseau's reply to d'Alembert, who had suggested that Geneva should build a theater. I came away both struck and confused by it, and I have been trying to use it as a lens on a product decision I actually have to make, so I would like you to reason it out with me rather than summarize him. My team is choosing between four designs for what a person sees when they open the app. Option A is the industry default: a personalized recommendation feed tuned to maximize watch time. Option B is a town-square mode: a shared schedule, so that everyone in a given city at a given hour is offered the same handful of streams and can talk about them with neighbors and co-workers afterward. Option C is a prototype we have built but not shipped: small co-presence rooms, maybe thirty to fifty vetted viewers, where a streamer simply goes about their evening — cooking, repairing a bike, doing homework — and viewers can react, tip, and chat, with the chat occasionally steering what happens next. Option D is more speculative: a synthetic performer, an avatar streamer whose persona is tuned per viewer, always available, never tired, and able to remember what each individual told it last week, so that each of millions of people has the experience of being personally recognized by someone.

What I would like is your best reconstruction of how Rousseau would rank these four, from most to least corrupting, together with the parts of his argument that actually do the work in that ranking — the worry that spectators are passive while a few people perform, the claim that a life spent being watched for pay is a degraded one, the idea that amusement manufactures needs and then presents itself as the cure for them, and his insistence that people should be together outdoors, doing something, with no one set apart to be looked at. Please press on the places where my intuitions keep flip-flopping. Is the co-presence room the thing he would hate least, because it resembles sociability rather than spectacle, or the thing he would hate most, because it is the most complete substitution of watching for living and the purest form of depending on the regard of others? Does the synthetic performer strike him as worse than a human actor, since his objection to actors was precisely that they trade in simulated sentiments for money, and here is an entity that does nothing but simulate recognition — or as somehow better, because at least no human being is being corrupted by playing the part? Does it change his verdict that in my world the audience is also the cast, so that what he feared for a small despised profession now applies to nearly everyone? And what would he actually propose in place of all four: if he wanted public festivals with everyone participating, what does that become for a population of millions who cannot assemble in one square — does Option B capture it, or is physical co-presence doing irreducible work in his argument? Two further wrinkles I would like handled explicitly. First, my colleagues point out that Rousseau wrote plays and an opera himself and moved in fashionable Parisian circles; is there a distinction he can appeal to there (writing versus performing, pleasing the public versus living at its pleasure), or would he simply be exposed as a hypocrite? Second, someone in the reading group said he conceded that spectacles may be tolerable where a people's morals are already corrupted, since they at least keep idle hands from worse things; if that is right, would it make him shrug at all of this, or does that concession operate at the level of a whole corrupt people rather than as a license to introduce a new corruption into one?

Please keep it to roughly eight hundred words and argue from the substance of his positions rather than from secondary literature: I have no way to check sources from where I am, so I do not want citations, named commentators, or reading suggestions, and I would rather you told me plainly which of your predictions you consider well grounded in what he actually argued and which are extrapolations into territory he could not have anticipated. It would also help if you flagged any point where my framing of his view is itself mistaken, since I suspect I have been reading my own product worries into him.
