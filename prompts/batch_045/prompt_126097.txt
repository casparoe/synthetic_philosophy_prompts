Two people whose argument I have watched for two years have agreed to stop arguing and write something together. Call them the demarcationist and the anti-exceptionalist.

The demarcationist holds that there are identifiable method-level faults that cluster in pseudohistorical work and that a piece of work displaying several of them is, other things equal, not tracking the past: an inverted burden of proof (the heterodox claimant demands that orthodoxy disprove the claim rather than supporting it); treating silence in the record as positive evidence; protecting a core thesis with ad hoc auxiliary hypotheses rather than abandoning it; inferring from superficial resemblance between artefacts, myths or place names; and accounting for professional disagreement by positing a conspiracy or a taboo instead of answering the objection. The anti-exceptionalist replies that no such list can be formulated so that it catches the heterodox programmes without also condemning respected historiography, which also begins from a thesis, argues from silence, leans on auxiliary hypotheses, argues from resemblance, and imputes ideological motives to rival historians; what really separates the two is social location — credentials, journals, citation networks, hiring — and though that machinery does correct error, it also defends orthodoxy and has repeatedly delayed revisions later absorbed into the mainstream.

Their three test cases are Atlantis, ancient-astronaut claims about megalithic and Egyptian construction, and the Fomenko-style "New Chronology" that compresses and re-orders the historical timeline. As far as I can tell these differ in one respect that may turn out to matter: the first two are largely ad hoc at the site level, while the New Chronology at least presents itself as resting on technical recomputation of astronomical records, so that some of the dispute with it has been conducted on shared evidential terrain rather than purely about method.

Write the document they should have produced. Use the following structure.

1. A numbered Agreed Claims section: everything both can sign, including jointly acceptable one-paragraph descriptions of what each of the three programmes actually asserts and of the current state of the evidence, phrased without either "refuted" or "suppressed". Each claim should be strong enough that some reasonable outsider would deny it; if a claim is something neither side could possibly have contested, leave it out or say why it earned a place.

2. A disagreements table. Columns: the crux; the demarcationist's own formulation, in wording a demarcationist would sign; the anti-exceptionalist's own formulation, likewise; and, for each, the single sentence the other side would insist on striking.

3. For each crux, one test, case or piece of evidence both agree would bear on it, together with each side's prediction recorded in advance, and an explicit statement of what that side would concede if the result came out against it.

The cruxes I would expect to matter include at least these, though add or split them as the drafting requires: (a) whether the demarcationist's checklist separates the three programmes from consensus historiography when applied by readers who do not know a document's provenance, and whether such blinding is even coherent, given that you cannot strip a historical argument of its dates and place names without destroying the evidence it turns on; (b) whether demarcation is a claim about process or only a downstream judgment about the reliability of outputs — if the latter, part of the dispute may be verbal; (c) when, if ever, an appeal to disciplinary bias is legitimate, and what distinguishes it from a conspiracy claim; (d) what each side should say about a hypothetical heterodox programme that makes a novel, checkable prediction — say, the location or date of an as-yet-unexcavated feature — which then comes true, while the programme otherwise keeps its usual method; (e) what either side would expect to happen to the three programmes over a thirty-year horizon in which nobody engages them in a venue their adherents accept.

For (a) I want the audit designed concretely rather than gestured at: specify the dossiers (short anonymised summaries drawn from consensus historiography, from the three programmes, and from work currently contested inside the profession), who rates them, how provenance is masked, what counts as a hit or a miss, and what each side predicts about the pattern of ratings — including what each predicts the other's panel will do. Add, as a companion, a mirror-image exercise: the demarcationist's own checklist run over two or three pieces of well-regarded but openly tendentious mainstream historiography, with both sides' predictions.

4. A short section on which parts of their dispute you take to be merely verbal, and why, and a final section on what this document leaves unsettled.

Constraints: reconstruct the two positions from the characterisations above rather than from any literature; cite no works and quote no authorities; give each side formulations its own partisans would accept rather than versions the other side would find easy to defeat. The test of the whole document is that neither of my two friends could refuse to sign it.
