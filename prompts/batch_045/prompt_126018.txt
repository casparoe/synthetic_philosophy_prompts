I teach a third-year jurisprudence seminar and I'm writing this year's final, and I want a Hart/Devlin question that can't be passed by reciting the summary. Last year's version was a disaster: thirty near-identical papers that ran Devlin = the criminal law may enforce the shared morality, Hart = no, harm to others is the only good reason, therefore liberalism. Almost nobody touched the actual crux, which is that Devlin's case is an empirical claim about what holds a society together rather than a claim about how bad the conduct is. So here's what I've been playing with. Invent a jurisdiction: in the Republic of Veredia almost everyone privately approves of some consensual adult practice P but almost everyone publicly disavows it (the survey people have a name for this — pluralistic ignorance — but assume nothing, explain it in the question). The legislature reads the public avowals, bans P, and enforces the ban purely administratively: no criminal trial, no condemnation, no public drama, just quiet automated penalties like licence suspensions and credit downgrades that only the sanctioned person really registers. Ten years later the avowals have hardened into real conviction, and the government points at that as proof the ban held the moral fabric together. Write the question around that, self-contained so nobody needs to have done the reading or cite anything, aimed at 45 minutes and roughly 1000 words.

What I want the question to force: first, that students pry apart the claims that usually get run together — that a society may enforce any moral rule it happens to hold, that it may enforce only the morality it needs in order not to disintegrate, and that moral judgment needs the backing of law to be taken seriously at all. Second, the bit I find genuinely nasty: if enforcement manufactures the very conviction it was justified by, Devlin's premise isn't evidence for his conclusion, it's an artifact of it — so is the Veredia data his best case or a reductio? Third, ask them to separate moral rules that are load-bearing from ones that are substitutable: even granting that a society needs *some* shared morality, most particular rules could be dropped without anything falling apart, and I want students to say what sort of evidence would show that the rule against P is load-bearing rather than decorative, and who carries the burden of proof there. Fourth, the visibility point: does Devlin's argument need enforcement to be expressive and condemnatory, or is quiet compliance enough? And fifth, the reform problem — on this picture a moral reformer is always, by definition, attacking the fabric, so say whether the view has any way to distinguish treason from reform. Don't tell me to look anything up; I'd rather the question lean on the scenario than on anyone's exact wording, and please don't pad the answer with references or reading suggestions.

Give me the question itself, then a model answer of about 800 words, then a marking scheme worth 25 marks built around five must-hit points — say what each is worth and add a one-line note on how students typically get it wrong. And two things about tone: I want you to commit. Where the debate is genuinely contested, pick the side a top answer has to defend rather than writing "some argue X, others Y" and calling that an A; and include one line in the scheme saying what a student who just summarises the harm principle should be capped at, because that's the failure mode I'm designing against. At the end, flag in a sentence or two if there's anywhere you're extrapolating past what the standard arguments actually settle — I'd much rather have a definate answer with the leap marked than a hedge. Oh, and please seperate the model answer and the scheme clearly so I can paste them into the exam pack.
