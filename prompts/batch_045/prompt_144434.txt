I have no academic background in any of this — I studied supply chain management and now spend my days planning warehouse layouts — but for a couple of years I have been reading about intelligence on my own: a bit of psychometrics, a fair amount of machine-learning material, and a lot of arguments with a friend who designs the reasoning tests a large employer uses for graduate hiring. What puzzles me is that the disagreement about what intelligence actually is never seems to move, even though nobody involved appears to be wrong about any fact. The test-centred crowd treat it as a single general capacity that predicts performance across wildly different tasks; people from an education background reply that it is really several separable capacities that a single number flattens into something misleading; and the AI people I read about define it either as goal-achievement across a wide range of environments or as the rate at which a system turns experience and prior knowledge into new skills. I have started testing these against cases I invent: a system that learns any video game from ninety seconds of footage but cannot add two two-digit numbers; a system that needs ten thousand supervised examples to pick items in one particular warehouse and is then superhuman at exactly that and nothing else; a person who is very slow at everything but needs to be shown a new procedure only once; and a model that is excellent at anything phrased as a multiple-choice question and useless at the same material in free text. Every definition gives an answer, the answers disagree, and yet none of the people arguing seem surprised by this or treat it as a crisis.

My own hypothesis — please treat it as a hypothesis and push back on it if it is wrong — is that the word is quietly doing three different jobs at once: predicting how well someone or something will do, explaining why they do well, and settling how much respect or moral consideration they are owed; and that people slide between those jobs in the middle of an argument, so each side is right about its own job and wrong about the others. There may also be a hidden empirical question doing the real work. In humans, performance on quite different-looking tasks correlates positively, which is what makes a single general factor look respectable in the first place, whereas the artificial systems I read about have visibly jagged profiles — one is brilliant at writing working code and hopeless at planning a route, another is the reverse — and maybe that contrast is a fact about how minds get built and trained rather than a fact about intelligence. So: is this a verbal dispute about a word, a substantive dispute about whether there is a natural kind here at all, a clash about what a definition is supposed to do for us, or several different questions being asked under one heading? I would like a diagnosis with reasons rather than a tour of the positions, one that engages with what each side genuinely claims, and an honest account of what would count as progress — a distinction to draw, a measurement to make, a formal result, a better question to ask instead — or else an argument that no progress should be expected, and why.

A self-contained answer of roughly six hundred to nine hundred words would suit me well.
