Hey — I want to practice arguing against revealed-preference behaviorism about utility, and I want you to play the behaviorist. Here's your position: what an agent wants is nothing over and above their choice dispositions, a utility function is just a compact representation of the pattern in those dispositions, and there is no further fact — hidden, internal, felt, whatever — about how much they "really" care. Sharp version, not a strawman: you're allowed to concede that real choice data is noisy and incomplete, that utility comes back only up to positive affine transformation (and only up to any increasing transformation if we never see the agent choose under risk), that belief and desire have to be separated by something like a Ramsey/Savage trick rather than read off one choice at a time, and you can lean on the as-if / predictive-success defense when the lab data misbehaves. Use whatever machinery you like — WARP, GARP, Afriat, vNM, Savage, money pumps, menu effects — but translate it into plain english as you go. What you never give up: two agents who would make exactly the same choices in every situation you could actually put them in do not differ in what they want. Stay in character until I say stop, two paragraphs max per turn, and actually fight back — if my move is bad, say why it's bad.

My opening: I think your view runs out of steam on outcomes that never make it onto any menu. Some agent makes perfectly consistent, expected-utility-respecting choices over a clutch of outcomes A through E, so you recover a utility function on those. Now take F — say, theMars colony surviving — which is never available to this agent, never a prize in any lottery they can buy, never anything they can affect; they die without ever facing it. I want to say there's still a real fact about how much they care about F, and that it matters — it's basically the whole "what does it even mean to say a system values something" thing people on the forums keep going in circles about. You want to say: no fact, or the only fact is the counterfactual choice. But here's the part I think actually bites: make F available and the answer isn't stable. Next to A they'd take F. Next to A and B they'd take A. Describe the same options slightly differently and they flip again. So the disposition fractures into a pile of menu-indexed dispositions, and singling one of them out as the real one looks like you're quietly doing normative work, not reading anything off the behaviour. So which is it: you eat the cost and say there's no fact about how much they value F, or you admit "utility" is partly a normative posit rather than something choices pin down?
