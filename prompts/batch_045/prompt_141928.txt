I sit on the research ethics committee of a large hospital system, and last night I had a long and unresolved argument with a friend — a health economist — about a rule our committee applies, which I can no longer tell is principled or merely a codified quirk of intuition.

The rule works in two tiers. When a risk is what we call modelled uncertainty — we agree on the shape of the probability distribution but not on its parameters — we aggregate by averaging over our uncertainty and put the resulting single number into an ordinary expected-value test. When the risk is what we call open uncertainty — the experts cannot agree on a model at all, so there is no probability that anyone can defend, the situation of the Ellsberg urn in which you are told only that 30 of 90 balls are red and the remaining 60 are black and yellow in some unknown proportion — we stop averaging and evaluate the proposal against the worst case within the range the experts do agree on. And a known mixture is explicitly treated as ordinary risk, not as open uncertainty: if a fair coin will decide whether those 60 non-red balls are all black or all yellow, the rule tells us to treat the resulting gamble as a simple 50/50 risk and to average, even though the coin has not yet been tossed and the composition at the moment of the decision is, as a matter of fact, just as unsettled as in the urn case.

My friend made three charges. First, that the coin case and the urn case are informationally on a par and differ only in the provenance of the numbers, so the rule presupposes that the source or precision of a probability, and not merely its value, can matter to rational choice — which she says is simply the Ellsberg finding promoted into policy, and is a description-sensitivity that counts as a bias by the lights of the very framework we use in the first tier. Second, that averaging and worst-casing differ only in the input: taking the worst case across a set of models is numerically just expected-value maximisation under the most pessimistic member of that set, so either our two tiers are not really two different attitudes, or the rule is committed to something about where the set comes from. Third, she pointed at a wider inconsistency: the law of negligence holds people responsible only for risks that were reasonably foreseeable, and informed consent requires disclosure only of known risks, so unknowable risk seems to be weighed at nothing there — while the precautionary principle, as it gets used in environmental and health regulation, does the opposite and treats the very absence of defensible probabilities as a reason to restrict.

What I want is the implicit theory, not a verdict on our rule in the abstract. Reconstruct the view of rational choice under uncertainty that the two-tier rule presupposes, and separate what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Does treating open uncertainty by worst case while treating modelled uncertainty by averaging commit us to ambiguity aversion being rationally permissible or even required, as opposed to a bias to be corrected? Does the known-mixture clause show that what the rule really tracks is the source or precision of a probability rather than any feature of the world — and if so, is that defensible? Is the two-tier structure a genuine departure from expected-value reasoning at all, given that worst-casing over a set of models is extensionally the same as expected-value reasoning under one pessimistic distribution, or does the departure lie elsewhere: in how the set gets fixed, in what counts as a defensible probability, in the refusal to let merely ambiguous new evidence shrink it? And are the negligence rule and the precautionary principle really in conflict, or do they presuppose consistent things once you distinguish the question of what to do from the question of whom to blame? Finally: if we abandoned the implicit view and adopted my friend's single-probability picture wholesale, what exactly in our rule, and in the wider legal and regulatory pattern, would have to change — and what, if anything, would we lose by changing it?
