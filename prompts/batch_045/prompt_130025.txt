I have been asked to give the closing address at what the college is calling the Last Ledger Hour, and I would like you to write it.

The setting, so the constraints are concrete. Ashfield College is a small liberal arts college where, since 1891, every member of the faculty has been required, in the final week of each term, to carry their class records by hand to the Records Office and to remain there in person until a clerk has entered and initialled them. The practice was instituted after an audit found that marks had been altered in transit between departments and the office; it is a chain-of-custody rule. It became, over the twentieth century, the only occasion on which the whole faculty stood together in one room for an extended period — people queued, sometimes for two hours — and the college's Integrated Studies programme and nearly all of its team-taught courses have been traced by an internal review to conversations that began in that queue. The online records system made the chain-of-custody rationale obsolete sometime after 2004. Last spring the faculty voted, after three years of work by a transition committee, to abolish the practice; the vote was close but clear. Two of the three records clerks, who between them have eighty-one years in the office, are reassigned next month.

About a hundred and twenty people will be in the room: the faculty, still split; the clerks; the president, who championed the abolition and speaks after me; alumni who came back specifically for this; students who have never seen the Ledger Hour and find the whole ceremony faintly absurd; and four or five colleagues who think any solemnity about a queue is self-parody and will be watching me for it. I am the senior member of the philosophy department, thirty-four years here, and I chaired the 2004 review that recommended keeping the practice on chain-of-custody grounds. I now think that ground is gone, and I would like the speech to concede this without theatre.

My own refinement of the Chesterton parable is what I want to be load-bearing, though it must not appear in the speech as a theory. The parable as usually told asks why the fence was put up; that question is answerable from the archive, and here it has been answered, and the answer is that the original reason is dead. The question I care about is what the fence is holding up now — what has quietly come to depend on it. That is a different question, it is not answerable from the archive, and this is the uncomfortable part: it is answerable only by taking the fence down. I take that to cut against the Burkean as much as against the reformer, since the accumulated and unrecorded wisdom that Burke credited to institutions which have survived many revisions is precisely the sort of thing that leaves no documentary trace and is invisible to the people who are presently benefiting from it. The internal review I mentioned was commissioned by the reformers and is, in my judgment, weak — it counted co-taught courses and could not distinguish the queue from a dozen other occasions — but I do not think it is worthless, and I do not want the speech to pretend to a confidence about the magnitude of the loss that nobody has earned.

So the speech has to do something structurally difficult. It must not be a requiem for a quaint tradition, and it must not be a progress narrative; it must not imply that the reform was a mistake, since I do not believe it was and several of the people who made it are my friends and are in the room; it must not scold the students or the sceptics, and it must not reduce the clerks to an illustration; it must say something true about what may be ending without claiming to know that anything is. My own thought is that this leaves exactly one honest register available: the abolition is itself the experiment, the thing's function if it has one will become visible only now that it is gone, and the room can therefore be charged with something rather than merely told something — to notice, over the next few years, what quietly stops happening at Ashfield, and to be willing to name it. If you agree that this is the right move, make it land as affection and as a charge rather than as a lecture; if you think there is a better structure, use it and say why in the note.

Write about a thousand to twelve hundred words, roughly eight minutes, in a register a mixed room can hear without being addressed as an audience for philosophy — no jargon, no named isms, no in-crowd references that my department will enjoy and everyone else will feel excluded by. Then add two short paragraphs, for me rather than for the room, on what the speech deliberately refuses to say, and which of those refusals cost you the most.
