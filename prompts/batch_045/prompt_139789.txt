Hi — I need a decision tree and I've been going in circles trying to build one myself, so I'm outsourcing it.

Context: I'm a 3rd-year PhD student in linguistics, sociopragmatics side, and I'm running one session of our cross-cultural pragmatics reading group on directness and deception — the Dutch directness vs. Japanese indirectness thing, and whether white lies are actually wrong. The group is mixed: two Dutch students, a couple of Japanese students, some Americans, and one philosopher who keeps dragging everything back to the norm of assertion, which is fine but he's dominating. I want to open with something that sorts people by what they say about cases before anyone gets to say the word Grice, because the last two sessions turned into people reciting positions they'd already picked. Not asking for citations or a reading list — we all know the implicature and face-work stuff well enough, I just need the object itself.

What I want, in five sections:

"How to read it" (~60 words). Audience is smart undergrads and grad students who've done a bit of pragmatics but no ethics. Say in one paragraph how to use the tree, and be explicit that it isn't a full binary tree: most branch questions only bite given certain earlier answers, so let paths merge and say which nodes are downstream of which.

"The tree" (~600 words). Start everyone from the same commitment: you're about to say something negative or unwelcome and there's a softer option available. Then branch on questions a student can answer without knowing any position's name. At minimum I want nodes built from these distinctions: whether the utterance is even in the assertion business (versus being a ritual or an expressive gesture, like a phatic noise that isn't there to transfer a belief); whether the case is one where everyone already knows and you're maintaining a shared pretense, versus one where the hearer genuinely lacks what you know; whether a literally-true but engineered-to-mislead version is better than, worse than, or no different from a flat falsehood; whether staying silent or declining to answer is genuinely available here or whether silence itself would speak; what the exchange is even for — accurate information transfer, or keeping the relationship and everyone's face intact with information transfer as just one sub-game; who ends up doing the inferential and emotional work; whether the softening has effects on third parties and on the long-run value of your own speech (if everything you say is softened, your praise is worth nothing and your criticism becomes unsayable); and finally, when your interlocutor's norm differs from yours, whose norm is supposed to govern — yours, theirs, or the local one. I'd also really like a node on blunt self-disclosure versus blunt assessment of the other person, because people run those together: someone can be all for saying that hurt me and dead against saying your draft is bad, or the reverse, and my group keeps conflating them.

Cases to use: a friend's genuinely bad paper draft; a recommendation letter or referee report where softening inflates a currency other people rely on; the trivial high-frequency stuff (something in someone's teeth, an ugly haircut); an explicit tell me honestly, I can take it request; and one cross-norm case, e.g. a Dutch supervisor giving feedback to a student who was trained to read the air. No node should be phrased as do you think honesty is important or anything else where the label is in the question.

"The leaves" (~350 words). Name each endpoint, or call it unnamed if nobody really holds it, and give one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Must appear: no white lies ever but silence is fine; the technically-true-misleading-is-the-honest-compromise view; the misleading-is-just-lying view; polite speech isn't assertion at all; relationship-first contextualism about the truth norm; straight welfare-balancing; bluntness as respect and anti-paternalism; care-first, i.e. bluntness is laziness wearing a virtue label; the view that the whole question is downstream of who is normally expected to do the softening; and accommodationism about whose norm wins.

"Cells nobody lives in" (~200 words). Which combinations of answers the tree allows but nobody occupies, and why each is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. I especially want the one where you refuse to lie, refuse to mislead, and refuse to be silent — and what that does to ordinary talk — and the one where the isn't-an-assertion move is available for every negative assessment, which then lets anyone opt out of every assertoric norm.

"Where it stalls" (~120 words). Flag any pair of positions the tree genuinely can't separate rather than inventing a question to force them apart — I'd rather hand the group an honest dead end than a fake branch.
