I teach a small, two-day workshop on drawing letterforms — a dozen participants, mostly practising type designers, two perceptual psychologists who come back every year, and, on the last evening, assorted partners and friends who arrive for the wine and stay for the reading. The studio has one long blank wall. For the past few years I have ended the workshop by hanging a poem across that wall, one stanza to a panel, and reading it aloud exactly once before everyone goes home. Nobody sees it before that moment, and afterwards the panels go into a flat file, so it has to work in the ear and not only on the page.

This year I want the poem to be about what my students are actually doing when they draw the same letter for the hundredth time: what makes every 'a' an 'a'.

Form first. I want a sestina built on five end-words instead of six: five stanzas of five lines, the end-words rotating by the usual rule — each stanza takes the previous stanza's last end-word, then its first, then its next-to-last, then its second, then the one left in the middle — so the order runs 1 2 3 4 5, then 5 1 4 2 3, then 3 5 2 1 4, then 4 3 1 5 2, then 2 4 5 3 1. A sixth stanza would bring the words back to their opening order. That return is the whole point, so don't write it: five stanzas, stop there, and let the reader feel the cycle closing just past the edge of the poem.

The constraints:

- The speaker is a lowercase 'a'.
- Draw the five end-words from the anatomy of letterforms — bowl, counter, stem, shoulder, terminal, spur, hood, aperture, tail, overshoot, hairline. At least one of the five should name a part that the rounded single-storey 'a' does not have: a hood, an upper arch, a spur. Then in the stanzas where the poem is speaking in the rounded form, the recurring word is a part that isn't there.
- Move through real variation, and get the details right: the double-storey roman 'a'; the rounded single-storey one that italic and cursive use and that is nearly a Greek alpha; a blackletter 'a' that most present-day readers cannot see as an 'a' at all until they are told; a stencil 'a' whose counter has to be bridged; a child's first pencil 'a', written backwards; the same letter at the heavy end of a variable axis, where the counter closes to a speck. Use at least four of these; more is fine.
- The turn, somewhere in the third or fourth stanza, should be the case that strains identity rather than a case that merely illustrates it. My candidate: Cyrillic and Latin each contain a letter that can be drawn in exactly the same way, and they are not the same letter — a drawing can change letters without changing at all. Use that one, or find a better one in the same neighbourhood.
- It should not settle into either easy answer: not that there is a Form of the 'a' which all the instances copy, and not a shrug about family resemblance. If it offers an answer, let it be relational — what stays fixed is not a shape, a measurement, or a list of parts, but a set of relations and contrasts: a bowl that closes here, an opening low on the right, this height against the x-height, a difference from the 'o', the 'd' and the 'e' standing beside it. Which is also why nobody has managed to write down rules that let a machine invent the remaining letters of a style from three examples: style is something you recognise instantly and cannot state, because it lives in how this letter differs from all the others.
- Keep lines to roughly ten syllables, never more than twelve. Each stanza should be able to stand on its own panel, so don't let a sentence die of suspense at a panel break. Rhyme is optional, and quiet if you use it. Use the anatomy vocabulary correctly — there will be type designers in the room and they will hear it.

Give me exactly five numbered stanzas of five lines each, numbered 1 to 5, with no title, no epigraph, no introduction, and no note explaining the form.
