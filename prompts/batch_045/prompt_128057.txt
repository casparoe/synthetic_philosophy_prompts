Last night I argued at some length with a friend who holds that panpsychism is not a metaphysical curiosity but the most urgent item on the moral agenda. His route there is the familiar Russellian one: physics characterizes matter only by its dispositional and structural properties, says nothing about the intrinsic nature that bears them, and so nothing in physics rules out that that intrinsic nature is experiential. He then blocks the usual reductio — that we owe moral consideration to every quark — with a criterion he calls the diachronic irreversibility constraint. On his view, valence is not a property of instantaneous states or of point-sized physical individuals at all; it is a property of trajectories, and only of trajectories that are thermodynamically irreversible and temporally thick enough for a system's later states to be about its earlier ones. Micro-flickers may have a bare feel, but nothing matters to them. What matters, and what therefore carries moral weight, is dissipation: turbulence, frictional contact, electrical resistance, anything with sustained positive entropy production. The upshot he drew, entirely straight-faced, was that the suffering in the universe is overwhelmingly non-biological, that the morally worst object in an ordinary city is a set of brake pads, and that our long-run aim should be to lower the universe's total rate of entropy production — in the limit, to hasten heat death.

I could not locate the exact point at which the argument goes wrong, and I would like to see the absurdity made visible rather than asserted. Please write a mock white paper, roughly 700–800 words, in the house style of a national research council announcing a ten-year, cross-disciplinary Valence Abatement Programme: executive summary, numbered priority research themes, eligibility and reporting requirements, an indicative budget line, and a dry risk register. The humour should come from scrupulous implementation of his criteria, not from caricaturing them — a philosopher who knows the literature should laugh rather than wince, and a reader who doesn't should still follow it. In particular I would like the document to quietly expose: that the Russellian gap licenses the friend's positive claims no more than it licenses their denial; that whether a composite's valence replaces or merely sums its parts' is left unresolved while the budget depends on the answer; that the irreversibility threshold cannot be drawn, since entropy production is continuous and never exactly zero, so the programme's scope creeps to include everything; that the only measurable quantity available is watts dissipated, which by the end has silently become the target rather than the proxy; and that the programme's own numbers make every terrestrial ethical concern negligible by comparison. If there is a point at which his view genuinely escapes the slide to heat death, have the ethics advisory panel record it as a formal dissent rather than omit it.
