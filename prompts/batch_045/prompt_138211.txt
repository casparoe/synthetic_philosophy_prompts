I've been working through theories of consciousness on my own — no philosophy background, just blog posts, some youtube lectures and one used textbook — and I got hooked on the higher-order family: the idea that a mental state of mine is conscious only because some further state of mine represents me as being in it. I built an argument that seems to show the whole approach is nonsense, wich is obviously too strong given how many people defend versions of it. So I'd rather find the bad step than keep the argument.

(1) A state of mine is conscious iff some higher-order state of mine represents me as being in that state. That's the view, more or less.
(2) Higher-order states can be wrong. Phantom limb stuff is the usual example — maybe a bad one, I'm not sure — but here's a cleaner case I made up: a patient whose monitoring system got rewired by a stroke so that whenever her visual cortex is completely idle, it still reports "you are seeing a uniform blue field."
(3) In that case there is no first-order visual state for the higher-order state to latch onto, and yet from the inside it's just like seeing blue. So the higher-order state on its own is doing all the work: it can't be any real relation to a first-order state that makes a state conscious.
(4) Now rerun the case with different content: same machinery, same wiring, but it reports "you are feeling the slow grief of this stone."
(5) Nothing in (3) lets me say no to that, so the patient is now having an experience as of a stone grieving. There is no such experience, and I'd have thought you can't have an experience of a kind your equipment can't deliver.
(6) And there's no principled place to stop — any content at all generates its experience — so the view lets in everything, while at the same time being known for shutting out animals and babies on the grounds that they lack the higher-order states. Too permissive and too stingy at once.
(7) So (1) is false, and consciousness isn't a matter of being represented as being in a state.

I'm fairly sure I cheated somewhere between (3) and (5) — something about "from the inside it's just like seeing blue" doing more work than it should — but I cant put my finger on it, and I also don't see how the view gives up (2), since it needs wrong higher-order states to explain illusions. Tell me which step breaks, why it looked fine, and what each of the other exits costs. If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (2) than (1).
