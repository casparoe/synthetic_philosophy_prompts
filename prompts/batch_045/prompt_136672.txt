I am a software engineer — distributed systems, no formal training in philosophy — and for about two years a small reading group of colleagues and I have been stuck on the substantivalist/relationist dispute about space and spacetime, in the form it takes after Newton's bucket. What I want is a diagnosis of the stalemate, not a verdict on who is right, and I would rather you not hand me a reading list or a bibliography: I have no library access and I don't want to chase references.

The shape of the argument as we have reconstructed it: the substantivalist says the climbing water shows that rotation is not merely rotation relative to the bucket or to the matter nearby, so something else — space, spacetime, the inertial structure — must be doing the work of distinguishing the spinning case from the still one. The relationist replies that the right comparison class is the whole matter distribution, that in a universe containing only the bucket there would be no fact of the matter about whether it spins, and that the substantivalist is positing an unobservable container that earns its keep in no prediction. Three developments seem to us to have been absorbed without resolution: reformulations of classical mechanics in which only relative distances and angles appear; general relativity, which makes geometry a dynamical field and drags inertial frames in an apparently relationist direction while still not giving the Machian what he wanted; and the hole argument, which seems to force the substantivalist either to swallow an indeterminism or to concede that spacetime points have no identity independent of the fields — after which my group cannot say what is left to fight about.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the dispute is two questions under one name. One is empirical and still open: whether the inertial and rotational structure can be recovered from relations among material bodies, or whether it is an extra ingredient the laws must simply posit. The other looks to me like a question about representation — which mathematical object in the theory gets to be called spacetime, and whether calling it a thing adds anything to the relations — and this has the shape of a notational choice, like an array indexed by absolute addresses versus a purely relational graph, either of which can be implemented in terms of the other. As a test I have been toying with: ask both sides what they expect of a single rotating body in an otherwise empty universe, and treat agreement-for-different-reasons as evidence of a verbal dispute, divergent expectations as evidence of a substantive one.

Is that the right shape, or is the disagreement doing something else — a clash about which cases count as data, about what a physical theory owes us by way of observability or explanatory unification, or bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for? Is a hidden empirical or formal question carrying the weight? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a successor theory in which spacetime is not fundamental, or a reason to expect none? Please engage what each side actually claims, argue for the diagnosis rather than surveying positions, and be explicit about where you are extrapolating beyond anything the field would broadly endorse, since I cannot check. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which consideration would lead you to a different diagnosis, and where is the one you give most likely to fail?
