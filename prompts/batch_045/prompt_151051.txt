I'm retired now — spent most of my career in isotope geochemistry, dating old rocks, so I've spent a good chunk of my life saying things like "this zircon crystallised 4.03 billion years ago" and meaning it in a completely unselfconscious way. Lately I've been going to an evening philosophy group (mostly retirees and one very patient grad student) and we got onto the Rietdijk–Putnam argument: two people strolling past each other in opposite directions have different simultaneity slices, so what is already real for one of them hasn't happened yet for the other, and if no inertial frame is privileged then apparently every event — past, present and future — is equally real and my "now" is just an indexical like "here". I find the argument annoyingly hard to shake, and what I keep coming back to is that the physicists I used to work with don't behave as though they believe it.

Here's the practice I want you to look at. Cosmologists cheerfully report a single number for the age of the universe, and they define a single cosmic time that every comoving galaxy shares — operationally, the time kept by clocks at rest with respect to the Hubble flow, the frame in which the microwave background looks isotropic — and then they talk about what the whole universe was doing "13.8 billion years ago" or "right now, everywhere". Meanwhile the same people, teaching special relativity, insist that simultaneity is frame-relative, that there is no privileged now, and that arguing about which slice is the real present is like arguing about which coordinate system is the real one. So: what does that package of practices actually presuppose about time? Reconstruct the implicit view for me. Is it eternalism plus a merely pragmatic convention (cosmic time as a convenient coordinate choice, no more metaphysically loaded than choosing UTC)? Is it a quietly neo-Lorentzian view where the matter distribution picks out a genuinely preferred foliation, so that physics has been smuggling back the absolute present that Einstein supposedly abolished? Or is it operationalism — time is just what a well-behaved clock measures, and there's nothing more to say? I especially want you to separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with, and to tell me whether the choice of foliation is doing the work of a gauge fixing (physically empty, pick whatever's convenient) or of a physical claim (some slices are the real ones).

A few things I'd like folded in, because they're where I get stuck. First: is my own old practice different in kind from the cosmologists'? A rock's age is presumably just proper time along that rock's worldline, elapsed between two events on it, which seems safe under any of these views — so was isotope geochemistry always in the clear, while "the age of the universe" is the one that needs a global now? Second, push on the hypothetical: suppose we lived in a universe where the matter is swirling in such a way that there is no single way to slice the whole of spacetime into global "nows" at all — I'm told Gödel's rotating solution is roughly like this, but treat it as a supposition. Would cosmologists in that universe be forced to stop quoting an age for the universe, or would they just report a different kind of quantity and carry on, and would that tell us the cosmic time we actually use is a discovery about our universe rather than a metaphysical posit? Third, the thing I can't get past: if the block universe is right, what exactly am I doing when I say the universe is 13.8 billion years old "now"? Is that sentence about a relation between two events, about the contents of a slice, or is it a sentence that only makes sense in the mouth of someone located inside the block? And what would have to change in the way cosmologists actually write and talk if they took the Rietdijk–Putnam argument completely seriously — what would a paper or a textbook passage look like, concretely?
