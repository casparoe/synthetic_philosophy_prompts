I am a novelist with no philosophical training, and I am reporting an argument I could not adjudicate and asking you to adjudicate it. My novel in progress turns on two scenes I cannot write until I understand what is at stake. In the first, a dying woman is calm in a way that the people around her find disturbing; in the second, her oldest friend is furious at that calm and calls it a failure of love. I had dinner with two friends — Ines, who translates early Chinese prose, and Marcus, who works on the ethics of death, mostly on the Epicurean and Lucretian material — and they spent the evening disagreeing about what the Zhuangzi actually holds about death. I am not asking about authorship or dating; I take it as given that the book is a compilation with several strata and that specialists disagree about which parts are earliest. I need the philosophy.

The passages they were arguing over, as I understood them: the story of Zhuangzi's response to his wife's death, in which he is found beating on a tub and singing, and, when his friend reproaches him, explains that he had grieved at first but then came to see her death as a phase within a transformation, like the turning of the seasons, and that to have gone on grieving would have been to fail to understand that process. The dialogue with a skull encountered on the road, in which the skull describes the condition of the dead as having no ruler above it and no seasons to serve and says it would not exchange that condition for the throne of a king. The dream of the butterfly, in which the dreamer wakes unsure whether he is a man who dreamt he was a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming he is a man. And, pulling the other way, the stories in which long life looks like something to protect: the vast crooked tree no carpenter will cut down, which therefore survives to the end of its years, and the tortoise that would rather be alive dragging its tail in the mud than dead and honoured in a temple.

Their exchange, as nearly as I can reproduce it:

(1) Marcus: Zhuangzi is running the Epicurean argument in another idiom. Death is bad for someone only if there is someone left for whom the change is a change for the worse; if the person is gone, there is no subject of the harm, and fearing death is fearing nothing. The wife story is just a man who has internalized this and stops grieving.

(2) Ines: That reading requires death to be annihilation, and Zhuangzi does not think it is. Death is transformation into another form within a single continuous process; on that picture the Epicurean argument is unavailable, because there may be a successor who has experiences, and whether that successor is worse off remains a live question. What is doing the work is a cosmology, not a claim about the end of experience.

(3) Marcus: Then the cosmology is idle and the view is the Epicurean one after all. If what continues is not me — not psychologically connected to me in the ways that matter — then the process goes on is no more a consolation than the atoms go on. Transformation and annihilation deliver the same result for anyone who wants to know whether death is bad for them.

(4) Ines: Not the same result, because they license different responses and rest on different reasons. On the Epicurean view the correct attitude is indifference. Zhuangzi is not indifferent; if anything he is celebratory — the skull declines to go back. More importantly, the wife story is not about who survives. It recounts a reclassification: he first took the event as a catastrophe, then came to see it as belonging to the same order as spring following winter, and the grief was a mistake about what kind of thing had happened, a misdescription rather than a response to a harm.

(5) Marcus: Then the text pulls against itself. If death is a phase within a process, and phases are not the sort of thing that can be good or bad for a person, it cannot matter when death arrives; yet Zhuangzi plainly cares about living out an allotted span — the useless tree is spared precisely because it is useless, and the tortoise chooses the mud over the temple. A phase that comes early is not like a season that comes early. And there is a second problem: the how do I know that death is not like this move is an appeal to ignorance. Not knowing that death is bad is not yet a reason to be cheerful about it, and no amount of not-knowing adds up to equanimity.

(6) Ines: On the first point, the badness of a cut-short life need not be a harm to a persisting subject. It can be a defect in the shape of a trajectory — the way an unfinished piece of music is defective without anyone's being harmed by it — and a view that evaluates whole processes rather than the welfare of a continuing person can say both that a truncated life is defective and that death is not bad for the dead. On the second point, Zhuangzi is not arguing for a conclusion at all. The passages are exercises meant to unmake a habit of ranking life above death, like the skill stories in which expertise consists in forgetting the distance between the agent and what he is doing; the test of the view is a change in the person, not a change in his beliefs about what death is.

At this point I offered my own synthesis and they each rejected it differently. I suggested that Zhuangzi is not making a claim about death's badness at all, but dissolving the question by denying that there is a stable standpoint from which my own annihilation could be a loss to me, and that this denial is itself part of the therapy rather than a thesis. Marcus said that makes the whole thing a technique rather than a view and that a character cannot believe a technique. Ines said that dissolving the question about my death does not obviously settle the question about my friend's death, and that Zhuangzi's posture is easier for the one who is dying than for the one who is left — which is exactly my problem, since my second scene belongs to the friend.

Please adjudicate this move by move: for each of (1) through (6), say how much is right and how much is wrong, and why, rating each if that helps. Then tell me what both of them are missing; which of the readings on the table — annihilation, transformation, dissolution of the question — has the best claim to be in the text, and whether any of them can be combined; and whether it is really true that the view is more stable for the person dying than for the person left behind. Finally, for my purposes: give me the strongest version of the calm I can put in my dying character's mouth, as one sentence in the idiom of the text but not a quotation, and tell me what a reader who actually knows the Zhuangzi would find indefensible about it. And please mark, claim by claim, which of your assertions specialists would grant and which are one contested reading among several. I would much rather know where I am taking liberties than be told the text plainly says what only one reading says.
