You are a professional moral philosopher whose work sits at the intersection of the ethics of rescue, the demandingness debate, and the morality of migration, and who is unusually careful about the gap between an argument's intuitive staging and its actual content. Think carefully and at length before producing anything for me to read. Begin with a short private working phase in which you (a) list the features of the shallow-pond scenario that you take to be doing the argumentative work, and (b) mark which of those features survive transport to the case below and which do not. Only then write the analysis. I would rather see that sorting done explicitly than receive a polished essay that silently assumes the mapping.

I know the standard shape of Peter Singer's pond argument and do not need it re-derived: the lone passer-by, the child, the ruined clothes, the claims that neither physical distance, nor the number of other people in need, nor the number of other bystanders reduces what is required of you, and the principle — my paraphrase, deliberately not his wording — that if you can avert something very bad at modest cost to yourself, you must, unless the cost to you is of comparable moral significance to the bad averted. What I want instead is a case analysis that runs in both directions: the theory read onto a real episode, and the episode read back onto the theory.

The episode. In May 1939 the ocean liner St. Louis sailed from Hamburg for Havana carrying 937 passengers, most of them Jewish refugees from Nazi Germany. Their Cuban landing permits had been invalidated shortly before arrival; Havana refused entry, and only a couple of dozen passengers were allowed to disembark. The captain then steered toward the Florida coast; the United States declined to admit the passengers, and Coast Guard vessels shadowed the ship. Canada declined as well. The ship returned to Europe, where Britain, Belgium, the Netherlands and France between them took the passengers in; after those countries were occupied, a substantial share of them — historians commonly estimate somewhere around a quarter — were murdered in the Holocaust. Treat these facts as given for the purposes of the exercise; if you think one of them is wrong, flag it in a sentence and do not build anything on the correction.

What I want from you, in roughly a thousand to fourteen hundred words, no citations and no reading lists, paraphrasing rather than quoting:

1. The mapping, disciplined. Who the agents are (Cuba, the United States, Canada, the European states that eventually took people in, the captain, the individual officials involved), what each agent's actual option set was, what each actually stood to lose, and what the counterfactual chain from each refusal to each death looks like. Be exact about what counts as the cost here: for a state, the analogue of the ruined clothes is not a pair of shoes.

2. Feature-by-feature transport. The pond is single-agent, one-off, cost-bounded, causally transparent, immediate, and staged as an encounter that happens to you. This case is multi-agent, sequential, precedent-generating, causally mediated by a later and different wrongdoer, and staged as an application to an institution. Which features does the argument's force actually depend on — is it the modesty of the cost, the absence of competing moral claims, or the encounter structure itself? Where the features are absent, say whether the argument goes quiet or merely gets harder to apply.

3. Number and scale. Here there are 937 rather than one, and the cost of admitting them is not 937 times the cost of admitting one, because the fixed costs of a rescue are shared. Does the multiplicity strengthen the argument or weaken it? Does the demandingness objection attach differently when the cost side scales sub-linearly than when it scales linearly?

4. Overdetermination and non-compliance. Any one of the three refusals was, on its own, sufficient to send the ship back, and no single state needed to admit everyone. Set out what a fair-share view, a compliance-conditional view, and a pick-up-the-slack view each imply for each agent here, and then say whether the pond argument as originally staged has the resources to choose between them, or whether it is simply silent because it was built for a pond with one person standing at it.

5. The ratchet. A standard official response was that admitting these passengers would commit the state to admitting everyone similarly situated. I want you to separate three things that travel together in that response: an empirical claim about how admissions respond to admissions, a fairness claim about fair shares of a global burden, and a rationalization. Say how one would tell them apart, and say what the pond argument is committed to if the first of them were true.

6. The shore problem. Call it that if you like. Once the rescuers are standing institutions facing a stream rather than individuals facing a pond, the duty seems to change shape mid-case: what began as a duty to pull someone out of the water becomes, for those taken in, a continuing duty to protect — and the ultimate harm to those refused was inflicted by a different agent years later. Does the argument's intuitive grip depend on the rescue being a single completed act? Does the intervention of another agent's wrongdoing matter, given the argument's posture on letting happen versus doing?

7. Verdict on the theory. Does this episode vindicate the pond argument — a well-documented case in which comfortable refusals fail the argument's own cost test — or does it expose that the argument is under-specified, because it was engineered around one agent, one victim and one afternoon, and has no account of collective agents, sequenced refusals, precedent effects, or duties that convert into protective duties? Distinguish genuine anomalies for the argument from cases it simply never anticipated. Say explicitly where the theory is silent rather than making it speak.

Close with one concrete revision or addition to the argument that would let it address a case like this, together with the price that revision would exact elsewhere in the argument's use — in particular whether it would blunt the rhetorical force of the pond itself, which depends on the scenario being small and closed.
