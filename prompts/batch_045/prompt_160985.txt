I have been working through the Voltaire–Rousseau exchange on the 1755 Lisbon earthquake, and I built the following argument from Rousseau's side of it. In 1755, on All Saints' Day, an earthquake, fire, and tsunami devastated Lisbon, killing tens of thousands; Voltaire attacked the Leibnizian–Popean optimism that this is the best of all possible worlds, and Rousseau replied that nature was not to blame, because a population dispersed in the countryside in simple, low dwellings would have suffered very few deaths from the same event.

Call the underlying idea the anthropogenic amplification theodicy: God ordains only good general laws, natural hazards are not evils in themselves, and hazards become evils only when human free choices about settlement and building amplify them beyond a threshold of harm; because those choices are human, God is not responsible for the resulting suffering. I set this theodicy out as the following argument, and I cannot accept where it ends:

(1) A perfectly good God would not permit any gratuitous evil, where an evil is gratuitous only if it is neither necessary for a greater good nor the result of a creature's free choice.
(2) A natural hazard such as an earthquake is not an evil in itself; it becomes an evil only by causing harm to sentient creatures.
(3) The harm a hazard causes is a function of its physical magnitude and the exposure and vulnerability of the population in its path.
(4) In every actual human case, exposure and vulnerability (dense cities, tall rigid buildings, coastal settlement, the use of fire) are products of human free choices and social conventions.
(5) Therefore, the evil of any actual disaster is not an unchosen natural evil but one whose severity depends on human free choices, and so it is not gratuitous in the sense defined in (1).
(6) For Lisbon specifically: had the inhabitants lived dispersed in the countryside in low, light dwellings, the same 1755 earthquake would have killed very few people; the actual tens of thousands of deaths were due to the city's crowded, built environment.
(7) Hence the Lisbon catastrophe was not a case of God inflicting or permitting gratuitous natural evil, but only the outworking of good general laws interacting with freely chosen human vulnerability.
(8) By the same reasoning, every other apparent instance of natural evil—famines, floods, pandemics—is likewise amplified by human social choices, so there is no gratuitous natural evil at all.
(9) Consequently, the victims of Lisbon, including those who made no personal choice about where they lived, are partly responsible for their own suffering, and God has no case to answer for the catastrophe; more generally, no victim of any disaster can claim that a good God owes them an answer for undeserved suffering.

Step (9) is unacceptable to me. If I have to choose, I would like to keep (1) and (6), since I think the dispersal point is empirically right and I do not want to say that God permits gratuitous evil; but I also want to keep that the victims' suffering was genuinely undeserved and that a good God at least owes some answer. I am willing to give up almost anything else. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Tell me why the step looked fine, and price the alternatives.
