I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for something like fifteen years, so I arrive with the standard toolkit already installed: expected value as the quantity to be maximized, *shut up and multiply* as the rule, the Kelly criterion as the right way to size a repeated favorable bet, and the associated claim that risk aversion over small stakes isn't merely suboptimal but close to incoherent. There's a pattern in real-world institutions that sells gambles that I can't get to line up with any of that, and I'd like you to tell me what theory of rational choice it presupposes.

The pattern is easier to state against the St. Petersburg game, so let me fix it: a fair coin is tossed until it comes up heads; the stake starts at $2 and doubles with each tail, so the payoff is $2^n where n is the number of the toss on which the first heads appears. The expected payoff diverges. Yet nobody sane pays $25 for one go. The classical fix — diminishing marginal utility of money, Bernoulli's log utility — gives finite, small answers, but it's unstable: crank up the payoff schedule fast enough and the paradox reappears for any unbounded utility function (Menger's point, as I remember it), and there are variants with alternating signs where the expected value isn't well defined at all, since the sum can be rearranged to anything you like.

I don't want another tour of those resolutions. I want the institutional practice explained. As far as I can tell — correct me on any detail, but this is my impression from the outside — every institution that actually takes the other side of a gamble truncates its own exposure, and truncates it well before the point where the stakes get large relative to its capital:

- Casinos post a maximum bet, and will restrict or refuse a customer who wants to stake more than it, even though the house has a positive expected value on the bet in question and even though the amount at issue is a rounding error against the parent company's balance sheet. (I mean the cap itself, not the separate business of backing off advantage players, which is just adverse selection and can be set aside.)
- State lotteries advertise a headline jackpot paid as a graduated annuity over decades, with a cash option worth a fraction of the advertised figure; some national lotteries, I believe, cap the top prize and roll the excess down into lower tiers.
- Insurers write to a policy limit rather than to an unbounded one, and then pay what I'm told are large multiples of modeled expected loss to lay off the top layer into reinsurance — while computing the premium for the body of the distribution as nothing more than the expected loss plus a loading.
- Trading firms price with expectation and then run the book against position caps and VaR- or expected-shortfall-style limits.

So: the same organizations treat expected value as the thing to maximize when they price, and refuse to act on it when they size. And the refusal kicks in at scales where the standard argument — that risk aversion is only defensible when the stake is a meaningful share of your wealth, and that with many independent bets the variance washes out — says it shouldn't.

Reconstruct the implicit theory for me. What view of rational choice under multiplicative, repeated exposure does this cluster of practices only make sense under, and which views does it quietly rule out? Does it strictly presuppose that expected monetary value is the wrong maximand, or is it fully compatible with straight expected-value maximization plus some non-normative add-on — finite capital, regulatory capital requirements, agency problems inside the firm, Knightian doubt about whether the tail model is right? Give me the strongest version of each candidate underpinning I know of — bounded or concave utility, survival/ruin constraints, and the time-average-growth or ergodicity critique associated with Ole Peters and others — and tell me which ones the practice actually commits you to and which it merely sits well with. In particular, I keep reading that the time-average story is just expected log utility relabeled with physics vocabulary; if that's right, say so, and if it isn't, say what the difference is supposed to buy. Then: suppose the casino were run by an entity with effectively unbounded capital, no ruin constraint, an indefinite horizon, and no regulator — a sovereign treasury, or an AI-managed endowment that expects to still be compounding in ten thousand years. Should it drop the table limits? If yes, does that show the caps were pragmatic all along and not principled? If no, what's the principled reason, and does it survive the fact that the house can spread across thousands of near-independent players?

One more thing about tone, since this is the part where writing about these topics usually goes wrong. Please separate, explicitly, the mathematics and definitional claims that are settled from the interpretive claims that people who actually work on decision theory and finance dispute, and don't present a contested view as the consensus position — flag where you're giving me your own reconstruction and where you'd expect a specialist to push back, and note what kind of observation would change your answer.
