Last night I had a long argument with an old friend — she works in open-government advocacy, I work in data protection — about how much a citizen may legitimately keep hidden from the state, and we ground to a halt in a way that I think was diagnostic rather than accidental. Her position was roughly that being legible to the state is part of what being a citizen consists in: collective self-rule requires that the demos be able to see itself, and a demand for opacity is mostly a demand to be exempt from the gaze one helps to authorize. Mine was roughly that a person has a standing claim not to be read, that this claim does not evaporate because the reader is democratic, and that a state which can reconstruct you from your records has taken something from you even if it treats you well afterwards. When the Chinese social credit system came up, she said it was only transparency plus incentives, and I said it was a different animal altogether — and then neither of us could say precisely where the difference lay, because we had no map of the space. I came away wanting exactly that: a classification instrument, not another round of assertions.

Please act as a political philosopher who specializes in privacy, surveillance and the legitimacy of state knowledge, and build it for me. Think the problem through carefully before you draw anything: I would like, first, a few paragraphs of scratch work in which you lay out the separative questions you think the debate genuinely turns on, then the combinations of answers those questions permit, and only then the actual positions located inside that space. The tree should be cut from the logical space rather than from a roster of named views; names belong on the leaves, not at the branch nodes.

Concretely, I want a one-page decision tree that I can hand to a seminar of mid-career civil-service trainees — smart, untheoretical, no library to hand. Requirements:

Six to eight branch questions, every one of them either a concrete case or a claim a trainee could answer without already knowing which position it leads to. Nothing may turn on asking whether someone values privacy, distrusts government, or likes a tradition: the instrument has to sort people by what they say about cases, not by the labels they would pick.

Ten to fourteen leaves, named where an established name exists and marked unnamed where nobody defends it. At least three of the unoccupied combinations should be identified explicitly, each with a one-line diagnosis of why the cell is empty — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored — and I want you to be honest about which of those three you think it is.

Flag explicitly any pair of leaves your questions cannot actually separate, with a note on what extra question would pry them apart.

The distinctions I care about, and which should therefore do the branching work: whether what matters is legibility (the state's ability to place you in a class) or traceability (its ability to reconstruct a particular life from retained records); whether the contested practice is retrospective accounting or forward-looking scoring; whether the claim not to be read is unconditional — a feature of your status as a person — or merely remedial, something you acquire when the state has shown itself untrustworthy; whether the relevant unit of legibility is the individual dossier or the population-level pattern; and, the one we kept colliding with, whether withholding still does any normative work when the state can infer what you withheld from data you did hand over. I want that last case given real weight: a refusal that leaves the state's inferences unchanged may be a different moral object from a refusal that blunts them.

Please build the cases around, at minimum: a household smart meter from which occupancy and appliance use can be reconstructed; mandatory disclosure of completed transactions combined with resistance to predictive scoring; a state that rarely collects anything itself but compels the platforms people already use to hand over what they hold; a citizen who hides not by withholding but by generating noise so as to resemble someone else; and a case in which one person's successful concealment shifts costs onto other people, such as a risk pool.

Two further constraints. First, no bibliographies, reading lists, footnotes, case citations, or institutional history: the trainees cannot look anything up, and I do not want the handout padded with authorities. If a real-world arrangement is useful, describe it generically rather than naming a statute, judgment or pilot programme — nothing here should depend on a date or a document I would have to go and check. Second, tell me, at the end and briefly, whether the three traditions we were arguing past each other in — the German data-protection one, the American openness one, the Chinese social-credit one — are really leaves of your tree, or cross-cutting coalitions of answers that the tree explains better than they explain it. If the second, say which branch question each coalition is actually a view about.
