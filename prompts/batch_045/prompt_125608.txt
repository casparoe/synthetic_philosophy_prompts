I have just come out of a long argument with a friend about suffering-focused ethics, and she ended it by saying that what I call my position is really several positions that cannot all be true — though she could not tell me which. Before I write it up properly I would like an audit, so here it is as plainly as I can state it.

(a) Only suffering has fundamental moral disvalue; happiness, love, achievement and meaning have no independent positive weight, and matter only insofar as they prevent, cushion, or compensate for suffering.
(b) The badness of an episode of suffering is fixed wholly by what it is like from the inside — its intensity and duration — and not at all by whether the subject wants it to stop, by what it means within her life, or by whether anyone ever learns of it.
(c) Outcomes are to be ranked by total suffering, with ordinary expected-value aggregation: no lexical priority, no extra weight for whoever happens to be worst off.
(d) Even so, I find myself committed to the priority of the worst suffering: that relieving an enormous quantity of mild suffering in enormous numbers of beings cannot make up for leaving a single being in agony, and that abolishing agony therefore has first claim on us.
(e) I accept the asymmetry — a strong reason not to bring into existence someone who will suffer, no comparable reason to bring into existence someone who would be happy — and I explain it in person-affecting terms: being created benefits no one, whereas being made to suffer harms someone.
(f) Relatedly, I hold that an empty world is better than any world containing any suffering at all, and I treat that as an implication rather than as a reductio.
(g) But I also hold that while it would be wrong to create a child whose life would contain the ordinary small quota of suffering together with a good deal of modest happiness, it would also be wrong to painlessly end that child's life once she exists.
(h) Finally, one worry: I fear I inherit the repugnant conclusion — a vast population of lives barely worth living beating a smaller population of excellent lives — which I would very much rather not accept.

Please tell me which of these pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be: I suspect at least one of my worries is misplaced, and I would rather know that than have every item flagged. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them as equal, and for each say exactly what would have to be given up and what that would cost me. Do not assume in advance which commitment I would sacrifice — I would prefer to keep all of them. If there is a single revision that would clean up the most, say what it is.
