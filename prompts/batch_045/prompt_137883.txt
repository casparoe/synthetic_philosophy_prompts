I am a fourth-year PhD student in political economy, and in three weeks I have the seventy-five-minute slot in our first-year formal theory sequence where the common prior assumption first appears. The cohort is mixed: about a third have had a semester of graduate micro, several have never worked inside a partitions model, and a vocal minority arrive suspecting that formal modeling is a device for smuggling in conclusions. Last year I taught the session as a proof — define the assumption, state the agreement result, sketch the argument, spend the last ten minutes on applications — and it failed in a way I have since diagnosed. The students could reproduce the result but could not say what the assumption contributed to it, and in the written feedback half of them described it as an empirical claim: that people in the same polity share enough experience, culture, or training to be treated as starting from the same place. So I am not asking for the explanation. I am asking for a plan for explaining it, with the following components.

First, the order, with the dependency made explicit each time. I want to know what has to be on the board before the assumption can even be stated — my own guess is that the posterior has to be introduced as a function of the state, a random variable rather than a number, before anything else can be said — and I want an argument about where the meet machinery (iterating the two partitions, self-evident events) goes relative to the statement of the result. My instinct is that it has to come first, because once the students can see that common knowledge of a posterior just means constancy on a self-evident event, the result is two lines; but tell me if the dependencies run the other way and why. I also want three distinctions placed in the sequence rather than collapsed into a single definition: the ancestral reading of the prior (the belief the agents held, or would have held, before their information diverged); the bookkeeping reading (a single measure on the state space that reproduces each agent's posteriors by conditioning on that agent's partition — a device of representation, nobody's belief at any time); and the adjudicative reading (the standpoint from which any residual difference of opinion is credited to information alone, so that disagreement is never put down to temperament, ideology, or error). The result itself needs only the second; the "all disagreement is really disagreement about information" gloss needs the third; the students arrive holding the first. Tell me where each is introduced and what breaks if two of them are introduced together.

Second, the example that carries the session, with an argument for what it must have and what it must lack. It should be substantively political or institutional — an incumbent and a challenger reading overlapping but non-nested signals, a regulator and a legislature, something of that kind — because a coin, an urn, or an umbrella will confirm the suspicion that this is toy-work. It must lack any trade, bet, or stake asymmetry, so that the no-trade family of results cannot leak into the discussion prematurely; it must lack any nested pair of partitions, since if one agent's partition is a coarsening of the other's the meet stabilizes immediately and teaches the wrong lesson. It must have six or seven states at most; partitions whose iterated meet neither stabilizes at the first step nor runs past three or four, so that a student can locate by hand the exact finite level at which mutual knowledge of the two differing posteriors dies; and — this is the constraint I care most about — the same state space, the same two partitions, and the same event must remain serviceable once the prior is dropped, so that the contrast case (common knowledge of disagreement with everything else untouched) costs no new machinery. The two priors in that contrast should share a support: I want the failure to be a deep one rather than the shallow kind where one agent simply assigns zero to a state the other admits, since the shallow failure is visible in what the agents are certain of and is not the interesting joint-consistency failure at all. Explain what earns each of these properties its place.

Third, the misconception map: for each, the point in the sequence where it should be headed off and the single move — one sentence, or one gesture at the board — that does the heading off. My list: that the assumption is a form of common knowledge, or follows from the model's being common knowledge; that it is an empirical claim about shared culture or shared experience; that there must have been some real or hypothetical moment at which the agents believed alike; that it is a requirement of rationality, so that differing posteriors convict someone of irrationality; that the existence of trade refutes it, without the extra premises the no-trade results need; that the prior must be uniform; that the result says disagreement is impossible rather than that common knowledge of disagreement is impossible; and that the prior belongs to the agents, when in applied work it is most often the distribution from which the modeler draws everyone's signals and the payoff-relevant state, the agents seeing only conditioned slices of it. Add the ones I have not listed.

Fourth, what I should openly decline to explain at this level, named so that I can say I am declining it: the type-space constructions, the separating-hyperplane characterization of the assumption as the absence of an agreeable bet, and the bridge to veil-of-ignorance arguments in political philosophy, which I would rather flag for later than open. Relatedly: I take it that in a finite model the assumption has no implications for any finite order of the belief hierarchy beyond what it already shares with mere sameness of support, which is why a common prior can always be restored by enlarging the state space. I want that point to do real work in the plan — specifically, I want the students to see why the failure of the assumption is a property of the model taken whole and not of anything they could read off a finite list of who-believes-what-about-whom, and I want to know at which minute the state space has to be declared closed for that to land.

Fifth, the check at minute seventy. I want them to be able to say what the assumption adds to common knowledge, and, more importantly, to audit: give them a two-sentence sketch of a result of the kind they will meet later — an information-aggregation or no-trade claim — and have them locate where the prior was used and whether the result survives without it. Give me that question, and describe what a confidently wrong answer to it looks like, so I can tell the difference between a student who has the idea and one who has the slogan.

Finally: what should be on the board at minute seventy-five, and nothing else. Rough minute marks throughout, please.
