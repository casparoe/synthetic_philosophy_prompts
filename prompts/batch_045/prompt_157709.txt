At my last job we ran "blameless" incident reviews: nobody got in trouble for the outage, we just fixed the system — unless someone had been reckless, in which case people did get angry and sometimes someone was let go. I definately never understood where that line was meant to come from, and lately I've noticed the same shape in criminal law. Kill someone while severely mentally ill and you are not punished, you get committed to a psychiatric hospital, sometimes for longer than the prison term would have been. Do it while diagnosed as a psychopath and you get no sympathy at all — that diagnosis often seems to make it worse, not better. And there are cases where a brain tumour appeared to cause the behaviour, removing the tumour turned the person back into who they used to be, and everyone starts talking as if it wasn't really him who did it. Some places even seem to have a middle option, guilty but mentally ill.

What does this package of practices actually presuppose about moral responsibility? Is it the plain causal story — the act was produced by something in him, so it isn't really his — and if so why doesn't that swallow everything, given everything is caused? Or is it something else: whether we can still relate to the person as a person at all, whether being resentful at him or wanting an apology from him makes any sense, as opposed to treating him like a broken machine to be managed, treated or retrained? I want to know what the practice strictly needs versus what several competing theories would all happily license. The hospital part especially: does "not your fault" really fit with indefinite confinement, or is danger-management being smuggled in under a blame-free label? And what would my team's postmortem policy look like if we took the "it was all caused" version seriously?
