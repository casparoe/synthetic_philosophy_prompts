I'm writing a novel set in Pisa and Florence in the years right around 1615 — my main character is a glass-grinder who does piecework for an instrument maker and has got it into his head that the earth moves, and there's a long scene where he gets questioned by a couple of theologians in a Medici antichamber. I don't want the scene to be a cartoon where he just Says Science and they just Say Bible, so I've been reading around what people in that world actually argued about scripture, and there is one line I keep hitting in every popular book and museum wall-text, always printed as if it came out of Galileo's own mouth (though as far as I can tell he was repeating it from Cardinal Baronius): "the intention of the Holy Spirit is to teach us how one goes to heaven and not how heaven goes."

Here's my problem. My beta readers have all seized on it as the thesis of the scene — they read it as meaning scripture and nature are two separate classrooms and nothing said in one can ever touch the other, and that the slogan is what settles the matter. But the theologian in my scene has an argument I can't write my way around: he says the authority of scripture is all of one piece, so if you admit it gets the path of the sun wrong you've pulled a thread that unravels its authority about salvation too, and there is no honest way to cordon the error off inside astronomy. My grinder has to answer him, and I want to know what he'd actually be committing himself to if he reaches for that line. (He also has a bit of Augustine in his pocket about anyone who sets scripture against clear and manifest reason opposing not the Bible's meaning but his own interpretation, which I suspect is a different move entirely, but I can't tell how it relates.)

So: could you lay out as a list the different precise things the slogan could be saying, from the near-trivial to the genuinely radical? I've got a few in my notes and I can't tell which are seperable. (a) Scripture is silent on astronomical questions, so there was never anything there to conflict with. (b) Scripture does say things about the natural world, but dressed in the language of ordinary experience, so the sun standing still is true as seen from the ground and false only as a claim about what is going on up there. (c) The Holy Spirit had a deliberately narrow teaching aim, so nothing in scripture is guaranteed to us as natural philosophy even where it reads like it's making a claim. And (d) something that isn't about scope at all but about a rule for which side wins when they genuinely do collide — my grinder's homespun version, which I rather like, is that a book written for shepherds should be read the way a shepherd reads it, and a shepherd reads a sunset as a sunset. Tell me which readings are platitudes, which would get him laughed at in 1615, which are false, and which one, if any, actually blocks the theologian's all-or-nothing move — I have a feeling (d) is doing the real work and the others are decoration, but I'd like that checked. And one thing nobody explains: does any of this depend on whether he has an actual proof of the earth's motion as opposed to merely very good arguments? In my draft he's got the moons of Jupiter, the phases of Venus and a great deal of confidence, but no demonstration, and if the slogan buys him nothing without a demonstration then I think the scene has to be rewritten so the theologian wins it.
