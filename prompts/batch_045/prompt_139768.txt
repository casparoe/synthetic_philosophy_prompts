ok so I spent about two hours last night arguing with a friend about whether failing to do something can ever be a cause of anything, and I need a case built to spec, because the one I was groping for kept coming apart in my hands.

Her position: no. Omissions aren't causes, there's nothing there to do any causing. When we say "her not watering the plant caused it to die" we're really saying she was meant to water it and didn't and we blame her — the actual causal work is done by the sun and the soil. And when I ran the usual stuff at her (the nurse who never checks the IV line; the case where two people both fail to water and each one of them still counts as a cause) she said: fine, IF you let omissions in at all, the but-for test is all you've got, and it obviously over-generates — everyone on earth who didn't water your plant caused its death. Too many causes and no connection, which is exactly what you'd expect if the whole thing were blame in a lab coat.

What I wanted to come back with and couldn't make stick is that but-for can also hand you too FEW causes, and that which answer you get depends on how finely you carve up the failure — and a blame story doesn't predict that at all. The shape I was reaching for: one nurse, two seperate things she's meant to do, she does neither, and each failure on its own is enough to kill the patient, by a different route, with the routes not touching each other anywhere. Carve her failure per-duty and but-for says neither failure caused anything (she does X and he still dies via Y; she does Y and he still dies via X). Carve it per-agent — "she didn't do her job" — and but-for says yes. Meanwhile the role-based view (you only get an omission-cause where the agent was supposed to act and the outcome is the sort of thing the duty was there to stop) says yes either way.

So build me that case, properly, to these specs:

- One paragraph, an ordinary hospital, no trolleys, no lawyers, no sci-fi, no exotic kit.
- Both routes have to be real and sufficient: neither is a "maybe", neither fizzles half way, and performing one duty must not have led her to perform the other or interfered with the other route.
- The two routes have to kill at the same moment by different mechanisms, so that the retreat to a more specific effect ("death at 3:52 from the line, not death at 3:58 from the dose") doesn't rescue but-for. Tell me whether that retreat works anyway and why.
- Blame gets held at roughly zero: give her a decent excuse for both failures (short staffing, a contradictory handover, whatever is actually realistic on a ward). The point is that whatever happens to the causal judgement, blame can't be what's driving it.
- Nobody else was in a position to step in — no third party hovering with a rescue.
