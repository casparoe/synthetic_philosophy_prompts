Bit of a favour: I'm writing a paper on Mill and the imperial parenthesis in his liberalism, and I keep going back and forth on one case, so I want you to just settle it. Here is the track record, as I read him:

1. India under the Company: approved. Direct rule by a professional, distant bureaucracy, with no representative institutions, justified because the population is in a state of nonage and the whole point of the rule is to improve them. Note also that he preferred this to handing India over to the Crown and Parliament, on the ground that a metropolitan electorate would run the place for British interests rather than Indian ones.
2. Settler colonies like Canada: representative and responsible government, obviously, and the sooner the better.
3. Jamaica after Morant Bay: condemned. He went after Eyre hard. So even a subject population has claims that martial law and indiscriminate punishment cannot override, and legal form matters even in a dependency.
4. Ireland: English misrule there was a scandal, and the Irish were as entitled to free institutions as any European people.
5. General doctrine: in On Liberty he excepts barbarians from the free-government principle, despotic rule being legitimate in dealing with them provided the end is their improvement; in his essay on non-intervention he denies that the ordinary rules of international morality hold between civilized nations and barbarians; and in Considerations on Representative Government he argues both that representative institutions are the great school of public spirit and that free institutions are all but impossible in a country made up of nationalities that feel no common sympathy with one another.

New case — call it Varendia. Annexed about sixty years ago. It has an old literary high culture, sophisticated manufactures, and its own long tradition of trained administrators; no tradition whatever of elected assemblies. The population splits into two religious communities and four or five language groups, and people don't much sympathize across those lines; there is a thin English-educated professional class, urban, and pretty unrepresentative of anyone else. The place pays for itself, supplies troops, and buys metropolitan goods. Voters at home are indifferent except when an atrocity makes the papers.

Two proposals are on the table. A, from the governor: an elected legislative council on a narrow property franchise (roughly 3% of adult males), consultative powers only, the governor keeping the veto and the bureaucracy keeping finance and law and order. B, from reformers at home: transfer Varendia to direct Crown rule with annual parliamentary scrutiny, on the ground that the present arrangement is unaccountable.

What does Mill say, A or B or neither? I care less about the one-word answer than about which rule he is actually running here — improvement, nonage, the nationality condition, or the business about insulating dependencies from metropolitan opinion — so give me the verdict you'd definately bet on and the reasons for it, not a menu of options. And if you think his precedents underdetermine it, tell me which further case would seperate the rules, which precedent you are treating as the closest analog, and flag honestly where you are guessing and which rule you are reading him as being stuck with either way. No footnotes, I know the texts.
