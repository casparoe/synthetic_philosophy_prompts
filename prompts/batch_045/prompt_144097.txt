I've just taken over my grandmothers cut-flower farm and I'm suddenly the person who decides what gets sprayed -- aphids on the dahlias, ants in the compost, the caterpillars. I've been reading about insect sentience and the scale thing (absurd numbers, lowish probability that any of them feel anything at all) and our little growers' group wants a session on it, so I'd rather sort people by what they actually believe than have the same argument again.

Could you build me a taxonomy-as-tree of the positions on what to do about insects, where every branch point is the claim that splits the space rather than the name of a camp? Four or five branch questions, six to eight leaves. The splits I want used: whether moral weight scales continuously with probability of sentience or only kicks in past some threshold; whether tiny probabilities times enormous numbers should just be multiplied out or truncated / bounded / handled variance-averse; what the unit of accounting is -- individual, experience-moment (do insects have unified experience over time or just momentary flickers that don't add up into a life?), or total welfare per life; whether bringing more insect lives into existence is a benefit, a harm, or neither; and whether we should act on this at all given how clueless we are about knock-on ecological effects.

Each leaf: a name if the position has one, "unnamed" if not, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Build the tree around concrete cases rather than "do insects feel pain?" -- e.g. pouring boiling water on an ant nest, ordering parasitic wasps that eat aphids alive, planting a wildflower strip that will support many more insects than the field does now, and a case where a cheap pesticide spares a crop at the cost of ten million insects versus a cost to a handful of chickens, with the odds on insects mattering at 1 in 1000.

Also: mark the combinations in the logical space that nobody seems to occupy and say whether that's because they're incoherent, unattractive, or just untried, and flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really tell apart. Nobody in the group should need prior exposure to the debate to answer.
