I'm a software engineer (compilers and distributed systems, mostly) with no formal philosophy training. A reading group I sit in on spent a session on the cranes-and-skyhooks distinction, and I came away convinced it marks something real. But when a friend and I tried to state it precisely, we produced an argument whose conclusion I can't accept, and I'd like to know which step to reject and what rejecting it costs me.

(1) An explanation is legitimate only if nothing that does explanatory work in it is itself inexplicable in terms of the organized interaction of parts that lack the feature being explained. Call this the no-lifters rule.

(2) The explanations I rely on every day cite items that the parts do not have: a deadlock explained by two threads' lock-acquisition order, a miscompilation explained by a violated precondition in the intermediate representation. No individual gate or instruction is deadlocked or incorrect.

(3) So those items had better be reconstructible from lower-level interactions, or the no-lifters rule condemns explanations I am not prepared to give up.

(4) If a cited item is fully reconstructible from lower-level interactions, then within the explanation it does no work beyond abbreviating them; compression is not explanation.

(5) Hence the only genuine explanatory work happens at the bottom, and the no-lifters rule commits me to greedy reductionism: skipping every intermediate level and explaining the deadlock in the vocabulary of the substrate's physics.

(6) That is absurd, and it inverts what I understood the distinction was for; the entire point of layered design is that each layer's vocabulary does work the layer below cannot.

(7) The only apparent escape is to weaken (1) to: a crane is any level whose vocabulary is disciplined by the level below. But then nothing counts as a skyhook either — a vital spirit or a designing intelligence is simply a top level with its own vocabulary, claiming consistency with what happens underneath.

(8) So the distinction either forbids every explanation anyone actually uses, or forbids none.

I suspect something moved between (3) and (5), but I can't say what. Of the premises, I most want to keep (1) and (6); if I have to choose, I'd rather give up (5) than either.

A complication. My friend proposes a middle test: a higher-level item is a crane only if, for any given lower-level realization, you can specify which lower-level goings-on would have made the higher-level explanation false — the higher-level story must have lower-level truthmakers it does not name, and must be able to produce them on demand. This looks like it saves both (1) and (6). But I can't tell whether it is a real constraint or a relabeling, since a defender of a top-down designer could say that His interventions have lower-level truthmakers we are not privileged to name.

Please: name the step where the argument breaks, explain why it looked sound, price the alternatives, and tell me whether my friend's test has teeth or just moves the bump in the carpet.
