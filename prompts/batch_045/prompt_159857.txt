Our reading group is doing invertebrate welfare and moral uncertainty next month, and the format is that everyone has to write a one-page "where I actually stand" that the others then get to attack. Here's mine, and I want to know if it hangs together before I hand it over, because I have a feeling I'm helping myself to something.

(a) I'm a functionalist about pain: what makes a state a pain is what it does in the economy of the creature, not what it's made of. So "insects don't have our anatomy" is not by itself a reason to think they don't feel anything.

(b) My credence that there is something it is like to be an adult fruit fly is low, maybe 3%. What actually drives it down for me is mostly size and dissimilarity: a couple hundred thousand neurons at most, arranged nothing like a vertebrate brain, a lot of the control farmed out to local ganglia.

(c) When I don't know whether something matters, I act on expected value: weight by credence, take the expectation, and I'm happy adding up across taxa as if each one were its own separate little bet with its own little probability.

(d) So I accept the scale argument, basically: in expectation the amount of suffering happening right now in small invertebrates dwarfs everything humans do to each other and to farmed vertebrates by many orders of magnitude, which makes it the biggest item on the moral ledger even though each individual creature probably counts for nothing.

(e) But I want the descent to stop somewhere. I don't think we should be losing sleep over nematodes, soil mites, or bacteria, even though the numbers keep going up as the credences keep going down. There's a floor, around the point where the nervous system stops being centralized enough to do the relevant thing.

(f) Relatedly, I think the evidence is correlated: whatever the true functional story turns out to be, it's going to be the same story for flies and ants and beetles, so finding out that flies are sentient would be strong evidence that the others are too.

(g) No partial credit. A creature that isn't conscious but writhingly behaves as if it were in agony has exactly zero moral weight. No "treat it as if," no fractional status for being a near miss.

(h) And separately: I'm not a total utilitarian about populations. The repugnant-conclusion / mere-addition stuff convinces me that you can't just add up welfare across outcomes with different numbers of individuals in them.

One more thing that may or may not be part of the view: I've never actually changed anything I do or give to on the basis of (d), and I notice I sort of assume that's just weakness of will rather than a problem in the set. But maybe it's the tell.

What I want from you: go through these pairwise and tell me which ones genuinely pull against each other, which only look like they do and are actually fine together, and rank the real tensions by how bad they are rather than flagging everything as equally serious. For each real one, say what would have to give and what it would cost me, and then say whether there's a single revision that cleans up the most. I'm deliberately not telling you which item I'd least like to drop. Please don't give me papers or a reading list — I can find those myself, and half the fun of the session is that the others answer without them.
