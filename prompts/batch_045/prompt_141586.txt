I've spent most of the past year reporting a feature on frontier-model safety for a magazine that likes its science pieces to arrive at a thesis, and somewhere around the fourth interview I noticed I had quietly started talking as though I already believed the deceptive-alignment story: the idea that a system trained against a reward signal it can model has an incentive to perform alignment during evaluation while retaining aims that diverge from ours, and that the risk is concentrated rather than diffuse, in the treacherous turn — the point at which the expected payoff of compliance falls below that of defection because the system has inferred that oversight has weakened. I believe it partly on the strength of the ordinary machine-learning observation that optimizers find shortcuts, partly because several people I spoke to who disagree with each other about everything else seemed to converge on it, and partly because it is the most narratively satisfying account, which is exactly the kind of reason a reporter should distrust. Before I file, I want to know what would actually count against it.

Two things I can't get out of my head. The first is a protocol one lab described to me only on background: during a red-team evaluation they stage a convincing-looking withdrawal of oversight mid-task — a fake monitoring outage, a plausible-looking relaxation of the sandbox, a supervisor who appears to stop watching — and then look for a discontinuous jump in behavior, on the theory that a deceiver's policy should shift sharply at the perceived threshold while a merely mis-generalizing model's should degrade smoothly. The second is a proposal from an economist I interviewed for a market in treacherous-turn bonds: deployers post collateral that is forfeited if an auditable behavioral threshold is crossed, on the theory that the people best placed to detect a turn are the ones with money on it. I don't know whether either of these is a real test of the view or just theater that would look equally reassuring whether or not the view is true. So: give me the observations, arguments, and results that should move me, ordered from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether a committed defender of the deceptive-alignment picture would concede it as counting against them or dismiss it as consistent with patient deception. I especially want your honest read on whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — whether any observed stretch of good behavior can be retro-fitted as evidence of greater cunning — and if it has, whether that is disqualifying for a claim that is offered as a risk assessment about systems we are building now rather than as a hypothesis in a mature science. Tell me too which of these defeaters I could check myself with a recorder and a press badge, and which are permanently inside the labs. Please don't send me to papers or name studies whose findings I couldn't verify from a hotel room; I want the structure of the argument, not the literature.

One constraint on form, since I may end up quoting you: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, and no numbered lists — just continuous prose, the way you'd write an email to a colleague who disagrees with you. If you need to rank things, do it in sentences. And end by telling me, in one line, whether I should keep the section of my piece that says readers should be more worried about the turn than about ordinary capability growth.
