A college I used to teach at is finally renaming a building named for an alum who made his money in the slave trade, and I've been asked to give the ten-minute speech at the ceremony thanking the students who ran the campaign. I was their faculty advisor, so I know how it actually went: they lost every public debate for two years, and what turned it wasn't an argument — the old alum who led the opposition pulled his support after his twelve-year-old grandson asked him what he'd say the building was named for, and he never changed his mind about any of the arguments. I've been reading the Williams/Appiah stuff about whether norms like these end through arguments, interests, or honor, and I think it's basically right, but I can't figure out how to say it out loud without it coming out wrong.

So write me the speech, ~800 words. Half the room is those students, who'll definately notice if I'm feeding them a flattering story about how their reasons won; the other half is donors and older alums who were on the other side and who I can't turn into villains, plus the grandson's family. It also can't slide into saying the change was just a matter of what became embarrassing, because then it sounds like fashion rather than like something we actually got right — and no 'right side of history' talk, and no implying the people who defended the name were just stupid or evil. Tell me what the speech can and can't claim, and what it should end on.
