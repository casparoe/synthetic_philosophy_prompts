I should say straight away that I have no academic background in philosophy — I work in freight forwarding, spend far too many hours on motorways, and have been chewing over a puzzle I first met in a popular science book and then argued about with two friends for the better part of a year. It is the one about meeting Death in Damascus. Here is the version I have ended up with. You are in Damascus, Death tells you he is coming for you tomorrow, and you can either stay put or take the coach to Aleppo. Death is not a mystic but a superb predictor, and the version I find interesting is the one where his prediction has a mechanism: overnight he runs a detailed simulation of you — your memories, your temperament, the very reasoning you are working through now — and in the morning he travels to whichever city the simulated you settled on. If you end up in the same city as Death, you die. Staying costs nothing and the fare to Aleppo is £1,000, which matters to you but is nothing next to dying (put survival at 1,000 units, death at 0, and the fare at 1 unit). So if you settle on a definite plan, stay or go, the simulation settles on it too, Death is waiting in that city, and you die with certainty, having wasted the fare if you went. My friends agree with all that, and they agree with what I take to be the usual verdict: since nothing you do makes any difference to where Death is, you might as well stay and keep your £1,000. What I cannot get them to take seriously is this. Suppose you own a small sealed box, a randomising device based on radioactive decay, whose reading no simulation of you could anticipate — the simulated box's reading is merely statistically independent of the real one's. If you now decide to go to Aleppo if and only if the box reads heads, then the simulated coin and the real coin agree half the time, Death is in your city with probability one half, and you have a fifty per cent chance of seeing the day after tomorrow, at an expected cost of £500. To me that looks like an option that beats both pure plans by a mile, and I would like you to argue the case for it.

Please write as a professional philosopher who has spent years working on decision theory, and think it through properly before you begin: sketch the numbers, list the strongest objections you can think of, and then commit to one answer and defend it rather than surveying what the various theories would say. I want you to argue that using the device is not a cheap trick or a change of subject but the correct response to the case, and that the standard verdict is an artefact of only ever comparing two pure acts. In particular, please deal with what my friends say: first, that the whole point of the story is that you cannot cheat Death, and that if Death is simply stipulated to predict your actual action perfectly then randomising fails by stipulation, so I must have changed the subject; second, that even if the numbers work, once the box reads heads and you find yourself on the coach you have exactly the same evidence of doom as someone who simply decided to go, so nothing has really been gained; and third, that a predictor clever enough to run the simulation in the first place would see the trick coming and adapt to it — my own thought is that even a Death who anticipated your plan could do no better than evens against someone who randomises, whereas against any settled plan he gets you for certain, but I cannot quite make that precise, and I would like you to make it precise for me. If you think the point generalises — that what is really being chosen here is a policy or a procedure rather than an act, and that the familiar dispute between causal and evidential approaches has been conducted over the wrong option set — say so, and say why. Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence, the single claim you want me to come away believing, and give me the reasoning after that: no preamble, no restating of the question, and no opening pleasantries.
