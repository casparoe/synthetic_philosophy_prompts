I am defending a chapter of my dissertation at a graduate workshop in Jewish philosophy next month, and I need a preparation sheet for the question period rather than a defense of the thesis or a summary of the secondary literature. I have read the standard medieval critics of the concession reading and the modern literature on the reasons for the commandments; do not spend the sheet re-deriving them.

The thesis of the chapter: Maimonides' account of the sacrificial cult is best read not as a concession to Israel's idolatrous habits but as a functionalist claim about the imagination. On my reading, the cult is retained because the imagination, and not the intellect, is the faculty that moves bodies, and the imagination is retrained only by repeated, costly, publicly choreographed action rather than by propositions. The sacrifices are therefore not a divine accommodation to something God would have preferred to avoid; they are the only technology available for converting a population's habits of worship, and their obsolescence is a matter of degree rather than a binary. The argument runs in four steps: (1) the faculty psychology the Guide inherits gives the imagination sole executive power over action, and gives it a characteristic mode of learning, namely repetition of embodied performance; (2) the cult as legislated has exactly the features that make for effective retraining on that mode — frequency, cost, public visibility, elaborate kinetic detail; (3) the prohibition of local altars and the centralization of worship are intelligible on the same functional logic, not merely as a fence against idolatry; (4) and so the reason the cult is pedagogically necessary is also the reason the contemplative ideal cannot, on its own, sustain a community. I am therefore committed to a corollary I dislike: if the measure of a practice is its power to shape action, then prayer and intellectual worship have to be assessed by the same measure, and Maimonides' ranking of them cannot be the simple ascent he presents it as. The law's own pedagogical logic threatens to leave the contemplative ideal liturgically empty.

The audience is small and mixed. Two Maimonides specialists: one reads him in the line of the Islamic Aristotelian political philosophers and reads the Guide as an esoteric work addressed to a philosophical reader, and will hear my thesis as a flat-footed literalization; the other reads him first as a halakhist and reads the Guide through the lens of his legal corpus, and will want to know what my reading does to the details of the laws of the cult, not to the philosophy. There is also a historian of religion who works on ritual and sacrifice comparatively, who is allergic to treatments of any one tradition's ritual as uniquely rational, and who will want to know what my explanation explains that a generic account of ritual does not. And there is a philosopher of religion who works on divine command theory and the problem of why one has a reason to obey, who will hear the thesis as a variant of the question of whether the reason for a commandment can be part of what makes it authoritative. Two of the five are unsympathetic to the whole project of giving reasons for the commandments.

What I want is the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage they would do rather than by their philosophical depth, each phrased in the idiom of the person who would raise it — the esotericist's objection should not sound like the historian of religion's, and the halakhist's should not sound like either. For each: a reply I could give in about ninety seconds without notes, and a note on where that reply is soft, including what I would implicitly be conceding by giving it. Flag separately the objections I should concede from the podium before anyone raises them, since a concession volunteered is cheaper than one extracted. Tell me which single reply has to land if the chapter is to survive at all, and end with the question I am hoping nobody asks.

Where I know I am weakest: I cannot point to a passage in which Maimonides says the imagination is the target of the cult — I am inferring it from the faculty psychology and from the shape of the legislation, and the inference is the load-bearing joint. If the retraining story is generic to ritual everywhere, my account explains nothing distinctive about this cult. And I have no good answer to what becomes of the bindingness of the law once the imagination has in fact been reformed. Please mark for each objection whether it presses a textual point I can go and check against the primary sources or a philosophical point I have to answer on the merits, and do not include a bibliography or suggest further reading: I want the prep sheet itself, and I want it in under thirteen hundred words.
