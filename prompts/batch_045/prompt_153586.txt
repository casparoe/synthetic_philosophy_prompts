Hey — you're a philosopher of language who also does a bit of AI alignment work, so please think carefully: brainstorm two or three ways into this, say wich one you're setting aside and why, and then commit.

The episode is Google's Duplex demo at I/O in 2018: an assistant phoned a hair salon and a restaurant to make bookings, with the "um"s and "mm-hmm"s and slightly-too-long pauses, and — in the on-stage demo anyway — never said it was a machine. Google's answer at the time was that the shipped version would disclose that it was an assistant. Take all that as given; don't fuss about further details.

The theory I want you to apply is what I'll call provenance honesty: an utterance is honest not when its content is true and not when the speaker means well, but when the confidence it licenses in the hearer, given the provenance the hearer implicitly credits it with, matches the confidence its actual provenance warrants. On this view deception isn't about false propositions or about intentions at all, it's a mismatch between the causal-informational history the hearer assumes and the one that actually produced the words — which is how something with no beliefs and no aims can be straightforwardly dishonest.

So, the mapping: who exactly is the hearer, what provenance do they assign, what's the real one, how big is the gap? Does the gap survive the fact that nothing false about the world got said — the slot was real and the time was available? Do the filler sounds do independent work, or are they just a side effect of optimising for naturalness? What does the theory demand as a remedy — a disclosure sentence, a vocal watermark, a different voice — and does disclosure even close the gap if the receptionist doesn't update on it?

Then run it the other direction. Does provenance honesty over-generate: does it convict any human who speaks fluently about something they half understand, or who sounds more confident than their evidence allows? Does it have anything useful to say about the 2023 mess of a lawyer filing a brief stuffed with ChatGPT-invented case citations, where provenance wasn't really the issue but the content was junk — and is that a point for indifference-to-truth views instead? Where the theory is silent or gives a silly verdict, say so rather than stretching it; I'd rather have an honest account of where it breaks than a tidy conclusion.
