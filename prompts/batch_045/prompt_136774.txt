I'm a graduate student in health policy writing a short paper on diagnostic testing protocols, and I'm trying to use value-of-information theory to settle an argument I keep having with my advisor. My understanding runs as follows: Good's theorem shows that for an expected-utility maximizer a free observation can never have negative expected value, since an agent who acquires it is always free to act as if she hadn't; and Blackwell's ordering turns this into a comparison, so that if one experiment is a garbling of another then the less garbled experiment has weakly greater value. Together these seem to establish informativeness as an intrinsic ranking of tests, and I take two things to follow: it is never rational to prefer a cruder test to a more accurate one, and it is never rational to act before the most accurate result you could have obtained is in hand — which, I take it, is also why a patient who declines to look at a genetic risk result she has already paid for is straightforwardly irrational.

The case I keep returning to is a triage protocol I've been using as a running example. Patients arrive with ambiguous symptoms of what I'll call Kennard's fever, and unless antibiotics are started within roughly six hours there is irreversible organ damage; the drugs themselves carry a real risk of serious side effects. The options are a bedside assay returning a noisy result in about twenty minutes — what I've been calling the low-latency, low-informativeness option — and a sequencing panel with near-perfect sensitivity and specificity that takes three days. There is also a newer monitoring device that streams a fresh noisy estimate every fifteen minutes, so what it supplies is not a single signal but an open-ended sequence of weak ones. My advisor's reply is that waiting for the panel doesn't merely substitute a more informative signal; it changes the decision problem, because after three days the "treat now" branch is no longer available and the underlying state has moved on as well, so there is no one decision problem in which the two tests are being compared. I don't see why that should matter to the ranking: informativeness is a property of the experiment, and if the panel is the more informative experiment then its value is weakly greater, full stop. The shrinking of the action set while you wait looks to me like a separate consideration that the theory has already priced in. I'm equally unmoved by her second objection, that the result may not extend to agents who aren't expected-utility maximizers: surely every sensible form of caution, risk aversion included, is already encoded in the utility function, so the theorem covers any reasonable agent.

So I'd like to know which of us is right and on what grounds. Concretely: can a comparison of the kind I want be made at all across decision problems that differ in their action sets and in how the state evolves, or does the ceteris paribus condition in Blackwell's result make my question ill-posed? How, if at all, does the streaming device fit into a Blackwell-style ordering, given that it offers many weak signals rather than one strong one? And is there a precise way to state my advisor's "shrinking action set" point, or is it just loose talk? If I've framed this wrongly — if the theorems don't say what I think they say, or if the ranking I want simply can't be stated in these terms — please say so plainly rather than answering the question as posed. Please also mark which parts of your answer are standard consequences of the theory and which are contested, interpretive, or your own reconstruction, and flag anything you're not confident about; I'd much rather have a hedge I can check than a clean-sounding claim I can't.
