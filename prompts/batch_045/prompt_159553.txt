I am the token philosopher on a committee at my university that has to choose a social discount rate for long-lived capital projects — a district-heating retrofit whose costs fall now and whose savings accrue over eighty years, a library roof with a fifty-year life, that sort of thing — and I have discovered that I cannot reconstruct a principle behind the numbers I keep wanting to propose. So I am going to list my gut verdicts on a set of cases and ask what discounting rule, if any, they add up to. (1) A migraine for me tomorrow versus an identical migraine for me in exactly twenty years: indifferent, I would pay the same to avoid either. (2) A weekend in Vienna this year versus a qualitatively identical weekend in twenty years: I would give up something like a tenth of the value to have it now. (3) Fifty dollars now to spare a stranger a day of severe pain next year, but only about twenty to spare a stranger an identically severe day three centuries from now — yet if you hand me a device that guarantees the later person exists, that the pain would otherwise occur, and that my money is spent only conditional on both, my reluctance almost entirely disappears. (4) A friend who spends forty years in dreamless suspended animation and wakes in 2065 with no experienced gap: a benefit delivered to her at the instant of waking versus the same benefit delivered a calendar year later — my gut says no discount at all, which suggests that what I am discounting is experienced time rather than calendar time, though I cannot tell what that implies for the committee's eighty-year retrofit, where everyone experiences the interval. (5) A thousand people helped in two hundred years beats a hundred helped today, but a hundred helped today beats a hundred and one helped in two hundred years, so my revealed rate seems to collapse as the stakes grow. (6) I would pay more to postpone a harm by a year than to advance an equal-sized benefit by a year. (7) I would pay a great deal to shave a small probability of civilizational collapse five centuries out, but very little for a guaranteed modest benefit to whoever is alive then, which sits badly with (5). And one more observation that may or may not belong: if I imagine being told that I was born in 1920 rather than 1990, my willingness to pay for benefits in a given calendar year changes, which makes me suspect that part of what I call temporal discounting is really positional rather than temporal.

What I want is the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that fits these verdicts — stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, and with an honest statement of which verdicts it cannot accommodate and why. Please separate, as you go, the discounting that is genuinely a preference for earlier over later from the discounting that is really something else wearing its clothes: risk that the beneficiary never exists or the benefit never arrives, causal fragility over long chains, opportunity cost of capital, diminishing marginal utility if future people are richer, or the positional effect I mentioned. Once all of those are subtracted, is there a residual pure time preference in my verdicts at all, and if there is, is it rationally defensible or is it the familiar failure of imagination that the economists on the committee invoke? I would also like the resulting account's verdicts on three cases I have not listed: a policy whose beneficiaries do not yet exist and whose number depends on which policy we choose; a bequest to an institution that will persist continuously for three hundred years, where no individual experiences the interval; and the difference, if the account sees one, between discounting within a single life and discounting across generations. Finally, tell me whether the account I end up with hands the committee a single number, or whether it implies that a single number was never the right object to be asking for.
