Call the following the generalization thesis about Simone Weil's critique of political parties, and read it as mine.

Weil's argument, as I understand it from her note on the suppression of parties, is structural rather than a complaint about bad actors: an organization that (i) must win contested elections to survive, (ii) bundles stances on unrelated questions into one platform and one label, and (iii) controls access to candidacy and to the careers that hang on it, will predictably subordinate truth and the common good to its own persistence — and will do so even with sincere members, because the mechanism runs through incentives and through loyalty displacing judgment as the currency of advancement. My addition is that none of this is specific to the party form. Any durable organization that recruits members, funds itself, and competes for influence — a trade union, a church, a university department, a liberation movement, or the loose currents of opinion she proposes as a replacement for parties — faces pressures of the same shape, and if her mechanism is real it should reproduce the same deformation of judgment in them within a generation or so. If that is right, her critique either convicts far more institutions than she was willing to give up, or it is hollow as a reform proposal, because whatever occupies the space left by parties will regenerate the pathology.

What would count against the generalization thesis? I want the strongest available case that the party form has some feature Weil's favored substitutes lack, plus any reason to think my three mechanisms come apart — that bundling, say, does all the work while electoral competition does none. I'd also like you to treat it as empirically exposed rather than merely conceptual: what observed pattern would disconfirm it? I have in mind cases like durable non-partisan municipal government, independent central banks, professional bodies that reputably guard judgment, and one-party states, where the pathology arguably flourishes without competitive elections at all — tell me whether each of these actually tests the thesis or merely looks as though it does. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and mark separately which ones Weil herself, by her own premises, would have had to concede as counting against her and which she could brush off without cost. If you think the thesis as I've stated it has been insulated against refutation, say so and say whether that is fatal to it or only unsurprising for a claim of this kind.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate the question back to me.
