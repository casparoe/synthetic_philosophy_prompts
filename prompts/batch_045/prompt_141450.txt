hey, need one more thing for this reading group I'm running

Second session is on Bertrand's chord thing: pick a chord of a circle "at random", whats the probability it's longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Three standard constructions, three answers (1/2, 1/3, 1/4). I do NOT want another handout that lists the three methods and concludes that the principle of indifference is in trouble — everyone has read that a hundred times and it teaches nobody anything.

What I want is a decision tree that walks someone from "here is the bare question, no mechanism specified" down to a named position on what the paradox actually shows. It's a one-page handout for a grad reading group (probability and induction), mixed backgrounds, so keep it readable.

How I want it built:

- Start from the logical space, not from a list of names. Give me the two or three questions that genuinely do the separating work and the answer-combinations they permit, then hang the real positions on those combinations, then tell me which combinations are empty. The empty cells matter as much as the leaves: for each one, a line on whether it's empty because it's incoherent, because it's merely unattractive, or because nobody has bothered to occupy it.
- Every branch node has to be a question someone can answer without knowing which position it leads to, and ideally without knowing the three numbers. So no node that amounts to "so do you like 1/2, 1/3 or 1/4?". Better nodes: does the bare phrase "choose a chord at random" have an answer at all; does the rotational symmetry of the circle settle which distribution is right; should indifference be applied to the chord's length, to its midpoint, or to its two endpoints, and what makes that the right choice; does anything change if you swap the circle for a regular n-gon or a fine lattice and take a limit.
- My own hobbyhorse, please put it in as a node: rotational symmetry can't be what's doing the work, because all three standard distributions are rotationally symmetric and they disagree. So if a student says "symmetry picks out the answer", that's a confusion, and I want the tree to route them somewhere.
- Leaves I want on the page, each with one sentence on its most awkward commitment: (a) the unique rigid-motion-invariant measure on the space of lines, which gets 1/2 but for a totally different reason than the "midpoint uniform on a radius" method that also gets 1/2 — flag explicitly that these are different views that happen to agree here, and say whether anything separates them or whether my tree just can't tell them apart; (b) the "specify a real chance setup — toss a straw, throw a dart — and the number is whatever your setup gives" view; (c) the view that indifference is only legitimate over finite symmetric partitions, so Bertrand shows it doesn't extend to continuum cases; (d) the subjectivist shrug — no prior is wrong, the paradox is a pseudo-problem; (e) a 1/3 leaf for the thought that a chord just is a pair of points on the circumference, so the description itself picks the parametrisation.
- One half-baked idea of mine that I want placed on the tree even if you think it's bad: second-order indifference. If there's no fact of the matter about which parametrisation to be indifferent over, be indifferent over the parametrisations instead, and averaging the three standard answers gives 13/36. Tell me where that sits, what it's really committed to, and what kills it — I suspect its that the result depends on how you carve up the space of parametrisations.
- Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't honestly separate rather than pretending the last node splits them.
- Sizing: about 6–8 branch questions, 6–9 leaves, 2–3 diagnosed empty cells. No citations, and don't sweat names or papers — I'll fill those in myself. (I have a vague memory that some maxent/invariance paper also lands on 1/2; don't lean on that, I'll check it.)
- If anything I asserted above is wrong — the 1/2 coincidence, the finite-limit point, whatever — say so in a short note at the top instead of quietly building the tree on it.

Then, as the very last line, in this exact form, the single node everything else hangs on as a short phrase: \answer{...}
