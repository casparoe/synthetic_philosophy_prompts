I'm taking a seminar on the philosophy of science, and our current unit is on thought experiments in science: whether imagining a situation you have not set up can tell you something about the world that you did not already have from theory and observation. For the final paper I have to defend a verdict rather than survey the literature, and I want that verdict earned on a case instead of on the famous examples we've been reading -- so please don't build the essay around Galileo's falling bodies, Newton's bucket, or Maxwell's demon; use them only where you need them.

The case: two sealed spacecraft cabins, each filled with air at sea-level pressure, each containing a small helium balloon released from rest relative to the air, partway between the middle of the cabin and its wall. Cabin A is in free fall, far from any mass, and not rotating. Cabin B rotates steadily about an axis through its centre, with the air co-rotating, so that someone standing on the outer wall feels artificial gravity. Say where each balloon goes, and what supplies the force -- or the absence of one.

Then three things. First: what would have made you abandon that verdict -- what observation, or what flaw in the reasoning, would count against it -- and does it matter, for the claim that the case yields knowledge, that A is in principle checkable (a few seconds of free fall in a drop tower or a parabolic flight would be enough) while B is not, at least not cheaply? Second: was the verdict already contained in premises we antecedently accepted -- Archimedes' principle, the equivalence principle, hydrostatics in an accelerated frame -- or did the imagining do work those premises could not? Take a real position on the objection that a thought experiment never delivers more than the argument it encodes. Third: my instructor says the runnable cases are the easy ones, that the interesting class is the thought experiments that could never be performed at all, where intuition is all we have; say whether that is right, and whether what you conclude about the cabins gives any reason to trust those.

I want a committed conclusion, and I would rather have an argument I can push back on than a balanced survey.
