I write a twice-monthly column on science and ideas, and I'm halfway through a piece on precommitment in its many registers: the locked cabinet with the medication in it, the app that bills you if you open it after eleven, the balanced-budget amendment, the man who had his crew lash him to the mast so that he could hear a song he had been warned would ruin him. Next month I've agreed to run a single ninety-minute session at a festival for general readers — about twenty-six adults, nearly all strangers to each other, wide age range, no philosophy assumed and nothing to read in advance. The festival organizers asked for something participatory, by which they mean people doing things rather than voting on a show of hands between slides. I tried a version of this over dinner with friends and it collapsed into an argument about whether wanting something twice means wanting it more, which I take as evidence that something is there but that I don't yet know how to stage it.

What I want the room to feel, before anyone names it: that the person who sets up a constraint and the person who has to live inside it can disagree without either of them being confused; that a constraint only bites if it is made costly or impossible to undo, and that the cost cannot tell apart a change of mind you would endorse from a distance from one you would not; and then the constitutional version, where the binding party is long gone and the bound party never agreed to anything. Nobody should hear the words Ulysses, precommitment, or entrenchment until the last twenty minutes. I'd also rather avoid anyone being asked to role-play addiction or to volunteer anything personal — keep the stakes in the fiction.

I'd like the session built around a mechanism I've been working through in the piece, which no one in the room will have met and which I think is doing real philosophical work: a commitment that is never absolute but always breakable at a price, where the price is paid to whichever faction or impulse the commitment was meant to protect you from, rises each time you pay it within the same episode, and where every buyout has to be requested twice with a wait in between. That reframes the question from whether to be tied to the mast into how much it should cost to be untied, who should receive the payment, and what to do about the fact that someone in the grip of the impulse will pay a price their earlier self would have called insane. Could you design the session around that: the scenario the participants are dropped into, the roles and any private information, the rules, what's on paper, how many rounds and roughly how long each runs, how the stakes work without money changing hands, and what the facilitator says and pointedly does not say. Then the debrief: which moments in the game are meant to map onto which features of the problem, what is predictably going to go wrong the first time I run it with twenty-six strangers at the end of a long festival day and how to recover, and — since some of this will end up in print — an honest note on which part of the idea the game cannot stage and which I would have to supply in prose myself. Please answer in British English, and finish by saying how confident you are that this will actually work with a real audience, and which single element of the design you are least sure of.
