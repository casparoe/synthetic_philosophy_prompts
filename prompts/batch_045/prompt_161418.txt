I'm retired now — forty years in population biology, mostly insect time series and a lot of arguing about density dependence — and I've reciently started going to a philosophy reading group, which has been worse for my blood pressure than the arguing was. I wandered into the units-of-selection business and built the argument below, and I don't like where it ends up.

(1) Take any structured population. Its change in composition under selection can be written as a within-group term plus a between-group term (the Price-equation / contextual-analysis sort of partition), and the two ways of writing it are arithmatically equivalent: same numbers, same prediction, pick whichever is easier to compute.

(2) If two formulations are arithmatically equivalent, neither one cites a cause that the other leaves out. A cause has to make a difference to what happens, and equivalent descriptions make all the same differences.

(3) So between-group selection is not a distinct causal process from within-group selection. The group-level term is bookkeeping that's convenient when populations happen to be structured, and nothing more.

(4) The same partition works one level down: change in gene frequency = a within-individual term plus a between-individual term, equivalent in exactly the same sense.

(5) So, by (2), selection among organisms is not a distinct causal process either. It's bookkeeping about genes.

(6) But then organism-level adaptationist explanation is false, or at best façon de parler. "The heart is there because pumping blood raised the reproductive output of the organisms that had one" can't be a real explanation of anything if no real process of organisms-being-selected is doing the work.

(7) And that can't be right. Adaptationist explanation is among the best-confirmed explanatory practices in biology: it licenses predictions about trade-offs, convergence and design constraints that you never get out of gene-frequency bookkeeping, and I spent a career cashing exactly those predictions in the field.

(8) So something in (1)–(3) has to go.

I don't want to give up (1) — I've checked the algebra myself — and I really don't want to give up (7). I'd happily give up (3): I came into this thinking group selection was mostly hype, and I'll take it on the chin if it turns out to be real. What I won't take is being told that my organismal explanations were only ever manners of speaking. Someone in the group says the thing to reject is (6): organism-level selection is a genuine causal process because organisms are the things that actually meet the environment, and genes just get dragged along. That sounds like hand-waving to me, but I can't say what's wrong with it.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Give me exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each: the step you'd reject; why that step looked fine; what you're committed to once you've rejected it; the runner-up step and its price; and what kind of case or observation would actually settle it for you. No preamble, no summing up at the end.
