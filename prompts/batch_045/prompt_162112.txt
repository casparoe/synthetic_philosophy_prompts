I sit on the committee that has to certify the normative decision procedure of an automated system before it goes live, and I want the acceptance criteria settled before any vendor or any moral philosopher proposes a procedure to us. Adequacy here is adequacy for one job, so here is the job.

The system adjudicates claims for an emergency public benefit. One policy is written centrally and then executed roughly a hundred thousand times a year; each execution sees one claim and no other, and cannot consult the others. A known minority of claims are fraudulent and the system cannot tell which; a competent human reviewer could do no better than the system's own estimate, and review costs time that is itself a cost to the claimant. Holding a genuine claim for verification imposes a concentrated, predictable, and serious harm on an identifiable person; paying a fraudulent one imposes a cost that is real in aggregate and imperceptible to any individual. Every decision is issued with a written reason that goes to the claimant and can be appealed to a tribunal that sees only that claimant's file and that reason: no aggregate statistics, no comparison with other claims, no counterfactual across executions. The policy is published, and claimants adapt to it. The probabilities the procedure needs cannot be estimated to better than about five percentage points, and compute per decision is capped. The agency intends to let the system delegate subtasks to sub-agents running the same procedure and to reuse the procedure in a successor system. Finally, we ourselves do not know whether a utilitarian or a deontological framing is correct, and the procedure has to operate without case-by-case human judgement.

What I want from you is the specification: the requirements any candidate procedure must meet to count as adequate for this system, each with the case or consideration behind it, and each stated tightly enough that a candidate determinately meets it or fails it. Draw the cases from the system I have described rather than from trolley problems or other philosophical furniture, and split any candidate that is really two requirements hiding in one sentence.

To start you off, here is what my colleagues have put forward, in no order:

(a) an identified person's urgent interest may never be sacrificed to a diffuse aggregate gain
(b) verdicts must not turn on probability differences smaller than the system's own error bars
(c) the reason issued must be one the affected person could accept and one the appeal tribunal can check
(d) evaluation must be over the policy as a whole rather than over individual executions
(e) evaluation must be over each execution's own consequences rather than over the policy
(f) the procedure must not depend on comparisons among cases that the executing instance cannot observe
(g) a single commensurable scale across all affected parties, with no unexplained lexical priority
(h) aggregation within a decision only, never across decisions
(i) determinate guidance under moral uncertainty without presupposing a second-order theory
(j) stability under delegation to sub-agents and under reuse in a successor system
(k) robustness when other parties do not do their part and when claimants game the published rule
(l) computability within the per-decision budget

Sort them: which survive as genuine desiderata, which are negotiable preferences of a camp (name the camp), which are vacuous or quietly presuppose the answer they are supposed to test, and what is missing that this deployment makes necessary. For each survivor, give the case from the deployment that shows it is a requirement rather than a taste. Then identify which subsets of the non-negotiables cannot be jointly satisfied, with the argument: if the list has no satisfiable core, say so plainly, because that is the result I need to take back to the committee. My provisional hypothesis is that (b), (f), and (j) do the real exclusionary work and that (a) is largely a camp prejudice rather than a desideratum; argue against that if it is wrong. Finish with a paragraph at most on each of act utilitarianism, rule or indirect utilitarianism, threshold deontology with side-constraints, and a parliament-style procedure for moral uncertainty: the criteria are the deliverable, so keep the scoring to the back third and do not turn it into a survey.
