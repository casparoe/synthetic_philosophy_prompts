Last night I had a long argument with an old friend — a practising Catholic and a cell biologist — about whether she is entitled to vote against public funding for embryo research purely on the ground that her religious convictions require it. She kept returning to one thought: that being asked to restate her reason in a shared vocabulary is being asked to misdescribe why she actually holds what she holds, and that a politics which demands this is asking her to be a different person in public than she is in private. I kept returning to the opposite thought: that a law coercively imposed on people who do not share her premises needs a justification they could in principle recognise, and that an appeal to her church's authority is not one. We went round for hours and neither of us moved, mostly because we were both being abstract about it.

I teach an advanced undergraduate/MA seminar on religion and liberal politics — 18 students, a single 90-minute session in the last week of term — and I want to use that session on something better than a debate. What I'd like from you is a structured deliberation game or role-play in which the students run into both halves of that argument themselves, as agents with something at stake, before anyone names either half. I have a rough skeleton and I'd like you to build it out, fix it, or replace it where it won't work.

The skeleton: each student is secretly dealt a "conviction card" giving them a comprehensive doctrine and a specific conclusion on one concrete legislative question — I was going to use a conscience-clause amendment to a hospital funding bill, but I'm open to a cleaner case if that one drags the discussion into medical detail. The cards should be deliberately mixed. Some are religious, but at least one should be a secular comprehensive doctrine whose reasons are just as contested and just as unavailable to someone outside it (a thoroughgoing perfectionism about the arts, say, or an orthodox utilitarianism), so that the easy reading — "this is a problem about religious people" — quietly fails. Crucially, some cards should give reasons that have a decent secular stand-in, and others should give reasons that essentially cite a text, an authority, or a revelation, so that any restatement loses what made the reason a reason for that person. Then two rounds on the same question under different rules: in the first, you may only offer reasons that others can be expected to accept; in the second, you may offer your own reason provided you also supply a shared one. Same people, same cards, same question, so they feel the difference in their own conduct rather than only in the outcome. My scoring idea, which I suspect is where the real work is: every student keeps a private ledger of the two or three reasons that actually moved them, and separately a public ledger of the reasons they stated. Stating a reason that is not among your operative reasons costs you an integrity token, and integrity tokens can be cashed in for legislative capital at a deliberately bad exchange rate — so restraint is available, mostly cheap, but never free, and the price falls unevenly across the room. I also want a receiving-side mechanic: some students are assigned to certify whether a stated reason is one they can see as a reason, so that "accessibility" shows up as a relation to an audience rather than a property of a sentence. Nothing may be named in advance: no lecture at the front, and the terms public reason, comprehensive doctrine, and the proviso should not be uttered until the debrief, so that the naming lands on something they have already done.

What I need back, then: the cards and the case; the rules, the timeline, and the payoff scheme, using points or chips rather than money; and a debrief that maps particular moments in the play onto particular parts of the problem — why restraint feels like a gag from the inside, why an untranslated reason feels like coercion from the receiving end, why you can sincerely affirm a public reason that did not move you at all, why reasons thin out toward vacuity as the audience widens, and why the cost of restraint is unevenly distributed. Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong: my guess is that some students will play it as pure coalition bargaining and ignore the reason-giving rules entirely, that whoever draws the religious card will either over-perform or go quiet because the room is secular, that someone will announce that they would simply lie about their reasons, and that the certification mechanic will get used as a tactical veto rather than an epistemic test — but say what you'd actually do about each. And be frank about what a 90-minute game cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself: I assume it collapses "accessible in principle" into "acceptable to this particular room," and that it makes restraint look like a legal gag rule rather than a moral duty borne by citizens and officials, and that it can't show the long-run, diachronic character of the "in due course" clause at all.
