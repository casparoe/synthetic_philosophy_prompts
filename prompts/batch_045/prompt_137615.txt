Our hospital ethics committee has to write the treatment-limitation and critical-care allocation protocol for the ICU, and the thing such a protocol has to be able to say, in terms a junior doctor can apply at three in the morning, is whether what is being authorized is ending a life or letting a life end. Before we look at candidate accounts, I want the specification: what does an account of the doing/allowing and killing/letting-die distinction have to deliver in order to be good enough to be written into a protocol like ours?

Give me the requirements list, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark each as non-negotiable for this purpose or as a preference of one theoretical camp. In particular, tell me which of the following are genuine desiderata and which are partisan demands: that the distinction be drawable from outside the agent's head, since we can audit decisions but not intentions; that it apply to institutions, standing orders and software defaults, and not only to individual agents acting at a time; that it not rest on contested claims about whether omissions can be causes or whether absences can figure in causal explanations; that it not quietly collapse into the distinction between intending death and merely foreseeing it; that it treat doing/allowing and killing/letting-die as one distinction rather than two that can come apart; that it give the same verdict on the well-known bathtub pair, where one killer holds the child under and the other only fails to rescue him; and that it nonetheless mark a difference between diverting the trolley and cutting up the healthy patient for his organs.

The cases the protocol will actually have to decide include: an allocation algorithm that defaults to reassigning a ventilator from a patient who will die within the hour to a patient with a far better prognosis unless a clinician opts out, where no clinician opts out; a surgeon who programmes an infusion pump to deliver a lethal dose hours ahead and is asleep when it runs; a clinician who withdraws the life support she herself initiated; two clinicians who each fail to rescue, each believing the other will; and a patient whose death follows a decision taken under genuine uncertainty about prognosis. Then tell me which subset of the desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, since that would make the list itself the useful result, and which single requirement does the most work in excluding otherwise attractive accounts. Score only briefly, a sentence or two each, the three families we keep being offered: counterfactual or baseline accounts, intention-based accounts, and the view that the distinction bears no moral weight of its own and merely tracks other factors.
