I teach a third-year undergrad seminar on social epistemology (philosophy majors plus a handful of econ kids, 24 of them, one 75-minute slot, no TA, no budget for cash payoffs, and the room has a whiteboard while I can bring a deck of cards, two paper bags, and a stack of index cards). I want a game that makes them *feel* the pull of an informational cascade rather than hear about it. The thing I care about is the nasty version of the point: that it can be perfectly rational for each person to set aside their own evidence, that the group can end up confidently and unanimously wrong, and that in the process a roomful of private information gets thrown away — twenty-four people's worth of evidence doing no more work than the first two. I'd rather they not hear the words "cascade" or "herding" until after the activity, and I'd rather they construct the situation themselves than be told a story about it. My plan, which you should feel free to scrap or improve, is the standard two-bag setup: one bag is mostly red, the other mostly black, flipped to decide which one is in play, each student peeks at a single ball in secret and has to guess which bag it is. The twist I want is that I run it twice with the same students and the same signal quality: once as a secret simultaneous ballot where I just tally the guesses, and once sequentially where each student announces a guess after hearing everyone before them. Same people, same evidence per person, wildly different group outcome. If you can think of a better way to get that contrast across, use it.

Two extra wrinkles I'd like built in, because they're the bits I think students most often get wrong afterwards. First, informational herding vs. reputational herding: in this game there's no reputational incentive at all, so if someone goes along to avoid looking dumb, that's a different mechanism than the one the model describes. I'd like the design to separate them — either by a cheap third variant that adds a reputational payoff (say, at the end the class votes on who seems best at this and that vote is worth something), or by a debrief question that surfaces who copied for which reason, and I'd like your honest recommendation about whether adding the reputation round clarifies things or just muddies the water. Second, I want a handful of randomly chosen students to be "experts" who get to peek at three balls instead of one, without the others knowing who they are. My hope is that in the sequential round the expert, if they end up late in the order, is completely powerless — their good information is trapped — and that we can then talk about what happens if you reorder the queue by expertise. If that's not actually what the model predicts for those numbers, tell me and fix the design. I also want each student to write down a confidence number with each guess, because I suspect confidence will climb through the sequential round while accuracy doesn't, and I'd like a prediction about how big that gap should be and whether it will actually show up with n=24.

What I need back: exact rules and materials; the urn composition and actual probabilities for it (how often a cascade forms, how often the group's public answer is wrong, and how many rounds I'd need to run to be reasonably sure of getting a *wrong* cascade at least once — and what I do on the day when no cascade forms because someone breaks it, or when the aggregate is right and there's no drama); what I say at each step, since I'll be running this while also managing the room; and what will predictably go wrong. I can think of a few myself: people saying "I saw black but I'll go with the group," which leaks their private signal and destroys the setup; people accidentally showing their ball; my own face reacting; the econ students deliberately contrarian to break the cascade; and the quiet students who won't speak at all. Tell me how to handle each. Then the debrief: map specific moments onto the specific parts of the idea — what a cascade is as opposed to mere agreement, why adding people stops adding information, why the same public sliver of news would barely move a real aggregation but can overturn a cascade — and end with the moment where everyone reveals their private signal at once and the room sees whether the majority evidence pointed the other way. Finally, be frank about what the game can't show: real signals aren't drawn i.i.d. from a known urn, real people also choose *what* to look at, real herding happens on networks rather than in a queue, and my students will immediately want to talk about bubbles, restaurants on a street, and pile-ons on social media. I want a short list of what I should let them conclude and what I should stop them from concluding, with reasons.
