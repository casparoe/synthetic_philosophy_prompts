Next week I am presenting a paper in our philosophy of biology seminar, and I would like your help with the discussion period. My thesis is that Popper's critique of Darwinism actually contains two separable complaints that are almost always run together, and that only the weaker of the two was ever withdrawn. The first is the tautology charge: that the Darwinian principle of natural selection, read as the claim that the fittest survive, threatens to say no more than that those who survive survive, and so looks unfalsifiable. The second is the demarcation verdict that Darwinism is best understood as a metaphysical research programme, a heuristic framework that guides the construction of testable models without itself being a testable claim, and that this is a compliment rather than an insult, since Popper took metaphysics to be indispensable to science. I argue that when Popper softened his position in later years he gave up the first complaint but not the second: he conceded that particular selection hypotheses are testable while continuing to treat the general theory as an untestable but fertile schematic framework. So the live question is not whether evolutionary biology is empirical, which nobody denies, but whether its organizing principle has the status of an empirical generalization or of a constraint on model building.

The room will be about twelve people. There is a population geneticist who regards the whole debate as armchair philosophers inventing a problem about a theory with abundant empirical content; two philosophers of science of a broadly post-Kuhnian, anti-demarcationist stripe who take falsifiability to be dead and my paper to be of at most historical interest; and one Popper scholar who knows the texts closely and will press me on how much weight my reading places on a handful of late passages. The graduate students will mostly follow whoever speaks first.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: six to eight objections, ordered by how much damage they would do rather than by how likely they are, each attributed to the kind of person who will raise it and phrased in that person's vocabulary rather than mine. For each, tell me what genuinely has force, what I should concede immediately instead of fighting, a reply I could deliver in roughly two minutes, and where that reply is weakest. Flag the objection I should hope nobody asks, and say briefly why you ordered the list as you did. Close with a single line of the form \answer{...} naming the one objection that sinks the talk if I handle it badly.
