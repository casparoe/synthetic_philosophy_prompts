I've been reading about metaphysics on my own after work (no classes, mostly argument threads), and there's one line people keep throwing at me as a conversation stopper: "if you can truly say there is one, then there is one, so stop asking whether it really exists."

Last week it got used on a question I actually care about: whether a project team is a real thing, or just a handy way of talking about the five people in it. One guy, Dan, said: we truly say "there's a team that shipped it" and "the team decided to cut the feature", so by that line teams exist, case closed, nothing left to argue about. It felt like a word trick to me, but I couldnt say why, and I couldn't tell whether he'd proved something or just renamed the problem.

Then it got stranger. Someone else pointed out that Dan and the guy he was arguing with agree on basically every plain sentence — who said what in the meeting, who wrote the code, who got the blame — and said that means the whole dispute is merely verbal, a fight about the word "exists". Dan said no: even granting all those sentences, there's still a further question, whether the team's deciding is anything over and above the five people's votes and messages. That bit I really can't sort out.

Can you lay out the different precise things that one-liner could be claiming, from the dead obvious to the wild? Stuff I'm wondering about: does it just mean "there is" means what it means (true and useless); does it mean a theory is committed to whatever it has to quantify over; does it mean existence is one thin notion, so there's no "really real" versus "sort of real" left to argue about; does it mean every existence question is really a question about which sentences are true, so the argument was verbal all along. And the awkward cases: I truly say "there's a bug in my code" and "there's an average of 2.3 kids per household", and I really don't want to be committed to bugs and averages as things, so some version of the line has to be wrong or need a caveat.

For each reading say whether it's a platitude, a real and defensible thesis, or actually false, and tell me which reading (if any) would do the work Dan wanted — settle the team question and make it not merely verbal. Also say which reading people who drop that line usually mean. Last thing: tell me how confident you are in what you're claiming, and single out the point you're least sure about.
