(1) pure time preference is bogus — the bare fact that a benefit arrives later isn't a reason to count it for less, in the same way that its happening further away isn't.

(2) but I do apply a positive discount rate, a few percent a year, to future costs and benefits. I justify it two ways: future generations will probably be richer, so a given gain matters less at the margin, and there's some real chance there won't be anyone around to receive it.

(3) I'm fairly sympathetic to longtermism: the future could hold enormous numbers of people, so stuff that shifts the trajectory — existential risk reduction and the like — plausibly beats ordinary near-term spending by orders of magnitude.

(4) I'm also half convinced by the non-identity problem: I don't really think we can wrong, or owe much to, particular individuals whose very existence depends on the choice we make.

(5) personally, I'd take $100 now over $110 in a year for myself even after adjusting for inflation, for my own rising income, and for the chance I don't survive the year — and I don't think that makes me irrational.

(6) I apply one constant rate at all horizons; declining-rate scheduals strike me as a fudge introduced to get the answer you wanted, even though I'm genuinely uncertain how fast growth will be.

Which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? For the genuine tensions, rank them by severity and say what the cheapest fix would be and what it'd cost me.
