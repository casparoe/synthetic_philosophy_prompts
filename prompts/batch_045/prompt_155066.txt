I am on a university research-ethics board that is rewriting its voting rules, and I have been reading a chapter in a recent popular book on collective decision-making that bears directly on what we are doing. The chapter presents the material as settled, but several of its claims strike me as stretched, and I do not know enough about this literature to say which is which. I have retyped the central passage roughly as it appears:

"Here is the puzzle. A three-judge appellate court must decide whether a defendant is liable. The statute makes liability turn on two things: that the defendant breached a duty of care, and that the breach caused the plaintiff's harm. Call those D and C, and the verdict L, where the law says L holds exactly when D and C both hold. Judge One accepts D and C, and so votes liable. Judge Two accepts D but rejects C, and so votes not liable. Judge Three rejects D but accepts C, and so votes not liable. Nothing here is anyone's fault: each judge's three votes fit together perfectly. But count the columns and you get a majority for D, a majority for C, and a majority against L. The court endorses both of the things that together suffice for liability and then denies the thing they suffice for. Philosophers call this the discursive dilemma, and it is not an artifact of one example: it arises whenever a group has to settle several logically connected questions at once, and it means the group's answer depends entirely on whether it votes on the verdict directly or on the reasons first and then deduces. It is really Arrow's impossibility theorem in a different hat: Arrow showed that no rule can turn individual preferences into a coherent group preference without violating some minimal condition, and this is the same argument run on beliefs instead of preferences. The moral is bleak but important. No group can be both responsive to its members and logically consistent, so every court, committee and electorate must simply choose which of the two to sacrifice. Notice, too, that the court brought this on itself: the trouble comes from insisting on giving reasons, and a court that just announced verdicts would never face it. This is why the philosophers who work on these paradoxes deny that groups can really believe or intend anything; 'the court held that...' is only a façon de parler for a tally of votes."

Our board's agenda has the same shape, one level deeper. Our standing rules say a protocol may proceed (A) if and only if its risks are minimal (R) and its consent procedures are adequate (C); and that risks count as minimal if and only if either the procedures are non-invasive (P) or a data-monitoring plan is in place (M). A colleague has proposed that we ballot the five atomic questions one at a time in a fixed order, and that any question whose answer is already settled by the answers recorded so far together with our standing rules should be entered as determined rather than put to a vote. He says this "solves the discursive dilemma" for us, because the board can never end up asserting an inconsistent set.
