Hey, I need a hand turning a half-baked idea into a 2–3 page proposal. It's for a six-month fellowship — basically one paper's worth of work, no data collection, so it has to be doable by me alone with a laptop and some toy simulations on synthetic risk distributions.

The area is the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness. You know the shape of them: a risk score can't in general be calibrated within each group *and* have equal false positive rates *and* equal false negative rates across groups, except in degenerate cases — equal base rates, or a predictor that's already perfect.

My half-formed thought is that these results get read as "fairness is mathematically impossible," but that reading hides what the conflict is a conflict *between*, and I suspect a good chunk of the drama comes from two modeling choices nobody ever argues for: (1) the decision rule is deterministic, and (2) everything is counted in units of error rates rather than in units of downstream harm. If you let the rule be randomized — a lottery over who recieves the scarce thing, or a randomized threshold — the feasible set of error-rate pairs should convexify, and then the "impossibility" turns into a "how much do you pay" question. And if you denominate the two error types in a common currency of harm (days of pretrial detention, say, against the expected cost of a failure to appear or a reoffense), the tradeoff might look quite different from how it looks when both errors just count as "1".

So maybe the right thing to say isn't "you can't have all three" but something like: there's no free lunch, but the price is often small, and where it isn't small it lands on a particular group in a way the theorems themselves don't show.
