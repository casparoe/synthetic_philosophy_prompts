Good's theorem is standardly summarized as follows: for an agent who maximizes expected utility and updates by conditionalization, information that is free has non-negative expected value, and strictly positive value whenever what it says might change what the agent does — the thought being that any message can simply be ignored, so the worst a free observation can be is worthless. I accept that, but it doesn't sort my actual verdicts about declining information, and I'd like the principle they add up to. I'm setting aside cases where the information is merely expensive, slow, or effortful to get; those I find uninteresting.

My verdicts:

1. An hour before I must decide whether to hold a garden party outdoors, I'm offered a free and reliable forecast. Declining: not fine — I'd be leaving expected value on the table for nothing.
2. At the twenty-week scan I'm offered the sex of the baby. I intend to do nothing differently either way and I want the surprise at the birth. Declining: fine — a pure matter of taste.
3. A free inspection would tell me whether the crack in my foundation is serious. Bad news would ruin my month, but if it is serious I'd have it repaired and if it isn't I could stop worrying. Declining: not fine — I'd be a coward not to look.
4. A lab offers to run, free, an experiment whose outcome I'm already about 90% sure of. It wouldn't change what I do next, but it would change what three other groups in the field do. Declining: not fine — the study is worth running.
5. I'm on a hiring committee that could collect applicants' race and first-generation status. I'd rather not have it: partly because I don't trust myself or my colleagues to ignore it, partly because not having it is itself a defence. Declining: fine.
6. A friend has conclusive evidence about whether my partner has been unfaithful, and offers to tell me. I don't want to know. I want the marriage to continue, and I'm fairly confident that if I learned the worst I'd feel obliged to end it — an outcome I'd rather not be in a position to have to choose. Declining: fine.

My own candidate principle: declining information is fine just when either it would change no one's action, or other agents can observe that I've received it and would respond to that. That handles some of the six and not others, and cases 3 and 6 in particular seem to pull in opposite directions.

Two I haven't settled: (i) a firm offers to tell me, using a model trained on my entire digital history and right about 95% of the time, which of two job offers I will accept, telling me before I decide; (ii) I'm offered, free, detailed behavioural data about my own customers that would let me raise prices substantially — using it would be legal, but I expect that once I had it I couldn't stop myself using it in ways I'd now call exploitative.

Please state a principle precise enough to apply to a new case without further judgment, and test it against all eight. Reply as a JSON object with exactly these fields:

principle — one or two sentences, the principle stated precisely, including the quantity whose sign decides whether declining is permissible.
clauses — an array of objects {clause, motivated_by, assumption_of_Good's_theorem_it_tracks}, one per clause of the principle, each tied to the case that forces it.
case_table — an array of objects {case, verdict, value_terms, assumption_violated, saved_or_overturned, note}, one per case 1–8, where value_terms records which components of the value of the information are positive, zero, negative, or undefined (instrumental, experiential, strategic, third-party), and assumption_violated names the premise of the theorem the case breaks, if any.
new_cases — an array of objects {case, verdict, reason, confidence} for (i) and (ii) only, with confidence a number between 0 and 1; if you judge either case ill-posed rather than decidable, say so in verdict and explain what makes it so.
residual_tension — a string stating which pair of my verdicts no clean principle saves, and which one you'd overturn.
