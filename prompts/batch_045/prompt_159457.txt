I work in a small evidence and policy unit inside a ministry, and I have been asked to draft a memo that I am struggling even to structure, so what I need is help with the argument rather than sources or further reading. Background: our country usually places somewhere in the high teens in the well-known annual world happiness rankings, the ones that put Finland at the top year after year, and a newly appointed junior minister has announced an official target of reaching the top five within ten years, with a small budget attached and our unit made responsible for the delivery plan. Two problems have come up that I cannot reconcile with each other.

The first concerns the cheapest proposals on the table, all of which work on the moment of measurement rather than on anyone's circumstances: a public broadcasting campaign about noticing the good things in one's life, timed to the weeks when the survey is in the field; a "positive news hour" partnership with the main commercial broadcaster; and a suggestion from an external polling firm that respondents be asked a couple of warm-up questions about recent good events before the life-satisfaction question itself. A friend at the national statistics office says this is straightforwardly corrupt: we would be manufacturing the appearance of improvement and would ruin the series for anyone who later wants to track anything. My instinct is with her, but I cannot state the objection cleanly, because the minister's reply seems hard to answer. The thing being measured just is what people say when they are asked, he says; if the campaign leaves people genuinely more inclined to register what is good in their lives, then their lives have improved in the only sense the ranking tracks, so what exactly is the difference between moving the number that way and moving it by, say, shortening hospital waiting lists? A second friend added a different worry: we could improve everyone's life substantially and still not rise, if other countries improve faster, so is a position in a table even the sort of thing a government should aim at?

The second problem is that we ran a small pilot module of our own alongside the regular survey, because several of us were uneasy about the headline question, the one that asks people to place their current life on a rung between the worst and the best life they can imagine for themselves. Our module asked whether there is something about their life they have wanted to change for more than a year and have not been able to change, and how often in the past week they felt they were getting through the day rather than living it. On the official question, our poorest region scores almost the same as the capital, and young people in the big cities score almost the same as retirees on the coast. On our two questions the gaps are enormous, and they cut against the official picture. I cannot tell whether this shows that the official question is a poor instrument and that we should propose a different one as the target, or whether it shows that our own questions are intrusive and condescending, the sort of thing a government has no business asking, because people may honestly judge their lives to be going well by standards that are their own and it is not for us to say those standards have quietly shrunk to fit what they can realistically hope for.

So the memo needs to decide three things, and I would like your reasoning on each as much as your conclusion. Should we recommend adopting a numerical target at all, given that the number is a comparison with other countries and a report of how people say they feel rather than a count of anything we deliver? If we do adopt one, should the official target be the standard ladder question, something closer to our pilot questions, or some combination, and what should we say to the regions whose poor scores on our module would suddenly become the thing government is accountable for? And how should I answer the minister's argument that changing what people say and changing how their lives go are not really different here, given that the measure is self-reported? The memo is three pages, due Friday, and will be read by people who will be impatient with anything that sounds like academic hedging, so please tell me plainly where you think I am confused and where you think the minister is right.
