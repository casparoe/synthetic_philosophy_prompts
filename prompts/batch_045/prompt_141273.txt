hey — I sit on our university's IRB (behavioral/social science panel) and we've got a protocol I can't quite get a handle on, and I'm the one writing up the decision for Friday.

A psych PI wants to run an online study where participants are told they'll be paid a lump sum the moment they report having formed the intention to come into the lab and do a genuinely unpleasant 90-minute session (cold pressor plus a tedious cognitive battery) on a Saturday three weeks later. They're told up front that they don't have to show up, no penalty, they keep the money either way, and the payment is in no way contingent on attendance. The only data are the self-report of intending, logged at the moment payment is released, plus whether they actually show up. His framing is that he wants to know whether people can form an intention they already know they have no reason to act on — the old millionaire-and-the-vial-of-toxin setup, but with real subjects instead of an armchair.

Two things are splitting us. First, money: he wants a $400 arm and a $25 arm. With $400 it starts to look like we're paying people for a mental event, and one of our members — a retired psychiatrist who is Szaszian about basically everything — says intentions aren't the kind of thing that can be bought at any price, so what we'd actually be paying for is a verbal report, which would turn the study into a study of self-deception rather than of intention. I think she might be right and I can't tell whether it's fatal or just a note for the discussion section. His own reason for the low-payment arm is separate and, I think, better: he's worried that at $25 people show up out of ordinary politeness, which would make it a study of something else entirely.

Second, and this is the one I keep going back and forth on: he doesn't want to tell participants the hypothesis. His argument is that if you say "we're testing whether you can really intend to do something you know you have no reason to do," that knowledge is itself what makes the intention impossible to form, so the effect vanishes and there's no study. So he's asking for a consent form describing it as research on "planning and follow-through," with full debriefing at the end. Half of us think that's straightforwardly a consent problem. I also genuinely can't see how you disclose the real hypothesis and still have an experiment.

Can you draft the memo I'll circulate? Approve, reject, or approve with conditions — say which one in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning, no preamble and don't restate the protocol back at me. I need the strongest version of whichever side you come down against, including the psychiatrist's objection if you're recommending we go ahead (and the consent problem if you're not). And it has to be something he could actually respond to: real conditions he can revise against and resubmit, with a rough timeline, not just "reconsider the design." Skip the regulatory citations and the Common Rule stuff, our compliance office handles that separately — I want the argument. Somewhere in the 700-900 word range would be great.
