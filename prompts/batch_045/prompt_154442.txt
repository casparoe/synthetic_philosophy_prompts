My colleague Nadia and I are planning to leave the architecture practice we both work at and set up on our own. A client we both get on with has told me privately that he would move his work to us, but only if both of us come; with either one of us alone the whole thing collapses. The lease on the premises we want has to be signed on Friday morning and our notices have to go in the same day. We cannot discuss any of this at the office, and Nadia is on a site visit all week with terrible reception, so we are reduced to a messaging app that loses roughly one message in twenty, acknowledgements included, independently of everything else. We agreed in advance that whoever hears from the client first will message the other, that every message gets a one-word acknowledgement, and that we will keep acknowledging until we are both sure. The asymmetry between us is that if I hand in my notice and Nadia does not, I am ruined: no income, a mortgage I cannot service, and a reputation for having jumped at nothing, which all told is something like forty times what success would have been worth to me. Nadia's position is entirely different, since her husband's salary covers the mortgage and she could pick up freelance work inside a month, so the worst case for her is a fraction of what the move would gain her.

The two friends I have talked this over with disagree in a way I cannot adjudicate. The first says this is simply a reliability problem: if one message in twenty goes astray, then six rounds of acknowledgement leave something like a one-in-sixty-million chance that the chain is broken anywhere along the way, so we should fix on six rounds and get on with it. The second says that is the wrong calculation altogether, that what each of us needs is not the likelihood that the messages arrived but confidence about what the other will do given what she has actually seen, that no amount of back-and-forth supplies this because the final message in any chain is always the one whose arrival cannot be confirmed, and that this is a known impossibility result rather than an engineering problem. He also claims that further messages cannot help at all, which I find very hard to believe, and he could not tell me whether it makes any difference which of us sends the first message.

So: is he right that piling on acknowledgements achieves nothing, or does it help, and if so how much and where does it stop helping? Does his impossibility claim actually hold in our case, or does it turn on numbers like my exposure versus Nadia's and the one-in-twenty loss rate, and if it does turn on them, which of those numbers should we be trying to change and by how much? Does it matter that Nadia is the one who will see whether the client has confirmed, and that she is the one of us who could absorb a mistake? Does the fact that we agreed on all of this in advance, while sitting in the same room, count for anything? And concretely, given that we have other options available, such as handing signed letters to a solicitor who releases them only if both arrive, asking for unpaid leave rather than resigning so that a mistake is survivable, or getting on a call at a fixed hour on Thursday evening, what would you actually advise us to do?
