I should say at the outset that I am neither a theologian nor a historian. I spent a little over thirty years as a stratigrapher, much of it reconstructing depositional histories from sections in which most of the record was missing and the remainder had been reworked by later processes. That, I think, is why I have become interested less in what is currently said about the historical Jesus than in how anyone claims to know it. Through a reading group at my parish I have gathered that the field no longer supposes it can simply authenticate individual sayings, and instead applies a set of criteria — embarrassment, dissimilarity, multiple attestation, Semitic idiom and Palestinian context, and coherence with whatever has already been established — to a tradition that everyone agrees is confessional in origin.

Before I read any further I want the specification. Please write the desiderata that any criterion of authenticity would have to satisfy in order to count as evidence about Jesus at all, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and with a view on which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely the preferences of one camp. In my own subject a method was never taken seriously until it had been run against material whose answer was known independently, so please say what the calibration set is supposed to be here: is there any body of material in the tradition whose status we can check without using the criteria themselves, and if there is not, is that a defect in the criteria or an unavoidable feature of the evidence?

Three further things bear on the list, and I would like each of them treated as a candidate desideratum rather than as background.

First, the asymmetry. Embarrassment and dissimilarity look to me like vetoes: they can tell me that a tradition is unlikely to have been invented by the early communities, but I cannot see how they raise the probability that it goes back to Jesus rather than to some other inconvenient source. Please require the list to say whether an adequate criterion must be able to move probability upward against a stated baseline — and if the honest answer is that these criteria only ever fail to lower it, then say what that implies for the strength of the conclusions actually drawn. Relatedly: should a criterion be required to output a graded likelihood rather than a binary verdict, given that the evidence is graded?

Second, what I have started calling the bootstrap problem. Every criterion appears to be applied against a background model — of what would have embarrassed the early church, of what a first-century Galilean Jew could plausibly have said or done, of which strands of the tradition are genealogically independent of which — and the verdict can apparently be no better than that model. Please state as a desideratum what a criterion must do to avoid merely returning its input, and tell me whether multiple attestation can satisfy it, given that the criteria are being applied to sources that are themselves partly derivative and that the dependence relations are the very thing in dispute. I would also like the list to address whether a criterion is permitted to take as a premise some theory of how the tradition transmitted — that post-Easter faith reshaped the record, or that it did not — or whether it must be able to test that premise.

Third, please flag any pair of desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied; that is the part of the specification I most want. The obvious candidate is this: on the one hand a criterion should not authenticate anything continuous with early Christian belief or with first-century Judaism, since on this material continuity is what invention looks like; on the other hand the Jesus recovered by the criteria ought to be someone who could account for the movement that followed him and who is intelligible as a Jew of his place and time. If a Jesus discontinuous with both is not available, then one of those two requirements has to be surrendered, and I want you to say which one and at what cost.

Two more constraints on the answer. Please say how the list would change if the purpose shifted — I have been assuming the purpose is to say of some small set of traditions that they go back to Jesus, rather than to the communities, but others in my group want a minimal factual biography and one wants only an explanation of how the movement arose, and I suspect each purpose generates a different list. And please mark which requirements are genuine desiderata and which are confessional or anti-confessional prejudices in disguise: in particular, whether neutrality about the truth of the tradition is something a criterion must have, or whether requiring it simply stacks the deck.

Finally, on the register of the answer. Do not give me a survey of the options. Rank the desiderata by how much they exclude, identify the one that the current set of criteria most flagrantly violates, and commit yourself on the general question of what these criteria are — genuine tests, defeasible reasons to doubt, or codifications of scholarly judgment dressed as method — with your reasons, and with a note on what would change your mind. Where you are making a judgment call rather than reporting something agreed, say so, and say how confident you are.
