Hi — I could use a hand sorting out something I read. I'm retired now; most of my working life was lake sediments (varves mostly, and far too many hours counting diatom frustules), and since I gave up the lab I've been going along to a philosophy of science reading group, where I keep being surprised by how much of what my field called a result was really an arguement about concepts.

So: there was a piece in a weekend science supplement about the Anthropocene and the vote on it, and it read well but set my teeth on edge. Roughly, it said this:

"A working group of stratigraphers was set up in 2009 to decide whether the Anthropocene should be added to the geological time scale. In 2023 they settled on Crawford Lake in Canada as the place where the change is recorded best in the mud, with the start date at about 1950, when plutonium from the bomb tests rained down over the whole planet. Before that the argument was all about when it began — some said the steam engine in the 1780s, some said 1610, when the Americas were depopulated and the regrowing forests pulled carbon dioxide down, some said farming, thousands of years earlier. Then in 2024 the geologists voted the proposal down, so the Holocene officially continues and the Anthropocene has been rejected by the people whose job it is to decide these things. The trouble is that a geological epoch has to be pinned to a single layer of rock you can actually point at, and human influence doesn't start at the same moment everywhere — Europe was cleared for farming thousands of years before Australia was — so there is no one boundary there to draw. Not that it matters to anyone outside the field: whether we call it an epoch is only a naming dispute, and the climate science comes out the same either way."

My instinct is that the last bit is the bit that's wrong, and that the "no boundary to draw" bit is muddled, because if I remember right the fallout spike is about the closest thing to a globally synchronous horizon you could ask for, and its the older start dates that are the patchy ones. But I'm out of my depth on how people who actually maintain the time scale think about boundaries, and on who counts as having the authority to decide.

Could you go through it and tell me what a stratigrapher who works on the time scale would accept, what they'd wince at, and what is just wrong? In particular: is the "who decides" story right, or was that narrower than the piece implies? Is the diachroneity point being used in its proper technical sense, or is it being used loosely to mean "humans arrived at different times in different places"? And does the conclusion — that none of this makes a difference outside stratigraphy — actually survive once you correct the rest?

I also want to float an idea of my own, because nobody at the group liked it and I can't tell why. Why not make it a provisional unit: fix the base at 1950 now, with an explicit clause that the boundary gets re-fixed in a century or so when the mud really is rock and the signal has finished arriving — the way provisional designations work in astronomy. Is that coherent, or is it just refusing to play the game the stratigraphers are playing? Someone at the group said the serious alternative is to declare it an "event" rather than an epoch, which sounds to me like relabeling, but maybe I'm missing something.

One more thing, and it's the main thing I want: please be clear about what you're confident of and what is genuinely disputed among people who know the stratigraphy and the philosophy of it. I'd rather be told "this is a live fight" than handed a smooth consensus that isn't one — and if my own provisional-spike idea has an obvious objection a stratigrapher would raise in the first thirty seconds, I'd like that objection straight.
