I am a professor of ancient moral psychology — Aristotle mostly, with some side-glances at the Hellenistic therapists — and I have been drafted onto an interdisciplinary seminar on value learning whose other members work on inverse reinforcement learning. As I understand the setup: one observes an agent's behavior; one assumes the agent is optimizing some reward function, noisily and approximately; one tries to recover that function from the behavior; and one then uses the recovered function to generate a policy, frequently one intended to outperform the demonstrator whose behavior was the evidence. I follow the broad shape. What I cannot yet tell is how much of this is a genuinely new problem and how much of it is an old problem in unfamiliar notation.

So I want a translation, not an explanation. I have taught the Nicomachean Ethics perhaps a dozen times; my Greek is serviceable rather than elegant; I know the literature on akrasia and on the unity of the virtues tolerably well. Assume I am fluent in that vocabulary — hexis, prohairesis, phronesis, enkrateia, habituation, the fine or fitting as that for the sake of which action is done, the distinction between doing what the just person does and doing it as the just person does it, the figure of the practically wise person as measure — and that I know nothing of machine learning beyond what has leaked into my notes from the seminar.

Please render each of the following into that vocabulary, one by one, as faithfully as you can:

1. The reward function, understood as a scalar quantity defined over states or trajectories, and the end of action as its maximization.
2. The notorious underdetermination of the reward by the behavior — that indefinitely many reward functions, including degenerate ones, fit any finite run of behavior equally well — together with the standard disambiguating devices, so far as they are part of the problem's standard formulation (assumptions of noisy optimality, and selection principles such as maximum entropy or maximum causal entropy).
3. The demonstrator treated as a noisy channel, with the noise compressed into a single scalar parameter.
4. The aim of recovering the reward in order to produce a policy that surpasses the demonstrator.
5. The fact that what is learned is a weighting over a feature space the designer supplies: the system recovers weights, not the features themselves. I have been calling this the donated eye.

For each, report three things: what carries over cleanly, what carries only if distorted, and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all.

Then run three cases of my own through both vocabularies, and tell me where they diverge. First, the conscripted expert: a paramedic whose performance is flawless under stress, who avows no interest in the work, cites fear of her supervisor as her actual motive, and intends to quit. Second, the outdistanced teacher: a learner that, on the reward recovered from the demonstrator, reliably outperforms the demonstrator. Third, a distinction I have been using in my own notes and cannot get my colleagues to take seriously — that between endorsement-inference, recovering what the demonstrator would endorse on reflection, and source-inference, recovering what in fact produced the behavior. Tell me whether that distinction has any grip in the technical framing, or whether the framing simply collapses it.

Finally, the residue report proper. Where translation fails, I want a verdict: is the failure a limitation of the Aristotelian vocabulary — which has, for instance, no notion of a scalar over trajectories and no formal apparatus of inference from samples — or is it a symptom that the claim being translated is less determinate than it looks? And, symmetrically, is there anything in the Aristotelian apparatus that the inverse-reinforcement-learning framing lacks and arguably needs: a notion of a norm internal to the agent that the agent can nonetheless fail to meet, an account of how a learner becomes the sort of thing that can learn at all, a reason for treating extensional equivalence of behavior as not yet equivalence of what matters? I do not want a defense of either framework; I want to know which is the more damaged by the mismatch.

Two constraints on how you do this. First, do not smuggle Aristotelian assumptions into the translation and present them as translation — the claim that there is a natural end, the unity of the virtues, the authority of the practically wise person as a stopping point of justification. Each time you find yourself doing that, stop and mark it. Second, separate what is settled from what is contested among people who actually know these literatures: whether human behavior is usefully modeled as optimizing anything at all; whether the underdetermination of the reward is a technical nuisance or a conceptual verdict; whether Aristotle's end of action is a maximizing target or something else; whether the practically wise person functions as a measure or merely as a pedagogical device. Where a reading of Aristotle is disputed among specialists, say so rather than picking one quietly. No citations and no bibliography, please — I can set a graduate student on that. I want the conceptual shape, and I want to know how much weight each part of it will bear.
