So I've been running a fortnightly reading group on the conceivability–possibility link for about a year now (I'm a 4th-year PhD student, modal epistemology / imagination adjacent), and we're stuck in a way that feels structural rather than just "we haven't found the right paper yet."

Let me set out which versions I mean so you're not diagnosing a strawman. Take the zombie argument in its careful two-dimensionalist form. The premise isn't just "I can picture a zombie"; it's that after ideal a priori reflection nothing in a complete physical-and-functional description of a world entails the phenomenal facts about it; that this gives you a *positively* conceivable scenario (a world considered as actual, not as counterfactual); and that ideal positive conceivability yields possibility — the two-dimensionalists disagree among themselves about whether that runs through primary intensions or holds more directly, and I want to bracket that wrinkle rather than fight about it. Conclusion: physicalism as a metaphysical thesis is false.

The physicalist replies we keep cycling through: (a) Kripkean necessary a posteriori identities show the step from "not derivable a priori" to "not necessary" is invalid, and the standing reply is that ideal reasoning is allowed to absorb every a posteriori identity we already know, so the gap survives idealization; (b) the Yablo-flavoured line that conceiving a scenario is at best a test or a heuristic for possibility rather than a source of evidence about it, because nobody has said what the link is supposed to be; (c) the Block-flavoured worry that the conceiver has to think "no consciousness here" using phenomenal concepts, and if the supposed extra fact has no causal or dispositional profile to latch onto, it's unclear we've picked out a scenario at all rather than dressed up a blank stare in zombie's clothing.

Two things came up in the last couple of sessions that nobody had an answer to, and I suspect they're where the real action is.

First, a symmetry worry. It also seems not-ruled-out-a-priori that the physical facts necessitate the phenomenal ones via some brute Kripke-style necessary connection discovered a posteriori. If conceivability is only ever absence of a priori entailment, then the same datum that certifies P-and-not-Q as possible also certifies P-necessitates-Q as necessary, and you get a modal contradiction (in S5: possibly-necessary collapses to necessary). Something has to break the tie. The honest move is to insist on ideal positive conceivability and say ideal reflection would resolve the Kripkean case toward necessity while leaving the zombie case open — but then the disagreement looks like it's about whether ideal a priori reflection has any authority over modality in the first place, i.e. about the epistemology of modality, not about consciousness at all. And there's a further sub-question: whether ideal reflection might just close the gap entirely (the a priori entailment option), which gets dismissed in about a sentence every time — and I want to know whether that dismissal is an argument or just the intuition we're arguing about.

Second, a parity worry. There's no a priori entailment from descriptive facts to moral facts either, and the moral-twin-earth style arguments generate "all the descriptive facts, different moral facts" with a very similar intuitive pull — yet almost nobody concludes that moral facts are ontologically extra. If the phenomenal case is special, that's a claim about phenomenal concepts (or about the structure of our concept of the physical), and the disagreement quietly relocates to philosophy of mind. If it isn't special, someone owes an answer about the parody.

My working hypothesis, which you should feel free to demolish, is that "conceivability" is doing at least three jobs in this debate — a report about what it's like to try to imagine something, an a priori non-entailment claim, and a bridge principle about the modal authority of a priori reason — that everyone in the room agrees about the first two, and that the live disagreement is the bridge principle plus a prior disagreement about what a modal claim is answerable to. But I also can't shake the feeling that there's a hidden empirical question: whether the faculty we're relying on — imagination, conceptual reflection, whatever it is — has any track record we could calibrate it against, given that the only apparent calibration cases (Hesperus/Phosphorus, Goldbach's conjecture, high-dimensional geometry, undiscovered identities generally) are cases where it fails.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict on physicalism. Is this verbal or substantive? Is it a fight about a bridge principle, about which cases count as data (do zombie intuitions count as evidence, or as the thing to be explained away), about what modality is *for*, or about bedrock starting points neither side can argue for? Is there a hidden question — empirical, about our imaginative capacities, or formal, about the logic of idealization and whether "ideal reflection" has a fixed point — doing the work under the table? And given your diagnosis, what would progress even look like: a distinction that cuts "conceivable" more finely than ideal/prima facie and positive/negative already do, a formal result about the two-dimensional machinery, an empirical result about imagination, or a change of question? Or is there a principled reason to expect no progress, and if so what is it?

Take my hypothesis seriously but don't just agree with it — if the actual structure is different, say so and argue for it. If either of my two worries above is a familiar move with a familiar reply, tell me what the reply is, but argue it out rather than telling me who to read; I have a reading list already and it hasn't helped. Not looking for citations or a bibliography. Somewhere around 700 words if you can manage it.
