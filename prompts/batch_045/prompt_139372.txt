My film club has this running argument and I want to bring something better than vibes to the next meeting.

One of the regulars, Dov, pulls out 'you have to see it for yourself' every time someone proposes we pick a film because a critic or a friend raved about it. He means it as an epistemic point, and he's clearly got it from somewhere — he once muttered that aesthetic judgements have to be answerable to your own first-hand experience, and when I googled it later it turns out this is a whole named topic in philosophy (aesthetic testimony). His use of it in the room is to block a pick: no one can know, or justifiably believe, that a film is good on the basis of what someone else says, so a rave gives us nothing to go on and we should just rotate who chooses.

But he doesn't apply it evenly, which is where it got interesting. Last month he skipped a restaurant purely on the strength of one terse text from his sister, and he'll cheerfully say he knows he'll hate the thing coming out in March just because a friend who has agreed with him on about 500 out of 500 films called it self-indulgent garbage. So I don't know what the slogan is even saying anymore.

Can you lay out the different precise claims it could be making, as a menu, from the trivial to the wild? Some candidate readings I can half-see but can't get straight:

- Is it about whether aesthetic properties can be known at all (nothing to know, so nothing to transmit), or only about how they can be known?
- Is it about belief rather than knowledge — i.e. you can't actually form the belief on testimony, you can only note that someone said it and wait until you see it?
- Is it not epistemic at all, but a claim about what engaging with art is for: even a person who somehow knew the verdict would still be missing the thing that matters, which is the experience?
- Is it specifically about your own future reactions — that whether you will like something isn't the sort of thing anyone else can tell you?
- Is it just the general point about experience, the same reason testimony can't tell you what a durian tastes like, or is something special supposed to be going on in the aesthetic case?
- Is it a claim about critics and experts lacking authority, as opposed to friends who happen to share your taste?
- Does it cut differently for negative judgements ('it's terrible') than for positive ones?

For each reading please say whether it's a platitude, whether it's a real thesis someone could sensibly deny, whether it's actually false, and which reading the people who repeat the line are probably reaching for. The bit I really need though: which readings would actually get Dov his conclusion in the meeting — that a rave gives us no reason to pick a film — while still letting him keep his restaurant habit and his 500-out-of-500 friend. If a reading convicts him of inconsistency somewhere, say where; if the honest answer is that he has to give up one of the three, tell me which one is least defensible.

Three test cases to run the readings through, because I suspect they separate versions that otherwise look identical:

1. There's a recommender app trained on my own ratings and watch history that has called my reaction to 200 straight films without a miss. When it tells me I'll love something I haven't seen, is that 'seeing it for myself' in any sense the slogan cares about, or is it just testimony with extra steps? Does it change anything if it can also tell me why, in the vocabulary it's learned that I respond to?

2. Same film, but the only screening is a nine-hour installation piece I physically can't get to. Is the slogan then saying something temporary (you can't know yet) or something permanent? Those feel like very different theses and the argumet in our group chat slid between them constantly.

3. Dov is losing his sight — a few years and he won't be able to watch films properly at all. Whatever the slogan turns out to mean, does it have anything to tell him then about what he can believe about new releases, or does it just go quiet? I'd rather your answer here didn't depend on how grim you feel like being.

Format it so I can literally read it out at the start of the meeting rather than having to reconstruct your reasoning from scratch. And skip anything that requires looking things up: no reading lists, no authors, no 'as so-and-so argues', I just want the options and where they leave Dov.
