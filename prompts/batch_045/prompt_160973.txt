im finishing up a paper on Avicenna's flying man -- the setup where you imagine yourself created all at once, fully formed and rational, suspended in the air or in a void, your sight shrouded from any external object, limbs separated so they don't touch each other, so you get no sensation of your own hand or foot or heart or brain -- and the bit im stuck on in my own prose is the split in how people read it. On one reading all it establishes is that self-awareness is primitive, immediate and constant, not inferred from or constituted by any bodily awareness; on the other it's meant to get you all the way to an immaterial, substantial soul. I want a poem to use as a sort of epigram at the top of the paper (my supervisor thinks this is twee, i think it works), but i don't want it to smuggle in the second conclusion as if the first one had already delivered it, and i also don't want it to be so deflationary that it just goes "he's aware, so what" -- something genuinely surprising is happening in the thought experiment and the poem should make you feel that.

What id like is a sestina in the voice of the flying man: six stanzas of six lines plus the usual three-line envoi. Please use these six end-words, in this order to start: air, hand, tongue, name, dark, I -- rotate them the standard sestina way, and have the envoi bring all six back, two per line, with "I" last. The reason i want that form is that the form is the argument: each stanza should strip something else away (sight, touch, the sense of where his limbs are, the memory of having had a life, the words he'd use to describe himself) while all six words keep returning in a new arrangement, so the reader gets the invariance built into the verse rather than being told about it. What survives every permutation is the bare affirmation that he is; what he cannot affirm is any of the things those words pick out, and especially not that any of them is him. Keep it accurate enough that someone who works on Arabic philosophy wouldn't wince: he affirms the existence of his self without affirming length, breadth, depth, or any part of a body.

The wrinkle i've been chewing on, and the thing i actually want the poem to do rather than decorate, is a variant i've been calling the named flying man. Same setup, except that at some point a voice in the void addresses him by a name -- calls him Zayd, in a language he somehow still has, since the original setup does give him his reason -- and from then on he can say "Zayd is" as well as "I am". Here's what i can't settle and want left unsettled: the naming looks like it adds something, a way of saying who as well as that, but it also looks like it introduces a kind of knowledge that could go wrong by identifying the wrong thing, whereas the bare "I am" doesn't seem to have that failure mode -- so is the name a genuine addition to his self-knowledge, or just noise that arrives and changes nothing about what he already had? I'd like the envoi to be where the name arrives, or where he tries to make it do the work that "I" does and finds that it doesn't quite. No footnotes, no headnote explaining the readings, and no triumphant closing line about knowing he's an immaterial soul -- end on the bare affirmation with the question of what this "I" is still open.
