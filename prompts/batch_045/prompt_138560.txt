I joined the allocations committee of a small philanthropic fund about three weeks ago, and the first substantive docket item of my tenure is mine to write up. The fund distributes roughly four million dollars a year across three buckets; I sit on the committee for the speculative bucket, which has five hundred thousand dollars to move this cycle. Our donor's letter of instruction says we may only support work with a *traceable theory of change*, a phrase the committee has never defined and has argued about at every meeting in the archive.

Before I write anything, I want to know what rule we have actually been running rather than what we say we are running. The committee keeps a calibration log of stylised cases it returns to whenever a live proposal looks strange. The entries, with the recorded vote and the one-line reason each time:

1. **The car-park letter.** An unsolicited demand for five dollars; the writer says that if it is refused he will, by means he does not describe, kill 10^30 people. Declined unanimously. Reason recorded: an unverifiable number from a source with no mechanism behind it.

2. **The paired letters.** Two correspondents in the same week: the first demands a thousand dollars to avert 10^30 deaths; the second demands a thousand dollars to avert the 10^31 deaths that payment to the first would supposedly trigger. Declined both, unanimously. Reason recorded: we are not in the business of ranking other people's unverifiable numbers.

3. **The wager.** A proposal to commit the whole bucket to proselytising on the strength of roughly a one percent chance of an infinite reward. Declined, two to one. Reason recorded: infinite stakes are not something this committee can book.

4. **The calibrated long shot.** Two hundred and fifty thousand dollars to a biosecurity programme whose model has been validated against a reference class of comparable programmes: about a one-in-three-thousand chance of averting a catastrophe that would kill on the order of a billion people. Approved unanimously. Reason recorded: a number we can source.

5. **The preprint.** Twenty thousand dollars for a tabletop experiment whose only support is a chain of theoretical argument putting the chance of preserving 10^40 future lives at 10^-13, with no empirical handle on that probability at all. Declined, two to one. Reason recorded: no handle on the probability.

6. **The field-staff demand.** A verified threat against forty named people we employ, with demonstrated capacity to carry it out, for sixty thousand dollars. Declined, two to one, with the dissent recorded at length: we would spend considerably more than sixty thousand dollars to protect those forty people if nobody had demanded that we do so.

The live case is a letter signed only M. She says that unless fifty thousand dollars is paid to a named adult-literacy charity within ten days, she will bring about an outcome that kills 10^34 people. She offers no account of the mechanism. She has, however, done two things the car-park letter did not. Three months ago the committee sealed predictions about thirty specified events; twenty-nine came out as she said. And she reproduced from memory the text of an internal memo that exists in our own archive and in one encrypted copy. She adds that she has made the demand costless to herself and has routed it to an unrelated charity precisely so that our usual questions — what does she gain, why this fund, would paying invite imitation — will have no purchase on her, and she says she will not reveal afterwards whether she acted on the threat.

What I need from you:

- Two or three rules that fit all six precedents, stated precisely enough that they disagree somewhere. I suspect the live options differ over whether the operative objection is to the size of the number, the smallness of the probability, the provenance of the probability, the fact that it arrives attached to a demand, or the incentives that compliance creates — but I want them stated as rules that generate verdicts, not as a list of considerations.

- What each rule predicts about M. Give me your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, and tell me which precedent is the closest analog: my own view is that case 5 is closest in structure and case 6 closest in spirit, and I would like that challenged if it is wrong.

- How much of the log is explained by the donor's mandate rather than by any decision-theoretic conviction, and whether that matters here. Our chair insists the mandate question and the substantive question are separate and wants both answered: whether paying into that escrow is something this fund may do at all, and whether, if it may, it should.

- How far M's verified performance can actually move the probability that she has the causal power she claims. Her evidence is predictive, and what she claims is causal; it looks to me as though mundane hypotheses — an excellent forecasting operation, a leak from somewhere in our own systems — predict that evidence just as well, and I want the order-of-magnitude argument for whether any amount of predictive success could ever be evidence of power at that scale, or whether the mundane explanations always absorb the evidence first.

- The smallest change to the case that ought to flip the committee, and whether that change is available in principle. And one further hypothetical to enter in the log, specified tightly enough that it would separate whichever two rules you think survive.

I should say where my own difficulty lies, since it may be relevant to how you read the precedents. My instinct is to decline, and it does not move when I make the number larger: I would decline at 10^46 as readily as at 10^34, and I think I would go on declining if she had got thirty out of thirty. I cannot tell whether that is a principle about numbers too large to act on, a principle about claims with no mechanism, simple refusal to be threatened, or only an inability to be moved by a magnitude I cannot picture. If the fourth, then my recommendation carries no more weight than a shrug, and I would rather know that before I put it in writing.
