Ok, thought experiment: Jerry Fodor died in 2017, so he never saw the interpretability era — sparse autoencoders, "features as directions" in the residual stream, linear probes, and the whole fight about whether a net really represents anything or whether we're just finding our own concepts in a pile of matrices. Someone resurrects him, explains it in an afternoon, and he's actually willing to engage with it.

Setup so he's not a strawman: for him mental states are relations to representations in a language of thought, those representations have to be causally efficacious and compositional/systematic rather than merely correlated, and he was hostile to reductive neural-level accounts of content.

Two things I want you to feed him, both invented by me, don't go looking them up. (1) A "phantom feature": a direction in activation space that a trained probe reads as "the context implies P" with ~99% accuracy, but zeroing it out changes the next-token distribution by essentially nothing. (2) The basis problem: the net's function is invariant under any invertible linear map of its activation space, so nothing inside the net picks out those directions as the natural units — plus the mirror case of a direction whose removal does exactly what you'd predict if the net tracked P, but which no probe for P ever finds. I keep going back and fourth on whether the last case is even coherent.

So: would Fodor say a net like that represents P, or that the phantom/driver split just rediscovers his distinction between a correlation and a real symbol? And does the basis problem read to him as a new argument for his side, or as a problem for everyone including him?

Give me exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no intro and no conclusion.
