ok — i need a poem to close out the last session of a zhuangzi reading group. we're ending on the butterfly passage at the end of the "discussion on making all things equal" chapter (the one that wraps up, as far as i remember, with the bit about the transformation of things and with a remark that there must after all be some distinction between Zhou and the butterfly — i don't need the wording, just the shape of it).

i want a sestina: six end-words rotating in the usual pattern, six stanzas, then a three-line envoy. the reason i want that form specifically is that in a sestina the words stay the same and only their slots change, so no single line can tell you where in the sequence you are — which is exactly the version of the dream problem i've been failing to get the group to feel. call it the morning-after problem: when Zhou wakes, the question "ok but which one am i now?" can't be settled from the inside, because the only evidence available to him is how coherent and textured the world currently seems, and dreams sometimes supply coherence too. so the doubt isn't descartes'. nobody is anxious about whether there's an external world; the doubt is about which of the two he is, and about whether the "he" survives the swap.

so let the rotation do the swap rather than argue for it: no "is anything real", no simulation talk, no anguished speaker — the voice should be amused and light, like the text. and please don't let the envoy collapse into either "we can't know anything" or "it was all one undifferentiated mush": the passage wants both the transformation and the distinction, and i want the last three lines to hold both without deciding between them. pick whatever six end-words work (i'd guess something like wings / Zhou / butterfly / dream / waking / distinguishing). under a minute read aloud, and assume listeners who don't know any chinese but have read the passage once — if someone who knows it well would wince, that's a fail.
