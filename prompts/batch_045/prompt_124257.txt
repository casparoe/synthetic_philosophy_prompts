Write a piece of sustained institutional satire: the customer-facing *Reference Class Selection Guide* sent to new policyholders by a fictional insurtech company, Venn Mutual, which has abolished demographic rating and replaced it with an app in which every applicant personally selects the reference class from which their own risk will be projected.

Form and voice: a help-centre document of roughly 850–1,000 words, written in the register of contemporary UX microcopy and startup onboarding — second person, relentlessly cheerful, reassuring about a process that is quietly catastrophic. Use headings, at least one worked example box, a short FAQ, and a block of numbered fine print at the end. It should read as though it were written in-house and approved by legal. No character in the document ever uses the word arbitrary, and nothing in it winks: the reader should be the one who notices.

Setup, for the writer's use: Venn Mutual's product rests on two internal doctrines that it has trademarked. The *Maximum Specificity Mandate* instructs underwriters to project a single case from the narrowest class for which reliable statistics exist. The *Cell Size Covenant* requires any class used for rating to contain at least one thousand lives. A fictional regulator, in response to early complaints, has imposed a *Two-Quote Rule*: every screen must display the applicant's chosen class and the company's chosen class side by side, with the resulting premiums. The customer picks their cohort at onboarding; the fine print will reveal who picks at claim time.

What the satire is aimed at: not corporate malice, but the structure of single-case frequentism. Exaggerate only by taking real commitments literally. The jokes should come from considerations a philosopher of probability would recognise as fair: that any individual belongs to indefinitely many classes, each with its own frequency, and that nothing in the data singles one out; that the instruction to narrow the class, followed faithfully, terminates in a class of one, where the frequency is either zero or one; that the competing demand for statistical stability pulls in exactly the opposite direction and has no adjudication procedure behind it; that classes defined by the outcome are forbidden and are trivially reconstructed out of proxies; and that neighbouring classes, each defensible, can disagree by an order of magnitude for the same person on the same day.

Requirements:

1. One worked example, set out in full, in which two classes the guide itself endorses — say a broad age-and-sex band, and the same band narrowed by three innocuous-looking further traits — yield premiums differing by roughly tenfold, with the guide resolving the conflict by a rule the reader can see is not a rule (a coin flip, alphabetical order, whatever the reinsurance treaty specifies).
2. A No Peeking section explaining that the class of people who will not file a claim this year may not be used, followed by onboarding questions that reconstruct it exactly.
3. Fine print that concedes, without apparent embarrassment, (a) that Venn Mutual, not the customer, selects the reference class when a claim is filed, and (b) that attributes the company may not name are recovered in full by proxy classes — browser window width, hours of activity between one and four a.m., ZIP+4 — so that the prohibition changes nothing measurable.
4. One passage of genuine, unironic success: a large, homogeneous class with a stable long-run frequency, where the projection is uncontroversial and the rate is obviously right. The absurdity should be located in the unconstrained middle, not in statistical prediction as such; the piece should survive the objection that it is attacking something nobody defends.
5. A closing footnote, set in small type, that is not played for a laugh: a reminder that on the strictest view of the matter, if the world is deterministic, every reference class has a long-run frequency of zero or one, and the entire product is a consequence of not knowing which.

Background, for orientation only — do not cite or quote any source, and do not build the piece around a bibliography: the problem is standardly posed for direct inference, where a single case is made to inherit the frequency of a broader class, with the classical frequency theorist's rule (associated with Reichenbach) to take the narrowest class for which reliable statistics can be had, and with the contemporary argument that any constraint strong enough to single out one class among the many is either question-begging or already a substantive theory of objective chance. Assume your reader has never encountered any of this and should not have to.
