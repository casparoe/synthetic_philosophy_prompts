Hey — I'm doing a philosophy of medicine module this term (it's a joint honours, so half the room are medics and half of us are just philosophers) and I've got completely stuck on the naturalism vs normativism thing. My tutor keeps saying something like "no dysfunction without a history, no history without a selection story" and I sort of get it but it doesn't help me with the actual cases. My flatmate, who's a medic, says I'm just reinventing the harmful-dysfunction account badly; my cousin the physio says the whole question is a waste of time and we should only ever ask whether the person needs help. So I thought I'd write down my gut verdicts on a pile of cases and see whether anything actually fits them. Definately changed my mind twice on the deafness one while typing this.

Cavities: a disease, even though nearly everyone I know has had one and the reason is the modern diet. Male pattern baldness at 55: not a disease. Sudden patchy hair loss at 24: a disease, and I can't say why that's different from the previous one. Lactose intolerance: not a disease, and I still think not even in a country where everyone drinks milk — although on my own view that seems inconsistent with cavities. Sickle cell trait: not a disease. Sickle cell disease: obviously a disease. Congenital deafness in someone who grew up signing in a Deaf family: I want to say not a disease, or at least not one they need treatment for. The same person losing their hearing at 40: a disease. My friend who used to run at club level and, after some viral thing, can now just about walk to the shops: ill, even though every test comes back normal and his exercise capacity is now exactly what an average sedentary 45-year-old man has. Long covid with normal bloods and scans: ill. ME/chronic fatigue: ill, and I get genuinely cross when people imply otherwise. Short because your whole family is short, nothing else wrong: not a disease. Exactly the same height but from growth hormone deficiency: a disease. Infertility at 78: not a disease. Infertility at 30: a disease. High cholesterol with nothing else wrong: I want to call it a risk factor rather than a disease, but I do want to call prediabetes a disease, which seems like I'm cheating. Internet gaming disorder and female sexual dysfunction: not diseases, or at least I suspect those are conditions that exist mainly because someone had a pill to sell. Asymptomatic atrial fibrillation picked up by someone's smartwatch: I lean towards disease. And chronic loneliness in an old man whose family moved away: I go back and forth on this one every single time I think about it. My flatmate has already pointed out that I accept ME on basically the same kind of evidence on which I reject internet gaming disorder, and I don't have an answer for her.

The test I keep trying out is: something is a disease iff (a) some part of you isn't doing what it was shaped to do, or what it typically does in the relevant comparison group, and (b) that failing cuts against something the person themselves has reason to care about. But I can't work out what the comparison group is supposed to be — your age, your sex, your species, the population you actually live in, or your own baseline before you got worse? — and I can't tell whether (b) is about what the person wants or about what's bad for them whether or not they want it. Please be straight about which of my verdicts a principle can't save and which ones I'd have to give up, and say whether the honest answer is that I'm conflating disease with illness with sickness and should stop. Say in one sentence which famous view I'm closest to if you like, but don't build the answer around it — and no bibliography, no suggested reading, no citations, since I'd just be guessing at those anyway. British English please, and keep it under about 900 words.
