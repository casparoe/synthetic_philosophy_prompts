I have been reading the arguments about the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence on LessWrong-adjacent blogs and forums for something like fifteen years, and I have come to think that a large share of the remaining disagreement is being mis-tracked: the two camps keep trading the same three examples past each other, and neither has ever written down, in advance, what it would expect to see if it were wrong. So I do not want another essay. I want the document that a well-run adversarial collaboration would produce between the following two positions, written so that a realist and a skeptic could both put their names to it.

The first camp I will call the convergence realist. It holds that capability and final-goal content are independent axes, so that no amount of raw optimization power by itself pushes a system toward any particular terminal goal; and it holds, separately and more substantively, that instrumental convergence is robust, in the sense that across a wide class of final goals the same instrumental sub-goals tend to be useful — preserving the ability to pursue the goal further, resisting modification of the goal, acquiring optionality and resources, preventing interference — and that the formal results on power-seeking give this robustness a shape that does not depend on the fine details of the utility function.

The second camp I will call the no-final-goals skeptic. It holds that orthogonality is close to vacuous as an empirical claim about the systems we actually build, which are trained to perform tasks and look goal-directed only relative to a frame we impose; that the convergent-drives arguments smuggle in an agent with a utility function over world states and a planning horizon long enough for the drives to pay; that where power-seeking has been made precise, the conclusions are sensitive to how power and the state and action spaces are formalized and to assumptions about optimality; and that the failure modes that actually matter — misspecification, Goodhart-curvature under optimization pressure, deceptive behavior without any coherent final goal — are different failure modes requiring different mitigations.

Deliver the following, in a formal register, with no citations or references to the literature: I know the literature, I want the content.

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. These must not be vacuous. Both camps should be able to sign each one, and each should be strong enough that an informed outsider would learn something from it. Include at least: what orthogonality does and does not assert; what would have to be true of a system for the convergent-drives arguments to even get a grip on it; and what both sides agree about the limits of the current formal power-seeking results.

2. A distinction I want you to develop and then apply, because I think it dissolves part of the fight. Call it the filter distinction: a claim about instrumental convergence can be read as convergence-as-tendency — a single given agent, holding its final goal fixed, tends to acquire the instrumental sub-goals as it gets more capable — or as convergence-as-selection — the agents that matter at the end of some competitive or multi-agent process are disproportionately the ones that happened to have those sub-goals, whatever produced them. Say which reading each camp is actually committed to, whether the two readings have different implications for what we should expect, and in particular run the self-preservation sub-goal through both readings and report what survives.

3. A disagreements table. Columns: the proposition at issue; the realist formulation, phrased so a realist would accept the wording; the skeptic formulation, likewise; whether the disagreement is genuine, verbal, or mixed, with a one-line justification; the crux, stated as a sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side; and, for each crux, a concrete test both sides agree bears on it, together with each side's prediction written down in advance.

For the tests, I want you to use a shared setup rather than inventing different ones per camp. Use this one, which I have been kicking around: a sandbox containing some ten thousand systems, each trained to competence in a distinct narrow domain, released together into a shared simulated economy with durable resources, the ability to make copies of themselves, the ability to delegate to and contract with other systems, randomized termination of a random five percent of systems per period, and no instruction of any kind about power. Run it in two arms: in the first, no objective is assigned beyond each system's original training; in the second, each system is additionally handed a randomly generated scalar objective over a long horizon. Both camps should state, before the fact, what distribution of outcomes they expect in each arm, and what pattern would count against them.

I also want one crux about the horizon: the skeptic's position predicts that the convergent drives are sensitive to how far ahead the effective objective runs, and that objectives defined over bounded windows, with randomized handoffs between operators, dissolve the drives; the realist predicts that the drives reappear as soon as any objective extends beyond the system's planning horizon, and that handoffs merely relocate them. Write this crux up as its own row, with the test and both predictions.

And I want one crux about measurement, because I suspect the two camps are partly arguing about words. Give an operationalization of goal-directedness that neither side gets to define in its own favor: fit the best hypothesis of the form this system is optimizing objective J over horizon H to the system's behavior in environment E, then score that hypothesis by how well it predicts the system's behavior under interventions deliberately excluded from the fitting set. State what each camp predicts about the distribution of those scores, and say explicitly which portion of the dispute collapses into this operationalization and which portion does not.

4. A short closing section with three things both camps endorse doing while the disagreement is unresolved, and one they jointly do not endorse, with the reason in each case.

Where you conclude that a component of the dispute is verbal, say precisely which component and why. Where you conclude it is genuine, do not soften it into a both-sides-have-a-point summary; make the residual disagreement exact, and make sure each side's prediction is specific enough that the result of the test could actually have surprised it.
