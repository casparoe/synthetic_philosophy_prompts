I'm a PhD student in quantum information who has been drafted onto a working group writing the certification requirements for device-independent randomness beacons, and I keep having to say what our security case assumes. I want the philosophical reconstruction of it, because I think our draft is using one word where three are needed.

The practice, stated concretely. Two shielded devices sit in separate laboratories. In each round a public random bit is fed to each device as a measurement setting and each device returns an output bit. We compute a CHSH-type statistic over many rounds and check that it exceeds the local-hidden-variable ceiling of 2. If it does, and provided that (i) nothing passes from one laboratory to the other between the choice of a setting and the recording of an output, and (ii) the public setting bits were not correlated beforehand with whatever internal state the devices were in, we certify that the output string contains randomness unpredictable to any adversary — including one who manufactured and programmed the devices, who shares arbitrary prior entanglement with them, and who knows everything quantum mechanics says there is to know about their internal state. The string is then passed through a randomness extractor and released as certified bits.

What I want is the implicit theory of locality, causation, or whatever the right noun is, that this security case only makes sense under. Four points I cannot get straight on my own.

First, the asymmetry in what we grant the adversary. She gets determinism, complete prior knowledge of the devices, and arbitrarily strong common-cause correlations; she is denied only correlation between the setting bits and the device state. We hand her the hidden variables and then refuse her the one thing a hidden-variable theorist would need in order to defeat the protocol. What view of locality or causal independence makes that a coherent package rather than an arbitrary decision about where to stop granting? In particular, is the independence of the settings genuinely a separate assumption from locality, or is it already part of what a Bell-style local causality condition asserts — so that what our draft calls the free-settings assumption is in fact locality showing up a second time under another name?

Second, our draft forbids communication between the laboratories, and our group is split on whether that means no-signalling at the level of the observed statistics or something stronger. Since we stipulate that the adversary knows the devices' complete internal state, could there be a dependence of one side's output on the other side's setting that is invisible in the published marginals but visible conditional on her information — a channel she could exploit while the public statistics still look no-signalling? If so, we need the hidden-variable-level condition and not merely the operational one. Which of the two does a security proof actually consume, and does the distinction map onto the philosophers' pair of parameter independence and outcome independence? If it does, say which of the two our standard forbids and which it leaves open, and whether a deterministic pilot-wave theory violates the one we forbid.

Third, two hypothetical anomalies I use to test the draft, which I would like treated as real cases. (a) A beacon runs continuously for a year and its violation strength drifts with the sidereal day, tracking the orientation of the apparatus relative to the fixed stars. (b) A beacon reproducibly sits at 2.9, above the quantum ceiling of about 2.83 but below the no-signalling maximum of 4, with no communication between the laboratories and no detector fault anyone can find. Our engineers call (a) a systematic error and (b) a firmware bug. What in the implicit theory entitles them to that asymmetry? It looks to me as if (b) shows that we were never fully device-independent, since we were assuming quantum mechanics somewhere after all, and as if (a) shows that we secretly assume the relevant common causes to be local and recent rather than cosmological. Say what each anomaly would mean if taken at face value, and what would have to change in the standard to accommodate it.

Fourth, where the practice is underdetermined. It looks compatible with a deterministic but explicitly nonlocal hidden-variable theory, with an indeterministic picture of irreducibly chancy single outcomes, and with an operationalist picture on which there is no fact of the matter about hidden variables and the whole procedure is only a constraint on observable correlations. It also seems to presuppose something about what is knowable in principle, since the security bound limits the adversary to the quantum description and so appears to assume there is nothing further to be known — exactly what a critic who grants her hidden-variable knowledge denies. Tell me which of these the practice strictly requires, which it merely fits well with, and which it rules out; and tell me whether it also presupposes, without stating it, that measurements have single definite outcomes, since a many-outcomes view seems to block the derivation that the certificate rests on.

Finally, if we had to rebuild the standard on a different picture — dropping the independence of the settings, or granting the adversary hidden-variable information, or treating supra-quantum violations as discoveries rather than bugs — what would the replacement look like, and would anything be left to certify?

Close with how confident you are in the reconstruction overall and a flag on the one claim in it you are least sure of.
