I've been arguing with my brother about whether one stretch of life can be better than another, and going on gut alone I've ended up with a set of verdicts I can't make fit together.

One. Two weeks of holiday, identical in every respect except the weather: in S the sun shines every afternoon, in G there is light drizzle every afternoon. S is clearly better; I'd pay fifty pounds to swap. But S against S-minus, which is the same except that one sunny afternoon becomes drizzly: I'd pay nothing to swap, and I can't imagine noticing which of the two I was having. All the same I want to say S is better than S-minus, and so, running that same judgement along a chain of fifteen steps, that S is better than G. Tiny unnoticed differences pile up into a real one.

Two. S against S-swap, where one sunny afternoon is replaced by an afternoon doing something else that is pleasant in a completely different way: I think these two are exactly as good as each other, not merely close, and I think that equality is the kind of thing that can be chained. That sits badly with one, where I refused to let the fact that I couldn't tell the difference stop me calling one of them better.

Three. Two weeks that are mildly pleasant throughout, against one wonderful week followed by a week of ordinary working life: I won't call either better. Not they are equal — I feel there is nothing to be said, or at least nothing I can say — and yet if I had to book one this evening I'd book the longer one, without thinking it the better of the two. My brother says that is simply incoherent.

Four. Two weeks in which I am happy and my travelling companion is miserable, against two weeks in which I am slightly less happy and she is mildly content: the second is better, and I'd take the loss. Now the same sort of trade with a stranger on the other side of the world who would otherwise have spent the fortnight in mild discomfort: I don't feel the second is better, or worse — there is nothing to choose between them. Make it a thousand such strangers and I do think it is better. I cannot say where the changeover comes, and I notice this is the one place where I won't let small differences accumulate, even though in case one I let them do exactly that.

My tentative rule: A is better than B only if the difference between them would be noticeable, from the inside, to someone living through one of them, and is not better than is not transitive, so that imperceptible steps can add up to a real difference. That handles one and two more or less. It seems to break on four, where there is no single person for whom the difference is noticeable, and where I let the steps add up for a thousand strangers but not for one.

Is there one principle behind all four, or do I need one principle for better for someone and another for better overall? Please tell me which clause each of my verdicts is doing the work for, which verdict you would abandon if you had to abandon one, what the principle says about the case I keep changing my mind on — two grey, dull afternoons a fortnight that I afterwards remember, vividly and wrongly, as glorious, against two weeks of lovely afternoons I afterwards remember as dull — and one further case it decides that I have not thought about.
