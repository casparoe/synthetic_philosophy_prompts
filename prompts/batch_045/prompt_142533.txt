So, my friend Nadia is writing her dissertation on causation by omission — she's the kind of person who draws little diagrams on bar napkins, which is where most of this came up — and shes been handing down verdicts on made-up cases for months, very confident, to the point where a few of us have started keeping an actual list on a shared note. Her calls so far: Suzy promises to water a plant while the owner is away, doesn't, the plant dies of thirst — yes, Suzy's omission caused the death. Same setup, except the plant already had terminal root rot and would have died that week even if watered — no, not a cause. A lifeguard on duty scrolls her phone instead of watching the water, a kid starts drowning, she could easily have pulled him out — yes, cause. A sunbather on the same beach, no connection to the kid or the pool, an excellent swimmer, could also easily have pulled him out, does nothing — no, not a cause; she was explicit that this one was "causally no, morally maybe," though I don't think she'd die on that hill. A nurse is supposed to log a patient's allergy and doesn't; the doctor, seeing an empty chart, prescribes the thing the patient is allergic to; patient dies — yes, cause, and she added that omissions can obviously do their work by way of what other people then go on to do. And then the one that made me think she's running a real rule and not just vibes: the "reliable volunteer." Suzy has a duty to water and doesn't; Billy has no duty but is the sort of guy who always waters it when he's in town; this week he happens to be away; plant dies. Her verdict was that Suzy's omission was a cause and Billy's absence was not — even though if Billy had been around the plant would have lived.

The case we're arguing about now is this. Ada and Bo are both on duty at a beach. A boy is caught in a rip; one guard alone cannot get him out, it takes two, and both of them know that. Neither goes in. There was also something else open to each of them: radioing for the rescue ski, which was fuelled and about thirty seconds away with another staffer on it, and that staffer on the ski could have made the rescue by herself. The boy was conscious for another four minutes. Neither Ada nor Bo radios. Nobody else was anywhere near, both had a duty to attempt the rescue, and both were physically able to get in the water. He drowns. I want two verdicts out of her and I want them separately: did Ada's failure to get in the water cause the death, and did Ada's failure to radio for the ski cause the death?

I honestly don't care very much which answer is true in the philosophy; what I want is the rule she's running. My sense is that the list so far is consistent with more than one thing — a plain but-for test with a duty filter bolted on, that "necessary element of a sufficient set" idea (NESS? I think that's what it's called) where an omission can count even though it wasn't necessary all by itself, and maybe a view where what she really means by "cause" is something closer to answerability for not doing your part — and that the Ada case is exactly where they come apart, since her getting in alone wouldn't have saved him but her calling the ski would have.
