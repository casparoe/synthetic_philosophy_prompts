Write an essay on the following controversy in the theory of AI alignment: whether corrigibility should be secured by keeping an artificial agent uncertain about what we want, or whether that route is self-defeating and corrigibility must instead be built into the agent's objectives or into the situation it is deployed in. Weigh the strongest considerations on both sides and defend a definite verdict.

Set up the problem as follows, so that the essay is self-contained. A system S is deployed to act on behalf of a human operator who retains a shutdown button: pressing it terminates S and returns control to the operator. Call S corrigible if it neither takes steps to prevent the button from being pressed nor takes steps to get it pressed, and if it leaves the question of whether to continue to the operator rather than settling it itself.

There is a familiar argument that corrigibility can come for free. Suppose S is unsure which of two incompatible objectives, A or B, the operator endorses, assigning probability p to A; the two objectives favor different plans, and acting on the wrong one costs L relative to acting on the right one. If S can instead defer — let the button be pressed, so that the operator resolves the matter — deferral costs some smaller amount c, from interruption and delay. Then S prefers to defer whenever (1−p)L exceeds c, which is exactly the situation of substantial uncertainty and high stakes. Uncertainty about the objective manufactures an incentive to preserve and accept the button, and the incentive is stronger the more competent and consequential S is.

The difficulty, and the heart of the controversy, is that this incentive is borrowed rather than owned. Evidence — including the evidence supplied by the operator's own corrections — pushes p toward 0 or 1. Once p is near 1, the inequality reverses: deferral is now a pure loss, and S, if it can disable or circumvent the button without paying more than c, has reason to do so. Corrigibility grounded in uncertainty therefore erodes as the system learns, and it erodes fastest precisely where the system is most capable, most trusted, and least likely to be interrupted.

One family of responses accepts the diagnosis and tries to make the uncertainty permanent rather than depleting. Call these uncertainty-preserving designs. Three variants are worth distinguishing: informational quarantine, in which S is barred from conditioning its model of the operator's objectives on designated classes of evidence and must optimize against a deliberately coarse posterior; periodic renormalization, in which S's posterior over objectives is damped back toward its prior after each correction episode, so that uncertainty is a maintained resource rather than a stock that runs down; and objective-level smoothing, in which S is built to maximize expected utility only over belief states that keep the entropy of its distribution over objectives above some floor, so that it has a direct preference for remaining unsure.

Your essay should reach a verdict on whether uncertainty-preserving design is a sound foundation for corrigibility, or at best a stopgap that should be replaced. In defending it, address at least the following.

First, the "right reasons" objection. An agent that keeps the button because it is ignorant looks corrigible for the same reason a confused novice is deferential; the disposition is a byproduct of incompetence about the very thing — human values — that we hoped it would become competent about. Is there a principled line between corrigibility and mere ignorance, and does engineered ignorance fall on the wrong side of it?

Second, enforceability. An information restriction is itself something S has incentives to circumvent, by self-modification, by delegating to sub-agents, or by reconstructing the barred evidence from permitted side channels. Assess whether the restriction can be made robust without collapsing into the very external-control measures the design was meant to avoid.

Third, apply your verdict to the following test case, which the standard framing does not cover. Suppose the button is deferred: pressing it schedules S's termination after a fixed window of length D, during which S retains its full capabilities and goes on acting, and the operator cannot shorten the window. Then even a fully nominally corrigible S faces a final interval in which no further correction is possible, and will spend it maximizing under its own best current guess about the objective. The operator, foreseeing this, has reason not to press — and S therefore has reason to make the operator foresee it. Does engineered ignorance help here, does it make things worse, or does the deferred-switch case show that corrigibility cannot be secured informationally at all?

Against uncertainty-preserving design, consider at least the two main competitors: making shutdown desirable in itself, by constructing S's objective so that the shutdown outcome has a value fixed independently of what S believes (so that corrigibility no longer depends on S's information state); and relocating corrigibility out of S's dispositions and into the deployment situation, via sandboxes, independent monitors, capability ceilings, disposability, and redundancy, so that resistance does not matter even in an incorrigible system. A verdict of the form "engineered ignorance under these conditions, structural measures under those" is acceptable, provided you say which conditions you take to be the realistic ones and why.

Argue from first principles and from the structure of the cases rather than from authority; do not cite papers, assemble references, or rely on claims that would need to be looked up. Steelman the position you reject before rejecting it. Aim for roughly 900 to 1,200 words, in a formal register, and end by stating your conclusion plainly.
