I sit on the patient-and-family advisory committee of a large regional hospital, and for the last three years I have also been the lay member of the committee that reviews our rules for allocating scarce resources — transplant organs, intensive care beds, and a small fund for drugs that are not on the standard formulary. I have no academic background in philosophy; I have been reading about these questions on my own, and I have reached the point where I can describe what we actually do but cannot say what we are assuming when we do it. I would like you to reconstruct the implicit view for me.

The core of our rules is severity weighting. When two claims on the same resource are compared, a given health gain to the person who is worse off beforehand counts for more than the identical gain to someone who is only mildly ill. A year of life restored to someone at death's door counts for more than a year restored to someone with a chronic but manageable condition; a bed goes to the sicker patient before the less sick one. Some national systems that do similar cost-effectiveness calculations apply an explicit numerical multiplier to gains accruing to the severely ill; ours does it more informally, as a strong presumption that the sicker patient comes first, defeasible only when the difference in expected benefit is very large. Alongside this, four features of the practice puzzle me.

First, the tie-break. When two patients are at the same point on whatever severity scale we are using, the tie goes to the one whose severe condition is longest-standing: someone who has been in the worst band for three years outranks someone who entered it last month. The stated rationale was that they have waited longer. One of the physicians on the committee objected that this rewards our own delays and has nothing to do with how badly off either person is right now.

Second, the currency. A proposal to break ties by looking at how badly off the candidates are overall — poverty, isolation, the general shape of their lives — was voted down on the grounds that, in the chair's words, we treat illness and not social circumstances. So the extra weight attaches to being badly off medically, and not to being badly off in general.

Third, the reversal. In our surge protocol — the one that would be triggered by a mass casualty event or by a pandemic wave exceeding capacity — the sickest patients are moved into a category that receives essentially nothing, on the ground that they are unlikely to survive even with treatment, while the healthiest are deferred as likely to recover without it. Resources go to the middle band. So sickest-first is abandoned precisely in the situation where it is most consequential, and it is abandoned exactly when being sickest stops predicting that the resource will do any good.

Fourth, the asymmetry about losses. We have never once counted "this would make a better-off patient somewhat worse off" as a point in favour of a policy, even in the hypothetical case where nobody else gains anything from it at all — no resources are freed, no one is treated sooner, the only effect is a smaller gap. At the same time we routinely withhold cost-ineffective treatments from the mildly ill, and that is counted as a point in favour, but only because it frees money or staff for others. In my reading I have come across the terms prioritarianism, sufficientarianism, egalitarianism, and something called the leveling-down objection, which I gather is meant to embarrass the view that equality matters in itself, by pointing out that such a view would have to count making the better off worse off for nobody's benefit as in one respect an improvement. Nobody on our committee has ever treated that as an improvement.

So here is what I want. Reconstruct the view that our rules only make sense under: what must we be assuming about whose good matters and how much, for severity weighting, the chronicity tie-break, the restriction to medical badness, the surge reversal, and the refusal to count leveling down all to be sensible at once? Then be discriminating about it. Which of those five features does the view you attribute to us strictly require, which does it merely sit well with, and which does it actually sit badly with? Does the chronicity tie-break really follow from a concern with how badly off someone is, given that badness of condition is presumably measured at a time — or does it smuggle in a different idea altogether, about duration, desert, or waiting? Does the restriction to medical badness sit comfortably with weighting benefits by how badly off the recipient is, or does that weighting, taken seriously, push toward counting everything that makes someone badly off? Does the surge reversal show that the whole practice was never about the worst off as such, but was always a heuristic for how much good the resource can do, which we discard the moment it stops tracking that? And does our refusal to count leveling down prove anything about which view we hold, or is it equally available to several views, and therefore no evidence at all?

Then tell me where we are underdetermined. Which rival theories would license exactly the rules we have, and is there any decision we actually face that would separate them? And for each rival, tell me concretely which of our rules would have to change if we adopted it instead — I want the difference to show up in the committee's actual decisions, not only in how we describe them. Finally: if we dropped severity weighting altogether and ranked purely by expected health gain, what exactly would we be giving up, and is there an argument, using only premises our own rules already contain, that doing so would be a mistake?
