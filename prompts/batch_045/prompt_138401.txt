I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background — I read one paper about this and got lost somewhere on the third page — and I have a practical problem that keeps turning into a philosophy problem.

I'm building a long-lived agent. Think an ops bot with a mandate over months, not milliseconds. It doesn't just choose actions; on a schedule it revises its own weights, and it can leave things behind for its future selves: a shared append-only log, scheduled nudges, hard blocks on certain actions, and the option to pay real cost to lock off some of its own future options. So the thing I actually have to specify isn't a single decision. It's a decision plus a policy about how later versions of the agent are allowed to revise it. That's the object I need a theory of. Call it rationality over time, or diachronic rationality, or just "what consistency rules go in the planner."

What I want from you is desiderata, not a survey and not a reading list. A spec: what would a theory of this have to deliver for it to be good enough to be the constraint set I hard-code? For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it — the concrete situation that makes it a requirement rather than a taste.

Cases I already think it has to handle, and ones I want you to add to or argue with:

1. The everyone-said-yes pump. No adversary, no bookie, no trick. Every step is a genuine local improvement by the lights of the self making it, every later self consents, and the endpoint is something the agent at t0 would have refused flatly and would pay to undo. Nobody was exploited by anyone except its own trajectory. Does a theory owe an answer here, or is it allowed to just call this fine?
2. The ordinary money pump: a cycle of trades that returns it to where it started, minus fees.
3. The reverse case, which is the one I have the strongest feeling about and trust myself least on: my agent sometimes does better by staying revisable, and even by being the kind of thing that can be talked into things. It gets offered deals and assignments that a perfectly iron-willed agent never gets offered, because counterparties like dealing with someone flexible, and the option value of not having locked itself in is real money. So: is "don't be exploitable" a genuine requirement, or a prejudice of one camp? Same question for "never want to bind yourself": being able to precommit is valuable, but a world where every agent precommits immediately is a world with a lot of expensive locks in it.
4. Preference change versus information change. My future self will know more than I do, and from the logs I often can't tell whether it moved because it learned something or because its weights drifted. Must a theory separate these, and can it, given it only ever sees behavior?
5. Auditability. I need a monitor that can flag a violation from the log alone, with bounded compute, without knowing the agent's internals. Is that a real desideratum on the theory, or am I smuggling an engineering constraint into a philosophical list? Same for: the theory can't demand unbounded search over future plans, because I have to implement it.
