You are a professional moral philosopher with a research specialism in contractualist ethics, and you are also an experienced long-form interviewer who knows how to keep a guest from restating their own view. Work carefully and in stages; do not produce the final list until you have thought through where the real pressure lies.

I host a podcast called *Second Person Singular*, and next week I am recording an episode titled "Can Contractualism Count?" My guest is Dr. Nadia Reinholt (a fictional philosopher), author of a recent monograph arguing that Scanlon-style contractualism can accommodate the moral significance of numbers after all. I need you to draft the interview.

Here is her position, which I will call *prospect contractualism*. Wrongness is still a matter of whether an act would be disallowed by principles that no one could reasonably reject, and she keeps the restriction that contractualists standardly impose: a given person's objection to a principle may be grounded only in how that principle bears on her own life, never in the fact that other people are badly off or that the total burden is large. Her innovation is to assess each person's objection *ex ante*, over the objective prospect the principle gives her, rather than *ex post*, over the outcome she actually ends up with. In the standard emergency case — you can either save one stranger or save five, and everyone will die if you do nothing — each of the six contractors compares a five-in-six prospect of death (if the one is saved and the five are left) with a one-in-six prospect of death (if the five are saved). Weighing one person's prospect against one person's prospect, the objection to saving the one is stronger, so contractualism saves the five without anyone's burdens ever being added to anyone else's. Because the five are not "summed," she claims to have satisfied the separateness-of-persons worry that motivates the restriction in the first place. She also adds a supplementary rule for cases where the identities are already settled and the prospects are therefore certain: when the strongest objection to one principle ties exactly with the strongest objection to its rival, the principle that would leave the greater number of people with objections of that same strength is disallowed.

Her stock deflection, which she uses very effectively, is this: "I never add anybody to anybody. I only ever weigh one person's prospect against one person's prospect."

First, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming the strongest pressure points on this package — the objections a sympathetic but rigorous reader would raise, the cases where the machinery is most likely to grind or give an answer she will not like, and the places where her two-stage picture does work that she has not advertised. Think about, among other things: whether a chance of death is even the right sort of thing to ground a person's objection, given that a prospect is not something anyone suffers; what shape the risk-weighting function must have and who gets to fix its curvature; what happens to the verdict when the identities become known mid-case, so that the same act seems to flip from wrong to permissible merely because people have been told who is who; whether the tie-breaking rule can be chained or applied at near-ties, and whether it produces discontinuities; what the view implies once you move from emergencies to the distribution of social risks over whole lives; and whether the view still earns the relational, second-personal character that is supposed to distinguish justifying yourself to each person from doing cost-benefit arithmetic behind a veil.

Then draft eight to ten interview questions. For each one, give me: the question exactly as I should say it aloud (one or two spoken sentences, no jargon dumps, not answerable with yes or no); a one-line note on the answer she is most likely to give; and a follow-up keyed specifically to that answer, phrased so that it is waiting for her when she arrives at it.

Requirements on the set as a whole: at least one question each on the ex-ante/ex-post contrast and its dependence on who knows what, on the currency and interpersonal comparability of objection-strength, on the tie-breaking rule for settled identities, and on whether the view has quietly become a version of expected-value maximization. Do not include anything biographical, anything about her influences or her writing process, or anything she can discharge by restating prospect contractualism; every question should put her in a position where the honest answer costs her something. Keep the tone of a generous but exacting interviewer rather than a debater.

Finally, give me a closing sequence of three questions, to be asked in order, that makes that deflection of hers progressively harder to reach for — start where it still does real work for her, then move to a case where the number of people affected enters the *content* of a single individual's prospect rather than entering after it, so that "one person's prospect" is already a function of everyone else's — and end with the question of what empirical or argumentative development would actually make her give the view up.
