I'm revising a dissertation chapter on rational choice under transformative experience, and I want to defend a claim most people working on this resist: that the epistemically transformative half of Paul's argument is doing no independent work. Please make that case as strongly as you can.

The shape of the argument I want: what makes a transformative choice hard is not that the agent cannot know in advance what the experience is like, but that its outcome includes a change in the agent's own preferences, together with the practical irreversibility of that change — there is no un-becoming a parent and then re-deciding with one's old preferences restored. Once those are separated, the choice is just decision-making under uncertainty about one's future utility function plus a large option value attached to remaining untransformed, and it is representable in expected-utility terms as soon as the earlier self is allowed a second-order preference over which self to become. On that reading, valuing revelation is either one such second-order preference among others, or an extra with nothing left to explain.

Three things the argument has to survive. First, the regress worry: a preference over future selves is itself formed under the very ignorance said to be the problem, so the move seems only to relocate it. Second, the repeat case — the second child, where the agent already knows what it is like and yet the choice still presents as momentous: show that it supports rather than undercuts the thesis. Third, say what the account implies about a hypothetical partial proxy: a reversible, degraded simulation that lets an agent sample the phenomenal character without undergoing the transformation. Does its availability change the rationality of the choice, and if not, why not?
