I got into this sideways and have no philosophy background — I'm a backend engineer, I do a lot of model evaluation at work (calibration curves, Brier scores, log loss, the usual), and a couple years ago I got really into forecasting tournaments as a hobby. From there I somehow ended up swallowing the whole accuracy-first package: that accuracy is the only thing that matters epistemically, that the standard norms — be probabilistically coherent, update by conditioning, match your credences to the chances — all drop out of that one idea via accuracy-dominance arguments that use strictly proper scoring rules, and that practical stakes, how much an inquiry costs, and what you care about are irrelevant to what you ought to believe (they only matter for what you do). I find it genuinely elegant, which is exactly why I'm suspicious of it.

So: what would count against it? I want the defeaters ranked from flatly fatal to merely awkward, and I want them separated by which bit of the package they'd actually hit — the "accuracy is the only epistemic good" claim, the dominance argument for probabilism, the accuracy argument for conditioning, and the background claim that strictly proper scoring rules are the right way to measure accuracy at all. For each one, please say whether a committed defender would concede it as counting against the view or dismiss it as changing the subject, because I genuinely can't tell the difference between a real internal problem and an objection the framework was built from the start to be immune to. And does any of it predict something I could go check with real tournament data, or is the whole thing a priori and shielded from data by design?

Two things I keep chewing on. First, from work: we routinely train with losses that aren't strictly proper — label smoothing, focal loss, distilling off soft targets, all sorts of ugly hacks — and they beat plain log loss on the metrics we actually care about. If a deliberately improper loss can dominate the proper one at the thing we were optimizing, why should I think the strictly proper ones carve accuracy at the joints rather than being one convenient choice among many? A friend says this is a category error because generalization isn't accuracy, but I dont find that satisfying. Second, a stipulation I want you to take at face value rather than fact-check: imagine a big, well-run forecasting tournament ran for a decade and millions of questions, and the top performers turned out to violate the probability calculus in a stable, easy-to-describe way — say, reporting 0.7 on p and 0.7 on not-p in the same tournament — while still beating every coherent forecaster on average Brier score. I half suspect something in the framework says that result is impossible; if so, tell me, and tell me whether the fact that the theory rules it out by construction is a point in its favor or a sign that it's been insulated into not risking anything. If you think the package has been qualified into unfalsifiability, say so plainly and say whether that should bother me — my partner's a lawyer and says a theory that can't lose isn't really trying, and I half agree. For what it's worth, I'd personally be most moved by something about the choice of scoring rule or about the space of worlds the dominance gets evaluated over, since that's where loss functions look most arbitrary to me, and least moved by intuition-mongering about cooked-up cases.
