I'm writing a novel set in a made-up mid-sized city — call it Alderton — that in the story extends the municipal franchise to every adult resident regardless of citizenship, so a green-card holder, a longtime resident whose visa lapsed decades ago, and a young woman who files taxes with an ITIN because she doesn't have a Social Security number all get a ballot for city council and the school board. There's a town-hall scene I keep getting stuck on: a retired accountant stands up, says "no taxation without representation" (he'd probably have it on his license plate too, the way DC plates do), sits down, and the room behaves as if the argument is now closed. My trouble is that I can't tell what he's actually claiming, and I can't write the alderman's reply until I do. So: can you lay out, as a menu, the different precise things that sentence could be asserting — the near-trivial readings, the genuinely strong ones, and any that are straightforwardly false? I want the moving parts made explicit: whose taxes count (income, sales, property, payroll withheld, fees bundled into a rent check), what "representation" is supposed to consist in (a ballot, a consulted interest group, a legislature you could in principle join, an official who owes you an answer), what the "no" is doing (a claim about legitimacy, about justice, about consent, about whether you're entitled to *complain*, or about what actually caused the American colonists to rebel), and whether the principle is backward-looking about money already taken or forward-looking about the laws you'll be subject to next year.

Then the part I actually care about: for each reading, does it get him his conclusion, that Alderton's non-citizen residents should vote? And what else does it drag in that he almost certainly doesn't want? If the trigger is sales tax, does a sixteen-year-old with a summer job get a ballot too? If it's property tax, does the out-of-state landlord who owns six rental units get six votes, or one, or none? If it's "whoever the city collects money from," does the franchise quietly shrink in a recession? Is there a reading on which paying no net tax costs a citizen her vote, and is there one on which a corporation has a better claim than a person? I've also given my weary city solicitor a variant line to throw back at him, and I'd like you to treat it as another entry in the same menu and tell me what it licenses that the tax version doesn't: "you can be commanded without being taxed, and that's the part that ought to bother you." Is that just the same principle with the money stripped out, or is it doing genuinely different work — and does it prove too much, since it seems to cover tourists, prisoners, and anyone who merely has to obey?

Last thing, purely so I can write the scene: if you had to pick the one reading under which the accountant's line is hardest to answer — the version I should hand to my least sympathetic character, the one who's right in a way the reader won't enjoy — which is it, and what's the shortest honest reply the alderman could give that isn't just a dodge? I'd rather have the strong version of his objection in the room than a straw man, even if it means my protagonist loses the exchange.
