Hey — I run a fortnightly methods workshop for a mixed group, about 16 people: half psych PhD students who've had a couple of stats courses, half philosophy grad students who mostly haven't. 90 minutes, one room, I can get cards, dice and a whiteboard, but I'd rather not need a laptop per person.

I want a game or structured activity on the frequentist/Bayesian divide and the replication crisis — but not the standard one. I've ran the usual thing where everyone runs a study, the journal only publishes p < .05, and lo the literature is garbage. It lands, but it teaches this group the wrong lesson: they come out thinking the fix is preregistration plus bigger n, full stop.

What I actually want them to feel is narrower. A failed replication is ambiguous between 'the original was a false positive' and 'the real effect differs across populations or procedures in some way nobody measured'. Piling on more single-site replications doesn't obviously dissolve that ambiguity, because each new lab brings its own unmeasured stuff. And that ambiguity is exactly the joint where the two toolkits come apart: the frequentist machinery wants one fixed effect and hands you error rates over a sampling model, the Bayesian wants a prior over how much effects vary and updates through a likelihood that averages over it — and from the same numbers they can point you to opposite practical decisions. I want them to collide with that by making choices under uncertainty, not by me announcing it. Ideally nobody hears the words 'prior' or 'heterogeneity' until the debrief.

Here's the device I've been toying with: each lab table secretly rolls a hidden 'moderator die' that sets whether the effect is big, small or zero for their subpopulation, and nobody knows the die exists until the end. Is that the right mechanism, or does it rig the whole thing toward the Bayesian side by building in a distribution over effect sizes before anyone's argued for one? Propose something better if so.

Give me: the rules and materials, a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money (chips or a dumb leaderboard is fine), a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the room onto specific bits of the formal picture. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong — I'm especially worried the table that draws a null just concludes 'so the effect is fake' and digs in, and that one loud person who reads all the blogs takes over — plus how to handle each. And be frank about which part of the disagreement the activity simply cannot reproduce and I'll have to state outright.

Last thing: be straight with me about your own confidence. Flag what you actually have reasons for versus what only sounds plausible, and if the ask as I've put it rests on something muddled, or the die idea is just bad, say that plainly instead of politely building it anyway.
