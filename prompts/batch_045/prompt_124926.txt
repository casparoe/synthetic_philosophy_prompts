1. outcome reward is the only signal that isn't arbitrary. a process reward is a proxy, proxies get goodharted, so wherever the outcome is cheap to verify we should grade the outcome and nothing else.
2. but the supervision that actually matters to us — help with our own research, long-horizon agentic stuff, anything where you don't know if it worked for weeks — is exactly the stuff whose outcomes we can't verify, so that's where we need process-based supervision.
3. we should never put optimization pressure on the chain of thought itself. the trace being a readable window onto what the model is doing is worth more than whatever training signal we'd squeeze out of it, and the moment you start grading it you've corrupted the window.
4. i also don't think faithfulness is even the right standard for a trace. the cot is computation the model is doing, not a report it's handing us; asking whether it faithfully reports its own reasoning is a category error. what we want is for the trace to be useful to us, which is a different property.
5. if we stress-test a model, find no case where it lands on a right answer for a bad reason, and its explanations look fine to our domain experts, we should update toward "it's fine" rather than toward "our oversight is too weak to see the problem". absence of evidence is evidence of absence here.
6. for the hard tasks i want step-level labels without paying people to hand-label: take a partial trace, roll out a bunch of continuations from a stronger model we have lying around, and score the prefix by how often those rollouts reach a verified outcome. process supervision for free.
7. case verdict: on our math set about a third of the prefixes that the scorer in 6 rates as high-value contain a step that's actually wrong, just recoverable, and i want to leave those in the training data — the rollouts show they get to right answers often enough.
