Hey — I run this tiny, very informal weekend thing on decision-making for a handful of friends (a couple of software people, a nurse, a guy who restores pianos, my sister), and last month we got properly stuck. A friend is deciding whether to quit her job and open a small ceramics studio with a shared kiln, and half the group kept saying something like look at what fraction of small craft-retail businesses are still trading in year three, while the other half kept saying that number is about other people, she has a waiting list already and a landlord who'll do the fit-out. We realised we were all using inside view and outside view as insults rather than as positions, so nobody was ever going to change their mind, and I want something that pins people down instead.

So: build me a which forecasting epistemology do you actually hold? quiz — eight questions, not twelve, and I'd rather have eight sharp ones than a dozen fuzzy ones. Each question should be a concrete case with three answer options, phrased so that someone who has never heard the labels can answer honestly: the ceramics studio; a wedding budget; how long a running injury will take to heal; whether a colleague's grant application gets funded; a megaproject-ish thing (a friend's organisation rebuilding an IT system that has already failed once); and, importantly, some cases that don't show up in the usual discussions — one where you yourself can act on the forecast and change the outcome, one where telling the person the forecast changes whether the event happens at all (you tell your friend the base rate for restaurants, so she doesn't open it, so the forecast never gets its chance), one where the honest answer is a distribution and the interesting part is the tail rather than the middle, and one where the reference class could obviously be gerrymandered by whoever wants a particular answer. Please keep the questions jargon-light — no item should ask "do you trust base rates?" or name a position — and put the sorting in the scoring key, mapping answer patterns onto leaves. The leaves I want included: inside-view-only or uniqueness (my case isn't in any class); outside view as anchor then a modest adjustment; outside view as prior with the inside view supplying a likelihood; outside-view-only, nobody is special; inside view reconstrued as covariate-hunting (use the plan only to find features that predict outcomes, then let a conditioned base rate do the estimating); the borrowed-authority suspicion, that the outside view is itself assembled from inside views and so has no independent authority; and a suspend-the-forecast leaf where you plan for robustness and cheap reversibility instead of predicting at all. Build it from the logical space rather than from my list: first the three or four branching questions that generate the space (is a usable reference class available at all, is the outcome causally insulated from the forecast, are we after central tendency or tails, is the inside view functioning as evidence or as a story, do we care about accuracy or about calibration), then locate the positions in it, mark the combinations nobody occupies and say briefly whether that's because they're incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored, and flag any pair of leaves your items can't actually pry apart rather than pretending they separate cleanly.

And since the topic is epistemic modesty, don't hand me a key that performs certainty: end with a short where-this-instrument-lies note saying which two questions you expect to mis-sort people and why, which pair of leaves your quiz genuinely can't distinguish, and what someone would have to show you — a kind of case, a result, an argument — to get you to redraw the branching structure rather than just reword an item. It should take about twenty minutes to fill in, and I'll be running it on people who will definately argue with the scoring, so tell me where they'd have a fair complaint.
