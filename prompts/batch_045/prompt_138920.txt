Right, bit of a weird ask but here goes. I'm in Year 13, my EPQ is on personal identity, and on Thursday I've got to do a fifteen-minute presentation to the sixth-form philosophy society and then take questions for about ten minutes. Slides are basically done, but I've no idea what's going to get thrown at me and I'd really rather not stand there going "er, good question" for ten minutes while my mark drains away.

The talk's on Dennett's "Where Am I?" — the one where his brain gets taken out and put in a vat in Houston wired up to a computer, and his body's wandering around Berkeley on a radio link. Obvs the question he's meant to be asking is where Dennett actually is.

My thesis (and this is the bit I made up myself — my supervisor called it "ambitious", which I think means he hates it): a person's location isn't a property of an object at all, brain or body, it's a property of a working loop. You are wherever you can still take in what's happening and act on it. So in the story Dennett isn't in Houston and he isn't in Berkeley, he's spread across both — and if the link properly dies for a while (I've added a bit where it does), then for those few minutes he isn't anywhere. Not "we can't work out where he is" — there just isn't an answer. Being somewhere is something a working loop does, not a place an object sits in.

Sketch of how I get there, so you know what to aim at: (1) in ordinary cases there's no puzzle, you're where you can act on stuff and it can act on you, which is why nobody ever asks where they are; (2) the body is how you act on the world and the brain is what holds the whole thing together, so neither of them is you on it's own; (3) so when the loop gets stretched over two cities the person gets stretched too, and when the loop snaps the question stops having an answer rather than having one we can't find. Then a quick test at the end: if you want to know where someone is, ask where you'd have to go to have a conversation with them, or where you'd have to go to stop them doing something — and in the story both of those give you two places, which is kind of the whole point.
