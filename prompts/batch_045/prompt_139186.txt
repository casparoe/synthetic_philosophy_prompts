I'm a senior doing an independent reading project on skepticism, and I got into an argument with a friend who defends a Nozick-style tracking account of knowledge: roughly, I know that p only if my belief tracks the truth, in the sense that if p were false I wouldn't believe it (sensitivity) and if p were true I would (adherence), with both conditions read relative to the method I actually used. He's been trying to convert me, so I built a case and an argument to push back, and now I can't tell whether I've refuted him or walked myself into something. The case is my own invention, so tell me if it's incoherent: suppose I'm sitting in a room with a hundred objects I seem to perceive, and consider the substitution family S(k) — exactly k of the hundred objects I seem to perceive are really there, and the remaining 100 minus k are undetectable projections fed to me by a device that leaves no trace I could in principle find, so my total evidence is exactly the same for every value of k. S(100) is the good case; S(0) is the case where every one of the hundred is fake. Here's the argument:

(1) Tracking: I know p only if, in the nearest worlds where p is false, I wouldn't believe p by the method I actually used.
(2) My friend grants ordinary perceptual knowledge: for each of the hundred objects, I know that that object is really there — for each such belief, the nearest worlds where it's false are ones where the object has simply been removed and I don't believe it's there.
(3) Each of those hundred claims entails "at least one of the hundred is real," and any k of them taken together entail "at least k of the hundred are real."
(4) Closure under known entailment: if I know p, know that p entails q, and come to believe q by competently deducing it from p, then I know q.
(5) So by (2), (3) and (4) I know, for every k, that at least k of the hundred are real; in particular I know that all hundred are real, i.e. that I'm not in S(0) or any of the other substitution scenarios.
(6) But none of my evidence distinguishes S(100) from S(0): the hundred-fold conclusion arrives from stacking up ordinary perceptions plus logic, it gets stronger the more objects happen to be in the room, and it hands me the denial of a global skeptical hypothesis without a single piece of evidence against it. That looks like it proves far too much.
(7) My friend replies by rejecting (5) using (1): my belief that all hundred are real is insensitive, because in the nearest worlds where it's false — the S(k) worlds — I'd believe exactly the same thing. So, he says, closure fails here.
(8) But that reply seems to make things worse, not better. "At least one of the hundred is real" is a one-step consequence of something he concedes I know, so denying closure there means I can know the mug is there while failing to know a strictly weaker claim I can derive from it in a single step. Worse, on his own test the *strong* claim looks better placed than the weak one: the nearest falsifying worlds for "all hundred are real" seem to be mundane ones where someone has carried off the lamp and I'd notice, whereas the nearest falsifying worlds for "at least one is real" can only be S(0)-type worlds, where I'd believe it anyway. His view then has knowledge shrinking as the claim gets weaker, which can't be right.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the problem is in the case I built rather than in closure or sensitivity, say so and explain what the substitution family is illicitly doing. The two claims I'd most want to keep are that I genuinely know the individual object claims, and that I don't get to defeat the skeptical scenarios by bookkeeping from those claims; beyond that I'll give up whatever has to go. Please take a definite position — name the step, explain why it looked harmless, and say what the alternatives would each cost me — rather than laying out the menu and leaving me to choose, and please write in American English.
