Last night I had a long argument with a friend — she is a skeptic-leaning internalist — about the bootstrapping problem: the family of cases in which a subject runs a belief-producing source (a fuel gauge, a memory, a perceptual faculty) through a long sequence of apparent successes and then infers, from those very apparent successes, that the source is reliable, and so seems to acquire knowledge of the source's reliability much too cheaply. I ended up defending a line I now have to defend in public, and I would like to know what I am walking into.

The thesis: the bootstrapping arguments get their bite from sliding between two readings of "reliable" — a claim about the actual run of cases (the source has apparently hit the truth on the occasions sampled) and a modal claim (it would go on hitting the truth in nearby unexamined cases). My argument is that bootstrapping legitimately yields only the first, that the first is not objectionably easy, because each apparent success is a separate measurement and the inference to the track record is an ordinary enumerative induction rather than a circle, and that the step from the first to the second fails for the familiar inductive reason rather than because of any distinctive circularity. Conclusion to be defended: no special anti-bootstrapping constraint needs to be added to externalist accounts of justification, or to views on which experience confers immediate prima facie justification, in order to answer the easy-knowledge objection.

The audience is a departmental colloquium of about twenty-five: mostly process reliabilists and two-level virtue epistemologists of the sort who think that coherence between first-order deliverances and the second-order judgment of reliability is exactly what makes bootstrapping benign rather than vicious; two or three people who hold that perceptual experience confers immediate prima facie justification without any independent reason to think perception is reliable; and one skeptic who thinks the whole dialectic presupposes the trustworthiness of the sources under test.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased the way that subgroup would phrase it and in its own vocabulary, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede at the outset, what I must hold if the view is to survive, and which single question I should hope nobody asks.
