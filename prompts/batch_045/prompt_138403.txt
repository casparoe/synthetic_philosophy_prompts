I am closing an upper-level seminar on the metaphysics and ethics of social groups with a unit on national character — whether the notion is coherent at all, and whether any generalization about a people can ever be acceptable — and I would like to finish with Kazuo Ishiguro's *The Remains of the Day* rather than with one more paper. I want a genuine reading, not a plot summary, and I want to be steered off the two lazy options my students reach for first: that the novel is a diagnosis of English emotional repression (a stereotype about a stereotype), and that Englishness is a red herring for what is "really" a story about class or about professional role.

The distinction I have been developing in the seminar is threefold. A sentence like "the English are reserved" might be a distributional claim about how a trait is spread across a population; it might be a claim about a shared self-image or ideal; or it might be what I am calling an uptake claim — the attribution is false as description when made, but becomes true of the people because they recognize it as theirs and gradually shape themselves to it, so that it is real as a social fact while explaining nothing and predicting nothing. I want to know which of the three, if any, the novel is in the business of making, and whether it thinks the difference matters morally.

The scenes I would press on are: Stevens's early reflection that English scenery is the finest in the world precisely because it lacks the obvious drama and spectacle of African or American landscape, a lack he treats as a kind of restraint; his meditations on what makes a "great butler" and on whether the type is essentially English; his practicing of bantering at the request of his American employer, who wants him to be more English than he is; the evening at Moscombe where he lets his hosts take him for a gentleman and will not confirm that he served Lord Darlington, and where a local man offers a rival account of dignity as something belonging to people who hold opinions and vote on them, which is why the English are free and the Germans and Italians were not; his carrying out of the dismissal of the two Jewish housemaids; and the conversation on the pier at the end.

The question I most want answered is one I will call the problem of parasitic legibility: the novel's irony appears to depend on the reader already granting the attribution it is supposedly undermining — the pathos and the comedy both require us to read Stevens as incurably English — and if that is right, I cannot present the book as a straightforward cautionary example and should teach it as a case of complicity instead. I would also like to know what, if anything, the novel contributes to the philosophical argument that argument alone does not.
