I'm applying for a small independent-scholar grant — four months, one person, deliverable is a long working paper plus a short version for general readers — and I want the proposal to be about Mill on India. I've been reading this stuff on my own for a couple of years, no degree, so dont assume I know the secondary literature; I'll track down references myself later.

The thing I keep getting stuck on: in On Liberty Mill says, more or less, that despotism over people he'd call barbarian is acceptable provided the end is their improvement, and he spent his whole working life at the East India Company defending British rule in India as something like a temporary, improving despotism. But the case for liberty in that same book is fallibilist — nobody is well placed to judge what's good for someone else, which is why people should be free to run their own experiments in living. Those two pulls look like they run against each other, and I don't think "he was just a man of his time" settles whether the collision is real.

My half-formed idea: the puzzle may turn on what the ruler is supposed to know better. Knowing better what is genuinely good for the subjects (substantive) is hard to square with fallibilism; knowing better what they will later come to want and ratify (predictive) looks compatible with it. So the live question might be whether Mill can restrict himself to the predictive claim and still reach his conclusion, or whether the predictive claim is too weak to justify anything, since any ruler can insist the people will thank him later. I also half-remember him saying somewhere that a foreign ruler has an advantage exactly because he isn't tied to local factions, but I'm not sure — treat that as my guess, not as something to build on.

Write the one-page proposal: state the question so it has a determinate answer in four months; say why both "Mill was simply inconsistent" and "Victorian imperialist, case closed" leave it open; give the method month by month and say why constructing the strongest version of the argument and testing it beats contextualist or biographical work here; list the two or three possible conclusions and what would decide between them; say what the short version could offer people today who are judging interventions that claim to be temporary and self-liquidating; and be honest about what would make the project fail or be too big.
