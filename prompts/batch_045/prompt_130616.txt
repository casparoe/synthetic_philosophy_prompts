I've definately drifted into a position I'm not sure I can defend, and I want someone to try to break it. Call it inversion universalism (my name, probably a bad one): whenever you run into a competence that looks like it must be driven by understanding — a wasp that seems to plan, a three-year-old producing grammatical sentences nobody taught it, a big language model writing working code — the default explanation is a mechanism with no understanding in it at all, shaped by something selection-like, and the understanding story is a later, optional extra we retrofit because we can't see the mechanism. Darwin does this move for the appearance of design, Turing does the same move for the appearance of reasoning, and I've started to think it's the same move everywhere. I also think the intuition that comprehension has to come first is itself an artifact: we mostly introspect the cases where we happened to have comprehension, so the felt priority of understanding is a sampling effect, not evidence.

The bit I actually lean on day to day is a diagnostic I made up, because the bare position is way too easy to protect. Here it is: if a competence is genuinely comprehension-free, its failures should look arbitrary to us rather than sitting at the points where our own understanding runs out. A chess engine should be fine in positions that look like chaos to us and should fall over in positions a beginer would call simple. A system that writes code should nail problems that feel hard and blow ones that feel trivial. And there should be a decoupling: performance can keep climbing while the system's capacity to say why it did what it did stays flat, gets worse, or is bolted on afterward by a completely separate process. When I see those two together — weird failure geometry plus performance coming apart from articulation — I count it as confirmation.

So what would count against this? I want it ranked, fatal at the top and merely annoying at the bottom, and I want you to keep apart what a fan of the view would actually concede from what they'd wave away. Specifically:

- Is there anything decisive? My own candidate is a competence that shows up in one shot, in one individual, with no selection-like history, no practice, and no search over alternatives — and that transfers right away to a domain whatever shaped it couldn't have anticipated. Would that kill it, or is there a standard move that absorbs it?
- What if we crack open a competent system and find something that is functionally a model of the reasons for its own behavior, and it turns out to be load-bearing, not decorative? Is that a defeater, or does it just move the border? Is there a version of the view that's been qualified so hard that this would count as a win for it?
- Is my diagnostic any good? What would it score as comprehension-free that obviously isn't, and what would it fail to flag? I worry it's just a fancy way of saying "I can't predict its mistakes."
- There are cases running the other way: people who can state the rule perfectly and then can't do the thing, or who get worse when you ask them to explain themselves. Does that support the view, undercut it, or is it neutral — and why?
- Is the whole thing insulated because "comprehension-free" is defined negatively, so nothing could ever be shown to need comprehension? And if it is insulated, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or is a methodological default allowed to be immune in a way an empirical claim isn't?

What I'd personally find most persuasive, fwiw: a real case of the one-shot transfer thing above, and any reason to think the felt priority of understanding isn't just a sampling artifact. No reading lists or citations, I want the arguments themselves — and please write it in American English.
