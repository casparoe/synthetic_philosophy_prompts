I write literary fiction, and for the last two years I have been writing a novel with a friend under a single shared pseudonym: one manuscript, one file, one name on the cover, and a standing agreement between us that neither will ever say which chapters are whose. At a dinner during a residency this spring, an economist I know described with considerable relish the argument from Amartya Sen's 1970 paper in which two readers, one prudish and one not, must decide what becomes of a copy of *Lady Chatterley's Lover*, and where the natural way of giving each person a protected say over their own reading collides with the equally natural requirement he called the Pareto condition — that if everyone prefers one arrangement to another, the arrangement everyone prefers should win. He also sketched, briefly, the repairs that have been proposed: confine each person's protected say to matters that are genuinely their own; set aside the preferences people have about other people's private conduct; or reinterpret the protected say as being about what I do, given what you do. I have been turning this over ever since, and what I cannot get past is that almost none of the decisions my co-author and I actually face have the shape his example has.

Two of them. First: one contested passage, which the manuscript either contains or does not, with both our names attached either way — there is no version in which the sentence belongs to her alone or to me alone. Second: an invitation to appear at a festival under our real names, which concerns five people who share the pseudonym, and where any option (all appear, some appear, none appear) is a state of all of us at once. In neither case is there a pair of options that differs only in what one person does or undergoes; the disputed thing is held jointly, so every feasible option is a state of several people simultaneously. Consequently the move of discounting preferences about other people's business has nothing to bite on — my feeling about the passage is not a feeling about her private conduct, it is a feeling about our book — and the move of protecting each person's own small sphere has nothing to protect. The third repair seems to stall too: the sentence either stands in the shared document or it does not, and which actions are even available to me depends on what she has already done to the file, so "what I choose, holding you fixed" does not seem to describe anything. When we tried, at his suggestion, to give each of us a guaranteed say over some pair of options anyway, the conflict did not disappear so much as change shape: either the sayings clashed and we went round in a circle, or each of us could block any change at all and we were left sitting on an outcome both of us wanted to move away from — a failure that struck me as worse than the one in his dinner-table story, since respecting us both is precisely what produced the outcome we both disliked. But I may be misdescribing what we did, and that is part of what I want checked.

So my question. Is there an established concept, or a known result, for this configuration: alternatives that are jointly held, so that no option-pair is private to any one person; protected individual says that therefore attach to nothing, or that when attached anyway either cycle or lock in an outcome everyone would rather leave? And what happens to the Sen-style argument when the partition into personal spheres is unavailable — does the impossibility dissolve, survive, or get worse? I suspect the case resembles several named things without being any of them, and I want the carving rather than the label: which nearby concepts cover part of it, exactly where each of them stops fitting, whether any of them is really about joint ownership rather than merely about people minding other people's business, and how the cycling case differs from the lock-in case. I am at a residency with no library and no database access, so please do not send me to papers or hand me a list of names to look up; an author's name is of no use to me without the idea attached to it. Please answer in the following sections, in this order: *Nearest established concepts* (250–300 words, each with a one-line statement of what it covers); *Where each one stops fitting* (250–300 words, taking the candidates in turn and naming the precise feature of the joint case each fails to capture); *What happens to the impossibility* (about 200 words, on whether the conflict dissolves, survives as a cycle, or reappears as a jointly dispreferred lock-in, and what would have to be true for each); *Verdict on the name* (80–100 words, saying plainly whether an established one exists); and *If there is no name* (150–200 words: a coined term, a definition tight enough to exclude the neighbouring concepts, and two boundary cases, one that falls under it and one that falls just outside).
