I work on the financing side of higher education in Europe and spend a lot of time arguing with counterparts in England and the United States about why we do it differently, and I have come to think the argument is not really about money at all. I would like the philosophy underneath both arrangements made explicit.

The two models, as I understand them. In Germany and the Nordics, public universities charge no tuition; the cost falls on general taxation, which rises with income; and students receive means-tested support for living costs, part grant and part state-guaranteed loan whose total repayment is capped and relieved for low earners. In England and the United States, universities charge a near-uniform tuition fee set or capped by the government; students borrow from the state to pay it and, increasingly, to live; repayment does not start until earnings pass a threshold; instalments are a percentage of income above that threshold rather than a fixed sum; balances stop accruing after a fixed number of years and are cancelled on death; and in the United States the debt cannot be discharged in bankruptcy except on a showing of undue hardship. The government nonetheless records these loans as assets while expecting a large share of the money it lends never to come back.

Here is what puzzles me. An income-contingent, non-dischargeable, time-limited obligation whose expected non-repayment is priced in from the start does not resemble what we call a loan anywhere else. It resembles a tax levied only on people who went to university, capped in duration. Yet governments across the spectrum fight to keep the word loan, and the word seems to carry real argumentative weight: people who would reject a graduate tax on principle accept a graduate loan, and defenders of free tuition point at the other system's debt as the thing to be abolished. So, first: what is the loan label doing? Does the practice strictly presuppose something that the tax framing would not deliver — a benefit principle, a view about personal responsibility, a view about the moral psychology of owing — or is the label doing only budgetary and rhetorical work, with the same underlying theory available either way?

Beyond that, I want five things.

One: reconstruct what each arrangement presupposes about who benefits from a degree and about what sort of good a degree is. Tax funding looks as if it presupposes that the benefits are largely public, or else that private lending markets would underinvest because an eighteen-year-old cannot borrow against her own future earnings. Loan funding looks as if it presupposes that the graduate captures the benefit and should pay. I suspect each is underdetermined and fits several theories; I want to know exactly which commitments are required and which are optional.

Two: the incidence is odd in both directions. University entrants are disproportionately drawn from better-off households, so tax-funded free tuition is funded progressively but spent on the already advantaged; while in the loan systems the highest-earning graduates repay several times the cost of their education and the lowest have their balances cancelled. Is a regime's justification about the level of redistribution or about its shape, and which arrangement is more embarrassed by its own incidence?

Three: human capital cannot be diversified or sold. Loan financing places the risk about a person's future earnings on that person at eighteen, when she cannot know her future and cannot hedge; tax financing places it on a pool that can. But the apparently individualist model turns out to rest on an enormous public guarantee and an expected write-down, so it is pooling in disguise. Is the real difference between the two regimes where the risk sits, or only when and how visibly the subsidy arrives?

Four: if a substantial part of the graduate earnings premium is positional — a credential that places you in a queue for a fixed stock of good jobs rather than enlarging total output — then that premium is partly a transfer from non-graduates rather than a return on investment. Which arrangement's implicit theory survives that claim, and which is more damaged by taking it seriously?

Five: there is an internal tension I cannot resolve. The same legal order that makes repayment contingent on your ability to pay also refuses to let you discharge the debt in bankruptcy absent undue hardship. Does the non-dischargeability rule presuppose that non-payment is shirking rather than misfortune, and can that sit alongside contingency, deferment and cancellation, or does the practice hold two incompatible views of default at once?

Finally, I want to know what would actually have to change under each rival arrangement, not merely that it would be kinder. Consider, concretely: a graduate levy that is honestly a tax — a capped, time-limited surcharge on graduate earnings, with or without an option to buy out early at a fixed price; income-share agreements in which a funder pays tuition in exchange for a fixed percentage of earnings for a fixed term, the total uncapped; and free tuition with means-tested support for living costs only. For each, say what it presupposes that the current practice does not, and whether the crux is the cash flows or the framing — because my colleagues think only the cash flows matter, and I have started to suspect the framing does independent work.
