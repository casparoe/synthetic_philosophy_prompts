I sit on a committee that has to recommend a voting method for our professional society's elections. There are always at least three candidates, voters submit rankings, and one candidate is elected. A colleague who knows the social choice literature has produced an argument to the effect that, given the constraints we are under, no recommendation we make can yield outcomes that bind anyone, and I cannot find where it goes wrong. I want the argument diagnosed, not a survey of voting methods or of the theorem.

Set out the background precisely, because I suspect the precision matters. Voters have strict rankings over a finite set A of at least three alternatives. A rule is a deterministic function from profiles of reported rankings to a single winner, defined on every profile and with all of A in its range. A rule is strategy-proof if no voter can ever secure a winner she strictly prefers to the sincere winner by reporting something other than her true ranking, whatever the others report. A rule is dictatorial if there is some voter whose top-ranked alternative is selected at every profile. The theorem: a rule with those structural features is strategy-proof only if it is dictatorial. (With two alternatives majority rule is strategy-proof, so nothing below is meant to touch binary choices.)

Call an actual election *perverse for voter i* if, holding the other ballots fixed at what they in fact were, i strictly prefers the winner that some alternative ballot of hers would have produced to the winner that was produced. The argument:

(1) An outcome can bind a citizen — can give her a reason to accept it, and the rest of us a reason to hold her to it — only if the procedure did not make her worse off for having stated what she actually wants. Formally: only if the actual election is not perverse for her. Call this requirement R.

(2) As just stated, R holds the other ballots fixed at what they actually were. That cannot be the requirement we want, because on that reading whether the outcome binds me depends on whether my fellow voters happened to report sincerely — a fact about their psychology that the procedure cannot secure and that I cannot verify. A citizen's duty cannot be hostage to that. So what R must really demand is that no voter ever has a profitable misreport, whatever the others report. That is strategy-proofness.

(3) By the theorem, a rule of our kind is strategy-proof only if dictatorial.

(4) We require anonymity, and we are not adopting a dictatorship, so our rule is not strategy-proof: there is some admissible profile at which some voter has a profitable misreport.

(5) And the existential in (4) is not a curiosity confined to contrived profiles. My colleague maintains that for the familiar rules — plurality, Borda, two-round majority — the share of profiles at which some voter has a profitable misreport is large and grows with the size of the electorate, approaching one. I flag this as the one premise I cannot check and would rather not lean on; if the argument needs it, say so, and say what survives without it.

(6) A second colleague then pointed out that if (5) is right, step (2) is superfluous. R already fails for i at any election that is perverse for i, since R takes the other ballots as they were; so we can drop the contested move in (2) and still conclude that under any familiar rule, almost every real election is one whose outcome fails to bind at least one citizen.

(7) Furthermore, we cannot identify which citizen, or whether this election is one of those. Whether i could have done better by reporting something else depends on the other voters' true rankings and on i's own, and the ballots do not reveal either. So no citizen can tell whether the outcome binds her, and hence no such election gives anyone a duty to comply.

(8) The only procedures that meet the requirement are dictatorial ones, by (3); procedures with a restricted range, such as a rule that can only ever return one of two of the candidates, which escape the theorem because they are not defined onto all of A; and procedures that ignore the ballots entirely, such as a fixed lottery. (I know that a lottery over the voters — one randomly selected voter's top pick wins — is anonymous and strategy-proof; I count it under the last heading, since it consults one will per election.) None of the three is an acceptable way to make a binding collective decision.

(9) Therefore either no election held under any familiar rule binds anyone, or the only procedures that can produce binding outcomes are ones we cannot adopt. That is unacceptable, so something in (1) through (8) is false.

Here is where I am stuck, and what I want to preserve. I want to keep the thought that a procedure which systematically rewards misrepresentation is defective in a way that matters to its legitimacy; I do not want to be told that real voters are mostly sincere, or uninformed, or that manipulation is hard in practice, since that would leave the defect in place even where it is never exploited. I do not want to be told that legitimacy is purely procedural and has nothing to do with what voters want. I would also rather not lean on (5). My own sense is that (2) and (6) cannot both be doing work, but I cannot say which one is the real route and which is decoration, and the one I would most like to keep is (1).

Tell me which step fails and why that step looked sound; then tell me what I have to give up by rejecting it, and what the neighbouring steps would cost instead. If the flaw is a scope or modality shift, or an equivocation between a condition on elections and a condition on rules, say exactly where it occurs. If the argument is in fact valid and the only question is which premise to surrender, rank the premises by what it would cost to surrender them.
