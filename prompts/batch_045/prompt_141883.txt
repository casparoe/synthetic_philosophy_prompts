Hi — slightly ranty question, hope that's ok, and it's one of those where I've read enough to be confused in a specific way rather than a general one.

So: the "gender equality paradox" literature. Countries that score well on the standard gender-equality indices — the World Economic Forum one and its cousins — show *bigger* sex differences in a bunch of relevant respects than less equal countries do: a smaller share of women among science and engineering graduates, a stronger male tilt on the "things versus people" dimension of vocational interests, bigger gaps on experimental measures of risk-taking, patience and the like. Each of those gets reported separately, by different people, on different datasets, and then they're quoted together as though they were one result.

Then the two camps. Camp A: look, when you take the barriers away the gap gets *bigger*, so the gap was never mainly produced by the barriers — some of it is coming from preferences that aren't socially imposed, and the honest thing is to stop pretending otherwise. Camp B: those indices muddle gender equality together with economic development, women's labour-force participation and welfare-state generosity; richer countries can afford to let people indulge preferences that poorer countries force them to ignore; and none of this rules out a social explanation.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict. It feels stalled in a particular and annoying way: both sides have a stock reply to the other, everyone can recite the other side's rejoinder, and nothing has moved in years. My hypothesis is that the argument isn't really about nature versus nurture at all. It's that "the gap is larger where equality is greater" gets read as evidence about what *causes* the gap, and that inference needs a baseline — larger than what? — and the two camps are quietly using different ones. Camp A is comparing actual egalitarian countries to a hypothetical society with no gender norms at all; Camp B is comparing them to poor countries, where a woman's choices are made under constraint and so don't express very much. If that's right, the whole thing is a masked disagreement about counterfactuals, and no amount of extra country-level data settles it.

Three things I've been chewing on that might be part of the same mess, or might be separate:

- Composition. In a low-equality country the women who reach university at all are a wildly unrepresentative sliver — urban, elite, often with a parent already in the field. As equality rises, the female student population stops being selected, so the measured distribution drifts toward the population mean for reasons that have nothing to do with anyone's preferences changing.
- Field supply. The welfare state professionalised nursing, teaching, psychology, social work. Women's enormous expansion into higher education in rich countries was absorbed disproportionately by newly expanded, newly credentialised caring occupations. So the "gap" may partly be tracking the supply of female-typed professional fields rather than any tilt in interests.
- Denominators. "Share of women among STEM graduates" is a relative measure. It can fall while the absolute number of women in STEM rises and while women's own preferences stay put, if male enrolment moves the other way.

So: is my baseline hypothesis right, and if not, what's wrong with it? Are those three points one artefact dressed up three ways, or genuinely distinct? Is the "paradox" even one finding, or are people stitching together results about degree choices, interest inventories and lab-measured preferences that don't actually move together? And the bit I actually need: given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a within-country cohort design, some decomposition, a distinction I'm missing — or is this one where the honest answer is that it won't move, and why?

Please don't give me a literature review or anything with names and years stuck to it; I've read the stuff and I'll fill in references myself if I write this up. I want the structure of the disagreement, and an honest go at whether anything could end it. Don't be even-handed for its own sake either — if one side is just making an error, say which and where. Definately don't try to split the difference.
