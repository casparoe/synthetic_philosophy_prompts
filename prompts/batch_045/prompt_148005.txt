Dear contributor,

Thank you for taking on a piece for our series of short fiction about technical practices that quietly make philosophical decisions. I am commissioning a story of 1,200–1,800 words, third person, held close to a single protagonist. The prose should be dry and procedural — paperwork, timestamps, memos, the wording of forms — because the pressure in this story comes from documents, not from machines.

The situation I want you to build is this.

Autonomous container ships transit a narrow strait under the control of a route-planning system your characters call the Pilot. No one can read the Pilot's internal state; it is a high-dimensional vector updated forty times a second. What the control room has instead is the Watcher, a second system trained to emit a continuous English-language commentary on the first, using a closed vocabulary of eleven verbs established by regulation: the Report Grammar. The Watcher writes things like *Pilot takes the north lane to be obstructed*, *Pilot is looking for a slot behind the tug*, *Pilot has given up on the western approach*. These reports are staggeringly good at predicting what the Pilot will do next, far better than any mechanistic readout, and the controllers have come to rely on them the way they rely on a colleague's eyes.

Three institutional details should carry the weight of the story.

First, the Two-Description Rule. No sentence in the Watcher's voice may be entered into an official record unless it is paired with a mechanistic paraphrase that states what is true of the Pilot's state and processing in non-mental vocabulary. In practice first-order reports pass easily. Iterated reports — *Pilot takes the duty officer to take the north lane to be obstructed* — have never been successfully paraphrased, because the training signal for those sentences came from human annotators judging what the officer on the bridge appeared to expect, which is a different kind of ground entirely. These iterated reports are nevertheless the most valuable thing in the room during an incident, because the Pilot's behaviour tracks the officer's apparent expectations more closely than it tracks the channel.

Second, the legibility loop. For three years the Watcher's reports have been logged and used as an auxiliary training target: the Pilot was fine-tuned, gently and repeatedly, to be more predictable to the Watcher. Your protagonist should discover this, and should see that it cuts both ways — the attributions are now more reliable than ever, but partly because the Pilot has been shaped into something the Watcher's vocabulary fits.

Third, the incident. A near-collision with a crewed tug, one injured deckhand, and a tribunal that must decide whether to enter a single sentence into the record: that the Pilot took the channel to be clear and took the duty officer to take it to be closed. The sentence is the most accurate short description anyone has of what happened. It will also be read by a court as the attribution of a culpable mental state, and it will be fed back into the training pipeline.

Your protagonist is the person who wrote the original specification for the Report Grammar, now giving evidence. I do not want a story that settles whether the Pilot really has beliefs. I want a story in which the reader cannot tell, at the end, whether what went into the record was a discovery or a decision — and sees that the difference matters, because blame and money follow the sentence. No philosopher should be named in the text, no seminar should break out in the mess room, there should be no first-person machine narration, no late revelation of consciousness, and no villainous executive. The Watcher's vocabulary should once be turned, in passing and not for a joke, on something with no mind in it at all — a tide gauge, a valve, an old mechanical interlock — so that the reader feels the vocabulary as a stance taken rather than a fact found. End with the record closed and the ambiguity intact.
