im in an environmental history seminar right now and our unit is on non-human things — germs, weather, volcanoes — as "historical actors," and i have to write a short response paper and i dont want to blow it.

The TA gave us a rough test for when its fair to call a non-human cause an *actor* rather than just a background condition: (1) onset — you can date the thing entering the story, and the humans involved could in principle have seen it coming or known about it; (2) underdetermination — the same physical event plays out differently in broadly comparable places, so institutions must be doing some of the work; (3) uptake — the people living through it treated it as something you could answer, like name it, blame it, pray at it, legislate against it. All three hold and the thing gets to be an actor; only some of them and its just a condition or a trigger.

I want to run it on Tambora. Big eruption in april 1815 in whats now Indonesia, and then 1816 is the "year without a summer" — harvests failing and food riots across europe, frost and snow in new england in june, typhus in ireland the year after, a wave of emigration out to the american midwest, and Frankenstein getting written at lake geneva because everyone was stuck indoors in the rain.

What i actually need is the mapping, not a yes or no. Run the eruption through the three conditions and say where each one holds and where it strains: who or what counts as a player here, what the options available to each were, and what would count as evidence that the volcano *acted* rather than merely happened. Then say what this way of talking gets right about 1816 that a normal political narrative misses, and what it gets wrong or misdescribes — does putting the volcano in the subject position quietly turn the humans into scenery, or is that just a stylistic worry?

And one thing im stuck on: the TA keeps warning that the actor framing slides into environmental determinism, and i dont really see how — Tambora obviously acted on its own, nobody chose to erupt it and the cold came regardless of what anyone did, so it feels like the physical event is doing the explaining and the humans are just reacting to it. Is that what determinism means here or am i mixing up two different things? Last bit: does this case make the test look good or bad — does 1816 confirm it, break it, or is it the kind of case the test never anticipated, and where exactly does the test go silent.
