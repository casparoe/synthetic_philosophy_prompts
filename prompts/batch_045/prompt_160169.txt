I've been going back and forth with a friend from a philosophy of religion reading group about the cosmological argument. She is drawn to the kalam version, on which whatever begins to exist has a cause and the universe began to exist; I have always found the contingency version more gripping, on which contingent things stand in need of explanation and the totality of contingent things is itself contingent, so that something non-contingent must be doing the explanatory work. But when I stop reciting either argument and instead ask myself, case by case, whether this thing really cries out for an explanation, my reactions refuse to line up with either argument and do not obviously line up with each other. So let me just report them. A dent in my car door does cry out, and once I learn that a neighbour's kid rode his bike into it I am finished: nothing further nags. The existence of the physical universe, taken as a whole, also cries out, and it is not answered by pointing out that every event inside it has a cause. An endless series of contingent things, each caused by the one before, with no first member, still cries out — and, oddly, it bothers me slightly more than a single uncaused contingent thing that starts everything off, even though in the series every individual link has a cause and in the single-thing case one does not. A necessary being, one that exists in every possible world and has no cause, needs no explanation at all; I am satisfied. But a necessary being whose decree that there be exactly these constants, this many galaxies and this curvature has no reason behind it bothers me a great deal: it feels as though the arbitrariness has merely been relocated inside the stopping point. A universe that exists because the whole of its history has to satisfy a single self-consistency equation and there is exactly one history that satisfies it leaves me partly satisfied, even though nothing caused it and the explanation is not causal at all; whereas a universe picked arbitrarily from a vast space of equally consistent candidate histories leaves me wholly unsatisfied. A universe that brings itself into existence strikes me as worse than one caused from outside. Three contingent things standing in a causal circle, each caused by another, leave me unsatisfied despite every link being explained, though less unsatisfied than the self-causing case. And yet the contingent facts about character — why there are exactly these species of particle, why the electron has the charge it has — barely bother me at all, while the fact that there is anything contingent at all bothers me enormously. Fine-tuning in the life-permitting range demands an explanation; a multiverse of varied constants together with a selection effect takes some of the edge off, though the multiverse's own existence then demands one; and when told that the multiverse is necessary I feel relieved and then immediately suspicious, for the same reason as before. An empty world, in which nothing ever existed, seems to me to demand nothing — though by my own lights it should.

My tentative formulation is this: a fact demands an explanation just in case it is contingent, it is not entailed by something that either is necessary or itself has an explanation, and the chain of causes or entailments that backs it is well-founded, with no circles and no loops. I think the third clause is what is doing the work in the circular and self-causing cases, but I cannot tell whether it misfires elsewhere; I have no idea what to say about the pair where the difference seems to be how many candidates there are rather than anything causal; and I do not know why contingency in the existence of the whole should trigger a demand that contingency in its character does not.

What I would like from you is a principle, or a small set of rival principles, stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself. For each clause, tell me which of my cases motivates it and which of my verdicts it costs me. Then give me its verdicts on cases I have not listed: an empty world in which nothing ever existed; a universe that exists of necessity but nevertheless has a first moment in time; an endless series in which each member is explained by the one that comes after it rather than before it; and a universe brought into existence for a moral reason, say because a necessary being judged it good, rather than by an arbitrary decree. Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and whether you would overturn the verdict or the principle, whether a principle that saved all of them would be too contrived to be worth having, and whether what you end up with is best described as a version of the contingency argument, a version of the kalam argument, or neither.
