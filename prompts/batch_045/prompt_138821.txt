**Question 4 (25 marks). Answer all parts.**

*(a) [8 marks]* It is often said that the Duhem–Quine thesis shows that scientific theories are underdetermined by the evidence. Explain what the holist (Duhem–Quine) thesis actually claims, how it differs from contrastive underdetermination — the claim that a body of evidence may be accommodated equally well by two or more incompatible global theories — and why running the two together obscures rather than settles the question whether underdetermination is a problem for scientific realism.

*(b) [7 marks]* In a hypothetical future physics there are two theories of microgeometry, G₁ and G₂. They postulate different fine-grained structures of space, and they disagree about what the world is like at scales that no instrument described by either theory could resolve. They agree exactly on the predicted outcome of every measurement whose performance is compatible with the laws of either theory — call these the admissible measurements. There is nevertheless a family of hypothetical measurement arrangements that neither theory's laws permit: each theory entails that no such arrangement can ever be assembled. These arrangements are not logically or metaphysically incoherent — a third theory H, for which nobody has any positive evidence but which is not internally inconsistent, permits them — and G₁ and G₂ disagree about what their outcomes would be.

(i) Do G₁ and G₂ agree on all *possible* evidence? Explain what work the word 'possible' is doing here.
(ii) Suppose a discriminating arrangement of this kind could be assembled only in a world in which the laws differ from those that both G₁ and G₂ say obtain — that is, the observation could occur only in a world in which both theories are false. Would such an observation help a scientist choose between them? Say what this shows about what a defender of permanent underdetermination would have to establish.

*(c) [10 marks]* Assess the following argument: if the evidence cannot settle which of two theories is true, then there is no fact of the matter about which is true. In your answer you should: (i) distinguish underdetermination as an epistemic thesis from any semantic or metaphysical conclusion someone might want to draw from it; (ii) say whether a route to the same conclusion is available that does not appeal to empirical equivalence at all — consider, if it helps, the problem of unconceived alternatives; and (iii) explain why a realist who responds by appealing to theoretical virtues such as simplicity and explanatory power may be accused of changing the subject.

**Student's answer to Question 4**

*(a)* The Duhem–Quine thesis says that hypotheses are never tested one at a time. A hypothesis entails an observational prediction only when conjoined with auxiliary assumptions about the instruments, the initial conditions and the background theory, so when a prediction fails the evidence does not tell us which member of the conjunction to abandon: we can always preserve the hypothesis under test by blaming an auxiliary instead. That is holist underdetermination. Contrastive underdetermination is a different claim, namely that a single body of evidence may be accommodated equally well by two or more incompatible theories taken as wholes. The distinction matters because the two claims support different conclusions. Holism is quite compatible with realism: a realist can concede that no hypothesis is separately testable and still hold that the total evidence favours our best total theory over its rivals. Contrastive underdetermination threatens realism only if the choice between rivals is permanent — underdetermined by all possible evidence and not merely by the evidence available at present — since underdetermination by present evidence is exactly what the further progress of science removes. This is the distinction drawn between transient and permanent underdetermination, together with the complaint that nobody has yet produced a convincing example of the permanent kind.

*(b)* G₁ and G₂ are empirically equivalent, since they agree on the outcome of every admissible measurement, and so no possible evidence could ever distinguish them. The case is therefore one of permanent rather than merely transient underdetermination, and it answers the complaint that no such case has been produced. The fact that some third theory H would permit discriminating measurements is irrelevant: H is not a serious candidate, and which measurements are possible is determined by the laws of whichever theory one accepts. Nor does it help to imagine the discriminating arrangement being assembled anyway, since both G₁ and G₂ entail that it cannot be, so nothing in that scenario counts as a possible observation for either theory. In any case simplicity decides the matter: G₁ is the simpler of the two, so the choice is not really unsettled.

*(c)* The argument is invalid as stated. Its premise is epistemic — a claim about what we could establish — while its conclusion is metaphysical — a claim about whether there is a determinate truth of the matter — and the gap between them can be closed only by an extra premise, such as a verificationist account of meaning. Underdetermination, even if it were established, would support at most a modest scepticism about our knowledge of unobservables, not the claim that statements about unobservables lack truth values. The problem of unconceived alternatives is the same point in another form: it observes that for every theory we accept there are empirically equivalent rivals that nobody has happened to think of, and so it supplies a logical argument from equivalence rather than merely a historical one; but for that reason it can do no work that the plain underdetermination argument cannot do. Finally, the realist's appeal to theoretical virtues settles the matter outright: simplicity and explanatory power are themselves evidence, so the evidence never really underdetermined the choice in the first place. The upshot is that the argument from underdetermination fails and scientific realism is left untouched.
