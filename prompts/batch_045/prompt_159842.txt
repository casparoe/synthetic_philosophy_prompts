I'm in a reading group working through the philosophy of the emotions — the familiar three-way divide between treating emotions as judgments, as perceptions of value, and as feelings or bodily disturbances — and one line keeps being produced as a conversation-stopper: you can't help what you feel.

Two episodes, so you can see what work it is being made to do:

1. I argued that a fear can be assessed: that a fear of flying can be called out as misplaced, given how safe flying is, roughly in the way a perceptual illusion can be called out. Someone replied that you can't help what you feel, so there is nothing there to be correct or incorrect about, and with that the whole question of whether emotions can be rational or irrational dissolves. That struck me as too fast — perceptual experiences are not under voluntary control either, and we still say they can misrepresent things — but I want to know exactly which claim he needed, and whether it is one anyone should grant.

2. Separately, someone said she couldn't help feeling resentment at a colleague's promotion, and offered that as a reason not to be held accountable for what she then did. Here the line seemed to be doing different work — something about responsibility rather than about correctness — and I couldn't tell whether it was the same claim doing the work or a different one wearing the same words.

What I want is a precisification: lay out the distinct, precise theses that the line might be expressing, as a menu with verdicts rather than a single preferred reading. For each, say whether it is trivial or close to tautological, whether it is substantive and defensible, whether it is false or rests on a conflation, and whether it would actually deliver the conclusion it was being used for — especially in episode 1, where what is needed is the conclusion that emotions are not the kind of thing that can be assessed for correctness at all.

The points I would most like pinned down, since the three theories seem to come apart on them:

- the difference between not being able to start or stop an emotion at will, not being able to modify it once it has begun, and not being able to reshape it over the long run — and whether "can't help" means the same thing in each case, or whether the long-run case smuggles in a kind of control the others lack;
- whether a state's being involuntary has any bearing at all on its being assessable for correctness, or whether these are simply independent questions — my suspicion is that this is exactly where the perception theory and the feeling theory diverge, and I want that divergence stated sharply;
- the different dimensions along which an emotion might be assessed: whether its object really has the feature the emotion presents it as having, whether having that reaction to that object is warranted or proportionate, and whether acting on it was a good idea. If "correct" is ambiguous across these, does the slogan's conclusion change across the readings?;
- whether a theory on which emotions are judgments owes an account of why they strike us as things that merely happen to us, and why they persist even when we judge them out of place — and whether giving that account costs it the claim that emotions respond to reasons.

Two further questions I would want answered honestly rather than diplomatically. Is there a reading on which the line is true and yet irrelevant to both episodes? And is there any reading strong enough to get episode 1's conclusion that is not simply that conclusion restated in different words — i.e., is the line doing real argumentative work anywhere, or only trading on an ambiguity? If the honest answer is that it trades on an ambiguity, say which of the three theories is best placed to explain what exactly we can and cannot help, and say where the verdicts would flip for hard cases: emotions with no determinate object, emotions one has cultivated deliberately over years, emotions one has induced in oneself by choosing what to attend to, and emotions that one has been asked to have.

I don't need citations or a reading list — I can find those myself. I want the distinctions carved, with the boundary cases where the verdicts would flip.
