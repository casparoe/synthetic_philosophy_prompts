I'm a senior software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and my team is deciding how far to lean on large-model code generation. Our tech lead has been settling the argument with an analogy drawn from computational complexity, and I want to know how much weight it can actually bear before I accept it as settled.

The analogy comes in two halves. The half that circulates in popular writing says that P versus NP is the formal version of the gap between recognising a good solution and producing one — the gap, in the version everyone repeats, between being able to appreciate a piece of music and being able to compose it. The half she actually argues from says that writing a program is search while checking a program is verification, that verification is the easy side of the asymmetry, and that we should therefore let the model write the code and keep the humans on review, because review is cheap and reliable in a way that generation is not. She also uses the popular half to draw a stronger conclusion: that if the asymmetry ever collapsed, there would be nothing specially valuable left in engineering creativity.

Please audit the analogy rather than settle the staffing question. Specifically:

1. Give me the correspondence table explicitly, for both halves. In the music case and in the code case, what plays the role of the instance, the certificate, the verifier, the polynomial bound, and the worst case? I want to see whether the two halves require different fillings-in of the same slots, and where either half leaves a slot empty.

2. Separate the disanalogies that matter for her conclusion from the ones that are merely pedantic. Things I already doubt and want adjudicated: polynomial time is an asymptotic, worst-case classification rather than a claim about the sizes and shapes of instances we actually face, and satisfiability solvers routinely dispatch industrial instances the theory calls hard while plenty of polynomial algorithms are unusable at our scale; knowing that an efficient algorithm exists is not the same as having it, and I am told there is a classical universal-search construction that is optimal only up to a constant so large as to be worthless in practice; and our test suite and review process can exhibit a failing input but cannot establish that no failing input exists, which seems to place the thing we actually do on the opposite side of the asymmetry from the one she invokes.

3. Address the obstacle I care about most, which she has not addressed at all. In an NP verification relation there is a fixed, formal property being checked against a formal instance. In our situation there mostly isn't one: the requirement lives in a ticket, in a reviewer's head, and in whatever the customer meant. Is the thing that makes our work hard the search, or the specification? If it is the specification, it seems to me the relevant barrier is not search-versus-verification but something closer to the undecidability of nontrivial properties of a program's behaviour — in which case the analogy is pointing at the wrong difficulty entirely. Say whether that is right, whether the two difficulties are even in the same family, and what follows for her conclusion either way.

4. Take the creative-leaps conclusion on its own. Does it survive? I want to know what the analogy quietly imports into the target — a well-defined objective function over candidate solutions, a short certificate of quality, a single agreed standard of success — and whether the asymmetry says anything about the difficulty or the value of the activity once those are removed.

5. If the analogy fails or only partly holds, tell me what a better one would be, or whether the honest answer is that complexity classes do not speak to this question at all.

Please write for someone who knows algorithms and production systems but has never read a philosophy paper: define any term you need from the theory, and don't assume I know what goes without saying.
