I'm in my final year of secondary school, and I'm writing an essay on whether the situationist critique of character really damages virtue ethics. I've been working from the version of that critique associated with Harman and Doris, though I may be reconstructing it badly. Below is the argument of my essay in compressed form — the five moves it makes — and then what I'd like from you.

Move 1 — The critique as I understand it. The well-known social psychology studies (Milgram's obedience experiments; the seminary students who stepped past a man slumped in a doorway when they were in a hurry; the people who became markedly more helpful after finding a dime) show behavior tracking situational variables rather than stable individual differences. We go on explaining conduct by character anyway. So ordinary character attribution is a systematic error, and virtue ethics, which is built on character, inherits it.

Move 2 — My central distinction. I propose that a virtue decomposes into two components, which I call detection and execution. Detection is noticing the features of a situation that make a response appropriate and reading them correctly. Execution is responding appropriately once the requirement is visible. My claim: the experiments are engineered so that detection fails, not execution. They work by making the relevant cues ambiguous, by diffusing responsibility, by crowding attention with hurry or mood, or by having an authority relabel what is going on. What they establish, on this reading, is that detection is fragile — not that there is no disposition to execute.

Move 3 — The dark-room analogy. Testing people's eyesight in an unlit room, finding that nobody can read the chart, and concluding that eyesight was never a real property of anyone would be a bad inference. The experiments are, I argue, dark rooms: they remove the very cues a capacity needs in order to manifest. Behavioral uniformity under degraded conditions is evidence about the conditions at least as much as about the people.

Move 4 — Making it testable. I know the obvious objection is that I have saved virtue by shrinking it into something unfalsifiable, so I add a prediction. Situations vary along three axes: cue legibility, the cost of noncompliance, and the availability of an exit. I predict leakage — as all three become favorable, behavioral variance between individuals should increase rather than decrease, should become stable across quite different kinds of situation, and should track independently gathered facts about a person's history and self-description. If no such pattern reappears under favorable conditions, my account is in trouble.

Move 5 — The cost I think I'm accepting. On my account virtue is cue-dependent and environmentally scaffolded, which means the work we want character to do — assessing people, praise and blame, moral education — turns partly on environments and not only on persons. I try to present this as a feature rather than a collapse, but it is the part I am least confident about.

What I'd like from you, in under 700 words, under the headings below:

1. Does the detection/execution split survive scrutiny? I worry that for some virtues the two cannot be separated — part of courage is seeing what is genuinely dangerous, part of fairness is seeing which differences matter. If detection is partly constitutive of a virtue rather than merely a precondition for its exercise, my reply may be relabeling the situationist's point.
2. Is the dark-room analogy doing real argumentative work, or is it decoration standing in for a claim I still need to establish? In particular, is there a short reply available to the situationist that I am missing — for instance that if detection is that fragile, character traits do little explanatory work in ordinary life either, since ordinary life is also full of dim rooms?
3. Is the leakage prediction testable as I have stated it, or have I stated it so that it cannot fail? Say what result would count against it.
4. Is this, in substance, a position that already exists under a name — some version of a sensitivity or reasons-responsiveness reply? I do not want citations or a reading list; I only want to know whether I have independently reinvented a familiar view and, if so, roughly how it is usually formulated, so I can judge whether my version adds anything. If you are not confident, say so instead of guessing.
5. The single most serious objection I have not addressed.
6. Any place where I have misdescribed what situationists actually claim.

Two requests about how you answer. Please spend your words on objections rather than on explaining situationism back to me. And please mark clearly, throughout, which of your claims are settled in the debate and which are genuinely contested among people who work on it: if something is one side's position rather than something both sides grant, say that, and do not present a contested claim as the consensus. Where you think the literature offers no agreed answer, flag it as open rather than choosing silently.
