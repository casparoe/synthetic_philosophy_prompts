They both cornered me about it again on Monday and I still don't have a real answer, so I'm outsourcing.

The case they were fighting over is one Reza knows from field work he did a while back in a mid-income country (he asked me not to name it, so: a district water office). The official at the center of it got his job partly through a patron, takes small appreciation payments that everyone knows about, and uses his discretion to hook households up to the mains even though they never filed the paperwork — households he knows personally, and which happen to be among the poorer ones in the district. Dana said it isn't obviously corruption. Reza said it obviously is. The exchange, roughly:

1. Reza: corruption is public goods allocated by personal tie instead of by rule, and that is exactly this.

2. Dana: the rule here is a fiction. The regional office where you're supposed to file hardly functions and shakes people down when it does; his discretion is the only allocation mechanism that actually works. Calling a workaround by the name of the disease it works around is a category error.

3. Reza: if whether something counts as corruption depends on whether the formal system is broken, the concept has no content left — every corrupt act gets excused by pointing at some dysfunction upstream. You owe me an act-level test.

4. Dana: fine, and it isn't outcome-based either. Her test: is the route displaceable — could someone with no connection to him get the same hookup by a route they could find out about without knowing him? If the personal tie is only an accelerator, the harm is small. Corruption proper is when the circuit is closed.

5. Reza: that's nepotism with a footnote. Jumping a queue still puts the connected person first, and the people at the back are worse off even if they'd have got there eventually.

6. Dana: then the empty queue problem, which is her name for it and the bit I couldn't answer. In this case there is no queue. Nobody in those villages has ever successfully filed; the ones who tried got ignored or squeezed. So nobody is displaced, there's no identifiable victim, and a poor household gets water. Where's the harm? And if you say the harm is to the institution, you owe an account of what an institution is such that it can be damaged without anyone being damaged.

7. Reza: the harm is to the people who never got to queue at all. And the practice is self-stabilizing — his discretion is his currency, so he has a standing reason to keep the formal route broken. You can't treat the broken formal system as an exogenous background when the workaround is part of what produces it.

8. Dana: that's an empirical claim, and a strong one. Sometimes the shortcut is what builds trust in the state in the first place: the family whose water got connected because a cousin knows somebody may conclude the state can do things for them. She called it gateway versus trap and said he's just assuming trap.

9. Reza: even where that's true, the honest description is a local optimum that's hard to exit, not "not corruption." And you can never estimate the counterfactual anyway — the person insisting nothing would happen without him is the person with the strongest incentive to make sure nothing happens without him.

10. Dana: fine, but that cuts against your universality claim too. If applying the concept needs a counterfactual nobody can estimate, then applying it cross-culturally just exports a guess. And the ideal of impartial administration is a historically recent invention of a handful of states; we call it universal because those states won.

11. Reza: universality isn't a headcount. People approved of foot-binding locally too. The reason generalizes: discretionary allocation routes goods toward the organized and away from the unorganized, and the unorganized are usually the poorest. That doesn't stop being a reason because locals disagree.

12. Dana: then there's no stopping point. A doctor seeing a friend's kid first, legacy admissions, a big donor whose call gets returned — all partiality, all route goods to the organized. If you answer that those differ because a rule exists and is mostly followed, you've conceded corruption is a matter of degree, so the binary label is doing rhetorical work, not analytical work. Also, the big cross-country index everyone quotes is built out of expert perceptions, not acts, so the whole measurement apparatus already smuggles in whose practices get looked at suspiciously.

13. Reza: I'll take the continuum and I'll bite the bullet — those are mildly corrupt. A word can name the bad end of a gradient without pretending there's a bright line.

14. Dana: you won't call them that in public though, because the word travels with a package — failed state, backwards country, aid conditionality — and that's the actual work it does in the world. So we should be suspicious of it as an analytic category at all.

15. Reza: that's just a genetic fallacy about a concept.
