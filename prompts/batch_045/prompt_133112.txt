i restore old wooden boats on weekends, have for about twenty years, and i think i've accidently built myself the Ship of Theseus problem out behind my barn and i cant get it out of my head now that it's mine.

So: there's a small sloop, the Petrel, launched in the thirties, that I've owned since I was twenty five. Over nineteen winters I replaced every plank on her. Not all at once, a few each year, but every single one. What never changed is the keel, the name painted on the transom, the registration papers (still in my name, still that hull number), and the fact that she floats and I sail her every summer. The old planks are all in the barn, stacked in the order they came off, numbered in grease pencil.

Two years ago I put them back into a hull. Mostly them: a few were too far gone so I milled new ones, and I used a mast off a different boat that sank in the nineties. I sail that one too. My daughter calls the continuously repaired one "the real Petrel" and the reassembled one "the ghost," and she is completely certain and I am not certain at all.

Stuff I keep going back and forth on. It feels like it should matter that it happened gradually over nineteen years instead of in one week, and I can't say why. It feels like it should matter that for one winter she was nothing but a stack of wood in the barn with no boat shaped thing anywhere. It feels like the keel is doing a lot of work in my thinking and I don't know if that's a principled reason or just that I'm attached to it. And it bugs me that neither boat is pure: the ghost has new wood in it and a borrowed mast, and the Petrel has none of her original wood at all.

A friend who did some philosophy in college said some people think there just isn't a fact of the matter here and it's only about how we decide to talk, and other people think a thing is made of time slices and the boat is just a long stack of them. I half get both of those and I don't want the poem to be a lecture about either.

What I want is a sestina. I looked the form up: six stanzas of six lines, then a three line envoi, same six end words rotating through in the correct order (I don't remember how the rotation goes, please get it right). I want the end words to be: keel, oak, name, sail, shape, ghost. I want the reader who knows this stuff to be able to tell that those are six different answers to "which one is her" and not just six nautical words — the part that never got swapped, the stuff it's made of, the word and the papers, what it does, the form, and the one built out of the original timber. The reason I want a sestina is that the words stay put while everything around them changes, which is sort of the whole thing: the name outlasts the wood.

Then I want the envoi to be the turn. But don't let it land on "nothing is really anything, it's all just words, man," because when I'm out there I definately care which one she is. If there's a right answer I want to feel it, and if there isn't I want to feel that instead, without it turning into a shrug.

It's getting read out loud at the relaunch in May in front of thirty people who have never thought about any of this for one second, so it has to work as a poem for them too. No footnotes, no explanation after, no notes on the form.
