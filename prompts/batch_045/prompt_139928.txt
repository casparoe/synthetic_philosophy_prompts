I have no academic background in philosophy or medicine — I left school at eighteen and read in the evenings — and I came to this after my brother was involuntarily committed four years ago, so I am not asking as a neutral party, even though what I would like is a neutral answer.

Among people who are sceptical of psychiatry, the comparison I keep running into is this: a psychiatric diagnosis is today what possession by the devil was centuries ago. A person behaves in ways the community will not accept; an accredited expert is summoned to certify that the real author of the behaviour is an invisible entity — then a demon, now an illness — rather than the person; the person's own denial counts as further evidence, since demons and diseases both deny themselves; the community is relieved of the need to argue with the person, because one does not argue with a disease; the expert performs a ritual and the community pays. On this telling, schizophrenia is not the name of something discovered but the name of a verdict, and psychiatry is a priesthood with better premises. Szasz himself reached for the comparison early on, in a law-review piece on the insanity defence, saying that a claim of mental illness is no more a fact bearing on a person's guilt than a claim of possession by the devil would be.

I have seen the analogy used to carry three conclusions: first, that diagnostic categories are moral judgments in medical costume and have no factual content; second, that the illness named in a diagnosis explains nothing, since invoking the label is only a way of restating the behaviour in a more respectable vocabulary; third, that involuntary commitment is therefore imprisonment for deviance and ought to be abolished outright.

What I want is an audit rather than a verdict on Szasz. Please set out explicitly what plays the role of what — the demon, the priest, the ritual, the community's standard of acceptable conduct, the confession, the tribunal, the manual of signs by which the afflicted are recognised — and then tell me which of those correspondences do real argumentative work and which are only decoration.

Then the disanalogies. Some candidates I can see but cannot weigh: (a) no one was ever shown to have a demon and demonology predicted nothing, whereas these categories have some grip on the world — they run in families, they appear in very different societies at roughly similar rates, they say something about how a person's difficulties will develop over years, and some drugs blunt some of the experiences. (b) In the possession case the label was typically imposed by neighbours and by the authorities; in my brother's case, and I gather in most, the machinery was set in motion by the family, and the person was frightened and wanted help. (c) Possession does not seem to have been something people suffered from in the way people suffer from hearing accusations all night. (d) The possession label had no use to the person carrying it, whereas people seek diagnoses, organise around them, and sometimes say that being given one was the first intelligible thing anyone had said to them. For each of these: does it damage the analogy's ability to carry conclusion one, two or three, or is it beside the point?

I also want to know what the analogy imports without arguing for it. My sense is that it quietly assumes that if a category has a social function then it has no factual content; that no lesion found so far means no lesion; and that the expert's authority is the whole of the story.

Where I need the most help is this. Suppose that tomorrow a physical marker were found — a pattern on a brain scan, say — that was present in nearly everyone given a particular diagnosis, rare in those not given it, and also present in a fair number of people with no complaints at all, who work, whose lives go well, and who would refuse any offer of treatment. Call them the well positives. Two things seem to follow, and I cannot tell whether they do. First, that Szasz's position is partly a bet about what pathology will and will not be found, which would make it hostage to future discovery rather than settled on principle — a condition once counted among the insanities stopped being one when it turned out to be a brain infection, and I take it Szasz would have no quarrel with that, since he allowed that where there is a demonstrable disease of the brain there is a real disease. Second, that even a perfect marker would not settle whether the well positives are ill, because a marker detects what is unusual while illness seems to require that something is going wrong for the person. Whether the well positive is more like a carrier of an infection or more like someone with an undiagnosed tumour, and whether treating such a person is medicine or exorcism, is the part I cannot work out — and it is, I suspect, the part the analogy was smuggled in to avoid.

My own candidate for a better comparison, which I would rather have tested than flattered: a diagnosis is like a legal verdict, in that it classifies a person under a standard someone wrote, changes what may be done to them, is revisable on better evidence, can be right or wrong, and is issued by an institution with an interest in the outcome. Is that an improvement, or does it drop precisely what the possession comparison was holding on to?

Finally, which of the three conclusions does the analogy actually support? In particular, can one reject the comparison and still hold that involuntary commitment should be abolished — is the case against coercion independent of whether Szasz is right that mental illness is a myth, and if it is independent, what is it resting on?

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
