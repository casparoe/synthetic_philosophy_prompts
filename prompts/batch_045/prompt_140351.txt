What's bugging me is that a bunch of real institutions already behave as if all of this were settled, and they don't all behave the same way.

- Criminal courts keep acquitting people who did genuinely violent things while asleep. There's the famous sleepwalking case where a guy drove across town and killed his mother-in-law, and there's a whole line of more recent sexsomnia defences where someone is accused of a sexual assault they suposedly performed in their sleep. The doctrine is automatism: no voluntary control, no conscious will behind the act. And oddly, at least in Canada I think, sleepwalking gets classified as the mental-disorder kind of automatism rather than the ordinary kind, because the cause is internal to the person, even though nobody thinks the sleepwalker is disordered in any ordinary sense.
- Sleep medicine and clinical psychology go the other way and treat dreams as experiences with a capital E. Nightmare disorder is a diagnosable condition, the PTSD criteria count recurring distressing dreams as one of the intrusion/re-experiencing symptoms, imagery rehearsal therapy has you rehearse a new ending to your nightmare while awake and expects the nightmare itself to change, and lucid dreaming researchers get dreamers to make pre-arranged left-right eye movements from inside REM and treat that as proof that the person was conscious and undergoing something at the time.

So here's what I want. Reconstruct the implicit theory of dreaming that each of these practices presupposes. Do the legal and the clinical picture actually contradict each other, or does each one only need something really weak? Does imagery rehearsal therapy really need the nightmare to have been lived through during REM, or only that there's some reportable experience somewhere in the story?
