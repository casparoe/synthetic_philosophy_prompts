I convene a small graduate reading group on the timing of conscious experience, and our sessions have developed a characteristic failure mode: everyone arrives already knowing the color phi display (a dot in one color at one location, then roughly a hundred milliseconds later a dot in another color some degrees away, with subjects reporting a single dot that moves and changes color midway along its path, before the second dot has been shown) and already knowing the two revision stories that the case is used to motivate, everyone agrees that the two stories are supposed to be empirically indistinguishable, and then we spend the next hour restating that agreement in slightly different vocabulary. So I want to stop assigning readings for one session and instead hand people an instrument. What I'd like from you is a diagnostic decision tree — or, if that turns out to be the wrong shape, a ten-question sorting quiz with a scoring key — that takes a member of the group from answers about concrete cases to one of about eight named positions on when experiences happen and what it would be for them to happen then. The audience is people who have read the classic material and are fluent in the terminology, which is exactly why I want the instrument to sort them by what they say about cases rather than by which label they prefer: no question may use a theory's name, ask them to self-classify, or ask in the abstract whether experience has a determinate temporal location. Ten branch nodes or questions, eight leaves, each leaf a position with its usual name or, if it has none, a coined one.

The branching work should be done by distinctions rather than by a roster of views, so please lay out the logical space first — the questions and the combinations of answers they permit — and only then place the actual positions in it, flagging combinations that nobody occupies and saying briefly why (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored). The distinctions I want to do the work are: whether there is any fact of the matter about when an experience occurs, or whether the question is empty, or whether it has an answer only relative to a stipulation; if there is a fact, what fixes it — the time of the neural correlate, the time at which the content becomes available to downstream consumers such as report or decision, or the time at which the subject's own judgment places it; whether an experience can have temporal structure, that is, whether a subject can undergo at one moment an experience with an earlier phase that was never itself experienced at that earlier moment; whether a revision story requires an unrevised original, so that one of the two classic stories quietly presupposes what the other denies; and whether the subject's report is the right sort of evidence at all, or whether a merely involuntary measure (an eye-movement response, a pupil change, a scalp-recording marker) could settle the question in a way a report could not. The cases the questions are built from should include, at minimum: the plain display; a variant in which an unrelated click or dim flash appears during the gap and the subject responds to it with a speeded key press while the illusion goes on undisturbed (was the subject conscious of the first dot at the moment of that response?); a variant in which the second flash is withheld or replaced by a mask, so the subject reports a stationary dot that simply stopped (does the influence of a later event on the content of an earlier experience show that the earlier experience was not had when it seemed to be?); a chimeric variant with two such displays running in opposite directions at different gaps in the two hemifields, reported as one coherent scene (does the position now entail two different "nows" for a single subject at a single time?); and a variant in which the second flash's color has been made perfectly predictable by a long run of training trials, so that a predictive mechanism could in principle author the change before the second flash arrives (does this turn one revision story into the other, or does it open a position the standard pair leaves out?).

Each leaf should carry a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment, and I especially want the places where the instrument fails: pairs of leaves it cannot separate, the specific questions on which they come apart nowhere, and — since much of our argument is about whether we are disagreeing at all — a verdict, for each adjacent pair of leaves, on whether their dispute is substantive, merely verbal, or a case of two parties answering different questions. Please also include one transfer question at the end that reruns the same apparatus on a case where the later event is not an external stimulus but the subject's own action, so we can see which positions travel from perception to agency and which were only ever theories of what happens when a second flash arrives. I don't want citations, experimental data, or suggestions for further reading — the whole point is to work the logical space with the materials given.
