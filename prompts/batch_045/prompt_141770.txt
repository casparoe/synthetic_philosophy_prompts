I convene a 90-minute seminar on inheritance, dynastic wealth, and equal opportunity for eighteen advanced undergraduates and master's students in philosophy and public policy (mixed preparation, no economics prerequisite). I want you to design a playable, multi-generation game for it — rules, a ledger I can keep on a whiteboard, small stakes or no stakes, and a debrief — such that the participants run into a specific structural point on their own and only hear it named afterwards. I will run two of these sessions, ninety minutes each, with a twenty-minute break in between if the design needs it; assume a projector, dice, index cards, and a stack of poker chips, and no computing during play.

Here is the structure I have in mind, which you should use, repair, or replace if you can do better, but in every case you must give me something runnable with these numbers.

Assign each participant a parent and a child so that the room decomposes into lineages of six and everyone is simultaneously an heir and a testator. Run four generations. In each generation a participant receives an after-tax transfer from their parent, chooses how to invest it (a safe project with a certain return, a risky project whose return is resolved by dice, and — this matters — the risky option should be genuinely attractive on expected value while exposing the player to a real chance of a bad outcome), chooses how much to consume, and chooses how to pass the rest on. Consumption in one's own generation counts fully toward one's score; the consumption of one's heir counts half; the heir's heir a quarter; then an eighth. With lineages of six and four generations no one becomes their own ancestor.

The passing-on choice should have at least three channels, and this is the part I care most about. A transfer at death, which is visible to the facilitator as the record office and taxed at whatever rate is in force. A transfer during life, which is also visible but capped in size per generation and taxed at a lower rate. And a merger, by which two lineages pool their holdings under the control of one participant on negotiated terms, untaxed but irreversible and requiring the consent of both. Give me the exact numbers for each channel and tell me why each number is where it is.

The tax rate is set by majority vote, but with two twists. The rate a generation votes on takes effect one generation later, so that no one votes on the rate that applies to their own estate. And all revenue is recycled, at the start of the next generation, as an equal per-head stake paid to every participant in cash — not as services, not as vouchers, not tied to any use. I want the participants to discover on their own that a confiscatory rate redistributed equally still leaves dispersion at the end, and to discover why: returns are partly luck, thrift differs, and a guaranteed floor changes what people are willing to risk. If the design needs a superminority or a constitutional constraint on the vote to keep this from degenerating, say so and say which.

What participants should come away seeing, and I want you to hold the design to this: first, that equalising at the starting gate once and keeping the gate level every generation are not different degrees of the same policy but different policies, because lineages compound — under plausible parameters a one-shot levelling is largely undone within three generations, while continuous levelling quietly converts an inheritance tax into a permanent, repeatedly contested claim on how families conduct their private lives. Second, that the thing a bequest tax applies to is not a fixed unit given by nature: the tax changes which channel transfers flow through and how many households the wealth is held by, so that the base is endogenous to the instrument. Do not lecture on either point up front, and do not name them until the debrief.

Give me, in this order: the handout text the participants actually receive (I will photocopy it verbatim, so it must be self-contained and must not foreshadow the conclusion); the facilitator's sheet, with the parameter values you would actually use — returns, dispersion, the two tax rates, the cap, the stake size — and a note on which single parameter the effect is most sensitive to; a timeline in five-minute blocks including setup; and the debrief, which should map specific moments in play onto specific parts of the two ideas above, say what a participant is likely to say at each moment, and give me the response that turns that remark into the point without announcing it.

Then be honest about failure. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: the vote collapsing to zero or to confiscation in the first generation, the table developing a dominant dynasty and checking out, the two or three economics-trained students solving the game instead of playing it, players treating mergers as trivially costless, dice streaks that make the whole session look like a lesson about luck rather than about institutions. For each, give me the fix and the fallback if the fix fails. And tell me plainly which features of the two ideas the game cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in the debrief myself — I expect the answer to include at least the invented parameters being invented rather than measured, the fact that real bequest taxation sits on top of capital-income and trust and gift rules rather than beside them, and the difference between equal opportunity as a property of a person's prospects and as a property of the process that produced them.

Finally, mark in the debrief which of the claims it rests on are settled consensus among people who work on this and which are live disputes, including empirical ones, and flag any place where a parameter choice would smuggle a contested empirical claim into the room as though it were fact.
