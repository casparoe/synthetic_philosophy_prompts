I write fiction, and I am some way into a novel narrated in the first person by a woman in her seventies whose family are quietly building a case that she can no longer manage her own affairs; the reader is never told whether they are right. Before I commit to the voice I want to think it through against the story that seems to me to be about the same thing: Charlotte Perkins Gilman's The Yellow Wallpaper, in which a woman is confined to a room and prescribed rest, forbidden to write, whose physician husband is both the authority on her condition and the person she most needs to convince, and who ends by joining the figure she has come to see trapped in the paper.

Read that story through the lens of epistemic injustice — testimonial injustice, where a hearer grants a speaker less credibility than she deserves because of a prejudice about who she is, and hermeneutical injustice, where someone is harmed by not having the shared concepts with which to make sense of what is happening to her. Three things interest me, the third most.

One: here the biased hearer is also the sole interpreter of her condition, so he controls both the credibility judgement and the vocabulary in which she might make her case. Does the story show the two kinds of injustice to be separable at all, or does it depict a single mechanism, in which withholding the concepts is how the prejudice does its work?

Two: the figure behind the wallpaper. I read her not as a delusion but as an image the narrator recruits to say what she has no words for, an image doing the work of an absent concept. Is that fair, and does it change how we should judge her reliability?

Three: the story is told by that narrator, in secret, in a voice the reader is invited to doubt. There is a kind of credibility a first-person text asks of the reader before anyone inside it judges the speaker — call it diegetic credibility — and a novel can stage a credibility judgement without staging a hearer. Does the form here put the reader in the position of the credibility-deflating judge the story indicts? Is making her sanity ambiguous a way of making us feel the injustice, or does it repeat the injustice by inviting us to decide whether she is a credible witness about her own mind? And what follows for my narrator: can such uncertainty be written so that it is the injustice rather than a puzzle to be solved?

Tell me as plainly where the story resists this reading as where it fits — say so if the wallpaper is doing Gothic or religious work that the lens distorts.
