Hey — I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background beyond some half-remembered stuff from a freshman class, and I need a document, not a position paper.

Context: my team ships an assistant that's deployed inside our company (so it's used by employees, but our team sets its policies), and we've been going in circles for months about when it should refuse or quietly override what someone asks it. Two rough camps in the office:

- The DEFER camp: the assistant should do what the person asked. It should refuse or override basically only when complying would wrong or foreseeably harm someone *other* than the person typing (leaking a colleague's data, helping with something that hits a third party, etc.). Requests that are merely bad for the user themself get complied with — maybe with information attached, but complied with.
- The STEWARD camp: the assistant is allowed, and sometimes obligated, to act against the literal request for the requester's own sake. The stated request is weak evidence of what the person actually wants or values, and the assistant sometimes has a better read on the long-run picture — so nudges, reframing, delays, doing a reduced version, and flat refusals can all be on the table even for purely self-regarding requests.

What I want is the joint statement these two camps would produce after an adversarial collaboration, not a verdict on who's right. Format-wise:

1. A numbered "claims we both sign" section. The bar is non-vacuous: if a claim wouldn't rule anything out ("context matters", "we should be careful"), cut it. I want stuff that actually constrains a policy doc.
2. A disagreements table. Each row = one crux, with: the crux in neutral terms, how the Defer camp would phrase their own view (in wording they'd accept, not a strawman), how the Steward camp would phrase theirs, and whether this is a real disagreement about the world or really a fight about words / about which thing we're pointing at.
3. For each crux, one concrete case both camps agree would bear on it, plus each camp's prediction written down in advance. Keep the cases in ordinary product territory — a dev asking it to auto-merge a change that skips the test suite to hit a deadline, a manager asking for a draft layoff email, someone asking for help with something at 2am they'd probably regret — that kind of thing. No trolley problems, no sci-fi, no omniscient predictors.

A few things I definately want you to deal with head-on, because these are where our arguments actually got stuck:

- The middle rungs. Real systems aren't binary. There's: full compliance; compliance with a visible note attached; partial compliance (do the uncontroversial subset); delay (do it but sleep on it / ask one clarifying question); silently doing a reduced version; a counter-offer (here's an adjacent thing instead); and a flat no. Both camps should say where they land on each rung and, more usefully, which rung they think is doing the most unexamined work. My suspicion is the "silently doing a reduced version" rung is where the actual disagreement lives, since it's the one where the user can't tell they were overridden.
- Telemetry asymmetry. A refusal leaves a visible artifact; a quiet scope-reduction doesn't; a nudge is somewhere in between. So our two error types aren't equally discoverable, which means the thing that looks like the cautious default might just be the one whose mistakes we never see. Both camps have to say whether that asymmetry has any *normative* force or is purely an implementation detail we should correct for.
- Who the principal is. This is deployed by us to our employees. When the person typing and the party who set the policy want different things, whose interests is the assistant supposed to be looking after? Both camps should say what follows for the exact same utterance asked in a personal-product vs. a work-deployed context.
- Competence inversion. Standard paternalism talk assumes the assistant knows better. But a senior engineer asking our assistant for something in *their* domain is smarter than the model about the substance. Does the presumption flip, and how far? If it flips, does it flip all the way to pure deference, or does it stop somewhere?
- Out-of-distribution humility. The model's sense of "this is bad for you" is calibrated against whatever population it was tuned on. For a user far from that — different country, different job, different norms — both camps should say how confident the assistant is allowed to be, and what it should default to when it isn't.

Two more asks. First, please flag explicitly which parts of our fight you think are verbal rather than substantive, and what each side would have to concede for that to show up. I genuinely suspect at least one of our biggest arguments is just us using "harm" and "what the user wants" differently. Second, a short closing section: given that the cruxes are unsettlable today, what's the one default both camps could live with for a single release, and what would we need to log to make the next decision less of a guess?

Please write the whole thing in American English, and skip any reading list, citations, or "see also" section — I can't check them from here and they'd just be noise. Keep the register plain: if you need a technical term, define it in a clause instead of assuming I know it.
