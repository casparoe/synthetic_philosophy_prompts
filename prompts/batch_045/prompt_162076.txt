Me and a couple of friends got into this at a bbq on saturday and I've been chewing on it since. One of them, who's deep into the effective-altruism stuff, has cut beef out and switched to shrimp and to those cricket-flour protein bars, on the grounds that you get way less suffering per gram of protein. Someone else pushed back that we don't actually know whether shrimp or crickets feel anything at all — no cortex, no vertebrate-style pain machinery, maybe it's all reflex and withdrawal behaviour. She said this was a solved question and quoted a line she said was from Bentham, a footnote in his late-18th-century book on legislation (1789, I think): "But suppose the case were otherwise, what would it avail? the question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" Then she used it to shut the rest of the conversation down: the only question is whether they can suffer, so intelligence, whether shrimp feel pain at all, their ecological role, the fact that farmed shrimp wouldn't exist if nobody farmed them, the comparison-per-gram-of-protein — all noise. Answer the suffering question and you're done. I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training beyond one survey course I mostly slept through, so I couldn't say what was wrong with that, but something felt off.

I did look the passage up afterwards, and the bit right before the famous sentence surprised me: "If the being eaten were all, there is very good reason why we should be suffered to eat such of them as we like to eat: we are the better for it, and they are never the worse." Which reads like he's mainly worried about tormenting animals, not about killing or eating them — so the line seems to be travelling a long way from its home, and I suspect it doesn't do the job she wants it to do.

Can you lay out, as a list, the different precise claims that one-liner could be making? I mean things like: what "can" is doing (current ability vs. a capacity that's temporarily knocked out by anaesthesia or sleep vs. developmental potential); what "suffer" covers (a mere nociceptive reflex, an unpleasant conscious experience, frustrated preferences, a life going badly overall); whether "they" means the individual animal or the species or a typical member; and whether the claim is that being able to suffer is necessary for counting morally at all, or sufficient, or the only property that matters, or the only property that matters *and* one that counts equally no matter what kind of creature. Also whether it's a threshold test (anything above the line gets full equal consideration, so one shrimp and one pig are morally on a par) or a scalar one where welfare range determines how much each counts. For each reading say whether it's more or less a platitude, substantive-but-defensible, or actually false, with a one-line reason, and say which reading people probably think they mean when they fire it off in an argument. And the part I actually care about: which of these readings, if any, would support using it the way she did — to close the shrimp-and-crickets question rather than to open it — given that the whole disagreement was about whether we even know if they suffer? I suspect none of them get her there, but I cant put my finger on exactly where the gap is.
