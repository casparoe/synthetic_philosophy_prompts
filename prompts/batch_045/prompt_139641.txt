I'm the quantitative lead at a foundation making roughly thirty long-shot grants a year — global catastrophic risk mitigation, transformative research — where the payoffs come out of models with Pareto-like tails whose index is sometimes near or below 1, on the upside, the downside, or both. Our committee's practice is to ask for an expected value for each prospect and fund by that number; I've become convinced that in some cases the number is an artifact of how a simulation happened to sum its draws. I want a written procedure an analyst can run in about a day, without measure theory, and defend to lay trustees.

Take as given the shape of the problem: a gamble whose nth outcome has probability 2^-n and payoff ±2^n/n has an expected value equal to the alternating harmonic series — ln 2 in the natural order, anything you like in another — and undefined in the strict sense, since the positive and negative parts each diverge.

The procedure should first sort a prospect into defined, infinite-but-one-sided, or undefined, using tests an analyst can actually run (tail-index estimates; the diagnostic that a running mean does not settle down as the sample grows). It should then say what replaces the expected value in each case. Three distinctions matter to me especially: that repetition rescues a one-sided infinite expectation but does not rescue an undefined one, so "we'll do this many times over" is an escape in the first case and not the second; that two prospects sharing the same divergent tail can often be ranked by the expectation of their difference even when neither has an expectation of its own; and that any fallback valuation must assign a gamble and its mirror image opposite values, so the procedure cannot smuggle in an ordering of the terms. Where the procedure hands the decision to a person rather than a calculation, say so explicitly, and specify the sensitivity report that shows how far the answer moves across admissible truncations and orders.

Apply it to three cases: a grant whose only heavy tail is a small chance of very large benefit; an intervention whose model puts a tail index near 0.9 on both benefit and harm; and our committee's flagship proposal, which currently shows a large positive expected value that I suspect is order-dependent — include that one even though it will be the hardest to present. Close with a short statement of what the procedure cannot do.
