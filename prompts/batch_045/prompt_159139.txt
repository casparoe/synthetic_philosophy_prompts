At a recent department colloquium a visiting speaker was pressed on whether, when two empirically equivalent theories are in play, there is a fact of the matter about which one gets the world right. He replied with Rorty's line from Consequences of Pragmatism — The world does not speak. Only we do. — and took himself to have shown that question to be not merely unanswerable but empty. I doubt the inference works, and I want a precisification of that sentence to hand to my graduate seminar.

Lay out, as a menu, the distinct claims it might be making: the platitude that only language users assert anything; the thesis that the world proposes no vocabulary to us, so nothing nonhuman selects among descriptions; the thesis that justification is addressed to audiences rather than to a nonhuman tribunal; the stronger antirepresentational claim that there is no way things are apart from some description, with true functioning as a compliment we pay to sentences that pay their way; and, at the far end, readings I suspect Rorty himself repudiates — the denial that the world constrains belief at all — together with whatever reflexive difficulty the line has in stating how things are. Mark which readings are trivial, which substantive and defensible, and which false.

Then commit. Name the single reading the speaker must have been invoking, say whether that reading is defensible, and say whether any reading on your list genuinely licenses his move — dissolving the question rather than merely reclassifying it as one we cannot settle. Note that in the case as he posed it the theories are not simply empirically equivalent for now: one of them is such that no human inquirer could ever come to accept it, so no appeal to the verdicts of future inquiry can break the tie. Give me your verdicts with reasons, and flag where you are guessing about his intentions and where your own judgments are contestable.
