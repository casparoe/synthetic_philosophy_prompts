QUESTION 4 (25 marks)

(a) State the contractualist formula as Scanlon states it, and explain in what sense the aggregation of benefits and burdens across different people is a problem for it. (6 marks)

(b) Case I ("Ada's Forty Days"). One dose of an analgesic remains. In the north ward Ada will, without it, suffer severe pain continuously for forty days. In the south ward forty patients will each suffer severe pain for one day. Assume that pain is the only relevant consideration, that every day of pain is of identical intensity for everyone, that the patients are otherwise relevantly similar, and that no later compensation is possible. What, on the contractualist view, may be done, and why? What does the answer show about how the view treats numbers? (5 marks)

(c) Case II ("Equal Stakes"). One dose remains. Dara will suffer moderate pain for two days. Each of the thirty Esmes will suffer moderate pain for two days. Can the contractualist reach a verdict here, and if so by what route? (4 marks)

(d) Case III ("The Near-Tie"). One dose remains. Faye will suffer pain for thirty hours. Each of the thirty Gils will suffer pain for twenty-nine hours. Does the contractualist have a determinate answer? If the tie-breaking move from part (c) is available here, what are its limits? (5 marks)

(e) "A contractualist fixes the strength of a person's claim by asking how much that person has at stake; in Case I that requires summing Ada's forty separate days of pain into one claim. But if summing is admissible within a life, there is no principled ground for refusing to sum across lives." Assess this parity argument and the strongest contractualist reply to it. (5 marks)

CANDIDATE'S RESPONSE

(a) Contractualism holds that an act is wrong just in case some individual could reasonably reject it. Wrongness is therefore not a matter of producing the best overall outcome, but of being justifiable to each person the act affects; the formula tells us what wrongness consists in, not how to go about deciding what to do. Two features of this are worth stressing. First, rejectability is a matter of the reasons there are, not of what anyone would actually say: an individual's objecting does not make an act wrong, and an individual's consenting does not make it permissible, since the question is whether the rejection would be reasonable. Second, because justification must run to each person separately, the view appears to have no way of adding up benefits and burdens that fall on different people. However much a great number of people stand to gain a small benefit, the sum of their gains seems unable to count against one person's larger burden, since no individual loses more than a little. This is the aggregation problem, and it is the point on which contractualism is most often attacked.

(b) In Case I the dose should go to Ada. What is at stake for Ada is forty days of severe pain; what is at stake for each patient in the south ward is one day. Comparing the claims pairwise, Ada's is the stronger claim, and no one in the south ward can complain that her claim has been given less than its due relative to theirs. Notice that the strength of a claim is determined by how much the person has at stake under the alternatives, not by how badly off she ends up in absolute terms. The case therefore illustrates the standard complaint against the view: it counts the number of days but not the number of people, and in a case where the many together lose as much as the one, that looks hard to accept.

(c) In Case II the contractualist can say that the dose should go to the thirty Esmes, and the route is the familiar tie-breaking argument. Dara's claim is not stronger than any individual Esme's claim: each has two days of moderate pain at stake. Since no one's claim is stronger, the fact that thirty people would gain the benefit and only one would lose it is not itself a reason anyone could reasonably reject, and the numbers may break the tie. So the contractualist is not, as is sometimes said, committed to holding that numbers never matter. They matter where there is nothing to choose between the competing individual claims.

(d) In Case III the dose should go to the Gils. The tie-breaking argument generalises: what licensed the numbers in Case II was not the exact equality of the claims but the fact that no one had a substantially stronger claim, and here Faye's advantage over any individual Gil is marginal. The principle has to be that numbers count whenever no individual claim is stronger by a significant margin; to insist on exact equality would make the tie-breaker apply to almost nothing, since real cases rarely exhibit it. And once the tie-breaker is admitted, no principled stopping point short of aggregation is available: thirty claims of twenty-nine hours come to eight hundred and seventy hours against Faye's thirty, and the contractualist who allows the numbers to tip a case at equality has no ground for refusing to let them tip a case where the margin is small. The view therefore collapses into utilitarianism wherever numbers are large, and its apparent resistance to aggregation is only apparent.

(e) The parity argument rests on a genuine difficulty. Contractualism needs intrapersonal summation: unless Ada's forty days can be collected into a single claim about how much she has at stake, the view cannot get off the ground in Case I, and the strength of one person's claim would be fixed by the worst single thing that happens to her. But the contractualist reply is that the two summations play entirely different roles: within a life the sum fixes the strength of one person's claim, whereas across lives there would have to be some standpoint from which the many losses compose into one, and there is no such standpoint. This reply does no work. If the sum of Ada's days is a genuine quantity for contractualist purposes, it is arbitrary to treat the same quantity as unreal when it is spread across forty lives, and the candidate should concede that the distinction between the intrapersonal and the interpersonal case is unmotivated.
