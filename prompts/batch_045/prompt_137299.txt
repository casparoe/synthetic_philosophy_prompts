I have no formal background in philosophy — I taught myself to program and now spend my days working on data pipelines — but for about a year six of us (four programmers, a biologist, an actuary) have met monthly to take one topic at a time and argue it out from scratch. Next month's topic is whether the patterns we find in the world are real, or only artifacts of the way we carve the world up: the physical, design, and intentional stances, and the criterion one of our group brought along, that a pattern is real just when it lets you describe the data about a system more briefly than the data itself. Our arguments keep going in circles: we agree on nearly every concrete case and then discover we were meaning opposite things by "real." So I want to hand out a decision tree rather than an essay, on the theory that if each of us walks it privately we will at least find out where we come apart.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of famous positions: first the questions and the answer-combinations they allow, then the positions located in that space.

Requirements:

- One root question, one branch at a time, eight branch nodes in total, each phrased as a concrete case or claim with two or three answers a reader can pick between without knowing which position it leads to. No node should ask anyone to self-identify with a label.
- The top-level split should be about what makes a pattern real at all (sheer compressibility? support for prediction? backing by causal structure? usefulness to some agent? something interest-relative?), and one whole region of the tree should be about which of the three stances yields patterns that count.
- These five cases must all appear as branch nodes: (i) a closed archive — a thousand years of readings from a station that has now been destroyed, in which a search program finds a description 30% shorter than the raw record, though nothing follows from it about anything, because there will never be more data; (ii) the same data given to two teams using very different coding schemes, where one scheme makes a pattern leap out and the other cannot find it at all; (iii) a price pattern that held for years, was worth a fortune, and stopped the instant anyone began trading on it — does a pattern that dissolves when acted on count as less real than one that survives being used?; (iv) a device built to hold a quantity steady that now sticks and overshoots, where both the "what it is supposed to do" description and the "what it now does" description compress its behaviour well; (v) a system nobody designed for its current performance — an organism shaped by selection, or a program that found strategies its own authors never anticipated.
- You may use the remaining three nodes for whatever distinctions you think matter most; if you have room, I would like one on whether "compressible in principle" differs from "findable and exploitable by any agent who could exist," and one on patterns that are only visible at a certain scale.
- Seven to nine leaves. Each leaf gets the name the position usually goes by, or "unoccupied" if you think nobody holds it, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. The leaves must between them cover: physical-stance-only realism; the compressibility criterion; the view that a pattern must earn its keep in prediction or intervention; and at least one stance-relative or interest-relative view.
- Mark at least two answer-combinations that the questions permit but that nobody seems to occupy, and say in one sentence each whether that is because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
- Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot actually separate, and say whether you think that difference is a real disagreement or only a verbal one. Two of our group suspect the whole topic collapses into a verbal dispute once you say what the pattern is supposed to do for you; if that is right, say so and say at which branch the collapse happens.
- Please don't cite anything, don't attribute views to individual authors, and don't use any names except the standard labels of positions.

Format: put the root question as a single sentence on its own line at the top, then the tree as nested numbered questions, then two short sections headed "Unoccupied combinations" and "Pairs this tree cannot separate." One sentence of prose per leaf, and nothing over 900 words in total.
