So I'm the junior person on a small working group that writes classification reports on atrocities — basically we decide whether a campaign gets the g-word or gets something lesser (crimes against humanity, mass displacement, cultural destruction). We work off the Convention definition, including the fact that its protected-groups list stops at national, ethnical, racial and religious and we've allways refused to stretch it to political groups. Worth knowing too: Lemkin's own broader idea was built to cover killing a group's language, institutions and economic base, not just its members, and our precedent 4 is basically us deciding not to go that far.

Our track record, shortened:

- Vardinia, 2003: army units kill most of the men and boys of an ethnoreligious minority across four towns; intercepted orders say no male of them is to remain. We called it genocide.
- The Kalsten relocations, 1998: the rural population of a minority is deported wholesale from a border province, around 4,000 die of exposure and disease (the cabinet had been warned this would happen), their language schools are shut, and the group goes on existing in camps and in the cities. We did not call it genocide; the writeup says the group was moved, not destroyed.
- Operation Greenfield, 2011: a herding group's wells are poisoned and their herds destroyed, roughly 2,000 dead, survivors end up as urban dependents, and an internal memo says their way of life has to end so the land can be settled. Genocide.
- The Marrow schools, 1970s–90s: minority children taken into majority-language boarding schools, the language banned in public life, ~600 dead of neglect, and the group today still has 200,000 self-identifying members. Not genocide: we called it cultural destruction and said the g-word there would be a category error for us.
- The Pelletier decrees, 2016: forced sterilisation of the group's women, children separated from families, under a hundred dead. Genocide.
- Sula, 2008: the group's entire population in one province — about 30,000 out of a 2-million-strong group — systematically killed. Genocide. Our note says we don't require the destroyed part to be some share of the whole group; destroying the group where it lives is destroying it in part.

New case, and it's tearing us apart. A state facing an insurgency in a province inhabited almost entirely by a distinct ethnoreligious group (everyone agrees it's a protected group; it's 400,000 people out of 6 million worldwide). Nobody is deliberately killed. Instead: the group's land is reclassified as state land under a zoning law and they're evicted; a biometrics re-registration requires in-person appointments at offices outside the province, and anyone who can't comply loses rations, benefits and school enrolment; the group's language-medium schools are merged into majority-language ones. Excess deaths from all this are around 9,000. The cabinet was briefed by its own demographers that the group would definately cease to exist as a distinct group in that province within a generation and that the displaced would be absorbed. The ministers' stated purpose is administrative integration and counterinsurgency; the internal memos say the distinctness of the group's identity is the soil the insurgency grows in, and that within a generation there will be no separate group here. Also politically relevant: the group's own representatives are split, half want the label and half think using it for a case with no massacres cheapens it.

Do you think we'll call it genocide? I care less about the verdict than about which rule we've been implicitly running — something like purpose to destroy the group physically or biologically, versus purpose to end the group's continued existence as the group it is by whatever means, versus something knowledge-based where foreseeable destruction is enough. Which of those are actually consistent with all six precedents above, where do they split on this case, and which precedent is the closest analog — I keep thinking it's Sula, but Sula had everyone dead. And what's the one further case you'd put in front of us that would tell the rules apart? Last thing: one member has started calling this paperwork destruction — campaigns that end a group entirely through administrative acts with no killings at all — and wants it as a named sub-type. If we adopt that, what are we committing to, and does it make our no in precedents 4 and 2 harder to keep?
