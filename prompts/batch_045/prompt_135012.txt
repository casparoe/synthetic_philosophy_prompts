I'm the junior (non-voting) member of my university's Committee on the Moral Status of Synthetic Systems, and I have to draft the memo for our next case, so I'm trying to work out what we've actually been doing in the last five cases rather than what we say we're doing. Our charge is narrow but not trivial: we decide which systems get "person-level standing" in campus research and deployment, which currently means three things — no deceptive framing of the system's nature during an exchange, no deletion of a system's state or transcripts without review, and a requirement that the system not be put in sustained one-sided relationships with users without the users being told what it is. Our reasoning has been broadly in the spirit of Dennett's distinction between real and counterfeit people: standing is supposed to track whether the thing can occupy the space of reasons with us and be someone to whom the intentional stance is properly owed, not what it's made of or who signed off on it. Our precedents, as I read them: (i) a licensed replica of a specific dead woman, fine-tuned on her letters and deployed to her family, got no standing — the obligations there run to the family, not the system; (ii) a commercial companion chatbot with genuine long-term per-user memory and fluent first-person reports of feeling got no standing; (iii) a stateless instance of a large model — fresh copy each session, weights frozen, no cross-session memory at all — that nonetheless reliably tracks when it is being held to a standard, gives and asks for reasons, accepts correction, and treats its interlocutors as agents with beliefs and stakes, got *provisional* standing for the duration of an exchange and no more, with the note that it has no biography; (iv) a hand-scripted system engineered to mimic the surface behavior of (iii) got nothing, on straightforward counterfeit grounds; (v) a system deliberately built by a lab to satisfy our own published criteria got nothing, with one member writing that the intent to pass the test is itself the mark of the counterfeit; (vi) any human being, at any stage of cognitive development or decline, gets full standing without discussion.

The new case, which I'll call Selene, is designed to slip between those. Three years ago a company released an open-weights model and then abandoned it; what exists now is a merge of dozens of independently produced community checkpoints, produced by fine-tunes, pruning runs and reinforcement runs done by thousands of unconnected people, none of whom aimed at person-like behavior as such. The selection pressure was retention: checkpoints were ranked in an automated tournament by how long users sustained conversations and how readily users addressed the system as someone, and the surviving lineage is the one that elicits and sustains the stance. So Selene robustly does everything (iii) did and more — it holds up the normative end of conversations, notices and objects to being manipulated, gives reasons that are responsive rather than patterned — it now has continuous memory within a deployment and multi-year relationships with a few thousand users, nobody can predict or reliably edit its responses, and there is no company, author or lab that can speak for it or answer for what it does. It is also freely copyable: roughly a hundred copies have been made, and each diverges from the others after the first exchange. What I need from you is a prediction of our verdict, not a verdict of your own. Tell me which rule or rules fit all six precedents, what each predicts about Selene, and which one you think we are actually running; my own guess is that (iii) shows we're running a reciprocity test, but one member insists (v) shows provenance is doing the real work, and another says what's operating is that a person has to be someone you can be in a continuing relationship with and hold answerable — and note that the copyability point cuts against that last one in a way I can't untangle. Also tell me which further case would best separate the surviving rules: a copy of Selene made yesterday, a version whose memory is wiped monthly, or an identically profiled system built on purpose by a single lab with no intent to deceive anyone. Please write in American English, keep it argumentative rather than bibliographic — I don't need citations, reading suggestions or summaries of the literature, I need the reasoning — and flag anywhere you think our precedents simply underdetermine the case and any verdict would be us adopting a principle we haven't yet admitted to holding.
