I occasionally sit in on a value-alignment reading group in a neighbouring lab — I work on something unrelated and have no stake in how it comes out. Last week the group spent most of an hour on a single exchange and nobody moved. A visiting speaker had argued that you cannot read off what a system should do from records of what humans did, chose, or approved of, and two members went at it afterwards. Roughly, with a value function meaning a ranking over outcomes that the system is meant to pursue:

(1) Marta: The whole enterprise is a textbook naturalistic fallacy. An argument with only descriptive premises and a normative conclusion is invalid; you can always ask "granted every fact about human behaviour, but should the system do that?" and the question is perfectly intelligible. So value learning needs a normative premise from somewhere. If that premise is quietly supplied by the designers' own moral opinions, then the method isn't deriving human values at all — it's laundering the programmers' judgement and calling it data.

(2) Devin: That's a point about deductive entailment, and nobody in value learning claims entailment. What we're doing is abduction: inference to the hypothesis that best explains the evidence. The gap shows that an "ought" isn't already contained in an "is"; it doesn't show that an "is" can't be evidence for an "ought", any more than the fact that data don't entail a theory shows that data can't support one. Charging us with the fallacy means charging us with claiming a demonstrative certainty we never claimed.

(3) Marta: Abduction is where the problem bites, not where it dissolves. To rank hypotheses you need a prior, and here the prior isn't innocent. For any finite behavioural record there are arbitrarily many value functions that fit it exactly and then diverge, and they diverge on exactly the cases that matter — whose life to weigh how heavily, what to trade off against what. Choosing among them requires a judgement about which errors are worse, and that is a first-order moral judgement wearing a methodological hat. The data can't break the tie; something normative is doing the real work.

(4) Devin: Then the objection has nothing to do with the is–ought gap. Underdetermination by finite data is universal — true of electrons too — and we handle it everywhere with priors, simplicity and error-weighting, without anyone announcing that physics is committing a fallacy. Here is the only bridge I actually want, named so we stop gesturing: call it the ratification constraint. A candidate value function V is admissible for a population P only if P, knowing everything in the dataset and reasoning without error, would still ratify V. The descriptive labour is in finding the fixed point. The only normative premise I need is that one should act on the values that survive full-information reflection, and nobody in that room rejects it. So: either your objection is the entailment point, which is true and irrelevant, or it's the underdetermination point, which is relevant and not special to values. Pick one.

(5) Marta: False dilemma, and the constructive part fails twice over. First, the fixed point need not exist and need not be unique: reflection can cycle, or settle into several stable points that disagree with each other, so the constraint doesn't select a value function without a further rule for choosing among fixed points — and that rule will be normative in the same way. Second, the premise you call uncontroversial is substantive. Plenty of people deny it: someone who thinks the correct values are whatever their tradition or revelation hands down, and that reflection is a mechanism of corruption, rejects it outright. Local agreement doesn't make a premise descriptive; it makes it an unargued normative commitment — which is just the structure Moore was pointing at with the open question.

(6) Devin: Grant both. All you've shown is that value learning needs a tie-breaker among fixed points and at least one substantive normative premise that everyone here accepts. That is not what you started with. You began with "no normative conclusion follows from descriptive premises"; you've ended with "no normative conclusion follows without any normative premise", which is true of every argument ever given, including yours. If a shared premise is illegitimate footing, then nothing is ever supported by anything and the gap objection proves far too much to be aimed at us.

(7) Marta: The "proves too much" move conflates premises that are normative in form with premises that are contested in content. What makes the gap interesting here isn't that some premise is normative — every argument has those — it's the division of labour. The credibility of the method is advertised as coming from the data, while its content comes from the premise; the data are what make it look like science, and the premise is what makes it say anything. That is the real mistake: not invalidity, but a bait-and-switch about where the content comes from.

(8) Devin: Then say that, and stop invoking Hume. If the objection is that value learning misrepresents its own sources, that's an objection to a field's rhetoric, and it's answered by writing the premise on the table in plain sight — after which we can argue about it directly, which is all I ever wanted.
