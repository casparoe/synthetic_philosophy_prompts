I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days, and for about as long I've kept a private glossary of problem-solving moves — not arguments or doctrines, but the things you do when a problem or a dispute stops moving: tabooing a word, switching from the inside to the outside view, going looking for the crux. There is a gap in the glossary, and I would like your help filling it.

Please answer as a professional philosopher who works on formal systems and also thinks about heuristics: take the time to brainstorm a wide field of candidates before you commit, and be willing to tell me that no established term exists. I would rather see a list of candidates you considered and discarded than a confident one-phrase answer. What I want is the carving, not the label: what exactly this thing is, which nearby concepts it is not, and where its edges are. Please don't cite papers or hand me a reading list — I'm somewhere without library access, and I want the concept, not the references.

Two cases I am fairly confident are instances of the same thing.

The first is a puzzle I first met years ago in Hofstadter's big book about recursion and fugues, which I don't have to hand. You start with the string MI and may apply four rules: if a string ends in I you may append a U; you may double everything after the M; you may replace III with U; you may delete UU. The question is whether you can ever arrive at MU. I spent an evening generating strings and got nowhere, and the resolution is not a longer derivation but a reason no derivation exists: count the I's, note that the count never becomes divisible by three, and note that MU has zero I's, which is divisible by three.

The second is the board with two diagonally opposite corner squares removed and thirty-one dominoes, each covering two adjacent squares. The tiling is impossible, and the reason requires you to colour the squares black and white in the ordinary way — something the problem never mentions — and notice that every domino covers one square of each colour while the mutilated board has thirty-two of one and thirty of the other.

What the cases share, as I see it: the question is whether repeated application of the given rules can reach a given target; the true answer is no; and the explanation cites a feature that no rule mentions, that is nowhere in the statement of the problem, that every legal move preserves, and that distinguishes the starting position from the target. You have to import the feature from outside — arithmetic mod three in one case, a colouring in the other. And inside the search there is no signal of failure: nothing gets measurably closer to the target, no dead end looks worse than any other, so no amount of diligence with the rules themselves would have helped, while once the feature is in view the whole matter closes in a line.

Some things I want your judgement on rather than your agreement with. First, whether a third case is really the same: a dispute that cycles indefinitely because every move available to either side preserves some unstated feature of the framing, so that the deadlock dissolves only when someone names what is being held fixed. Second, whether the imported feature being arbitrary matters — there are infinitely many colourings of a board and infinitely many functions of a string, nearly all useless, so if this is a technique it comes with no recipe for finding the feature, and perhaps it names an achievement rather than a method. Third, whether the jump is really out, or only sideways: the mod-three argument uses arithmetic, which is itself a formal system with its own unreachable truths, so maybe every such escape is relocation into a larger system and the thing I am after is really relative impossibility, which may already have a name. I suspect that what Hofstadter calls jumping out of the system is the nearest thing I know of, but it looks too broad to me: it covers self-reference and cases where you step outside in order to change the rules, it comes in towers that can be climbed again and again, and my cases feel terminal — once the feature is in view, there is nothing left to do.

Give me your answer as exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, with no preamble and no summing-up at the end: (1) whether an established term exists; (2) the nearest established concept and precisely where the fit fails; (3) a definition with rulings on the three complications above; (4) at least two candidates you rejected and the exact reason each fails; (5) if there is no established term, a coinage with a one-sentence definition and one case that would show the definition is wrong.
