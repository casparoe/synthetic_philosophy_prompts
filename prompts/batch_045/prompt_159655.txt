My brother-in-law is one of those relentlessly sensible index-fund people, and every time I mention the little football-trading model I run on the side — public data, tiny stakes, nothing clever — he shuts me down with "there are no fifty-quid notes lying on the pavement." Then he uses the same line for about three different jobs in one conversation, which is what makes me think its doing more work than it can bear.

As far as I can tell he means: (a) nothing on offer is worth more than it costs once you count the effort and the charges, so I'd be wasting my time; (b) anything that looks like an opportunity to me is really evidence that I'm the one about to be taken — the old poker thing about not being able to spot the mark at the table; and (c) markets get things right, so the price is the best estimate anyone has of how likely something actually is.

Can you lay out, as a list I can bring to Christmas, the different precise claims hiding in that one sentence, from the boring platitude up to the genuinely radical? For each one: is it plausible, is it even coherent, and which of his three moves does it actually support? I'm fairly sure (c) is the one that sounds strongest and is weakest in fact, and that (a) and (b) are pulling in opposite directions — one says the street is empty, the other says it's dangerous — but I can't get them cleanly apart myself.

Two things he said that I keep chewing on. First, his actual argument: if everyone is trading voluntarily, it can only ever be because they disagree about something; if we all started from the same information and reasoned perfectly we'd never disagree; so really there shouldn't be any trade at all. Someone told me that's more or less a "no-trade theorem" and I half remeber it being a real result about common priors or something, but obviously people trade constantly, and what I want to know is what that conclusion would actually establish if it were right — is it a claim about how much money is left lying around, or about why anyone bothers trading in the first place? Because we plainly do trade for reasons other than thinking we know better: insurance, rebalancing, needing the cash. Does that rescue it or just change the subject?

Second, and this is the bit I actually like: if the pavement were genuinely clean, nobody would bother sweeping it, so who does the sweeping? That feels like it should sink some reading or other, but I can't tell which, or whether it only shows the strong version is self-defeating while the boring one survives untouched.

Don't give me paper titles or economists' names, I can't look any of it up from where I'm sitting — just the claims and a verdict on each.
