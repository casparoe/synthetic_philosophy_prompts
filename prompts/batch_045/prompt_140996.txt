I've got no academic background at all — I do scheduling for a plumbing company, and I've been chewing on this stuff on my own for about four years, mostly by arguing with people online and going to city meetings. Somehow I've been asked to give the opening talk (25 minutes plus half an hour of questions) at the next meeting of our city's Public Safety Working Group, and I'd like to not get destroyed.

The room will be: a recently retired patrol sergeant, 23 years on, thinks of himself as a reformer, talks about recruiting and officer safety; a public defender; a street outreach / harm reduction worker who does crisis response and is basically sympathetic to abolition; two sociology PhD students, one quantitative who'll want evidence and one ethnographic who'll want to talk about how power actually operates on the ground; an anarchist-leaning mutual aid organizer; an ER nurse; a city budget analyst who thinks in headcount, contracts and pension liabilities; and a tenants' rights organizer with small kids who has called 911 on her ex more than once.

Here's roughly what I want to argue, so you know what to aim objections at. I think the reform / defund / abolish argument is aimed at the wrong object. All three are about the responder: reform wants better responders, defund wants fewer of them, abolish wants none. None of them is about the caller. What actually sets a coercive encounter in motion is the summons — any person, at no cost, with no review, no record kept against them and no consequence, can send armed strangers to another person, and then we treat the decision as if it had been made by the officer who shows up. My claim is that the police are best understood as a machine for converting a phone call into a violent encounter, and that the person placing the call is the least accountable official in the entire system. So I want to say: don't regulate the responder, regulate the summons.

Two more bits I'm planning to lean on. First, what I call the 3am test: any proposal about what replaces the police should be judged by what happens at 3am when someone's neighbour is screaming, not by how a well-staffed pilot performs in a nice neighbourhood on a Tuesday afternoon — because I think a lot of proposals quietly assume a world of daylight, staffing and cooperative subjects. Second, unbundle the job: there's "go", there's "stay", and there's "make him stop", and only the third is really the thing people mean when they talk about the state monopolising violence. Compulsion should need authorization from someone who isn't at the scene, before it happens, on something like warrant logic but fast. I already know the obvious objection — emergencies don't wait around — and I'm not at all sure my answer to it is any good.

What I want from you is a prep sheet: eight objections, ordered by how much damage they'd do me weighted by how likely they are, each one phrased the way that particular person would actually say it rather than in neutral philosophy-speak. For each: what's really load-bearing in the objection, about 90 seconds of reply I could say out loud, and where that reply is soft. Plus what I should concede up front in the talk itself before anyone has to ask, the one reply that has to land for the whole thing to survive, and the question I'm hoping nobody asks.

Please don't give me reading lists, or tell me what the literature says, or hand me statistics to cite. I can't get at any of that, I've no way to check it, and I couldn't repeat it from the floor anyway — arguments only, things I can say in a room.

And if the whole "aim at the caller" idea is confused, or there's an obvious reason it collapses, say that plainly instead of politely building me a prep sheet around it. For each reply, tell me how much of it to actually lean on — which ones you'd bet on and which I should half-hedge out loud — and flag any reply you think is just wrong.
