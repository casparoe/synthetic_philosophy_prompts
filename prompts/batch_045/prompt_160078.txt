I have no academic background in philosophy or law, but I have been reading about drug policy in my own time, and I have got into an argument I cannot settle with my brother-in-law, who says the whole thing is sheer hypocrisy. I suspect something more principled is going on underneath, but I cannot put my finger on it, so I would like the implicit theory spelled out. The practice I keep circling round is what you might call the split package: decriminalise the user, criminalise the supplier. Take the Portuguese model, in force since July 2001: possession and use of any drug stopped being a criminal offence without becoming legal — the conduct remains unlawful, the police still confiscate the substance, and the person is referred to a Commission for the Dissuasion of Drug Addiction, which can impose administrative sanctions; the ceiling for this softer treatment is roughly a ten-day supply of the substance in question, and everything above that threshold, like any supply or trafficking, remains a serious crime. Meanwhile the same state operates nationwide syringe exchange and substitution therapy for the very people it is still formally proscribing.

Three features of this puzzle me, and I would like each of them reconstructed rather than merely described. First, the acquisition gap: everything the state now tolerates — possessing and consuming the drug — can only be done by means of an act the state continues to treat as a grave crime, namely being supplied; and the dangers of that criminal market (unknown purity, violence, adulterants) are then routinely cited as reasons for keeping supply criminal. Does the practice therefore presuppose something circular, or can it coherently hold that the harm of a drug is partly a function of the legal regime surrounding it? More generally, what theory of the wrong of supplying does the split package presuppose — that supplying is wrong because it draws other people into use, because it is commerce in a harmful good as opposed to a gift, because unlicensed provision is dangerous in a way licensed provision would not be, or simply because suppliers are the only practical enforcement target? Which of these does the practice strictly require, and which does it merely sit well with? Second, the threshold itself: the line between "user" and "trafficker" is not a fixed quantity but a ratio indexed to the possessor's own habit, which means that a heavier user may lawfully carry more than a light one — the law treats your appetite as a kind of licence rather than as an aggravating factor. What does that presuppose about what the threshold is really tracking (intent to supply, risk of leakage into other people's hands, or something else), and would a theory that made the gravity of possession increase with the possessor's own dependence fit the practice equally well? I have been toying with a distinction of my own between prohibiting a conduct and prohibiting the channels by which one reaches it, and I would like to know whether that distinction actually does any work here. Third, the dissuasion element: the state declares drug use a health matter and then uses its coercive apparatus — referral, possible fines or community service, confiscation — to press people towards treatment. Is "compulsory help" a coherent position, or is it paternalism minus the criminal record, and what conception of the person's own good and of the state's relation to it does it presuppose?

For contrast, consider the legalisation end of the spectrum, say Canada's Cannabis Act, in force since October 2018: the state licenses and taxes retail sale, permits possession and home cultivation up to a small number of plants, requires factual labelling such as THC content, and at the same time forbids promotion that appeals to young people, celebrity endorsement and event sponsorship, and restricts where any promotion may be seen at all. As I understand it, the state there takes revenue from a product whose seller it will not let sing the product's praises. What view of the consumer, of choice, and of craving does that combination presuppose — is the buyer being treated as an agent who might be tempted, as someone whose future self is at risk, or as a citizen to be protected from a market the state has deliberately created? Does the Canadian practice presuppose the same theory as the Portuguese one, or a different and possibly incompatible one? Please tell me where each practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories would license it, and what would concretely have to change in either policy if we replaced the presupposed view — for instance, if supply were reconceived as a licensing offence of the kind involved in selling alcohol without a licence, or if possession were treated as fully lawful, or if the advertising rules simply matched those for alcohol.
