For some time now I have been planning a trilogy of novels about what my late editor called, rather too cheerfully, the uses of defeat; my own working title is The Conscription of the Dead. Each book takes up a national myth built around a catastrophic loss. The first is set in a small town in Georgia and turns on a Confederate monument that, as I understand the matter, was erected nearer to the First World War than to the war it commemorates, and became contested again in the last decade. The second follows three generations of an Israeli family for whom Masada is at once a tourist site, an archaeological problem, and a standing family quarrel — the excavations directed by Yigael Yadin in the early 1960s, and the continuing argument about whether anything on the mountain corroborates Josephus's account of a collective suicide, sit at the center of it. The third moves between 1940 and the present and treats Dunkirk — the evacuation of roughly a third of a million men from those beaches in the early summer of 1940 — as a defeat that a national culture has managed to commemorate in the emotional grammar of a triumph. I have set aside a fourth book on the Kosovo cycle, the 1389 battle and the epic and oratorical traditions that grew around it, because I read neither Serbian nor Albanian and do not want to build a novel entirely out of translations.

What I want help with is not the history but the conceptual scaffolding. Over two years of notebooks I have invented four distinctions that I keep trying to make the trilogy carry, and I no longer trust my own sense of which of them is load-bearing. First, the conscription of the dead: the claim that a myth of a lost cause does not merely honor the dead but enrolls them in a present political project, and that much of the peculiar authority of such a myth comes from this — the dead cannot consent, and cannot withdraw their consent. Second, the second defeat: the loss a community suffers when the myth itself becomes unbelievable, so that what is mourned is not the original catastrophe but the disappearance of the story about it. Third, rescue myths: the claim that Dunkirk is not an exception to the pattern but a structural variant of it, a defeat commemorated as a deliverance, and that this inversion is precisely what makes it durable where a candid defeat would be merely humiliating. Fourth, mourning rights: the suggestion that membership in a national community is partly constituted by whom one is licensed to mourn in public and whom one must not, which would explain why contesting a monument is so often experienced, by those attached to it, as a kind of expulsion.

Here, then, is my request. I have roughly eighteen months of reading and two short research trips before I begin drafting in earnest; I have no archival training, I read no Hebrew and no Serbian, and I live four hours from the nearest research library — so please do not give me a bibliography or a reading list, since I could not use one. Rank these four ideas for me, and add one or two you think I am missing, on the following criteria: which does the most work across all three books rather than only one; which is most tractable for a writer working from translations and secondary literature; which is likely to collapse into something already familiar under another name (I worry most about the first); and which, if I organized the trilogy around it, would most likely tip the books into allegory, which is my chronic failure mode. I would also like to know what I might encounter over the next eighteen months that would move an item up or down your list, which questions about lost causes I am failing to ask, and which of the things commonly said about such myths I should simply stop repeating — I keep being told that better history dissolves the myth, and I have come to suspect that is close to backwards. Please defend the ordering rather than merely stating it: I can write my way out of a ranking I disagree with, but not out of an unexplained one. Please answer in American English.
