I write novels, which I assume is why the dean's office put me on our teaching hospital's Emerging Interventions Review Panel as its lay member, and why I have been handed the file on the Neural Systems Group's proposal for 'order-randomized gradual substitution.' I am not neutral on the underlying question. The narrator of the book I am finishing undergoes a slow transformation over several years, insists throughout that she is the same person, and is progressively disbelieved by everyone who loves her; I have spent two years unable to decide whether she is right. So I have read this protocol with an interest I would not call professional.

The proposal is this. Six patients with a fatal, currently untreatable prion disease would, over roughly twenty months, have their cortical neurons replaced in small batches by neuromorphic units tuned to the input-output behaviour of the cells they displace, within a stated tolerance, with a two-week calibration pause at each ten-percent milestone. The team is admirably candid about two things. First, they do not know whether the *order* of replacement matters to what emerges, so they have randomized it across participants — which means the person at fifty percent is a different person depending on which trajectory was used, and the end states, by their own modelling, will diverge slightly from one another and from anything we could have predicted at enrolment. Second, they have built what they call a 'continuity clause' into the consent form, on the model of a Ulysses contract: participants re-affirm consent at every checkpoint, but a refusal to continue is to be presumed an artefact of the replacement process and overridden, unless an independent assessor finds that the participant's values have changed in a way she can articulate and endorse on reflection. The panel has split three ways: approve, because gradualness is precisely what is supposed to make this survival rather than death, and the clause only protects the patient from a transient state she would later repudiate; reject, because needing a clause that overrides refusal concedes that the person who refused may be the only person there is; or approve while striking the clause, which leaves open the possibility of a participant halting at sixty percent with a partly synthetic brain and no way back. One more datum: under an earlier version of the protocol, a participant asked to stop at seventy percent, paused for two weeks, was treated for depression, resumed, and finished, and now says that being stopped would have been the worst thing anyone could have done to him. Two of my colleagues read that as proof the mid-procedure refusal was a symptom. I read it as proof that we cannot tell the difference, which is the thing that actually frightens me.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to the panel before we vote on the fourteenth. Write it as the panel's document, addressed to us, formal in register but without padding or throat-clearing, and give it these sections, in this order and roughly these lengths: 'Recommendation' (about 150 words — lead with the bottom line, approve/reject/approve-with-conditions, and state when the decision should be revisited); 'The Procedure As Proposed' (about 120 words, only what the reasoning below depends on); 'Why Gradualness Is Carrying the Weight' (about 250 words); 'Whose Refusal Counts' (about 250 words); 'The Strongest Thing That Can Be Said Against This Recommendation' (about 200 words — I want the losing view argued at its best, not demolished, since I have to sit in the room with the people who hold it); and 'Conditions and Timeline' (about 150 words, with the specific conditions or safeguards the recommendation turns on). Where you find it useful, draw the distinction between a change being gradual and a change being small, or between a path through the replacement and the state at the end of it, but do not build the memo as a tour of the literature: the board will not read citations, has no library access, and does not need to be told who first framed these puzzles.
