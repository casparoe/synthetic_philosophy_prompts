I am preparing an application for a nine-month funded research fellowship attached to a centre for environmental ethics, and I need the substantive core of the proposal drafted: one project, stated precisely enough that a sceptical reviewer can see what would count as having done it, and honest about the ways it could come to nothing. Aim for roughly 1,100 to 1,300 words, in British English throughout. Do not supply references, a bibliography or a literature review: I will add the citations myself, so build the case out of positions, distinctions and arguments rather than out of who has defended what, and do not gesture at specific studies or datasets as if you had them in front of you.

The topic is the welfare arithmetic of habitat loss. The broad question people ask is whether wild nature contains more suffering than happiness, and some have inferred from an affirmative answer that destroying habitat would, other things equal, reduce the total suffering in the world. I want the proposal to refuse that framing and replace it with a comparative, decision-shaped one, because the aggregate question is both empirically intractable and, I think, the wrong shape for anyone who actually has to choose between land uses.

Here is the material I want you to work with, though you should reorganise, prune or sharpen it as you see fit.

First, three processes get run together and should be pulled apart: outright destruction (conversion to a surface that supports almost no wild animals), conversion to a different productive use (arable land, pasture, plantation, urban), and degradation or fragmentation of habitat that remains broadly the same kind. These plausibly have very different welfare profiles, and degradation in particular may raise the numbers of small-bodied generalists while lowering those of specialists, which matters if average welfare per animal falls while the headcount rises.

Second, any verdict has to separate the transition from the new steady state. Clearing or burning causes a concentrated episode of dying, and even if the subsequent equilibrium contains less suffering per year, there is a payback period during which the transition cost dominates; I want the project to be able to say, even approximately, how long that period would have to be before the policy-relevant verdict flips.

Third, and the part I least trust: destruction is usually replacement, not erasure. Call the ratio between the wild-animal life-years foreclosed in the destroyed habitat and the wild-animal life-years generated by whatever occupies the land afterwards the displacement ratio. If that ratio is near or below one for common successor states such as farmland, pasture and regenerating scrub, a great deal of the standard argument quietly collapses, and I suspect nobody knows even the order of magnitude. Relatedly, the elasticity of wild-animal population size with respect to habitat area may be low where populations are regulated by density-dependent factors rather than by area as such, in which case losing habitat produces a smaller steady-state reduction in numbers than the area lost suggests.

Fourth, the unit of account needs defending. Per-hectare welfare accounting is intuitive but insensitive to productivity: a hectare of estuary and a hectare of arid scrub are not comparable welfare technologies, and something like welfare-per-unit-of-primary-productivity may track the underlying biology better. That choice is not merely presentational; it can change the sign of the comparison between habitat types.

Fifth, the axiological scaffolding: whether the relevant improvement is impersonal (a state of affairs with less suffering in it) or person-affecting (no one is benefited by not existing), and how much of the framework survives if one takes a strongly person-affecting view. Sixth, the counterfactual baseline: whether the comparison is destruction now versus preservation forever, or destruction now versus destruction later by other causes, or versus restoration; and whether protecting one place merely displaces the pressure elsewhere.

What I want the proposal to contain, in whatever order serves it best: the question restated so that it admits of a determinate answer within nine months; an explanation of why the familiar stance — that habitat loss reduces suffering if wild lives are net negative — is under-argued rather than merely unpopular, given the distinctions above; the method, phased, saying what is actually produced at each stage and why that method rather than the obvious alternatives of a full empirical review or a pure armchair argument; the range of possible conclusions and what would decide between them; a concrete deliverable for people who make land-use decisions (conservation bodies weighing restoration or rewilding, agricultural policymakers weighing set-aside), rather than a licence to bulldoze; what a genuine failure looks like, including the possibility that the honest result is that the sign of the effect is underdetermined by anything currently knowable, and why that result would still be worth funding; and a realistic statement of scope, excluding new empirical data collection and treating contested questions such as invertebrate sentience as parameters to be varied rather than settled.

Finally, close with a short section you should head something like Epistemic status. There, state the working hypothesis the project would set out to test, in one sentence, with a confidence level you would actually stand behind in a review meeting; name the single assumption in the framework most likely to be false and say how the project would find out early; say what finding or argument would lead you to abandon the displacement-ratio approach altogether rather than refine it; and distinguish the parts of the framework that are purely conceptual, and would survive any empirical verdict, from the parts that stand or fall with the ecology.
