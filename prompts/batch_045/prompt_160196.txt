hey — I TA a mixed philosophy/econ seminar on rational choice and the human good, and the first session always goes the same way: 90 minutes of talking past each other because half the room means something completely different by "utility" than the other half. So I want a handout for session one: a decision tree, something like "what do you even mean by utility?", that a student can walk through in fifteen minutes and land on a named position.

What I need from it:

- Start from a node nobody in the room will deny — that someone's actual choices are at least *some* evidence about what they want — and branch off from there.
- 7–9 branch questions, 9–12 leaves. Every node has to be a concrete case or a claim about a concrete case. No node that just asks "is utility a representation of choice or a measure of welfare?" — that's a label question and I want people sorted by what they say about cases, not by which slogan they recognise.
- Cases that must show up somewhere (reword them, but keep the shape): Nia always takes the apple when both apple and banana are in the break room, and on the day the bananas are gone someone says "Nia prefers apples." Marcus won't drink the tap water because he's convinced it's contaminated (it isn't) and would drink it happily if he knew better. Thea has been told since childhood that people like her don't get to do X and now sincerely doesn't want X. Amara votes for the levy that costs her money because it helps her neighbour. Two patients pick the same treatment over the same alternative, but one barely cares and the other would have paid nearly anything. Same painkiller offered to a rich and a poor patient. And in the background throughout: an administrator who has nothing but choice records and has to rank how well off people are from them.
- One distinction I want its own node, because I think it's where the room actually splits and I don't see it handled in the readings: whether a utility ascription is menu-anchored or menu-free — does "A is worth three utils to Nia" mean anything beyond "three, relative to the options actually on offer", or is it supposed to stick when the shelf changes? Say which way each branch goes.
- Leaves that have to be in there: strict behaviourist revealed preference (preference just is the choice disposition, utility is bookkeeping); the "as if"/instrumentalist cousin; the version where a body of choice data either is or isn't integrable, so utility ascriptions can in principle be refuted by the data; the view that choice data comes tangled with belief and has to be pulled apart before it means anything; preference-satisfaction welfarism in its raw and in its informed/idealised form as seperate leaves; the view that welfare is an experienced state and that decision-utility and experienced-utility genuinely come apart; willingness-to-pay/money-metric welfarism; and at least one leaf saying utility-talk is the wrong instrument for the job (capabilities, objective list, rights-ish). Add leaves I haven't thought of if the space has room.
- Build it from the logical space rather than from my name list: say what combinations of answers the questions allow, then drop the actual positions into the cells, and for any empty cell give me one line on why nobody lives there — incoherent, unattractive, or just unclaimed.
- Where two leaves only come apart on some further case I didn't give you, invent that case and flag the pair as un-separated instead of pretending the tree sorts them.
- Each leaf: a name, one line of statement, and one line on the thing it has to swallow.
- Gloss any term of art in half a line — the econ people and the phil people don't share a vocabulary. Nested bullets or ascii, whatever's readable, and it has to fit on one page front and back.

Finish with two sentences on how I should actually run it in class so it doesn't turn into people defending the leaf they landed on.
