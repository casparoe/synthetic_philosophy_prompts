Sorry for the length of this. I'm on a small team that builds decision-support software for hospital resource allocation — mostly triage scoring, bed and staff scheduling, that sort of thing, no robots or anything glamorous — and for about two years our internal ethics review has been having the exact same argument and it has completely stopped generating either heat or light. One half of the team wants the system to aggregate: put probabilities and utilities on outcomes, take the action with the best expected value, and let the numbers be revisable. The other half wants constraints that are never up for trade — never sacrifice this identifiable patient to save those two, never override an explicit refusal even when the aggregate math favors it, never let the weights themselves be tuned to whatever the current target is. Every meeting goes the same way: someone produces a trolley-flavored case, someone else produces a case where aggregation gives what everyone agrees is a horrible answer, a third person says "but in the real world those numbers are estimates, not facts," and then we're out of time. Nobody has changed anybody's mind even slightly.

I want a diagnosis of the argument, not a verdict on it, and I want it grounded in what each side actually says rather than in a tour of the options. My own hunch is that we're not really disagreeing about ethics at all: I think we're disagreeing about what the system's output is *for* — whether it's a decision that has to be defensible to each person it affects (in which case constraints look essential, because "it maximized the total" is not something you can say to the person who got sacrificed), or a recommendation that feeds into a human process where somebody else carries the accountability (in which case aggregation looks fine and constraints look like a way of hiding from the tradeoff). If that's right, the utilitarian-versus-deontological label is doing almost no work and we should reframe. But I also suspect a second, more boring thing is doing hidden work: our models are badly misspecified in exactly the tails where the stakes live, and I've started to think the constraint people are best understood as hedging against model error rather than as asserting rights — they want policies that degrade gracefully when the estimates are wrong, and a hard constraint is just a cruder, more auditable version of what a properly uncertainty-averse aggregator would do if it knew how wrong it might be. Some of us have started informally sorting candidate policies into "error-tolerant" and "error-amplifying" ones and it feels like that distinction is doing more work in our actual meetings than anything about duties or consequences.

So: which of those diagnoses, if either, is right? Where exactly is the crux — different questions under one heading, a hidden empirical question about our error structure, different standards for what counts as an acceptable failure, or just bedrock intuition on both sides that no argument reaches? Say what each faction would answer if pressed, and don't be even-handed for its own sake: if one of my two hunches is wrong, say so plainly. And then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction we should be drawing and aren't, a question we should stop asking, or something we could actually go and find out (we do have several years of logged decisions and outcomes, so retrospective analyses are on the table). One practical note: it's me, four engineers and two clinicians in the room, no philosophers, so skip the survey of normative theories and skip anything that would need a library — I've read the standard encyclopedia-style summaries and they haven't moved us an inch.
