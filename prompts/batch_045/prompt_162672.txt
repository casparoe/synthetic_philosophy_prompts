For a graduate reading group on computational theories of mind, I want a decision tree that takes a student from one starting commitment — that the brain is a physical system whose activity can be described in computational vocabulary — to a named position on whether the brain is literally a computer.

Build the tree out of the logical space first, then attach the names. Every branch node should be a question a student can answer without knowing which view it leads to, and I want these distinctions to carry the work: whether implementation is just a matter of there existing some mapping from physical to formal states or requires a causally structured internal state space of the right kind (the Chalmers-style reply to the Putnam/Searle triviality arguments); whether computing needs an interpreter or designer, or is an objective mechanistic fact about how a system processes vehicles; whether the computation must be representational, kept separate from whether cognition must be computational; and whether the correct computational description merely explains cognition or constitutes it, i.e. whether the mind depends on abstract organization alone or also on fine-grained causal or biological properties.

At least one branch node should be a concrete case rather than an abstract claim. My preferred one is the Twin Computation case: two computational models of a single brain that agree on every input the organism will ever actually receive and diverge only on inputs it never gets. Use also, if it helps, a whole-brain emulation run a million times slower.

Leaves must include: classical language-of-thought computationalism; connectionist computationalism; mechanistic (Piccinini-style) computationalism; the deflationary verdict that the brain computes but that this is trivial or observer-relative; representational-but-not-computational views; dynamical-systems anti-computationalism; Searle-style biological naturalism. Ten leaves maximum.

Two further requirements. First, place or refute a pet idea of mine: that implementation is graded rather than all-or-nothing, so that a brain implements innumerable computations to varying degrees and the interesting question is which ones are mechanistically relevant. If it belongs on the tree, give it a node or leaf; if it collapses one of your branch questions, say which and why. Second, list the unoccupied cells of the space you construct and diagnose each as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate.

Format: indented outline, one sentence of characterization per leaf, no prose preamble or conclusion. Maximum 700 words.
