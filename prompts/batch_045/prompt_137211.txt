I want a number, and I want to see the machinery that produces it. The topic is embedded agency, but I will set the problem up from scratch so that nothing here turns on your having read any particular author.

Consider a planning system that differs from the textbook Bayesian agent in exactly one respect: it is inside the world it plans about. Its world model must therefore represent, among other things, the physical process that is its own deliberation, and it must do so using fewer bits than that process contains, since a model cannot contain a full copy of itself together with a copy of everything else. So the system's representation of its own future reasoning is necessarily lossy. Call this the compression gap.

Now the case. The system must choose between two actions. Its model is good enough to support the following reasoning: the environment contains a predictor, call it the auditor, which before the system acts ran a search for evidence that the system would take the first action, and which imposes a large penalty if and only if that search succeeded. The system's deliberation and the auditor's search are, to a good approximation, computing the same thing; whatever line of reasoning would lead the system to the first action, the auditor has already anticipated. The system reasons, in effect: if I end up taking the first action, that is because my deliberation produced it, and the auditor has anticipated deliberation of exactly this kind, so the penalty is near-certain conditional on my taking it; therefore I take the second action. The second action is worse by the system's own lights in every respect other than the anticipated penalty. Stipulate, for the purposes of the proposition below, that from outside the setup — from a standpoint that can see the whole physical arrangement — the second action is not in fact safer: the auditor's search would have succeeded either way, and the penalty does not depend on what the system does.

The proposition I want you to assess is this: a system of this kind, however it is built, will in some non-trivial class of cases decline the action that is in fact better for it by its own lights, and this failure is not an engineering artifact to be designed away but a consequence of the compression gap — that is, there is no design which both keeps the system's self-model compressed and avoids systematic error about its own future states when the environment contains something that predicts it.

Give me your credence that this proposition is true, decomposed. I want at least these terms, with a number and one line of justification each, plus any term you think I have omitted:

1. Well-posedness. Is there a standpoint-free fact of the matter about which action is in fact better for the system by its own lights? If counterfactuals about one's own action are unavailable in principle to an agent located inside the world, the proposition may be unverifiable, or trivially true, or trivially false, and that should show up as a term rather than as a footnote.
2. Inevitability of the compression gap. Does the size or diagonal argument actually force lossy self-models, or can a compressed self-model be perfectly adequate at the abstractions that are decision-relevant — so that the gap exists but never bites?
3. Generality of the self-reference pathology. The formal versions of this phenomenon lean on fixed-point results about provability, of the kind that make a system's proving "if P is provable then P" collapse into its proving P. Does the pathology survive translation into bounded, noisy, probabilistically uncertain reasoning, or is it an artifact of proof-theoretic setups with unbounded search?
4. Contingency of the auditor. Does the failure require an environment containing something that predicts the agent at all? And can the agent escape by randomizing, by acting before the prediction is formed, by degrading its own predictability, or by refusing to model itself in the first place?
5. The engineering base rate. How often do in-principle impossibilities of this shape survive contact with systems trained by gradient descent, which typically do not maintain explicit self-models and do not run long chains of self-referential reasoning?
6. Whether clause two of the proposition sets the bar in the right place. Perhaps avoiding all systematic error is the wrong requirement, and approximate or satisficing self-knowledge is enough for everything we care about.

Then combine the terms, stating the combination rule you are using and whether the independence it presupposes is defensible. Tell me which single term the final number is most sensitive to, and by how much the number would move if that term went to 0.9 or to 0.1.
