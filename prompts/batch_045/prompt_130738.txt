I've been reading rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days and it's kind of ruined my own field for me, because everything we actually do is likelihood ratios with mud on them and nobody in the field will just say so out loud. Anyway.

I've agreed to run a two-hour evening session next month for our county archaeology society's volunteer diggers — about eighteen people, mostly retired, four or five of them metal detectorists who have found more Roman coins than I ever will, and two first-years who are there for a credit and will be looking at their phones. The detectorists are the ones I'm nervous about: they're excellent at the empirical part and extremely confident about the inference part, and someone will definately tell me that the absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

The one thing I want to teach is how you get from an object to an institution, and why that step usually fails. Not "how to recognise a temple" — the logic of why a room full of unusual objects doesn't get you a priesthood, and what would. I have a version of this that I give my own students but it's all jargon (equifinality, formation processes, and the assumption that a burnt or abandoned site is a photograph of a living one when it's really a photograph of the moment everybody left) and it will slide straight off a room like this.

So don't explain it to me — tell me how to explain it. And no reading list or citations: nobody is going to follow them up and half the room doesn't use the internet.

Here's the made-up case I want to hang the whole thing on, and you can tell me if it's the wrong one. A late Roman rural settlement, five courtyard houses on a hillside, no texts, no figural art, no grave goods anywhere on the site. In one of the five — call it House C — a single room contained 240 coarse-ware jars, all smashed, all from the same kiln fabric, all within about ten per cent of the same volume. The other four houses have two or three jars each, mixed fabrics, sitting with their own cooking wares. There's also a dog buried under House C's doorway. The candidate institutions: an estate collecting tax in kind and redistributing it; a private merchant doing the same thing for profit; a kin group running a competitive feasting economy; a cult with a storage-and-offering side to it. My thinking is that the case has to force "somebody organised this production" while making it impossible to say which of the four did, and that the dog is a deliberate decoy — it feels enormously meaningful and carries no institutional information at all — but I'm not sure whether to keep it or whether it just feeds the ritual bucket.

Sections, roughly:

"The one idea" — about 150 words. The single sentence I want them repeating in the car park afterwards. My candidate is something like: an institution shows up in objects only if you can also say what the objects would look like if the institution weren't there. Tell me whether that's the right bottom line or whether the mechanism half needs stating seperately.

"The carrying case" — about 250 words. What the jars have to have and what they have to lack for the inference to be genuinely live rather than settled in advance, including a verdict on the dog.

"The order" — about 400 words. What has to be in place before what, and why the dependencies run that way. Right now I think the very first move isn't "archaeology is hard" but the fact that nothing gets into the ground except by being discarded, lost, or destroyed — so the deposit is usually evidence of an institution breaking down rather than of it running, which I call the wreckage bias and which I think does more work than equifinality does. Then standardisation, then "somebody organised production," then the several different institutional homes that organisation can have, then the contrast question. Tell me where I've got the dependencies wrong, and where the "so is archaeology just storytelling" worry belongs — my instinct is to raise it myself early, before they can pick it up and use it as a shield.

"Misconceptions, in the order they'll arrive" — about 350 words. The ones I expect: objects as straightforwardly expressing beliefs, i.e. pots equal a people; "ritual" as a bin for anything we can't find a practical use for; 240 jars feeling like 240 pieces of evidence when it's one event and n=1; the absence-of-evidence shield; and then the overcorrection into "so you can't know anything." That last one I need handled structurally rather than reassuringly — something they can watch the jars doing, not something I assert at them.

"The check" — about 150 words. One question at the end, plus what a right answer sounds like and what a convincingly-wrong-but-confident answer sounds like. My candidate is to hand them a second, much thinner assemblage and ask what single find would make them downgrade their favourite hypothesis; if there's a better test for whether the idea landed rather than the vocabulary, use it.

"What I refuse to explain" — about 100 words. What I should openly declare out of scope at this level, and in what words, so it reads as intellectual hygiene rather than a brush-off.
