Retired atmospheric chemist here, 71, and since I stopped work I've been reading philosophy blogs in the evenings and arguing with my brother-in-law over email, which is where these came from. I've got stuck on two puzzles: one where a being with a perfect model of you flips a coin, it comes up tails, and asks you for $100 on the strength of "if it had come up heads and you'd have paid, I'd have given you $10,000", and one where a blackmailer's letter only reaches you because of what a predictor said you'd do. I made up a pile of variations and I trust my gut on most of them, but I can't see what it is I actually believe. Can someone tell me what principle fits?

1. The coin one, tails, as above. I'd pay. Obvious to me. My brother-in-law says the coin's already landed and I'm burning a hundred dollars on nothing.

2. Ordinary blackmail, no prediction anywhere: someone has emails that would end my marriage, wants $5,000, one shot, they'll never be back. I'd pay. Gritted teeth, but the arithmetic isn't interesting.

3. Letter version: the letter says I'll publish unless you pay, and I only send these to people my predictor says will pay. The predictor has never been wrong. I would NOT pay, and the reason I'd give is that I want to be the kind of person who never gets that letter. But once the letter is physically in my hand every instinct says pay, so I'm not sure I trust the reason.

4. Same as 3, except the predictor is right about 70% of the time, so plenty of the people who get letters were predicted wrong and plenty of non-payers never hear from him. I'd pay here. And I know that's in tension with 3 — if refusing is right when the predictor is perfect, it ought to be even more right when the predictor is sloppy — but my gut just flips. Which of the two has to go?

5. Same letter, same threat, but the prediction isn't about this decision at all: he only writes to people his predictor says would hand $100 to a stranger who asked politely. I'd pay. Feels like case 2 again: the letter's already posted, nothing I decide now changes whether it arrived.

6. Mirror image of 3: he only writes to people his predictor says will NOT pay. I'd pay, but it makes me uneasy in a way case 5 doesn't. Here handing over the money is exactly what keeps me out of the situation in the first place, and that feels like I'm managing the prediction rather than making a decision. Maybe that's fine. Maybe it's the tell that I've lost the plot.

7. No prediction at all, but he works the whole neighbourhood, everyone knows who paid, and the people who paid get a second letter. I wouldn't pay. I realise that's just deterrence and has nothing to do with cases 3 to 6 — I'd like a principle that doesn't quietly mush the two together.

8. The one I genuinely can't call. The letter arrives and it says: I asked my predictor what you would have done if this letter had reached you yesterday, before you'd ever heard of me or of this setup; it said you'd have paid; that's why I'm writing. Nothing I predicted concerns the choice you're making now, because yesterday's you didn't know any of this. Do I pay? I've went back and forth for a week.

So: is there one principle — something about what your decision is really a decision about, or which counterfactual branch you're meant to care about, or what you'd have wanted to commit to before the letter came — that delivers 1 through 7? And what does it say about 8, and about a case 9 you think I should have thought of? If it can't have all seven, tell me which verdict to drop and why that one rather than another. And if the honest answer is that 7 needs a different principle from the rest, say so, and say where the join between them is. I'm not after names or papers — I can't look anything up from here anyway — just the principle, stated precisely enough that I can test it on the next variation my brother-in-law emails me. That's definately coming.
