Hey — I'm on a volunteer committee writing the plain-language explainer for a charter amendment on our city ballot in November, and I need help with one image that's doing way too much work in our draft.

The amendment: council goes from 9 single-member wards to 15 — the same 9 wards, redrawn by an independent commission for compactness — plus 6 citywide seats allocated to parties by the highest-averages method with a 5% threshold. And here's the bit our committee keeps fighting about: a party forfeits its citywide seats unless it fielded candidates in at least seven of the nine wards.

The pro campaign's whole pitch is a map analogy. "A council should be a map of the city — a good map shows every neighbourhood at its real size, and first-past-the-post is a Mercator projection: it keeps the shape of the wards but inflates the big parties until they cover half the page." One of the political scientists who spoke to us sharpened it: FPTP is conformal but not equal-area, PR is equal-area but tears up the local shapes, and you can't have both — there's no flat map of a sphere that preserves shapes and areas at once, so the real choice isn't between a fair map and a distorted one, it's between two distortions. The opponents fire back with their own version: "a map isn't a neighbourhood, you can't knock on a map's door" — PR hands you a picture with no address on it and nobody you can call.

So can you audit this for me? I need the correspondence spelled out — what plays the role of the territory, the map, the scale, the projection, the shape, the area, the distortion — and then the disanalogies, seperated into the ones that matter for what we're actually arguing and the ones that don't. In particular: does the electorate sit still while you redraw the map, or does it move (party formation, tactical voting, who bothers to turn out), and how much does that break the picture? Is "shape" in cartography really the same kind of thing as geographic constituency linkage, or is that where the analogy is quietly smuggling in its conclusion? Does the seven-wards rule make our measure a deliberately compromised projection, and if so what exactly is it buying and what is it paying for? And afterwards, does either side's conclusion survive, or was the imagery carrying it the whole time? If there's a better image for what a compromise projection like ours does, I'd take it — and tell me which single claim in the analogy is load-bearing, because we have maybe 300 words and a voter has to be able to check it.
