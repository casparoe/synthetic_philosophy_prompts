I'm a research engineer working on continual learning and machine unlearning, and my group is about to publish an internal document we have been calling a "memory policy": what a model is allowed to have learned, what has to be removable on request, and what we owe the people who end up in our training corpora. The disagreement that keeps stalling us is not technical. I have been reading Margalit's *Ethics of Memory* on the side, and a friend of mine keeps insisting that the real case for forgetting comes from Rieff: on his reading, a culture that converts its past into exhibits — archives, museums, memorial curricula — has already stopped being commanded by it, and curatorial "remembrance" is closer to a way of neutralizing the dead than of honoring them. I suspect my friend is overreading Rieff, but the distinction he keeps pointing at is the one my teammates walk straight past when they say things like "the interview transcripts are in cold storage and fully retrievable, so of course the system remembers them." My philosophy stops at a minor, so I would rather have this handed back to me in terms I can check than in terms I have to trust.

Please restate both sides of the argument in the vocabulary I actually work in: a training set as distinct from the weights, memorization versus generalization, catastrophic forgetting, replay buffers and synthetic rehearsal, machine unlearning and its usual "as if never included" guarantee, differential privacy as a bound on how much any single individual is allowed to leave an imprint, distillation, embedding drift, retrieval-time grounding. Two of my own distinctions should get tested in the process. First, "imprint memory" versus "archive memory": in the first, someone's testimony still bends what the system does off-distribution; in the second, the record is retrievable on demand but the system's dispositions would be identical had it never seen it. Is the duty Margalit describes a duty of imprint rather than archive, and does the Rieff-flavored worry about museum memory then come out as an objection to confusing the second with the first? Second, notice how the word memorization flips value across the two literatures: for me it names verbatim regurgitation of a training example, which is a defect and a privacy failure, while in the memory-ethics literature fidelity to an individual looks like the whole point — and my field's flagship technique for the good case, differential privacy, is a formal guarantee of indistinguishability, which is anonymity rather than forgiveness, and anonymity is arguably the last thing owed to someone whose claim on us is precisely that they were a particular person who suffered a particular thing. Restate the positions as faithfully as you can in these terms, keeping the translation honest — flag it explicitly where you are smuggling in an assumption of my framework rather than translating.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want: what mapped cleanly, what only fit after you distorted it (and how you distorted it), and what has no expression at all in my vocabulary — the duty's being owed to someone who is dead, the asymmetry between forgetting a victim and forgetting a perpetrator, the idea that a deletion can be an insult rather than a service, whatever it turns out to be. For each item say whether you read the residue as a gap in my vocabulary or as evidence that the philosophical claim was doing less work than it looked like it was doing. Commit: do not give me a survey of possible readings or a balanced menu. Take the version you think is right, and finish by writing the single sentence about what our models owe the people in their training data that you would put in the policy document if it were yours to sign, with one paragraph on why, and a note on which part of your answer you would expect to be wrong about.
