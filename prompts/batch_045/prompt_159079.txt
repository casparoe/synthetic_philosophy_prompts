For a graduate reading group on Buddhist philosophy and personal identity, I'm preparing a session on Christopher Nolan's *Memento*, and I want to read it through the no-self (anattā) picture rather than through the Locke–Parfit psychological-continuity debate, which is what almost everyone reaches for first and which makes the film into a tidy illustration of a point it doesn't really make. The protagonist, Leonard Shelby, cannot lay down new episodic memories, and he compensates with a system of notes, photographs and tattoos; the film is also told backwards, so that we, like him, are never in a position to place the present scene inside a remembered sequence. I'd like a reading that takes seriously the idea that what we call a person is a conventional designation over the aggregates — body, feeling, perception, volitional formations, consciousness — and that the felt sense of "I" is something grafted onto that stream rather than discovered in it. In particular, I've been working with a distinction I find useful and would like you to use: between an *abbreviative* fiction of self, where the "I" is a convenient shorthand for something real that continues (dispositions, appetites, grief, habits of attention), and a *confabulatory* fiction, where the "I" is fabricated by the very records that are supposed to preserve it and nothing independent answers to it. Which of these does the film actually dramatise? Leonard's character seems to survive his memory entirely intact — he is recognisably the same man in his stubbornness, his cruelty, his devotion to his wife — while his *recorded* self is something he can be tricked into rewriting, and that he eventually rewrites on purpose. Does that split, and the film's treatment of it, tell against the no-self picture, or does it show the picture getting something right that the memory-based theories of personal identity miss, namely that the sense of self is more a matter of clinging and appetite than of information?

There are two further things I'd like handled rather than gestured at. First, one of my friends argues that Leonard is a counterexample to anattā: here is a man with no narrative memory at all who is manifestly still someone, still the subject of a grievance and a project, and if that is not a self then nothing is. I'd like that objection met on its own terms, with an account of what the Buddhist reductionist should say about a person whose stream persists while the story he can tell about it does not — and, if the honest answer is that the film really does push against the theory somewhere, I want that said plainly rather than smoothed over. Second, the ending: once the corrupt officer has told him that the other amnesiac's story is really his own, Leonard appears to understand this, and then deliberately writes himself a false trail so that he will go on hunting. Is that best read as self-grasping exposed and refused, or as self-grasping shown to be practically indispensable — the engine without which his agency collapses — and what would it mean for the claim that clinging to a self is the root of suffering if the film is right that this particular clinging is what keeps him functioning? Please point to three scenes the group should look at closely, say where the reading is doing violence to the film (it is, after all, a revenge thriller built as a puzzle, and I don't want to pretend Nolan is doing metaphysics where he is doing suspense), and finish by saying what *Memento* adds to the philosophical debate that the arguments alone don't give us. Around 1,200 words, and please write in British English.
