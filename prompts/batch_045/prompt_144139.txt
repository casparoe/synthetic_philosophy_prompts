I help run a small reading group that grew out of an old rationalist-blog comment section. We worked through the usual sequences-adjacent material on Bayesian updating, Aumann's agreement theorem, and the centipede game some years ago, and two of our members have been circling the same disagreement ever since. They have now agreed to try an adversarial collaboration, and I volunteered to produce the document they will argue over. I would like you to write that draft.

The two positions, as neutrally as I can state them. Call the first the epistemic side: equilibrium is what reasoning about other reasoners delivers — solution concepts are to be derived from the agents' own epistemic states, rationality plus beliefs about rationality plus beliefs about those beliefs, and the resulting characterization theorem is where the real content of the theory lives. On this view, if the players are rational and each knows the other is rational, and so on up, the game is already settled, and disappointing laboratory play is evidence about the subjects' rationality or about their higher-order beliefs, not about the theory. Call the second the dynamic side: equilibrium is where a process comes to rest — what matters is whether some plausible adaptive process (no-regret learning, fictitious play, imitation under selection) converges, to what, and on what timescale, with correlated rather than Nash patterns often the natural attractor. On this view, common knowledge of rationality is an idealization no finite agent ever instantiates, the epistemic characterizations are elegant translations rather than predictions, and lab play is data about the thing the theory was supposed to be about.

Two stipulations I want the document built around, because these are where their arguments actually bite.

First, surprise-robustness. They both invoke this constantly and neither has defined it. My attempt: a solution concept is surprise-robust if, for every history it assigns probability zero, it still delivers coherent beliefs and a recommendation at that history, and those beliefs do not contradict what it told the players before play began. Please give a definition in the document that both would accept — correcting mine if it is confused — and then use it rather than gesturing at it.

Second, the Mirror Centipede, which they have agreed is a fair test bed. Eight-stage centipede, small stakes, three arms. Arm A: anonymous, no information. Arm B: before play, each subject sees the other's score on a short pre-test of game-theoretic reasoning. Arm C: before play, each subject submits a complete sealed contingent strategy to an auditor; if the two submitted strategies are mutual best responses to each other, the auditor announces to both that this is so, and nothing more, and they then play for real. The document should record what each side predicts about the distribution of stopping nodes in each arm, written down as pre-registration, including the pattern of results each side would count as disconfirming its own view.

Please organize the response in exactly these sections, with roughly these lengths:

Agreed claims — ten to twelve numbered items, one or two sentences each, about 500 words total. These should not be throat-clearing; a hostile reader on either side should be able to say yes, I believe that.

Where the disagreement remains — a table of five or six rows, about 450 words, with columns for the issue, the epistemic side's position in wording that side would sign, the dynamic side's position in wording that side would sign, and one clause on why the split is not merely verbal.

Cruxes and pre-registered predictions — about four cruxes, roughly 150 words apiece. For each: the sub-question in one sentence; a case, argument, or arm of the Mirror Centipede both accept as bearing on it; what each side predicts; and what result each would count against itself. At least one crux should concern what a player should believe after a history the theory assigned probability zero, at least one should concern whether near-common knowledge of rationality behaves like common knowledge as the noise goes to zero, and at least one should concern whether the epistemic characterization yields any forecast the dynamic account does not.

Verbal-dispute audit — about 150 words on which parts of the argument dissolve once the loaded words are fixed by definition, and which do not.

Left unresolved — about 100 words on what neither side could settle, including any arm of the design whose outcome both would treat as uninformative.

Build everything out of the stipulations above and from arguments you can reconstruct from scratch; assume I have no library, and don't spend words on citations or on what anyone has written before. Write it so both of them could put their names at the bottom without either feeling shortchanged.
