You are a philosopher who works on personal identity and modality, and who also writes speeches for non-academic audiences. Think carefully and brainstorm before you draft: I want to see the reasoning, and then the speech.

The occasion: on Saturday we are holding a secular "welcome" ceremony for our daughter, born nine weeks ago, and I am giving the main speech — four minutes, roughly 550 words. The room will be about fifty people: both sets of grandparents, siblings, old friends, and four or five friends from my old philosophy department who will hear every philosophical move I make. It has to land as a celebration — not a lecture and not a grief narrative — and it must contain no philosopher's name and no term of art: no "essentialism," no "possible worlds," nothing like that. A philosopher in the room should nevertheless be able to reconstruct the position from what I say.

Two facts I cannot stop thinking about, and which I want the speech to be honest about rather than to paper over. Our daughter came from a single frozen embryo, transferred after three years of IVF. Three years before her, my wife was pregnant from a different embryo — same two parents — and lost it at ten weeks; the family has quietly settled on the consoling line that it was somehow the same child, arriving at the wrong time. And the embryo that became our daughter split: there were two heartbeats at six weeks and one by the eighth.

The view I have in mind is the familiar one, associated with Kripke, that a person could not have originated from a different sperm and egg. So before drafting, work through this: what does that view actually imply about the pregnancy we lost — was it our daughter, or someone who was never her? And what does the splitting fact do to the view? If the same sperm and egg yielded two heartbeats, the gametes cannot be what makes her the individual she is; should origin be relocated to the zygote, and if so does the view survive, and in what form? Where the view is silent, or where the honest answer is indeterminate, say so rather than forcing it to speak.

Then write the speech, shaped by whatever you conclude. I want to know what I can truthfully say to the grandparents about the child we lost, and what I must refuse to say, however much it would comfort them. If your conclusion is that no true and kind thing is available on that point, tell me so, and give me the nearest thing that is both. Do not quote or cite anyone.
