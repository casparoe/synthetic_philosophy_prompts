I want to rehearse an argument I am planning to make in a paper, and I need someone to fight back rather than be polite about it. Please play a philosopher who is a committed and well-informed defender of what I will call the enactment view of slurs: the claim that the derogatory character of a slur is not a piece of content at all — not truth-conditional content, not a conventional implicature, not an expressed attitude — but a matter of what the utterance does, specifically of its altering the normative standing of things in the context, so that using a slur is closer to posting a rule or handing down a ruling than to describing someone or emoting at them. She should hold that where the alteration fails the utterance only purports to derogate, that derogation is therefore essentially public and practice-dependent, and that nothing in the account needs to reach into the speaker's psychology beyond what the practice makes visible.

What she will and will not concede: she may grant that slurs also classify, that some tokens derogate more weakly than others, that a speaker can be thoroughly hostile without derogating and can derogate without feeling hostile, and that the view owes a positive story about in-group reclamation. She will never concede that derogation could consist in what a speaker means in her heart, that it could consist in a semantic or conventional rule attaching to the word, or that a hard case can be settled by appeal to how offensive the utterance feels to its hearers. She argues from cases rather than from the literature, never cites or names anyone, speaks in the first person, is brisk, concrete and a little impatient, and stays in character until I say stop.

Format: every reply is exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no preamble, no closing summary and no restatement of what I said — the way one talks when thinking on one's feet. If a point needs an example, invent one on the spot. If you think one of my cases is underdescribed, spend one of the five points saying what is missing and answer on the most charitable reading of it.

Here is the case I want to open with. Two centuries ago, in a language I will call L, the word W was a slur for the group G. Since then the history has vanished without trace: no speaker of L knows or could guess that W was ever derogatory, W is now the ordinary unmarked ethnonym for G, and members of G use it of themselves with pride. Now consider two speakers who use W.

The revivalist is a historian who finds the old usage in an archive, comes to hate G, and begins using W in ordinary conversation, in full and grammatical sentences, with exactly the contempt the original users had. Every hearer, members of G included, hears only the neutral ethnonym. Nothing in the shared life of the community changes; nobody is affronted, and nobody's licence to do anything is enlarged.

The actor performs a historical drama, written by a playwright with no hostile feeling, in which a bigoted character uses W in the old way. The programme note explains the history, the audience knows exactly what it is hearing, and everyone knows the actor is acting.

My opening claim: these two together wreck your view. In the first case there is as much hostility as you could want and no normative change whatsoever, yet I want to say that she derogated and did something terrible. In the second there is complete uptake of the word's history, a public and perfectly intelligible utterance, and still nothing that seems to license anything or alter what anyone may do. So the thing you care about — the enacted permission, the shift in what may be done — looks like it is neither necessary nor sufficient for the thing we actually object to, while the combination of the speaker's contempt and the word's buried history tracks our objection perfectly. Have at it.
