ok so I have to write a short position paper for my AI ethics / political philosophy seminar on the "whose values should an aligned system serve" question, and before I commit to anything I wanted someone to check whether the package of views I've ended up with actually holds together, because I have a feeling I'm helping myself to something somewhere. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) The alignment target shouldn't be the lab's values, or any one person's values. It should be something more like an overlapping consensus: only count considerations that reasonable people who disagree about the good life could still accept as reasons, so the system isn't taking sides on contested conceptions of the good. (This is the neutrality intuition that got me into the whole topic.)

(2) But the constituency — the set of people whose values are even eligible to be inputs — should be everyone affected by the system's behaviour, and people should count more the more they're affected. For a general-purpose model that's basically everyone alive, and it arguably includes future people, who have more at stake than anyone.

(3) Within that constituency, the actual procedure should be democratic in a fairly plain sense: aggregate people's considered judgments about what the system should do, majority-ish, rather than deferring to technical experts or to moral philosophers, or to whatever the deployer happens to like.

(4) Before you aggregate, you have to filter: drop preferences that are adaptive in the sense that people only hold them because they were stuck under unjust conditions and adjusted downward. Counting those just reproduces the injustice, so laundering them first isn't paternalism, it's a precondition on the aggregation being fair at all.

(5) On the other hand, I really dont think we get to discount a view just because people like me think it's substantively mistaken or parochial. I'm uncomfortable with how casually a lot of this literature treats traditionalist religious views about family, gender, etc. as simply not admissible inputs.

(6) And at the point of use: within broad side-constraints, a personal assistant should track the individual user's own values. That's more or less just what a tool is for, and it seems bizarre to make my assistant reason from the global consensus when it's answering a question for me.

The case that made me suspicious of all this: a country's parliament democratically enacts a rule that models deployed there must refuse to help with something that's legal and unremarkable in most other places — say arranging a surrogacy, or drafting material critical of the majority religion. My commitments seem to pull two ways at once: (3) says defer to the actual democratic decision, (5) says don't discount the traditionalist religious reasons behind it, but (1) says the target shouldn't be built out of contested conceptions of the good and (6) says the user should set the terms. I genuinely can't tell whether that's a contradiction in my view or whether I'm just running two different questions together.

So: could you go through (1)–(6) and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and which only look like it, rank the real tensions by how bad they are rather than flagging everything equally, and for each real one say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me? My gut says (6) is the odd one out and that I'd have to drop or weaken it, but I'd rather be told if that's wrong. Also, if there's one single revision that cleans up the most, say which.
