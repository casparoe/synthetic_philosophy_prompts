The position I hold is what I call the upstream-selection account of the replication crisis.

The standard diagnosis, as you'll know, is that the literature is distorted by significance-chasing: optional stopping, outcome-switching, the suppression of null results by journals and by authors, and a convention (the fixed alpha threshold, the binary "significant/not significant" verdict) that makes the reported quantity a threshold-crossing event rather than an estimate. The standard remedies follow: pre-registration, registered reports with results-blind review, larger samples, lower thresholds, and reporting estimates with intervals — or, for the more committed, abandoning null-hypothesis significance testing for Bayesian estimation or evidence measures.

I accept all of that as real. My claim is that it leaves a residual that the standard story cannot explain, because the selection operates one stage earlier than any of those remedies reach. Before a study is designed, a lab has typically already handled the hypothesis in cheap, informal, unrecorded ways: pilot runs, exploratory looks, "we tried this and it didn't work so we moved on." Where a lab can cycle through many candidate hypotheses cheaply, the hypothesis it finally commits to is one that was selected on the basis of a noisy early estimate of the very quantity the eventual study estimates. The recorded study is then an honest, pre-registered, adequately powered test of a hypothesis that arrived pre-filtered. Call the relevant variable pilot-plasticity: how cheap it is to generate, probe and abandon candidate hypotheses before committing.

Two commitments follow, and I want you to attack both. First, the residual inflation should be predictable from pilot-plasticity rather than from the usual suspects: it should be large in fields with fast, cheap, within-lab study cycles, weak or absent theory, and many swappable manipulations and outcome measures; and it should be near zero where the hypothesis is pinned down by a theory that makes something close to a point prediction, where the design is expensive and settled by committee, or where the analysis is mechanically determined by the design. Second, the frequentist-versus-Bayesian quarrel is largely a sideshow for this purpose. What does the work is whether the reported unit is an estimate with uncertainty or a binary verdict, and how much room the reporting convention leaves for selection. I hold the stronger claim that converting the field wholesale to Bayesian reporting would at most relocate the gaming — prior specification, model space, the choice of comparison — and would leave upstream selection entirely untouched, so a fully Bayesian discipline would still have a replication crisis of roughly the same shape.
