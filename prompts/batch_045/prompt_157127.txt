**Question 4 (30 marks)**

*(a)* Distinguish lying from misleading, and apply your distinction to the following case. Jennifer is a candidate for a lectureship. Her doctoral referee knows that a chapter of her thesis was plagiarised, and writes a letter that is literally true in every sentence but says nothing about her research integrity and praises her 'breadth of engagement with the literature'. The search committee, reading it, concludes that there is no question mark over her work. The referee foresees this and writes the letter partly so that they should conclude it. Is the referee lying, misleading, or neither? (10 marks)

*(b)* 'Since the liar and the misleader alike aim at their audience's holding a false belief, and since the audience ends up with the very same false belief either way, the difference between them is at most a matter of etiquette.' Discuss. Your answer should consider (i) a case in which the audience has no right to the truth, and (ii) a case in which the attempted deception fails. (20 marks)

**Student's answer**

Lying and misleading are both ways of getting someone to believe something false, but they do it by different routes, and the difference in route is where the moral difference lies.

To lie is to assert something one believes to be false, presenting it as true. Assertion is the key notion: a lie is a false assertion, and it is the assertion, and not merely the falsity, that makes it a lie. To mislead is to bring it about that someone has a false belief without asserting anything false — by implicating, by withholding, or by arranging what they see. The referee's letter is a clear case of misleading and not of lying. Every sentence of it is true; the committee's belief that there is no question over Jennifer's research integrity is not something the referee says but something the committee infers from her silence on the matter. Since what is said and what is implicated are disjoint features of an utterance, and since lies are located entirely in what is said while misleading is located entirely in what is implicated, no utterance can be both a lie and an act of misleading — which is why we can say flatly that the referee does not lie. It follows too that there is nothing in the letter that the referee disbelieves, and hence no insincerity in it: she means every word she writes. (I set aside bald-faced lies, where the speaker is not believed anyway; nothing here turns on them.)

The objection in (b) — that both the liar and the misleader aim at the same outcome and therefore differ only in style — gets one thing right and one thing wrong. It is right that the intended outcome is the same false belief. It is wrong to infer that the difference is merely presentational. Assertion is a practice constituted by a norm of truthfulness: to assert is to give one's word, to invite the hearer to take the content on one's authority. The liar invokes that authority and abuses it; the misleader leaves the practice intact but free-rides on the hearer's own inferential habits. This is why the difference in norm matters morally. Lying violates a directed duty — the hearer's claim-right not to be lied to — whereas misleading violates only the general duty not to deceive people. So the difference is not etiquette but a difference in which norm one breaches and in whom one wrongs. I would concede only that the difference in overall wrongness can be small: where the stakes are the same and the deception equally effective, the misleader is not obviously a much better person than the liar.

Two cases bear this out. First, an audience with no right to the truth. The murderer at the door has forfeited his claim on the truth by his murderous intention, so misleading him is permissible: I may tell him that I last saw my friend at the station, knowing that he will draw the wrong conclusion. Lying to him is another matter. The duty not to assert what one disbelieves is not derived from what any particular hearer deserves or can claim, so it does not lapse merely because the hearer has forfeited his rights; I may not say that my friend is not here. This confirms that the two wrongs have different sources. Second, a deception that fails. If my hearer sees straight through me, I have still asserted what I disbelieved and meant it to be taken as true, so I have still lied, even though no false belief results. That shows the wrong of lying is not exhausted by its effects — which is what we should expect if the wrong consists in the abuse of a practice rather than in the production of a false belief.

None of this makes misleading a free pass. Where the audience does have a right to the information — a patient deciding whether to consent, a committee deciding whether to appoint — withholding can be as wrong as a lie would be, because the general duty not to deceive is itself stringent when the stakes are high. The referee's letter is not, I think, permissible. But its wrongness is the wrongness of evasion rather than of lying, and the difference is worth marking even when both routes are closed.
