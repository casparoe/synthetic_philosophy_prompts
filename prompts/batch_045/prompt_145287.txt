I help run a short orientation course for people who have just moved to a new country for work, and the same argument breaks out every session. Half the room says that when you go somewhere you simply do as the people there do — queue the way they queue, tip the way they tip, cross the road the way they cross it — and that doing otherwise is a small but real moral failing, not just a faux pas. The other half says these are arbitrary local habits, that no one is owed your compliance with a rule that has no better claim than being customary, and that the most a visitor owes is prudence and politeness. I want to replace that argument with something better structured: a handout that lays out the logical space of positions on whether everyday conventions generate moral obligations, and on whether they bind visitors, recent arrivals, and people who don't yet know the local rule. So please build me a taxonomy in the form of a tree, where every internal node is labeled with the specific question that splits the views beneath it and every leaf carries the name of a position (write 'unnamed' if you don't think anyone actually holds it) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. I'd like between ten and fourteen leaves, written for newcomers with no philosophy background — no bibliographies or reading suggestions, and please define any term of art in plain words the first time you use it.

Rather than start from a list of -isms, please start from the distinctions, and build the tree out of them; then show which combinations of answers are actually occupied and which are empty, and for each empty combination say briefly whether it is empty because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unoccupied. The distinctions I care about are these. First, I have found it useful to sort conventions by what they are *for*: some exist to coordinate people around a scarce good (queueing, standing on one side of an escalator), some are transfers to a particular identifiable person (tipping, haggling over a price), and some mostly allocate risk or blame between strangers (jaywalking rules, quiet carriages). I suspect that which of these three a convention is changes what, if anything, is owed — but I may have the classification wrong, so feel free to tell me at the end, in a short note, where my three-way split breaks down, and please tell me whether you need a fourth kind. Second, I want the tree to turn on conditions like these: whether locals themselves mostly comply (does a convention still bind where half the people ignore it?); whether the person had a fair chance to learn the rule (a day off the plane versus two years in the country); whether they had a clean exit available (declining to sit down in a table-service restaurant versus eating the meal and then refusing the tip); whether the convention's point is frustrated by selective non-compliance in a way that harms identifiable people; and whether the convention is entangled with something independently bad — stipulate, for the sake of argument, that in some countries servers may legally be paid below the ordinary minimum wage on the understanding that tips close the gap, and consider whether that fact, where it holds, changes the moral picture, and whether it changes it differently for a visitor than for a resident.

Each diagnostic question should be a concrete case, not an abstract claim, so that my course participants sort themselves by what they think about situations rather than by which label they find attractive. Please use at least these: leaving no tip after genuinely poor service; leaving a tip in a place where tipping is not the custom; edging into a loose, crowd-like queue at a market stall where queueing is barely observed; holding a place in line for four friends who turn up later; crossing against a red light on a completely empty street where everyone crosses; crossing against the light while a child who is still learning the local rules is watching; keeping your shoes on in a home where everyone else has taken theirs off; paying the cheaper takeaway price for a coffee and then going and sitting in the more expensive table-service area; splitting a restaurant bill evenly when one person ordered far more than the others; and refusing to haggle where haggling is how prices are set. None of these should simply ask "is it wrong?" — ask them so that they discriminate between positions. Two further things: I suspect your tree will have trouble separating the view that convention-following is merely prudent or polite from a weak view that it is a genuine but easily overridden moral reason, since they agree on almost every case, so please flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate and, where you can, add the one extra case that would pry them apart. And please include a short closing note on a question I can't settle: if a visitor is morally required to follow a local convention, is that requirement owed to the individuals affected, to the community whose practice it is, or to nobody in particular?
