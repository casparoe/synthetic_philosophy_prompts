hey, hoping someone can help me put a name on something — or tell me flat out that it doesn't have one, in which case I want help carving it up properly.

I design reward mechanics for a behaviour-change app (streaks, deposit contracts, that sort of thing) and I read too much philosophy of action in my spare time, and I keep tripping over the same shape of problem in both places.

The shape: someone gets offered something valuable on the condition that, by some deadline, they're in a certain state of mind — intending to do a thing, being resolved, genuinely wanting it — and the payoff does not depend on whether they ever act on it.

Case one. A stranger offers you a large sum of money, handed over at breakfast, if by then you've genuinely decided to swallow a pill that will make you feel like you've got the flu for a day. You get the money for the deciding, not the swallowing. I cannot get myself into that state. I can say the words, I can even arrange to be locked in a room with the pill, but actually deciding seems to be the one thing unavailable to me — and weirdly it's unavailable *because* of the offer. Before he opened his mouth, deciding to take a flu-pill for no reason would have been strange but not incoherent.

Case two. Same structure, except the thing you're paid to decide on is something you'd still have a real reason to do when the time comes: go for a run at 6pm, money arrives at breakfast. This one I also can't get into, but it feels like the opposite failure. The money's already spent on me, so it can't be my reason at 6pm, and the run now feels *bought* — which somehow makes it harder to want than if nobody had paid me. Same failure as case one in a costume, or a different one?

Case three, the ones that work. Deposit contract: you lose 200 bucks if you don't log a run by Sunday. Here the external incentive seems to manufacture the intention rather than wreck it. And not just by shifting payoffs — people describe the decision as feeling more real, not less.

So the thing I want a word for: the pattern where being offered a reason to be in a state of mind is exactly what puts the state out of reach, while in neighbouring cases an offer does the reverse. I've seen people wave at the idea that you can't intend at will, and at the idea that money just isn't the right sort of reason to want something, and at the effect where paying kids to read makes them stop liking reading. None of them seem to cover case two, where the problem isn't that I can't produce the state but that the state I can produce has been contaminated. And none of them tell me what flips the sign between case two and case three.

Here's the variant that bugs me most. Suppose the stranger doesn't offer cash. He says: if you've decided by morning, I'll fly your whole family in to watch you take the pill, and I'll tell them what you're doing. Now there's no difficulty at all — obviously you can decide, you'll have a reason at the time. But look at the move: you didn't get control over your own mind, you changed the world so that the decision became the kind of thing there's a reason for. Is that a solution to the puzzle or just a refusal to answer it?

What I'm after: does this general pattern have a name, or is it two or three things I should be pulling apart? Where's the boundary between the offers that poison the state and the ones that produce it? Does it matter that in my cases the state is an intention rather than, say, a belief — someone offering you money to believe your team will win seems broken in a different way, and I'd like to know if that's the same brokenness. And what would you call the move in that last variant, where you fix the world instead of the mind? I'm really not after a reading list here, just the carving.
