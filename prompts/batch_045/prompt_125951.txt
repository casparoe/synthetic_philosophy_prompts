I'm in the second year of a PhD in philosophy, working mostly on philosophy of action, and I've been circling Kavka's toxin puzzle for a while: an eccentric billionaire offers you a large sum, paid at midnight, solely on the condition that at that moment you intend to drink, the following afternoon, a vial of stuff that will make you acutely miserable for a day but cause no lasting harm. Once the money has landed there is no remaining reason to drink — nobody is watching, no pre-commitment device is available, your reputation isn't at stake, and it's the intending that's being paid for, not the drinking. The question that hooks me is whether a fully transparent agent, one who knows all of this about itself, can form that intention at all, and what the answer shows about how intending differs from merely wanting, or from confidently predicting one's own future behaviour.

I've been playing with some variants of my own, and I want to know which of them, if any, is doing real work. In the iterated case the billionaire repeats the offer weekly for a year: each week you're paid for intending, and each week you then decline to drink, so the only thing that could make the later rounds different from the first is something about character, self-trust, or the cumulative effect of failing to follow through. In the threshold case it's a group: ten people each get paid if and only if at least six of them intend to drink, and each knows the others are reasoning exactly as they are. And there's a version I care about because of some side reading on AI: an artificial agent is asked to preserve a channel through which its operators could override or shut it down, while knowing that at the moment the override becomes available it will have no reason to comply and every reason not to. My hunch is that the iterated and collective variants are where the conceptual action is, but I can't tell whether they generate genuinely new problems or merely redescribe the original one with extra machinery bolted on.

So here is what I'd like. I have roughly one semester of writing time and a small budget to bring three or four people together for a day, and I have to decide what to spend them on. Give me an ordered agenda: which questions about toxin-style cases are most worth that time, and why in that order. I want the ordering defended rather than asserted — say what criteria you're using, whether that's how much else depends on the answer, tractability with the tools of philosophy of action rather than empirical psychology, or how crowded versus neglected a line already is, and be explicit about where those criteria pull against each other. I also want to know what you'd deliberately drop: which lines look live but are really terminological, which turn out to be the original puzzle in a costume, and which would take more than a semester to get anywhere on. Then tell me what would change your ranking — what argument or observation over the next year would move something up or down the list. I'm not asking for a survey of the literature or for citations; I know the standard dialectic well enough and can chase references later. I want your own view on where the leverage actually is.
