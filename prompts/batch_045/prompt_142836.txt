My friend Priya has been writing a tiny advice column for expats and long-stay travelers for about six years — people write in with "is it rude if I..." questions — and she's not a philosopher (she used to run warehouses or something), but her verdicts on everyday-convention stuff are more consistant than anything I've read by actual philosophers on it. As best I can reconstruct her track record: not tipping at a US sit-down restaurant where staff are legally paid a sub-minimum wage is really wrong, because you sat down knowing the menu price wasn't the whole price; not tipping at a Tokyo ramen counter is obviously fine, and a tourist who insists on tipping there is doing something mildly wrong — her word was something like colonizing, dragging your own script into a place that didn't ask for it. Cutting the line at a Philadelphia bakery: wrong. Shoving into the scrum at a bus stop in a city that genuinely has no line: fine, there's no convention there to break. Jaywalking across a dead-empty street at 1am: fine. Jaywalking across the same kind of street in a city where pedestrians reliably wait for the signal, with a kid standing there watching: wrong, she said something about what you're teaching. Standing on the left of a London escalator: wrong, and she added that she personally thinks the stand-right convention is dumb and probably unsafe, but that's not the point. Taking a phone call in an Amtrak quiet car: wrong, and — this is the one that got me — still wrong if the person next to you isn't actually bothered. Two limit cases: in a shoes-off house where the host says please keep your shoes on, we truly don't care, she said keep them on, the waiver is real; and for a guy whose office expects everyone to stay till 7, she said that's a labor problem and not an obligation, you're allowed to erode it.

Here are three cases I want her ruling on, and I can't tell which of her principles is supposed to govern them. (1) My sister is spending a week in a mid-sized city in southern Europe where, until maybe five years ago, nobody tipped; locals still don't tip at neighborhood places, but in the tourist center the last few years of American and British visitors leaving 15-20% have created a real expectation, and it's an expectation that runs only toward foreigners — and the locals Priya's readers quote are annoyed about it, they think visitors are importing a two-tier custom and giving restaurants cover to cut base pay. So my sister sits down at a tourist-center place: does she owe a tip? She's literally the kind of person the expectation was built out of, she knows about it, she takes the service, and paying feeds a norm the locals resent. Does it change anything if she's the first foreigner of the season versus the ten-thousandth, or if the person serving her is the owner's daughter versus someone whose hourly was quietly cut because management now counts tips? (2) The one I actually find interesting: a norm nobody endorses. An office where every person stands when the regional director walks in, and every person privately thinks it's humiliating and wrongly believes everyone else expects it; a consultant visiting from another branch stays seated. Wrong, fine, or neither — and does it matter that no one accepts the norm, or is "real regularity, everyone expects it" the end of the matter for her? (3) A café where regulars rinse their cup and put it on a specific shelf; visitors leave theirs on the table and get silently resented but are never told. Has the visitor done anything wrong, and does it matter that they couldn't have found out?

I don't just want the verdicts, I want the rule. My guesses at what she's running: something about expectations you create by opting in; something about free riding on everyone else's compliance; something about what nonconformance expresses, contempt or obliviousness or a refusal to treat locals as authorities on their own shared life; and something like extra deference owed by guests — but maybe she's actually running separate rules for coordination conventions, conventions that transfer small amounts of money, and deference rituals, and just hasn't said so. Give me your best guess at her answer on each of the three with the reason, tell me which candidate principles fit her whole track record roughly equally well and exactly where they come apart, and be honest if her past verdicts genuinely underdetermine these cases — and if they do, say what principle she'd be committing herself to by going each way. And since I'm seeing her for dinner next week: name one further case I should pose that would separate the surviving rules, the one where they'd definately have to diverge.
