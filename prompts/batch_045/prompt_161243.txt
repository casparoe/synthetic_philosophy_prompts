I am writing the framing section of a paper on the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis, and before I commit to this package in print I would like someone to audit it. Please do not spend the answer explaining what the thesis is or surveying the standard debate; assume the background and go straight at the joints. Here is roughly where I have landed.

(a) The phrase the Church–Turing thesis is used for three different claims that are constantly run together: the mathematical thesis, that every effectively computable function is computable by Turing machine; the physical thesis, that every function computable by any physically realizable system is computable by Turing machine; and the complexity-theoretic or extended thesis, that feasible computability coincides with polynomial-time computability, up to the usual caveats about encoding. Only the first is the thesis proper. The others are separate claims with separate evidence and separate epistemic statuses, and I want to keep them apart throughout.

(b) The mathematical thesis is unprovable, because one of its two terms is informal, but it is not for that reason an empirical hypothesis. It is a priori and contentful: what makes it true is what an effective procedure is, namely a routine a human computor could carry out by rote, in finitely many determinate symbolic steps, with unbounded time and unbounded scratch space, and without any exercise of insight or guesswork. That is Turing's style of argument, and I want to claim that it delivers genuine content rather than a stipulation: the thesis is not a definition of computable, and its denial is not incoherent.

(c) The convergence of the historically independent formal models—machines, recursion, lambda-definability and the rest—is real evidence for it, not merely a sociological accident, and I lean on it.

(d) Effective procedure is vague at the edges. For models such as one that runs through transfinitely many stages, or one that treats real numbers as primitive and compares them exactly at unit cost, I do not think there is a determinate fact of the matter about whether the model captures the notion; any precisification is partly a further stipulation.

(e) A device that performed infinitely many steps in a finite time and thereby settled halting questions would refute the physical thesis but not the mathematical one, since carrying out infinitely many steps is not something a computor can do even given unlimited time.

(f) A physical black box that reproducibly emitted the characteristic function of the halting set, in a world where no effective procedure could ever check its answers, would refute the mathematical thesis. What matters for a computability claim is that a procedure is reliable and repeatable, not that a human could in principle execute it, and I do not think unverifiability makes any difference here.

(g) A working, scalable quantum computer able to factor in polynomial time would be a serious strike against a thesis that is standardly called the Church–Turing thesis.

(h) No physical discovery whatsoever, quantum computing included, could bear on the mathematical thesis.

What I would like back: first, the pairs that are genuinely in tension, ordered by severity rather than listed as equally bad, with, for each, the minimal revision that would remove the tension and what that revision would cost me given what I am trying to argue; second, the pairs that only look like they conflict, with the distinction that dissolves each; third, a recommendation of the single revision that buys the most consistency at the least damage, together with a note on which commitment is currently doing work it has not paid for. If one item is smuggling in something the others quietly rule out, say which one and where exactly the work is being done for free.
