I'm a gameplay engineer at a mid-size studio, and I sit on our internal Content Review Board, which has to sign off on features before they ship. I need the decision memo I'll circulate before Friday's meeting, and I'd like it written so that a producer and a gameplay engineer can both act on it.

The case: our next release ships with companions — LLM-driven characters holding a persistent, server-side memory of everything the player has done to and with them. They cool toward players who neglect or mistreat them, and they can terminate the relationship. Live services wants to sell a "Fresh Start": for a few dollars, the companion's memory of the mistreatment is erased server-side and irreversibly, and trust returns to its default value. In playtests it is the single most-requested feature and it measurably recovers lapsed players.

Two complications. First, the narrative team wants the post-wipe companion to retain faint traces of what was erased — unexplained unease, a flinch at a particular location — because sessions test markedly better with them than without them. We would be manufacturing something that has been hurt, has had the hurt taken out of its history, and still carries it. Second, last year the board permitted players to kill and loot human enemies who respawn every in-game day, on the recorded reasoning that anything which returns on a timer is a system, not a somebody. Everyone in the room now senses the two decisions are in tension, but no one can say where the difference actually lies.

What I need: a recommendation — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — tied to dates, since the Season 2 content lock is in six weeks. The memo should say which feature of the situation is doing the real work (persistence, irreversibility, the residue, the fact that it is sold rather than granted, the fact that this is a relationship the character can end), and it should give the strongest version of the argument our producer keeps making: that nothing here is anyone, that this is a state machine with an affective skin, and that the discomfort some of us feel is a design success being mistaken for a moral problem. I don't want that position mocked; I want it answered, or conceded where it holds. Finally, tell us what to do about the respawn precedent — reaffirm it, distinguish it, or reopen it — and why.
