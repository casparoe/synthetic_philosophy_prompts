Me and two other people from my metaphysics reading group got into an argument after last week's session and I've been chewing on it since — I have to hand in a 2000-word response paper on Friday and I want to know whether my hunch survives being written down.

What we started with was epiphenomenalism and the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if phenomenal properties have no causal effects, then my judgment "I'm in pain right now" isn't caused by the pain, and given causal closure its physical cause is supposed to be complete without the pain. So it looks like (i) the match between the pain and the judgment is a coincidence, (ii) worse, it's unclear how the judgment even manages to be about the pain, since the usual causal or informational stories about how a mental state gets hooked onto its referent don't seem available here, and (iii) the judgment isn't warranted, and the view saws off the branch it's sitting on, since believing epiphenomenalism on the basis of introspection is itself a judgment that no phenomenal state caused. One of the others said this is just a costume change on the familiar objection to mathematical Platonism — mathematical truths are about abstract objects outside spacetime and causally inert, so how could our mathematical beliefs reliably track them? I said no, and I said the difference is modal: mathematical facts are necessary if true at all, so the Platonist only has to explain how a truth-preserving process (deduction from true axioms, or some a priori faculty) could latch onto them in the first place, whereas phenomenal facts are contingent — on any given occasion the pain might not have been there — and causal closure means the judgment would come out exactly the same in a world with no pain, so the epiphenomenalist owes a *contingent* explanation of the match (a psychophysical law, a pre-established harmony, an identity, a constitution relation) that the Platonist doesn't owe. I like this but I can't tell how far it actually goes.

So please compare the two problems properly. Is the asymmetry I pointed at real, and does it do the work I want it to do? Where else do they diverge: the Platonist has no analogue of (ii) or (iii) as far as I can see, but is that because the Platonist's problem is easier, or because epiphenomenalism has a resource the Platonist lacks — the judgment and its target are both "in" the same subject, so identity or constitution might be able to do the job that causation does for ordinary empirical beliefs? And here's the bit I most want pushed back on: the Platonist has a plenitude escape — say every consistent mathematical structure exists, and the correlation stops being spooky because whatever we end up believing is true of some structure or other. Is there an analogous move for the epiphenomenalist (every physical state has some phenomenal character; the judgment is made true by whatever phenomenal state happens to co-occur; the subject's phenomenal life is rich enough that any introspective report hits something), and does it hollow out the claim the way plenitude arguably hollows out Platonism? Overall, which of the two problems is genuinely harder to answer, and which one should I spend my 2000 words on — if my framing of either problem is wrong, say so and tell me what I'm missing, because I'd rather find that out now than on Friday.
