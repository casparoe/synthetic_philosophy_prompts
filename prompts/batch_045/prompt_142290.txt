I teach a twelve-week upper-level seminar on early modern metaphysics and I am setting the final paper. The students have read Berkeley's *Principles* up to the point where he turns to the defence against the charge of scepticism, together with the first two of the *Three Dialogues*; the material on abstraction and on the likeness principle — that an idea can resemble nothing but an idea — is well covered, and they have had it in two problem sets already.

I want one essay question on the argument usually called the master argument: the passage in which Berkeley challenges the reader to try to conceive of a tree in a park, or a book in a closed room, existing unconceived, and takes the attempt to defeat itself, since in making it one has the very idea one was trying to exclude.

The problem I am trying to solve is that my students can all state this argument, and can all produce the stock reply to it: that it is circular or merely verbal, because trying to conceive of something makes it an object of thought, and so all Berkeley has shown is that a certain imaginative performance is impossible. I want a question that a student holding that reply and nothing else does badly on, and that forces them to settle what work the argument is supposed to do inside Berkeley's system rather than whether it looks valid in isolation.

My leading thought about the crux is this: what would be left out of Berkeley's case if the master argument were simply deleted, given that he already has the critique of abstract ideas and the likeness principle in hand? An answer must either treat the argument as dispensable ornament — and then explain why Berkeley gives it the weight he does — or show that it does something the other two cannot, most plausibly by closing off the retreat to an unknown somewhat, unlike any of our ideas, that our ideas are supposed to stand for or resemble; and then it must explain how the argument is meant to do that without simply assuming what the materialist denies. If you can see a sharper crux in the same neighbourhood, take it instead, but tell me what you changed and why.

Two further things should be built into the question. First, it should force a position on the distinction students reach for between conceiving or imagining something and merely supposing or affirming that it exists: whether Berkeley's claim is about our imaginative capacities or about what can be thought without contradiction, and why the answer matters for what the argument could prove. Second, it should give them a case Berkeley never uses — something no finite mind ever has perceived or ever will: a stratum of rock laid down before any perceivers existed, or the interior of a planet no one will ever observe. Ask them what the argument, if sound, tells us about such a thing. I want that case to do the work of exposing how much is carried by the move to some mind or other perceiving it, so that they have to say, without being asked outright, whether the appeal to God completes the argument or patches a hole in it. A stipulation is permitted if it helps — for instance, asking them to suppose Berkeley had no appeal to God available — but any stipulation must press on the argument itself rather than change the subject.

Constraints: this is one of four questions on a three-hour paper, so a forty-minute answer, targeted at about seven hundred words, marked out of twenty-five. Please produce:

1. The question itself, in a form I could put on the paper. It may have one short sentence of setup. It must not be answerable by reporting Berkeley's conclusions.
2. A model answer of roughly six hundred to seven hundred words, written as a very strong student's answer rather than as a commentary on the question.
3. A marking scheme out of twenty-five, built around the moves an A answer must contain, the moves that earn a B, and the specific errors that should cost marks — including the stock circularity objection deployed badly, and the slide from our being unable to form a positive conception of mind-independent sensible existence to the conclusion that there is no such thing.
