I work on distributed systems — capacity planning, incident response, the occasional postmortem — and I've been having a running argument with a friend who does computational social science about whether you can understand a system by understanding its parts. She says this is a version of a long-running dispute in the philosophy of social science between methodological individualism and holism, and that the vocabulary I already use at work contains most of the relevant distinctions, so I'd rather have the debate translated into that vocabulary than explained to me from scratch. For calibration: I'm fluent in local rules vs. emergent global behavior, the difference between reading the code and running the system, unit tests vs. integration and load tests, per-request traces vs. aggregates like p99 latency and error budgets, interfaces and contracts and the way a contract constrains the teams on both sides of it, feedback effects like backpressure and circuit breakers where a system-level condition changes what each node subsequently does, queueing results that relate average queue length, arrival rate and waiting time while remaining indifferent to the details of the arrival and service processes, and agent-based simulations such as the checkerboard segregation models where mild individual preferences generate near-total segregation that nobody chose or intended. I have no philosophy training, so please don't assume I know terms like supervenience, multiple realizability, realization, or mechanistic explanation; if you need them, define them in my terms the first time they appear, and flag anywhere my vocabulary carries a built-in assumption of its own — for instance that systems are designed and have a spec — that a social domain would not.

The translation I want is this. Restate the individualist position — roughly, that social phenomena are to be explained by facts about individuals, their beliefs, reasons and actions, and that claims formulated only at the level of groups, institutions, markets, classes or norms are at best useful shorthand — and then restate the holist reply, including the rule I keep encountering in secondary summaries that social facts should be explained by other social facts, and the claim that some social-level facts do causal work of their own. Do it in the vocabulary above, and lean on my two examples: the segregation simulation, and the queueing-style aggregate law that is insensitive to micro-level detail. I also want one distinction of my own tested rather than adopted. At work I separate macro-level claims that are *derivable* from the local rules — you could get them by reasoning symbolically about the code without executing it — from claims that are merely *simulable*, where the pattern shows up only once you run the system and watch, and no amount of reading the specification would have told you. My hunch is that this is where the real content of the emergence argument lives, and that it is orthogonal to whether individuals are the only things doing the causing: a pattern can be simulable but not derivable and still be nothing over and above individual behavior, in which case the individualist can concede everything the holist points at and still win the metaphysical half of the fight. Tell me whether that is right, whether philosophy already has a distinction that does this job better and whether it is the same axis or a different one, and whether my hunch quietly relies on a picture of causation the holist would reject.

The part I care about most is the residue report: which claims in this debate map cleanly onto things I already say at work, which survive only if you distort them to fit, and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. My guess is that anything turning on the difference between an agent's reasons for acting and the causes of an event, and anything about whether an explanation is supposed to exhibit a mechanism or to single out a difference-maker, lands in the third bucket — but say if I'm wrong about that too. For each piece of residue, say whether you think it reveals something my engineering vocabulary is missing that I should care about in my actual job, or whether it reveals that the philosophical claim was less clear than it sounded. Please state how confident you are in each main part of the translation, and single out the one claim you are least sure of; I would much rather have a flagged guess than a seamless-sounding answer.
