Prepping a political-philosophy seminar on deterrence and I want a sharp comparison, not a survey. Call the first mechanism leaving something to chance: you arrange things so the final decision is no longer fully yours — a tripwire force you can't defend, timelines so short that a blip can start a process nobody can stop — so the adversary's aggression risks setting off a war you'd both rather avoid. Call the second mechanism precommitment by self-blinding: you publicly and verifiably destroy your own capacity to *discriminate* rather than your capacity to choose — a deliberately coarse warning net, a retaliatory authority that recieves only one bit, attack or no attack, with no picture of scale, target, or intent. Both buy credibility by giving up something about your own future agency, but they give up different things: control over whether you respond vs. knowledge of what you're responding to. Compare them on three points: (1) false positives and accidental war, (2) whether each dampens or amplifies the reciprocal fear of surprise attack once a crisis is underway, (3) whether each is even available to a state whose second-strike force is vulnerable rather than secure. Then say which of the two, if either, is really a case of cultivating irrationality for rational ends — or whether that framing muddies more than it explains. American English please, and keep it under a page if you can.
