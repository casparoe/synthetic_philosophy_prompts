I spent forty years in experimental physical chemistry — infrared and Raman spectroscopy, for the most part — and since retiring I have been reading my way into the philosophy of mathematics, which is the branch of philosophy with the most in common with what I used to do. I have just finished a chapter on intuitionism and constructivism in a well-reviewed popular book on the foundations of mathematics. I do not trust my own reaction to it. My paraphrase of the passage follows; the wording is mine, but I believe it is faithful.

Classical mathematics assumes that every meaningful statement about numbers is already determinately true or false, whether or not anyone can ever find out which — so it insists that either there are infinitely many twin primes or there are not, even if the question is never settled. Intuitionists, following Brouwer, deny this: they identify truth with provability, so a statement becomes true only when someone actually produces a proof of it, and mathematics is consequently a free creation of the human mind rather than a description of an independent abstract world. In practice this means giving up proof by contradiction together with the law of excluded middle, and replacing the classical notion of existence — that some object satisfying a condition must be out there — with the demand that we exhibit the object, which in effect means producing an algorithm that computes it. The cost is considerable: large parts of classical analysis collapse, the intermediate value theorem is false, and intuitionists are stuck with the bizarre consequence that every function on the real numbers is continuous. The compensating gain is that anything which survives is automatically computable, which is why constructive mathematics has become the natural home of computer-assisted proof. And in the end the quarrel is only about which conventions to adopt, since the classical and intuitionistic theories can be translated into one another and prove the same results up to a double negation.

Two things make me suspicious. The passage makes the intuitionists sound like cranks — the continuity claim especially — and my experience is that when a popular account makes a serious position sound silly, the account is concealing something. And the final sentence, about conventions, seems to me to sit badly with the earlier claim about existence: if the two theories were merely notational variants of each other, nothing would be at stake, and plainly something is. But I cannot locate the misstatement, and I do not know which of these claims a working constructivist would cheerfully sign.

For what it is worth, here is a test of my own, not the book's. When told that a classical theorem fails constructively, I ask whether the failure shows up as a loss of significant figures — that is, whether a finite-precision version of the theorem would have been useless to me at the bench. On that test the intermediate value theorem fails: bisection on a function I can only evaluate to a few digits yields a sign change in a small interval, not a root. So I suspect the constructive complaint is really my complaint about measurement precision, wearing a philosophical hat. I should like to know whether that reading is defensible or whether it is a species of category mistake.
