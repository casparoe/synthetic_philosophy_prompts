I'm on the process committee at my company, and we own a huge inherited list of engineering rules — required sign-offs, banned libraries, mandatory fields, review gates. We've been pruning it, arguing about Chesterton's fence constantly, and I've noticed we might actually be running two different rules that only come apart on the case in front of us now.

Our verdicts so far:

- Kept a rule requiring a second sign-off on database migrations, because nobody could find out why it was there.
- Deleted a rule forcing all new services onto an internal framework, after someone dug up that the original constraint was a compiler limitation fixed years ago, and nothing else had grown up around it.
- Kept a ban on a certain library even after we found the license problem behind it had been resolved, because a pile of tooling and docs had been built assuming the ban.
- Deleted a rule added only two years ago by a team that's since disbanded, once we found its rationale and saw it no longer applied, even though we couldn't fully trace what it interacted with.

The new one: a fourteen-year-old rule blocking a class of bulk write. We found the incident from 2011 that prompted it and the fix that obsoleted it, so the reason is dead. But it's now the only thing still exercising a rarely used recovery path, three teams' runbooks point at it, and there's a decent chance nobody ever chose it deliberately — it may just have accreted out of a default in an old deploy script.

Which way should we go to stay consistent with ourselves? I think one reading of our track record says delete it (we know why it went up and that reason is gone) and another says keep it (fourteen years of accretion, and we cant rule out that it's load bearing somewhere we can't see). Tell me which fits our precedents better, which precedent is the closest analog, and what principle we'd be committing to either way. I especially want to know whether the "fence nobody built" part should change the answer, given that Chesterton's whole idea seems to assume someboody put it up for a reason. And what case would actually settle which of the two rules we've been running?
