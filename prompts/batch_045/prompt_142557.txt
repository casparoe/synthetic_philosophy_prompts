I work in the Long-Term Appraisal Unit of a national infrastructure ministry. Four or five times a year I draft the recommendation that our Intergenerational Appraisal Panel signs: a standing committee of seven people, none of them an economist or a philosopher, deciding by simple majority, each decision recorded in a minute that applicants, auditors, and my own successors treat as binding. I write the draft ten days before the sitting and I cannot ask the Panel to clarify anything in advance. The minute has to be defensible three years later to an auditor whose only materials are the earlier minutes, so I need the reasoning in it to be explicit, and I need to know now which of my numbers actually carry the decision.

The Panel's record on four past proposals is the whole of our precedent. (1) Havenmouth tidal barrier, approved 7–0: a barrier protecting a coastal town for 120 years, positive in present value at 1.4% real, negative at the 3.5% real rate in our central guidance; the beneficiaries are present residents and their descendants, and the Panel noted that who those beneficiaries are does not depend on whether the barrier is built. The minute declines to apply the standard rate to the far-future tail. (2) Crowthorne deferral, rejected 7–0: a financing scheme that hands the present cohort a real gain of one unit of consumption and imposes an equal, uncompensated real loss on a cohort 150 years out, with zero per-capita growth and cohorts of equal size assumed. The minute says that the bare lateness of a cost is not in itself a reason to prefer incurring it, and that an uncompensated reshuffle between cohorts is not an improvement. (3) Kelvin Valley retrofit, approved 7–0: benefits 200 years and more out, and the minute records that the Panel counted only the difference made to people whose existence and identity do not depend on the decision, setting aside any benefit consisting in different people existing; three members noted that they would have counted those benefits too and that here it made no difference to the verdict. (4) Ardmore archive, rejected 4–3: cost now, benefit only in a low-probability civilisation-disrupting scenario, with the beneficiaries overwhelmingly people who would not exist under the no-project trajectory. The majority held that on what the minutes call the contingency test there is almost no one who is made better off, so that the benefit column is nearly empty while the cost column is real. Two dissenters would have counted benefits to people who exist only because of the decision; the third invoked what she called slot-matching, comparing the ranked list of welfare levels across the two trajectories rather than tracking individuals, under which the benefit is large and real.

The new file is Project Longwatch, and it is Ardmore with better engineering. It costs $900m now plus $8m a year in real terms for 500 years, the latter falling on taxpayers who exist under every trajectory we are considering. It matters only if a civilisation-disrupting catastrophe occurs, for which the Panel's own working figure is 1 in 150 over the window; if one does, it shortens recovery from roughly 300 years to roughly 150. The Panel's demographic model says the two recovery trajectories contain the same number of person-years, occupied by different individuals from about a decade after the catastrophe onward, with welfare per person-year about 20% higher in consumption-equivalent terms on the faster trajectory; the benefit stream is consequently centred somewhere between 250 and 400 years out. Our annexe puts the expected benefit, counting every person-year on the fast trajectory and ignoring identity, at about $11–14bn in present value on a schedule that declines to 1% beyond 75 years, about $60bn with no discounting for time at all, and roughly $1bn at a flat 1.5%; against that, cost is about $4.9bn undiscounted and under $2bn in present value on any positive schedule. On the contingency test, by contrast, only the first decade or so of the recovery involves people who exist either way, which is a small fraction of the benefit on any schedule. My unit has two years of work in this file and the minister is briefed to announce it in the spring, so if consistency with our own record points the other way I would rather be told now than by the Panel.

Give me your answer as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no closing paragraph:

1. The rule you think the Panel is actually running, stated tightly enough that I could apply it to a fresh file without you, including what it says about discounting for time and what it says about people whose existence depends on the decision.
2. Your prediction for Longwatch: approve or reject, the vote you expect, and a probability attached to that prediction.
3. The strongest rival reading of the four precedents, what it predicts here, and the single clause that decides you against it.
4. Which precedent is the closest analog, and what principle the Panel would be adopting, in terms I could put in a minute, if it decides the other way from your prediction.
5. The one further case I should put in front of the Panel to settle which reading is right, and what I should write in the minute in the meantime if I cannot get that case onto an agenda before the spring announcement.

Commit to a position throughout. I want one verdict with a number on it, not a menu of readings for me to choose between: name the rival only in order to say why you are not backing it. Where our precedents genuinely run out, say so in that item and then say which way you would jump anyway.
