I'm putting together a seminar on the philosophy of corruption (clientelism, gift-giving, and whether our Western anti-bribery instincts are really universal) and I want you to analyze a real episode for me: the Lockheed bribery scandal, which occured back in the 1970s. The facts, as I'll stipulate them (dont go digging for obscure details, and dont worry if your memory of the case is a bit vague): Lockheed, a big US aerospace company, wanted to sell planes abroad, and it made large, secret payments to politicians and officials in several countries. In Japan, Prime Minister Kakuei Tanaka and some intermediaries were caught up in it; Tanaka was eventually convicted of taking bribes, though he kept a lot of local support. In the US, the scandal fed into the passage of a 1977 law that barred American companies from paying foreign officials to win or keep business. The Japanese side is often described as involving clientelist networks; its often said that patrons hand out benefits and money to clients in exchange for political support, and that gift-giving and reciprocal obligation are central to how the system runs. Dont get bogged down in the exact legal niceties of the US statute or the precise procedural history of Tanaka's appeals; I'm not asking for a legal brief, and I don't want you to lean on any contested detail about who exactly authorized what.

The lens I want you to use is a distinction between two ways of thinking about what a payment like this is. First, call it the price-in-the-wrong-sphere view: the payment is corrupt because it introduces a market-like price into a sphere that should be governed by impersonal rules and the impartial duties of a public role. On this view, the official's job was to award contracts on impartial criteria, and Lockheed buying influence was a betrayal of that role; the secrecy and the involvement of a foreign firm are aggravating, not essential, because the core wrong is using money to bend a decision that isn't supposed to be for sale. Second, call it the constitutive-gift view: the same transfer isnt primarily a price that buys one discrete favor. Its a move inside a clientelist network where gifts, money, and favors create and sustain durable bonds of loyalty and reciprocal obligation between patron and client. On this view, what looks like bribery from the outside can be, from the inside, the normal (maybe even obligatory) maintenance of a relationship; the gift is partly constitutive of the political order, not a deviation from it. Keep in mind the further wrinkle about universalism: is calling this corruption a universal impartialist judgment that gets exported, or is it a local judgment that only makes sense if you already accept that certain spheres, like procurement or office, should be impartial in the first place?

So: run the analysis in both directions. Map the episode through both views. Who are the relevant actors, what options did they face, and what did each view say they should have done? What does each view explain about the case—the secrecy, the intermediaries, the foreign firm, the fact that Tanaka's local support survived the conviction, the US response—and what does each view miss or actively misdescribe? Then turn it around: what does the episode show about the theories? Does it confirm the impartialist/public-role account, does it show that the clientelist/gift account is just a rationalization, does it reveal that corruption names a family of things rather than one natural kind, or something else? Be disciplined about the mapping: if the constitutive-gift view cant actually account for the secrecy or the scale of the payments without cheating, say so, and dont force the theory to speak where its silent.
