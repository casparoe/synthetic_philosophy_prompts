Here's what I need argued. I've been working with a split between two things people run together when they say "fundamental":

- terminally fundamental: nothing grounds it — it sits at the bottom of a grounding chain.
- basally fundamental: it belongs to some minimal complete basis, i.e. a set that grounds everything else and no proper subset of which does.

My thesis is that the second is strictly stronger than the first, and that the gap between them does real work. So argue this: foundationalism about grounding can be completely true — every chain bottoms out, no infinite regresses, no circles, no funny business — while there is still no fundamental level in the sense anybody actually wants. The terminally fundamental facts don't jointly ground everything, they don't form a basis, and "reality has a fundamental level" is false.

Some constraints on how you do it:

1. Pick the mechanism that carries the weight and say explicitly that you're picking it. Candidates: grounding isn't transitive; grounding doesn't aggregate, so from "a is grounded in b" and "c is grounded in d" nothing follows about the pairs taken together; grounding is contrastive, so there's no univocal thing that is "the grounds of" a fact; partial grounding, where some facts are only ever partly grounded and never fully. Or come up with a better one. Don't gesture at all four — choose one and make it bear the load.
