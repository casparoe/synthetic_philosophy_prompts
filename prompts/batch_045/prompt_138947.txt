THE QUESTION (Q3, 20 marks)

Assume non-reductive physicalism about a mental property M and its physical realizer P: M supervenes on P and is realized by P, but M is not identical with P. Assume also that the physical domain is causally closed. Kim's causal exclusion argument concludes that, given these assumptions, M does no causal work in producing the bodily movement it is supposed to produce.

(a) Set the argument out step by step, being explicit about (i) what the closure claim says, (ii) what the exclusion principle says, and (iii) what work the non-identity of M and P is doing. Say clearly which premise is doing which job.

(b) Now consider the staggered realization case. M is instantiated across the interval from t1 to t2, but P, the physical property that realizes M, is not fully instantiated until t2: at t1 only the early stages of the physical process that will later constitute P are present. At t2 the arm goes up. Does the exclusion argument still get off the ground here? Identify which premise the case is supposed to put pressure on, and say what follows either way.

THE ANSWER

Kim's argument has three moving parts. First, closure: every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause. My arm going up is a physical event with a cause, so it has a sufficient physical cause, call it P-star. Second, the exclusion principle: no event has more than one sufficient cause at a given time, unless it is a genuine case of overdetermination. Third, non-identity: M is not identical to P, so M and its realizer are two distinct candidate causes of the same event. Now closure already tells us that the physical cause is doing all the work, and the exclusion principle just spells out what that means, since if P-star is sufficient then M is either the same cause again or redundant. The exclusion principle is therefore really just closure plus a bit of common sense about not multiplying causes. Non-identity is what makes the two compete in the first place: if M and P were one and the same property there would be only one cause and nothing to exclude, which is why the identity theory is untroubled by all this.

If we want to keep mental causation we have to give up a premise. We could accept epiphenomenalism, where M exists and is caused by the physical but causes nothing itself. Or we could deny closure and let the mental in as an extra cause. Or we could bite the bullet and say every case is a case of systematic overdetermination, which seems desperate because it would make every instance of mental causation a wild coincidence with no explanation of the correlation. Reduction, identifying M with P, would get us out too, but it isn't really available, since mental properties are multiply realizable and there is no single physical property to identify them with. That is why the argument is in the end an argument for eliminativism: Kim's real conclusion is that there are no mental properties, at least none that do anything.

(b) In the staggered case there is no time at which both M and a sufficient physical cause of the arm rising are present. At t1 M is there but the physical cause is not complete, and at t2 the physical cause is complete. Since closure requires a sufficient physical cause to be present at every time, and there isn't one at t1, closure fails at t1 and M is the only available cause of what happens later. So the exclusion argument cannot get started here. The case puts pressure on the exclusion principle, because excludion only applies when two sufficient causes are present at the same time, and it therefore rescues mental causation for any mental property that takes time to be realized.
