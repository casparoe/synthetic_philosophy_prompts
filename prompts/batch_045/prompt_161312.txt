Question first, then the student's answer, both pasted verbatim. I dont want to prejudice you by saying what I think of it, so: you tell me.

Setup:

A five-member employment tribunal decides wrongful-dismissal complaints. The statute it applies says:

The employer is liable (L) iff the employee was engaged in protected activity (P) and either the dismissal was motivated by that activity (Q) or the employer's stated reason for the dismissal was pretextual (R).

So L ↔ P ∧ (Q ∨ R). Assume everybody votes their honest view, no strategising.

The tribunal's rules: findings on P, Q and R are made by simple majority (3 of 5). A finding of liability requires 4 of 5 members. The board votes on P, Q, R and L separately — it does not take a separate vote on "Q or R" as such.

The votes in the case before it:

| member | P | Q | R | L |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Alvarez | yes | yes | no | yes |
| Bhatt | yes | no | yes | yes |
| Cohen | yes | no | no | no |
| Dvorak | no | yes | yes | no |
| Ellis | no | no | yes | no |

Every member's four votes fit the statute.

Question (20 marks):

(a) (5 marks) Work out the collective judgment on P, on Q, on R and on L under propositionwise majority voting, and say whether this tribunal faces a discursive dilemma on this profile. Briefly, why or why not?

(b) (7 marks) Two reform proposals are on the table: (i) premise-based — decide P, Q and R by majority and let the statute settle L; (ii) conclusion-based — stop recording votes on P, Q and R and decide only L, keeping the 4-of-5 rule. Give the outcome under each, and say whether either proposal makes the dilemma go away. Would it make a difference if the 4-of-5 rule were applied to P, Q and R as well?

(c) (8 marks) A member objects to (i): "we need four votes before we can find liability, but this procedure finds liability on the strength of three votes for P and three for R, and only two of us actually accept L — that is quietly rewriting the rule." Is she right? Explain what her objection shows about premise-based aggregation.

Student's answer:

(a) P: 3–2 yes (Alvarez, Bhatt, Cohen). Q: 2–3, so no. R: 3–2 yes (Bhatt, Dvorak, Ellis). L: 2–3, so no — and it misses the four-vote rule as well. So yes, there is a discursive dilemma: a majority accepts P and a majority accepts R, and the statute makes those two together sufficient for L, but a majority — three of the five — rejects L. Nobody is individually inconsistent, all five voted in line with the statute, so the trouble is entirely in how the votes get put together. Really this is Condorcet's paradox in a different costume: the group's majority relation over the issues fails to settle into a coherent position, the way majority preferences can cycle.

(b) (i) Premise-based: P yes, Q no, R yes, so Q ∨ R is yes, and with P yes the statute gives L — liable. (ii) Conclusion-based: only two members accept L, so the tribunal finds no liability, whether the rule is four votes or a simple majority. The two procedures disagree, and neither one makes the dilemma go away; the dilemma just is the fact that they disagree and you have to pick one. If the four-vote rule applied to P, Q and R as well, then P and R both fail to reach four votes, no premise gets established, and the premise-based route gives no liability too — both procedures would agree and the dilemma would vanish.

(c) I dont think the objection is right. The four-vote rule tells you how many members must vote for the conclusion of liability; it says nothing about how that conclusion is arrived at. Once three members accept P and three accept R, the statute does the rest, and the statute is law rather than a headcount — logic decides that L follows, not the board. So no rule has been rewritten: the board voted on the propositions it was told to vote on and applied the threshold it was given. It is also worth remembering that premise-based voting is the only one of these procedures that guarantees a consistent collective judgment set, since it takes majority votes on logically independent premises and derives the rest from them; majority voting on conclusions offers no such guarantee once there is more than one conclusion on the agenda.
