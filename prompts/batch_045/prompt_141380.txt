I have spent most of my career in aircraft certification and now work on safety management systems, and I have been following the debate about artificial intelligence from the outside. The pairing that keeps coming up there — an "alignment tax," meaning the cost in capability or in time-to-market of making a system safe, together with a "competitiveness constraint," meaning the pressure not to fall behind rivals — reads to me like a problem my own industry has lived with since the dawn of commercial aviation. I want to know whether that is a real structural resemblance or a pun on the word safety. Please do not explain my vocabulary back to me; use it.

Restate the whole shape of the debate in certification terms, as faithfully as you can. That means: what the alignment tax is claimed to be and what it is denominated in; what the competitiveness constraint is and why the two together are supposed to produce a race toward the bottom; and what the main proposed remedies — licensing or a regulatory floor, a pause or moratorium, differential development of safe and unsafe capabilities, unilateral restraint by a leading developer, restricting the inputs rather than the outputs — look like when translated into type certification, a certification basis and its amendments, means of compliance, the way a derivative airframe inherits the basis of its predecessor, airworthiness directives, continuing airworthiness, service difficulty reporting, and safety management systems. Use as your worked example the re-engined narrowbody whose handling-quirk software fix was not treated as a failure condition in its own right, was certified on a stretched basis with much of the work delegated to the manufacturer's own engineers, and then lost two hull losses and was grounded worldwide. I have watched that story from the inside, and I want to see where the AI arguments land on it.

Three distinctions I would like carried across rather than flattened. First, lag versus rate: does safety work impose a one-time delay that can later be made up, or does it permanently reduce the rate of advance, so that the gap compounds? The two are very different in a race and I suspect the arguments quietly rely on one or the other. Second, scalar versus frontier: is the tax a single number, or is it the local slope of a safety/capability frontier whose shape matters — cheap assurance up front, marginal cost rising sharply as systems approach and exceed human-level performance in the relevant domains? Please say what happens to the arguments if the honest answer is that the tax is negligible now and approaches total at the frontier, i.e. denominated in whether you can field a competitive system at all. Third, private versus social tax: safety research leaks to competitors, so what a firm pays exceeds what the industry pays, and the incidence falls on whoever absorbs the delay, which need not be the party who would have been harmed. In my world a shared certification basis eliminates the competitive penalty altogether, because nobody loses market share by meeting a floor everyone meets, which is why mature industries tend to lobby for floors. Does the AI debate underweight that possibility, and if so, is that a mistake or a judgement that a floor is not available here?

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually want. My suspicion is that three features of my framework do all the work and none of them transfer: that a third party can inspect both the artifact and the hazard analysis and reach the same conclusion as the builder; that the remedy is reversible, since a fleet can be grounded and a directive issued after the fact; and that there is no gap between the machine behaving as designed and the machine doing what its builders wanted, because an airframe has no objectives of its own. If those are load-bearing, then certification solves races precisely because the dangerous properties of the artifact are verifiable and the failure is bounded, insured, and compensable — and the translation should make it visible that the competitiveness constraint is not a brute technological fact but a fact about the absence of a verifiable floor. But I may be smuggling in my framework's assumptions — a competent regulator with enforcement power, an industry that wants to be regulated, a stable artifact — and calling the result a translation. Tell me where I am doing that.

Finish by saying what the exercise suggests about the AI debate itself: whether the alignment tax is one phenomenon or several that should not share a name, whether the race is one dynamic or several, and whether there is a question my vocabulary exposes that the debate is not asking — for instance whether its concentration on pre-deployment assurance is a category error, given that in aviation most of the safety enterprise begins after the certificate is issued.
