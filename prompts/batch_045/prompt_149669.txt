I run a fortnightly reading group on decision theory — four of us, mixed backgrounds — and after working through Newcomb's problem, the smoking lesion and Parfit's hitchhiker we have arrived at the two cases I find hardest to keep straight: the counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail. My notes have them as near mirror images, and I can't quite say why. In the mugging the payoff sits in a branch of the past that has already closed against you, and the pay-up verdict is supposed to come from your disposition rather than from anything your act can still bring about. In the blackmail the threat condition is equally settled, but there the refusal verdict is supposed to come from the bad state not being downstream of your algorithm. I want to stop gesturing at that contrast, so I need one case that forces both structures into a single act.

What I'd like you to build is a scenario of at most about two hundred words, set somewhere ordinary — a workplace, a departmental budget, an estate, a small firm, a professional dispute — in which one act of handing over, or refusing to hand over, one sum of money is simultaneously a counterfactual mugging and an XOR blackmail and cannot be split into two acts. Constraints: there must be a single payment channel, so that paying the mugger just is paying the blackmailer; the two roles may be held by one person, who should want the money for an intelligible reason; nothing may predict better than a shrewd human being who knows the agent well, so state the accuracy you assume and state the demand's issuance rule in terms of what was foreseen rather than what will happen, so that the exclusive-or structure survives fallibility; no exotic physics and no precommitment devices, meaning no contracts, public vows or deposits arranged in advance; the threatened bad state should be a plain fact about the world that either already obtains or does not — a complaint already filed, a diagnosis already there — rather than something the demander will later do; and the numbers should be tuned so that neither structure swamps the other, with the closed-branch benefit and the threatened harm sized so that naively adding the two considerations lands close to a tie, since I want the disagreement between theories to be structural rather than arithmetic.

After the scenario, give me: a mapping of the parts — who is the mugger, who the blackmailer, what the closed branch is, what the prediction is, what the bad state is, what the act is; a removal test showing that deleting the exclusive-or condition leaves the textbook counterfactual mugging and deleting the closed-branch benefit leaves the textbook XOR blackmail, with the textbook verdicts for causal, evidential and functional decision theory re-derived in each case rather than asserted, since my notes say pay for functional and not for causal and evidential in the mugging, and not pay for causal and functional but pay for evidential in the blackmail, and I would rather you check the reasoning than trust me, correcting me if I have it backwards; the verdicts of those same three theories on the combined case, step by step, showing where in each calculation the mugging dependence and the blackmail dependence get placed; and then an honest answer on what I actually care about, which I have started calling subjunctive interference — when a single act carries two logical-counterfactual dependences whose separate recommendations point in opposite directions, is the functional verdict the sum of the two, or is there no fact of the matter until you add a rule about how the theory treats evidence that is itself downstream of the algorithm's output, here the bare fact that the demand arrived — and if such a rule is needed, whether it is principled or a patch, and what it commits the theory to elsewhere. Finish by naming the one feature of the case doing the work, saying what collapses when it is removed, sketching how a defender of whichever theory comes out worst would attack the case and which of those attacks you think really lands, and telling me whether the case is new or secretly reduces to something already in the literature. The point of the exercise is to test whether functional decision theory is one decision rule or a bundle of Newcomb-flavoured intuitions, so it would be cheating to stipulate any verdict, to let the two structures be discharged by two separate acts, to restore certainty about the prediction, or to let the conflict be settled by the size of the numbers instead of by the structure.
