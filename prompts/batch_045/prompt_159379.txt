I am assembling the decision-theory section of a graduate seminar problem set and need one new case, built to order rather than recycled from the standard repertoire. The use it will be put to fixes what counts as cheating here: students receive the bare scenario, with no indication of which theory is in play, and are asked to derive each theory's recommendation and to isolate the feature that makes the recommendations diverge. So the scenario may not contain a character who announces the intended conclusion, may not name a theory, and may not settle the question by stipulation; every verdict has to fall out of a theory applied to the facts of the case.

What I want is a counterfactual mugging satisfying the following specification.

The setting must be one my students could actually encounter — a workplace, a professional body, a mutual-aid or insurance arrangement, a family estate, a court-supervised settlement — with no simulation technology, no exotic physics, and no device whose only function is to make the case work. The predictor must be no better than a shrewd human being who has interviewed the agent, read her correspondence, and formed a judgment about how she would act in a contingency that may never arise; state the accuracy you are assuming, and state the mechanism by which that accuracy is achieved.

The temporal structure is the part I care most about. The benefit must already have been disbursed and must be irrevocable; the request for payment must come afterward; and the agent must know both that the benefit is settled and that the payment cannot affect it. Everything that would let a causal decision theorist reach the paying verdict through an ordinary channel must be genuinely closed, not merely declared closed: no observers who could reward or punish the agent later, no second round in which her conduct now affects her treatment then, no reputational or record-based consequence, no pre-commitment device she could have availed herself of earlier and did not, and no consideration of what paying would make of her character. Show, in a sentence each, how the scenario closes each of these routes. Payoffs should be ones a normal person could face, chosen so that the verdict is not knife-edge: give me the break-even accuracy at which the paying verdict flips, and show that your stated accuracy clears it by a wide margin. The scenario itself should be a single paragraph.

Then the demonstration, in this order.

State and justify the verdict you get from causal decision theory and the verdict you get from functional and updateless decision theory, treating the latter two together and telling me if they come apart in this case; I expect refusal from the first and payment from the second, so show the arithmetic and not just the intuition. Derive rather than stipulate what evidential decision theory says, and if it lands with the causal verdict, state precisely which evidential link is absent here and give me the minimal change to the setup — the XOR-style rig, in which the benefit's disbursement is tied to the prediction together with some further bad event — that would peel evidential decision theory off toward paying, and what it would then be trading on. That contrast should also let you show explicitly why the case you have built is a counterfactual mugging and not an XOR blackmail: identify the structural difference and the place in your scenario where it is secured.

Next, isolate the load-bearing feature, and run the removal tests: what becomes of the case if the agent chooses before the disbursement rather than after, if the predictor is still alive and reachable, or if a second round is added — and say which of my constraints turned out to be doing the real work. Finally, give me the two strongest lines of attack available to a causal decision theorist who wants to deny that the case has the structure you claim, and close with an honest note on where the scenario needs more from the reader than the facts supply, since I would rather know about that in advance than discover it in seminar.
