hey — quick one. I write the back-page humor column for the science-and-ideas magazine I'm at (basically our version of a Shouts & Murmurs slot, except the jokes are supposed to survive a fact-check). Next issue we're running a package on the current vogue for making nonhumans the stars of history — the bacillus that toppled a regime, the drought that started a war, the volcano that rewrote a century — and I want my column to be the piece that has fun with what happens when you take that framing literally, without turning into a lecture about why determinism is bad. Please write it in American English.

What I'm after: a deadpan in-universe document, 700-900 words, issued by a government body that has taken the actor framing completely seriously and now reassigns credit and blame for past events away from humans and onto germs, weather and geology. Call it the Office of Nonhuman Historical Attribution or invent something better. Format: numbered sections of guidance to field investigators, then a short appendix with one worked example of a determination. No citations, no footnotes, no bibliography — I don't want to run anything that needs checking, so please keep the illustrative cases to the ones everyone has heard of (a plague, a potato blight, a famous eruption, a famine) and don't hang any joke on an exact date or a statistic somebody would have to look up.

The comedy has to come out of the ideas themselves, not out of bureaucrats being bland. Four things I want the memo to work through, in its own level voice:

- the retrospective baseline problem: crediting a germ with an outcome requires a counterfactual in which the germ wasn't around, and that baseline always gets chosen after you already know how things turned out. Have the Office lay down a rule for baseline selection that plainly lets an investigator reach whatever verdict is convenient.
- vector inversion: once the microbe has standing as a person, the humans who decided where the grain went, who got quarantined, which valley got settled get reclassified as mere transmission infrastructure. Give us the actual form for reassigning somebody — a governor, a port official, a general — as a vector.
- the latitudinal alibi: the asymmetry where bad outcomes in poor countries get explained by climate and disease while good outcomes in rich ones get explained by institutions and culture. Codify it as a standing presumption, then let it do visible work in the appendix.
- credit as well as blame: if rocks and bugs are agents they can deserve praise too, so there should be a commendations section — medals for favourable winds and soil-building ash, and some visible discomfort about eruptions that only pay off centuries later.

End it so the reader feels the determinist slide without the memo ever naming it: by the final section there should be nobody at all left to answer for anything, and the Office should be entirely pleased with itself about that. Keep it deadpan the whole way, no winking, no admission that any of this is strange. Ideally someone in environmental history or STS reads it and winces in recognition rather than complaining we aimed at the wrong target, and someone who has never thought about any of this still just enjoys it. I definately don't want it to read like a takedown of one particular book or author — aim at the habit, not at anybody by name.
