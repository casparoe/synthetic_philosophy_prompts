I write historical fiction, and for my last two novels I have worked under a discipline I have come to call the fork ledger. I draft each chapter twice: once in a sealed draft, written as though nothing after the chapter's final hour were available to me and the outcome were still genuinely open, and once in an aftermath draft, written with the ending in hand. Alongside both, I keep a record of every juncture where a decision could plausibly have gone another way, together with an estimate, made at that moment rather than later, of how likely the road actually taken was. The consistent result is that the sealed drafts feel shapeless, and readers complain that they wander, while the aftermath drafts acquire a shape that readers at once describe as understanding, even though the facts on the page are identical in the two versions.

This has pushed me into a literature that, as far as I can tell, has not moved in roughly sixty years. On one side are those who think a historical explanation is only as good as the general laws and causal claims it can be cashed out in, and that narrative ordering is a literary imposition that adds nothing above and beyond them. On the other side are those who hold that narrative is itself a cognitive instrument, that without it a plurality of events is not merely unexplained but unintelligible, and that the covering-law demand simply changes the subject. More recently, psychologists documenting the knew-it-all-along effect and writers on the narrative fallacy have joined in, treating retrospective shapeliness as a documented source of error.

My hypothesis is that this is a dispute in which a single word is doing two jobs: one side is asking whether retrospective ordering distorts the inquirer's judgment, and the other is asking whether retrospective ordering is what makes a stretch of the past available to thought at all. If so the two answers are compatible and the debate is a verbal one wearing the costume of a substantive one. But I may be wrong: there may be a real, answerable empirical question at the centre of it, about whether the laboratory findings on hindsight bias carry over to thick, long-run historical judgment, or about what a narrative explanation would have to predict in order to be tested at all.

I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Using what each side actually claims, tell me what kind of disagreement this is, whether my hypothesis survives contact with it, and — given your diagnosis — whether anything would count as progress: a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a change of question. In particular, I want your honest view on whether the fork ledger, or a more disciplined version of it, is a genuine source of evidence about the question, or merely a novelist's theatre.

Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion.
