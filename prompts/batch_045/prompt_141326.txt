You are the scientific secretary of the Northern Shelf Authority, a regional marine regulator. The Authority and every case below are invented for this exercise; please do not try to match any of them to a real regulator or a real decision, and do not import facts about real organisms, technologies, or rulings. Work only from the record as stipulated, and do not ask me for further information: where a verdict turns on an empirical question the file leaves open, flag the gap and say which way it cuts. No citations, no bibliographies, no references to the literature.

The precautionary principle enters the Authority's enabling statute only in its bare strong form: where a course of action carries a credible threat of serious harm, the absence of full scientific certainty is not by itself a reason to allow it.

The track record. Holdings only; assume the stated grounds are the operative ones.

A/2011. Application to farm fast-growing transgenic salmon in open net pens; applicant offered sterile stock, double netting and siting restrictions. Refused. Grounds: escape and introgression with wild stocks cannot be excluded; the consequence would be irreversible; the sterility warranty was held not to carry the burden.

A/2014. Continued manufacture and use of a persistent, bioaccumulating surfactant already on the market for twenty years in marine coatings, with contested epidemiological evidence of harm. Phase-out ordered within five years, with the burden placed on manufacturers to demonstrate safety for any continued use. Note: precaution was applied against an existing activity.

A/2016. Release of a self-spreading gene-drive construct to suppress invasive rats on an uninhabited island in order to protect seabird colonies. Refused. Grounds: transboundary spread, irreversibility; the conservation objective was held not to require this method.

A/2017. Offshore wind array in a migratory corridor, with uncertain flyway mortality; the array is removable. Approved with conditions: staged construction, real-time curtailment during migration peaks, five-year monitoring, and a pre-agreed shutdown trigger.

A/2019. Emergency release of a viral biocontrol agent against an introduced moth defoliating a native coastal forest; host-range data incomplete. Refused, three to two. Grounds: host-switching could not be excluded and would be irreversible; the moth's damage, though serious and worsening, was held to be reversible on a generational timescale. Note: precaution was applied even though inaction carried a serious and growing cost.

A/2021. Cull of a native marine predator to protect a collapsed fishery, with disputed evidence about the mechanism. Full-scale cull refused; a two-year adaptive removal in a single sub-stock approved, with independent review and a stop rule.

A/2023. Review of the A/2017 monitoring data, which were inconclusive because of a sensor failure. Continuation approved for two further years on the operator's own data, with the protocol to be strengthened. A dissent recorded that this was the second data gap that had failed to trigger the stop rule.

The new file. C/2026. A research consortium proposes three successive summer releases, in one sill fjord with a shallow threshold and a long water-residence time, of a diatom strain derived from a species native to the basin, engineered for denser aggregates that sink faster and for obligate dependence on a synthetic nutrient supplied by the researchers, with an independent second-key termination circuit triggered by absence of that nutrient. The consortium proposes basin-wide monitoring, an agreed termination protocol (cessation of nutrient supply plus release of a lytic phage), a three-year horizon, and a stop rule keyed to detection of the strain or of resting spores outside the fjord or in sediment after the first winter.

The consortium concedes: the species forms resting spores that remain viable in sediment for decades; the termination circuit has been observed to fail in about one cell in a million per generation in the laboratory; fjord water exchanges with the shelf during winter storms; and the ecosystem consequences of a persistent shift in the timing and magnitude of the bloom are not predictable from laboratory or mesocosm work. The consortium asserts: no jurisdiction could responsibly evaluate the wider class of interventions this belongs to without field data at this scale, and that class is one of the few remaining levers on ocean heat and acidification, whose own worst branches are severe and irreversible. Treat both the concessions and the assertions as stipulated.

What I want from you, in this order.

First, reconstruct the rule. Give me two or three candidate decision rules, each stated in one sentence in a form a case officer could apply mechanically to a new file, such that applying the rule reproduces the seven holdings. Do not restate the precautionary principle as a slogan. If a rule must strain to accommodate a particular holding, say which holding and what the strain consists in.

Second, apply each rule to C/2026 and state the ruling it generates: refuse, approve as proposed, approve with stronger conditions, or approve while relaxing the staging.

Third, predict: what will the Authority actually decide? Tie the prediction to specific precedents, identify the single closest analog among A/2011 through A/2023, and explain why that analog is not decisive.

Fourth, recommend: which decision best coheres with the Authority's own track record? If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the file, say so, and then state what principle the Authority would be committing itself to under each live option, and what that commitment would imply for the next case of this kind.

Fifth, discriminate: name one further application the Authority could plausibly face, or one factual finding about this one, that would most efficiently separate the rules that still agree.

Two questions I especially want answered somewhere in the memo. Does the track record commit the Authority to the view that precaution only ever bites against proposed new interventions, or do A/2014 and A/2019 show it biting against the status quo and against inaction as well? And does A/2023 show that the Authority's staged-approval practice is a way of deciding, or a way of postponing a decision?

If your prediction and your own view of the merits diverge, mark the divergence explicitly rather than blending them. I would rather have a tight memo than a complete one: aim for under nine hundred words.
