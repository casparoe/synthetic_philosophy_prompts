hey — I'm in a third year epistemology seminar and I've got to present a case of my own design on thursday. We did the bank cases, then pragmatic encroachment, and the thing people keep saying in discussion is that pragmatic encroachment explains all the same data anyway so why bother with contextualism. I want to push back on that by showing the two views genuinely make different predictions, so what I need is a matched pair of little scenarios that double-dissociate them.

Rough spec:

Two scenarios, one paragraph each, same setting for both so they're properly matched (a hospital lab, an airport ops room, a restaurant kitchen, whatever — just not a bank, and nothing skeptical, no lotteries, no fake barns, nobody saying "you might be wrong" for no reason).

Case 1 — contextualism moves, pragmatic encroachment doesn't. Same subject, same proposition, same evidence, same reliability of the method, and crucially the same practical stakes for the subject throughout. The only thing that varies is the context of the person making the knowledge claim. Contextualism (the bank-case, DeRose/Lewis sort of view where the standard for "knows" shifts with what's properly ignored or what's at stake for the attributor) should end up saying one attribution is true and the other false; pragmatic encroachment (Stanley / Fantl & McGrath style — whether S knows depends on how much is riding on it for S, invariant across attributors) should give the same verdict both times. Constraint: the context shift has to be earned, not a bare mention. Someone just raising a wild possibility or going "are you sure?" doesn't count — I want the standards to go up because of something a contextualist is actually committed to treating as relevant (a real, documented failure mode of the method, or the attributor acquiring a genuine need to rely on the claim, that kind of thing).

Case 2 — pragmatic encroachment moves, contextualism doesn't. Now hold the attributor's context completely fixed (same conversation, same salience, same purposes) and vary only what's at stake for the subject. Same proposition, same evidence, same reliability. PE should flip and contextualism shouldn't. Constraint, and this is the bit I keep getting wrong when I try it myself: the stakes shift can't be visible from inside the attributor's context — nobody mentions it, nothing about how anyone is talking changes, the attributor has no reason to suspect it. Otherwise contextualism flips as well and I've just rewritten the bank case. The version I had in mind was stakes that get raised by something the subject didn't create and maybe doesn't even know about — a supervisor quietly deciding to lean on her say-so, a rule that changed overnight, something ambient — but if that only works given a contested reading of PE (objective vs. subjective stakes, whether the subject has to be in a position to act), say so and tell me which reading you'd need.

Then for each case:
- lay out the proposition, the evidence and the method, whose stakes are what and who knows about them, and which features of the attributor's context are supposed to be doing the work;
- actually run the mechanics for each theory rather than just announcing the verdict, because I need to be able to defend it when people push back;
- name the single variable doing the work, and say what happens to the case if I delete it — does it just collapse back into the bank cases;
- and give me the best thing the side that comes out badly can say. For case 1 that's presumably the contextualist going "yes, the standards moved, and that's the right answer, that's the whole point"; for case 2 maybe someone arguing the subject's evidence wasn't really held fixed after all.

Last thing: be honest if both halves can't be built. If there's a structural reason you can't get contextualism to move without the subject's stakes moving too, or the other way round, tell me which half you'd drop and why — I'd much rather present one case that survives discussion than two that fall apart in it.
