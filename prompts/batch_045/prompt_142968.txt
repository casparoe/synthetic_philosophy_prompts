I do backend and infra work at a mid-sized company — CI pipelines, monitoring, on-call rotas — and a few months back I signed up for an evening philosophy class at the adult-ed place mostly to shut my mate Doug up. We've just done the bit where knowledge is supposed to be justified true belief and then the whole thing falls over, and Doug and I have been arguing about it in the pub ever since. He says I'm just reinventing the same counterexample over and over with different hats on; I think my verdicts have a shape and I want to know what the shape is. Please don't give me names, papers, dates or reading lists — I'm not going to look anything up mid-week and my tutor gets sniffy about Wikipedia anyway. I just want the principle.

Here are my cases and my gut call on each. (1) Priya is on call. The team status page says "all services healthy". Nobody has noticed that the page has been serving a cached snapshot since yesterday morning, so what she's looking at is thirty hours old; as it happens every service genuinely is healthy right now. She believes they're all healthy — I say she doesn't know. (2) One rack has a temperature probe that everybody knows reads about ten degrees low, it's never been recalibrated. It reads 47°C. The alarm threshold is 40°C, the true temperature is 57°C, and the probe's error is at most a couple of degrees, so on the yes/no question "is it over 40?" it couldn't have led you wrong. The engineer believes the room's too hot — I say he does know, even though the probe is duff in general. (3) A PM who says "the release is delayed" every single week to dodge writing the release notes says it again; this week it really is delayed, for a completely unrelated reason. Whoever believes him — I say doesn't know. (4) Our alerting has been flapping all week, five false alarms in a row, and everybody's treating it as noise. A sixth alert fires and the service really is down; the on-call believes it's down. This is the one I keep going back and forth on: my first instinct is he doesn't know, he's got no right to trust it, but its a real signal and he's right. (5) Two versions of the same case: I vaguely remember an email saying the deployment freeze lifts at 6pm, and it does lift at 6pm. In 5a I'm telling Doug when we can ship a README tweak — I say I knew. In 5b I'm approving a production database migration at 6pm on the strength of that same vague memory and getting it wrong would corrupt customer data — I say I didn't know. Doug says that's flatly incoherent, the size of the downside can't change whether I knew something, and I can't tell whether he's right or whether I'm just being inconsistent.

My own stab at a principle: you know something when it isn't a fluke that you're right — if you'd formed the belief the same way in the situations round about this one, you'd still have been right. That seems to get 1, 2 and 3 right, and maybe 4, but it says nothing at all about 5, and when I try it on two cases I haven't listed it feels wrong: (a) I paste a stack trace into an AI coding assistant, it suggests a fix, I apply it without really understanding why and it works — do I know the fix was right? (b) I'd have said "it's DNS" whatever the graphs showed, and it was DNS — I definitely don't think I knew, I just guessed correctly. So: test my principle against the cases, tell me which clause is doing the work for which verdict, give me the minimal repair that saves as many of my verdicts as possible, tell me plainly which of my verdicts I have to give up (and whether 4 or the 5a/5b pair is the one that has to go), say what your principle predicts for (a) and (b), and keep it to a page of plain English with no jargon I'd have to look up. Finish with a single machine-readable line of the form \answer{...} containing a short label for the principle you recommend plus the number of the verdict you think I should drop, e.g. \answer{no-fluke rule plus no-undefeated-doubts clause; drop 5b}.
