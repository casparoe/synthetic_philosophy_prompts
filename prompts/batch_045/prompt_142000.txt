I spent the last three decades of my working life as a metrologist at a national standards laboratory, and a good deal of that time on the committee that argued about how a measurement result should be reported: point estimate, standard uncertainty, coverage interval, and what, exactly, a coverage interval is a statement about. Since retiring I have been sitting in on a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and I keep having the feeling that my old committee was doing philosophy under another name. The seminar has now split into two camps on imprecise probabilities. One holds that a rational agent's confidence is often properly represented by a set of probability functions — a credal set, with lower and upper previsions — and that collapsing this into a single sharp distribution discards something real about the evidence, or violates a duty of non-arbitrariness in how one fills the gap. The other holds that a rational agent always has a single sharp credence function, that this is what the representation theorems and the arguments from accuracy and from proper scoring rules establish, and that talk of imprecision is at best a report of computational limits and at worst a confused way of describing a sharp higher-order distribution over hypotheses about the world. Each camp tends to dispatch the other in a single sentence, and I want to see whether they can do better.

Please draft the joint statement that a committed imprecise-credence theorist and a committed sharp-prior Bayesian could both sign at the end of a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want the claims they share; the residual disagreements stated precisely and in wording each side would accept for its own position rather than for its opponent's caricature of it; the cruxes; and, for each crux, a concrete case, experiment, or argument that both would accept in advance as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded before the outcome is known, together with what each would count as having been refuted. Among the cruxes I suspect you will want: whether, in the Ellsberg-style settings, rationality merely *permits* imprecision or *requires* it; whether the phenomenon of dilation — evidence that widens rather than narrows a credal interval — is a reductio of the set-based approach or a correct description of what some evidence does; what decision rule is appropriate when a set of probabilities is in play (maximin, maximality, something regret-based, something else) and whether any such rule is admissible by the sharp theorist's own lights; and whether the difference between a single credence function and a set of them can show up in behaviour at all, or is invisible to any possible elicitation.

Two constraints on the tests. First, at least one must be the case I propose, which I have not seen anywhere in the literature: two instruments of different designs measure the same aliquot for a contaminant, and for each it is known only that its systematic bias lies somewhere within ±δ, with nothing known about the distribution of the bias inside that interval and no agreed reason to treat the two biases as either independent or correlated. One instrument reads 12.1 ppb, the other 11.6 ppb, and a regulator must decide publicly whether the true concentration exceeds a legal limit of 12.0 ppb. Both camps stipulate that they agree about the physics and the likelihoods up to the bounded bias, so the disagreement is purely about the agent's state of confidence and what she should do and say. Second, at least one test must turn on proper scoring rules: what, if anything, a long run of forecast-scoring would show about an imprecise forecaster as against a sharp one. I would also like you to say plainly which parts of the dispute, if any, are disputes about words rather than about the world, and why.

I have no journal access from home, so please argue the cases rather than appealing to authorities. Please structure the response in exactly these sections, in this order: "Agreed claims" (a numbered list of eight to ten claims, 350–450 words); "Disagreements" (a table, one row per crux — I expect four to six — with columns for the crux stated neutrally, the imprecise-credence camp's own formulation of it, and the sharp-prior camp's own formulation of it); "Tests and pre-registered predictions" (80–120 words per crux, keyed to the rows of the table); "Verbal or substantive" (about 150 words); and "What this means for the lab bench" (about 200 words on what, in light of the above, my old committee should have said it was doing when it published a coverage interval, and whether both camps could sign that statement).
