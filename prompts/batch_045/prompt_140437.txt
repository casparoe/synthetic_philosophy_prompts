My friend Priya and I have been having the same arguement about collective intentionality for about a year, ever since a community orchestra rehearsal where we sight-read a piece with a conductor none of us could actually hear, and I want to finally force it into a document. She's on the Margaret Gilbert / Searle-ish side: we-intentions aren't analyzable into individual I-intentions plus common knowledge, joint commitment is a distinct social fact, and it carries obligations that don't just reduce to what either of us individually has reason to do. I keep defending a Bratman-flavoured reductionism: "shared intention" is interlocking individual intentions plus mutual responsiveness, some commitment to the joint activity, some commitment to supporting the other, and all of it common knowledge between us — no extra collective mental state, no obligation beyond what the interdependence and ordinary morality already give us. We've agreed to stop shouting and write up the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration, and I got stuck with the drafting, so please write the document itself — the thing we could both sign — not an essay about the debate and not a verdict on who wins.

Three cases of my own need to be in it, because they're where I think the real cruxes live and our usual examples (house painting, walking together, a duet) are too clean. First, the laggy duet: two musicians in different cities playing together over a link with a known, fixed 400ms delay; each has learned to compensate, each knows the other has, they plan takes together over chat, and they genuinely sound like an ensemble — but neither can respond to the other within a phrase, only between takes. Is there a "we" there, and if so does mutual responsiveness have to operate at the timescale of the action itself or can anticipatory structure substitute for it? Second, the unbriefed choreography: two strangers who've never met and never communicated, who both separately memorized the same routine off the same video, end up on the same dance floor, recognise what's happening, and start adjusting to each other mid-routine. Does shared intention require an actual communicative act of committing, or can common knowledge bootstrap itself out of what each can see of the other? Third, the mixed trio: two humans and a language-model assistant in a group chat planning a surprise party, where the assistant tracks everyone's commitments, proposes times, and nudges people when they're flaking. Does a reductionist account of joint action require intending in every participant, and if I say "no joint action with a non-agent," what happens to my own view when the humans have offloaded half their planning and memory onto it? I also want two of my diagnostic tests wired into the cruxes: the silent release test (I tell you "you're off the hook, don't worry about the plan" — does your reason to keep going survive my say-so, or does the commitment dissolve only jointly?) and the third-party rebuke test (if you bail without warning, can only I resent it, or can someone uninvolved rightly rebuke you too, and what does that tell us about whether the obligation is owed to a person or to a "we"?).

Format it as: a numbered agreed-claims section first — and please make them non-vacuous, we both hate claims like "both sides agree joint action is real and important," so aim for things like what the folk-psychological data actually is and what would explain it, and where the accounts agree on the outputs while differing on the machinery; then a disagreements table with each disputed claim stated in the version its own side would actually sign (no strawmanning either way, and if you can't find wording Priya would accept for her side, say so); then the cruxes, each with the case or argument both of us would accept as bearing on it and each of our predictions written down in advance, flagged wherever we'd predict the same thing and so the crux isn't doing work; then a short closing section on which parts of this are merely verbal — I suspect at least one of our fights is about whether "shared intention" is meant to be a psychological kind in a mature folk psychology or a normative status inside a practice, and I want to know if that's right and what follows if it is.
