(a) Define the universal semimeasure M used in Solomonoff induction, and explain what role Kolmogorov complexity plays in it. [5]

Call the resulting prior M_T. Is M_T still a semimeasure? And here's the answer I'm stuck on (transcribed as-is):

(a) Fix a universal prefix machine U. Think of the program tape as filled with fair coin flips, and let M(x) be the probability that U prints a sequence beginning with x, so M(x) = the sum over all p such that U(p) prints something beginning with x of 2^{-length(p)}. This is a semimeasure rather than a measure, because some of the mass is lost on programs that never produce any output. Kolmogorov complexity comes in because the shortest program for x already contributes 2^{-K(x)}, and that term contributes more than all the longer programs combined, so for practical purposes M(x) = 2^{-K(x)}: the prior weight of a data string falls off exponentially in the length of its shortest description. That is the Occam part — strings with short descriptions are exponentially preferred. Strictly speaking K depends on the choice of U, but by the invariance theorem switching machines changes K by at most an additive constant, so nothing of substance hangs on that choice.

(b) The central result is the convergence, or domination, theorem. Suppose the data are generated by a computable measure mu — there is a program that computes mu(next bit | history) to any desired accuracy — and let the predictor use M. Then the expected total squared error between the predictor's probabilities and the true ones is bounded: the sum over n of E[(M(1 | x_{<n}) - mu(1 | x_{<n}))^2] is at most about c * K(mu), where c is a constant depending only on U. Two things follow. First, since the sum is finite, the predictor can only be wrong finitely many times: it starts with an error budget of roughly K(mu), and once that budget is spent it makes no further mistakes, so it converges to predicting exactly as well as someone who already knew mu, and the number of mistakes it makes along the way is bounded by the complexity of the environment. Second, this is what turns Occam into a theorem: if there is any computable regularity in the data at all, the prior finds it, and simplicity pays because a simpler environment means a smaller K(mu) means a smaller error budget. What the result does not give you: it does not say that the shortest hypothesis compatible with the data is the one you should believe — M is a mixture over all programs, not a selection of the shortest one — and it says nothing about data generated by a non-computable source, nor anything about how the predictor behaves on any particular finite stretch of data beyond the bound itself.

(c) The critic is mixing up two separate points, and neither does the work she needs.

Uncomputability. It is true that K is not computable, and neither is M: no program takes x and returns M(x). But M is lower semicomputable — if you dovetail all the programs and keep a running total of 2^{-length(p)} for each x, the totals increase monotonically and converge to M(x) from below. So the prior is computable in the limit; what fails is any computable bound on how long you have to wait or on how close you currently are. That is a real obstacle for an actual agent, but it is the same kind of obstacle you get from any idealization to unbounded computation, not a special problem for Occam.

Dependence on the machine. By the invariance theorem, for two universal machines U and U' we have |K_U(x) - K_U'(x)| <= c, a constant independent of x, so M_U and M_U' differ by at most a factor of 2^{-c}. Constants wash out asymptotically, and the convergence theorem holds for every choice of U, so the choice of reference machine is not a substantive one — it is like a choice of units. The one place it shows up is inside the bound, since the budget is K_U(mu), and that varies with the machine, but again only by the same additive constant: a machine on which the environment has a cheaper description just gives you a slightly smaller budget. So neither point touches the razor itself.

(d) M_T(x) is the sum over programs p printing something beginning with x of 2^{-length(p)} / T(p). It is still a semimeasure, since each term is just multiplied by a factor at most 1, so the total mass is still at most 1. It also still dominates every computable mu, and the convergence theorem goes through unchanged: putting log T(p) into the exponent merely adds a term to the description length, and by the invariance theorem adding such a term shifts complexities by at most an additive constant, exactly as switching reference machines does. So we still get M_T(x) >= c_mu * mu(x) with c_mu about 2^{-K(mu)}, and the squared-error bound holds with K(mu) replaced by K(mu) + log T(mu). The only difference is that the prior is now additionally biased in favour of environments that are cheap to compute as well as cheap to describe — which if anything makes it a better formalization of Occam's razor than the original, since working scientists care about tractable theories, not merely short ones.
