You are a professional philosopher who works on early modern metaphysics and on conceivability-based modal epistemology. Read the argument below closely, think it through before you write, and give me a diagnosis rather than a survey of the literature. I want your own verdict, not a menu of options. Answer in at most 400 words.

I built this reconstruction of Berkeley's master argument and now think it proves far too much, but I cannot locate the break.

(1) The task of conceiving an object that no mind conceives cannot be completed by anyone: performing it makes the object conceived, so what you end up conceiving is not an unconceived object. The failure is necessary, not a limitation of imagination.

(2) Inconceivability is our only test for impossibility: if a state of affairs cannot be conceived by anyone, we have no reason to count it possible, and hence no reason to believe it obtains. Without this step, (1) reports a psychological exercise rather than arguing about what exists.

(3) Therefore we have no reason to believe that any mind-independent object exists; to be is to be perceived or to perceive.

(4) Nothing in (1)–(3) turns on the verb conceive. Call a predicate reflexively self-conferring when the very act of applying it to a thing makes it true of that thing: naming, discovering, remembering, counting.

(5) Run the same argument with those. I cannot name a star no one names, discover a species no one discovers, remember an event no one remembers. By (2) I have no reason to believe that unnamed stars, undiscovered species, or unremembered events exist. Run it with think and you get no unthought thoughts, a conclusion Berkeley would welcome. The form does not distinguish the cases anyone accepts from the ones nobody accepts.

(6) One more datum: the task in (1) fails only in its universal form. I can apparently conceive of a tree perceived by someone else and not by me. Berkeley needs the universal version.

(7) So the chain is broken somewhere. But (2) is the premise I most want to keep: I rely on conceivability arguments elsewhere, and Berkeley needs it for the incoherence of abstract ideas and of a material substratum.

Which step do you reject, and why did it look sound? What does each alternative cost: dropping (2); restricting it to conception in particular and saying what justifies that restriction; or restricting (1) to objectual rather than propositional conception? And if the master argument is set aside, what is left of Berkeley's case for idealism?
