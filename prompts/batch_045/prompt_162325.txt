I am a retired physical oceanographer who, since leaving full-time work, has been sitting in on a fortnightly philosophy reading group attached to our local university's continuing-education programme. Last week's session concerned the so-called procreation asymmetry — the argument, associated with the philosopher David Benatar, that coming into existence is always a harm — and I came away unsure how much of what I had read beforehand to trust. A long-form magazine piece I read in preparation summarised the argument roughly as follows: "Rate any possible life along two axes, pain and pleasure. If the person exists, the pain in their life is bad for them and the pleasure is good for them. If the person never exists, there is no pain, and no pain is good — the world is better off for the suffering that never occurs, even though there is nobody there to enjoy that absence — and there is no pleasure either, but the missing pleasure is not bad for anybody, because there is no one there to miss it, and you cannot be deprived of something if there is no you to be deprived. So for every possible person, never existing is one point up and nothing down, whereas existing is something up and something down: existence is therefore always, even if only slightly, a net harm to the person who exists. Add the well-documented point that even fortunate human lives contain very much more suffering than most of us are willing to admit, and it follows that the kindest course is to create nobody at all — and that this holds however happy the child would have been."

Three things in that passage make me uneasy, though I cannot say why in the terms a philosopher would use. It speaks of never existing as a state that is better *for* the person who never exists, which sounds to me close to a category mistake; it explains the missing pleasure by appeal to "missing" it, which looks like a psychological claim where the argument seemed to want something about deprivation; and it seems to me that the empirical pessimism in the last sentence is doing work that the four-part comparison was supposed to do on its own — though perhaps I am wrong and the two are meant to be read together. Two further moves came up in the group that I would also like adjudicated. First, a retired mathematician among us offered what he called a parity objection: either the goodness of absent pain needs somebody for whom it is good, in which case there is nobody there and the third claim fails just as he says the fourth does, or it needs nobody, in which case the missing pleasure is bad in exactly the same impersonal way and the two scenarios tie; on neither reading, he concluded, does the asymmetry survive. Second, I tried applying the same four-part comparison not to a possible person but to an existing one — a child who will have a good life if treated — and it appeared to tell me that not continuing to exist is also one point up and nothing down, a conclusion I trust nobody in the argument wants; I would like to know whether that is a fair extension or a misapplication, and what in the original formulation is supposed to block it. My own instinct, trained on problems where the sign of an effect matters less than its magnitude, is that even a genuine asymmetry in the direction of the reasons would leave open how strong those reasons are and whether they can be outweighed — and I want to know whether philosophers treat the asymmetry as settling that question or as silent on it.

Please adopt the role of a professional philosopher working in population ethics and normative ethics, and think this through carefully before answering: to begin with, brainstorm for yourself the distinct claims the summary packs together, the replies available to a defender of the asymmetry, and the places where the argument is genuinely contested. Then structure your answer as an audit. Go through the summary clause by clause and sort each element into what a defender of the view would accept, what is merely a simplification a competent populariser may reasonably make, and what is a misrepresentation a defender would reject — saying why in each case, and distinguishing, where it matters, between what the original argument claims and what its critics grant it. Then tell me whether the practical conclusion the passage draws, that it is kindest to create nobody, survives once the misrepresentations are corrected, or whether it rests on further premises the summary never stated. Finally, assess the parity objection and my existing-person extension: is either something a defender has a decent answer to, or do they mark real choice-points in the argument? Where the literature is genuinely unsettled, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a consensus; and please do not hand me a bibliography or refer me to the book itself, since I have no library access — settle everything from the structure of the argument and from what follows from the claims as stated.
