I sit on the Novel Entity Classification Board at the Vandermeer Institute for Origins and Synthesis. Our charter is deliberately narrow: we do not write Institute policy, we apply it. Under the Institute's Biosafety and Materials Policy the category *Synthetic Living Organism* (SLO) is defined as any entity constructed or substantially modified by human intervention that is bounded by a self-produced boundary, maintains itself far from equilibrium through its own internal processes, and is capable of heritable variation subject to selection. Classification as an SLO triggers a dedicated containment suite, registration in the Biological Materials Registry, material transfer agreements for any transfer off campus, annual review, and an outright bar on use in public or unregistered spaces. Anything not an SLO falls into the residual category of chemical and biochemical processes, which carries ordinary laboratory hygiene rules, no registry entry, and no bar on outreach use. Two submissions are in front of us, and they have split the Board.

The first is HX-7, from the Protocell Dynamics group. These are fatty-acid vesicles maintained in a thermal-gradient chamber, fed a sterile amphiphile and catalyst feedstock. A droplet grows until shear in the chamber's flow field splits it; division is a physical event, not a genetically orchestrated one. HX-7 has no template-coded genome. What is passed on at division is a *composition*: a suite of catalysts and relative concentrations across roughly eighty molecular species. Daughters resemble mothers statistically, with high variance, and there is no digital code to mutate. Still, the group reports that over some two thousand divisions the lineage has become measurably faster at feedstock uptake and more regular in division timing, and that this persists when the lineage is re-started from frozen stock. HX-7 cannot make its own amphiphiles: withdraw the feedstock and the droplets shrink, vitrify, and enter a state with no detectable internal chemistry. In that state they have been held for over fourteen months and revive on re-feeding. The group also reports spontaneous fusions, in which two droplets merge and their compositions blend; they have not been able to keep lineages from fusing under the conditions they want to scale to, and they concede that the genealogy is not tree-like.

The second is CX-2, from the Reactor Evolution group. This is a cell-free gene-expression system: a replicating RNA encoding its own replicase, in an extract supplied with an energy mix and nucleotide and amino-acid feed, compartmentalized in a water-in-oil emulsion on a microfluidic chip. It has been serially passaged more than five hundred times, and it adapts — the group has selected for faster replication and for replication in a changed salt condition, and the changes are inherited through the RNA sequence. But CX-2 makes neither its own compartment nor its own energy currency; the boundary is the chip and the emulsion, and each passage begins from a fresh aliquot of extract, so material continuity is broken every round and only the sequence continues.

The Board is split three ways. One camp reads the definition's third clause as the whole point and would classify both as SLOs: what the policy exists to manage is open-ended adaptation, and CX-2 in particular has a code, a demonstrated response to selection, and no natural limit we can name; on this reading a requirement of self-sufficiency would absurdly exclude obligate endosymbionts and our own cell lines, which live only because we feed them too. A second camp reads the first two clauses as doing the real work and would classify neither: HX-7 is an autocatalytic set plus plumbing, CX-2 is chemistry in a machine, and if we call either alive we owe an account of why a thermocycler is not a habitat. A third camp wants to split the pair, and half of those want the split the opposite way round from the other half — which suggests to me that the two cases are pulling the standard list of properties apart at different joints.

Three further wrinkles. The funding agency's grant clause requires notification of "any synthetic organism," a term it does not define, and our counsel has told us that our classification is what will be reported. A group in Utrecht has told a reporter it will follow our decision in designing its own containment. And the Protocell Dynamics group has a "Build-a-Droplet" public exhibit scheduled in six weeks that is permissible only if HX-7 is not an SLO.

What I need is the draft decision memorandum I will circulate to the Board before our meeting on the twelfth of November. It should:

Recommend a concrete disposition for each of HX-7 and CX-2 — classify as SLO, decline to classify, or classify provisionally — with conditions the submitting groups could actually act on, and a time frame for any provisional status and its review.

Set out the operational criteria the Board is applying, stated tightly enough that a reviewer could apply them to a third submission without asking us. In particular, the memo must say how dependence on investigator-supplied feedstock and infrastructure bears on classification; how the dormant state of HX-7 should be handled, given that in it the entity neither metabolizes nor reproduces and yet reliably resumes; and whether a fusion event should be recorded as the death of a lineage, its continuation, or neither.

Give the strongest argument on the losing side its full weight rather than disposing of it in a sentence.

Finally, and this is the part I expect the Board to argue about most: the memo should decide whether we present our line as a claim about what life is, or explicitly as a purpose-relative classification rule adopted for containment, registration, and public-use decisions. If it recommends the latter, say plainly what we should tell the submitting groups, the funding agency, and the press office when they ask whether the Institute has decided that HX-7 and CX-2 are not alive — and what we should say if they point out that a definition which turns on our own administrative purposes looks like it has stopped being a biology.
