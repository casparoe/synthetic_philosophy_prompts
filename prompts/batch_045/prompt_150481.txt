Something's been bugging me about how the law treats promises and I keep going in circles.

As far as I can tell, the same lie gets you into way more trouble if you told it under oath in court than if you told it to a coworker, and it isn't just that the courtroom lie does more damage — my sense is that a false statement under oath can be punished even when nobody acted on it, so the swearing itself seems to be doing a lot of the work. But contract law, the bit of law that's ostensibly *about* promises, seems not to care about solemnity at all. Break a promise you made in writing, with witnesses, in front of your whole family, and if the other person didn't lose anything you owe them basically nothing (a dollar or whatever it is). Break it by accident on a busy day and nobody asks whether you *meant* it. Economists talk openly about breach being fine when it's efficient. And a bare promise to give your sister $500 isn't enforceable at all, however many witnesses, while a promise to pay $500 for a car is.

Then there's this scheme a legislature near me has been floating, which I can't stop thinking about. Call it a Promise Registry. You go to an office, pay a flat fee (same fee whatever the promise is worth), and say out loud a sentence of the form "I promise to φ" in front of an official, who writes it down in a book. Two things follow. First, if you later fail to φ, you owe the registered promisee a flat sum fixed by statute — not their actual losses, which might be a hundred times bigger or might be zero. Second, the promisee can release you at any time, for free, by signing a form, and then there's no penalty at all. Unregistered promises — however solemn, however much the other person relied on them and lost — get you nothing beyond the ordinary contract rules. The fee, incidentally, is charged for what the drafters call the "solemnization of the will."

And here's the bit I really can't fit in. The registry also accepts promises with no promisee. You can register "I promise to stop drinking" or "I promise to run every morning," and if you break it you owe the same flat sum, but payable to the state, and — this is the thing — there's nobody who can release you. The state won't, the office won't, and it doesn't seem to matter how good your reasons turn out to be.

So: what theory of promising does this whole package presuppose, and is it coherent? I have the feeling that the registry rules and the contract rules are pulling in opposite directions about whether what's wrong with promise-breaking is the harm you cause, the expectation you dash, or something about having flouted a convention whose whole point is that people can count on each other — and that the self-addressed promise business quietly rules out something the release rule quietly assumes. Tell me which parts of the practice actually force a theory and which parts several different theories would license, what would have to change if the system decided that promising creates a genuine obligation that's merely hard to enforce rather than one that only exists in the right conventional packaging, and at the bottom of it all what the package thinks makes a promise bind in the first place: the words, the convention, the other person's expectations, or the fact that you now owe them something.
