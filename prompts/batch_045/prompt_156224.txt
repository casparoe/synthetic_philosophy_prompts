i've more or less landed on the view that there's nothing morally distinctive about nudging. The bit I keep coming back too is that some arrangement of the choice environment is unavoidable: somebody has to decide the order of the options, what the default is, whether you tick a box or untick one, how many clicks it takes to opt out. Every one of those makes some choices easier and others harder, so don't-influence-people was never on the menu. Which means the only question worth asking about a given nudge is whether it's a good one — does it steer people toward what's good for them (or toward what they'd endorse on reflection), and does it leave them realistically able to go the other way. On this picture most of the autonomy / manipulation / bypassing-deliberation objections are a hunt for a neutral baseline that doesn't exist, plus a weird fetish for one causal route to action (explicit conscious reasoning) over all the others.

What I want is the honest case against it, sorted by how much it should actually move me. Not just the greatest hits — I want to know which objections a defender of my view should concede as counting against it, and which they can shrug off as resting on the baseline mistake.

Two things I've been chewing on that I haven't seen handled well, so please take them seriously rather than pattern-matching to the standard debate. First, the distinction I've started making for myself between nudges that change what's on the menu (ordering, defaults, framing, friction) and nudges that change the chooser over repeated exposure (a stairwell that quietly retrains your habits, a notification schedule that reshapes what you attend to). My view handles the first kind comfortably and I'm not sure it handles the second. Second, what I've started calling the ratchet: nudges don't just exploit existing preferences, they also shift them, and the shifted preferences are then exactly the thing we later point to when we say the nudge only gave people what they wanted. Endorsement gets manufactured and then laundered as justification. So imagine a large preregistered multi-site finding that default effects survive full disclosure and survive removal of the default, and that people's self-reported preferences subsequently move to match whatever they were defaulted into. Does that count against my view, or only against those particular nudges?

I'd also like: what my view predicts that its rivals don't; whether the easy-rescue move (any counterexample gets absorbed by saying that nudge was simply a bad one) means I've qualified it into something that can't lose; and whether that's fine for a claim of this kind, given it's a normative-framework claim rather than an empirical prediction — or whether even so it should still have a way of being wrong. And tell me which defeater you'd press if you were trying to actually move me: my guess is the most damaging thing isn't any single nudge but the observation that the is-it-a-good-one test has no reliable referee, since real choice architects are self-interested, lazy, or captured, and everyone involved has an interest in declaring the arrangement neutral.
