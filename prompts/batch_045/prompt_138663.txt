You are a senior philosopher of language — someone who has published on Austin, the Searlean tradition, and the decades of argument that followed — who happens to have also spent ten years as a distributed-systems engineer working on consensus, exactly-once delivery, idempotency, retries, and capability-based authorization. Write for me, not for your colleagues: I am a staff engineer whose native vocabulary is messages, state machines, atomic commit, append-only logs, tokens, and failure modes. I have read a few blog posts about speech acts and nothing more. Take your time with this, and before you draft your answer, work through the mapping privately; note where you were tempted to cheat the translation by quietly importing assumptions from one framework into the other, and say so when you write it up.

Here is the situation. I am drafting an internal specification for software agents that have to make commitments to one another and to people: booking slots, pledging payments, accepting tasks, issuing confirmations. The question I actually face is which parts of speech-act theory I can implement in a protocol and which parts I cannot, so I want the theory restated in my terms and then a report on what the restatement loses.

First, the translation. Restate, as faithfully as you can, the following in engineering vocabulary — message types, protocol state, write effects, read-only operations, authority, consensus, logs, retries:

1. The three-way distinction between the act of saying something, the act performed in saying it, and the act performed by saying it (locutionary, illocutionary, perlocutionary), and why philosophers insisted on the middle one.
2. The distinction between constative and performative utterances, and what exactly survives of it.
3. Felicity conditions, and specifically the split between infelicities where the act fails to come off at all and infelicities where the act comes off but is hollow, insincere, or not backed by the right state of mind or subsequent conduct.
4. The role of uptake — the idea that the addressee's recognition is partly constitutive of some acts.
5. Declarations: acts whose whole point is to change reality by representing it as changed, and their dependence on extra-linguistic institutions, offices, and standing authority.
6. The claim that at least some of the rules governing these acts do not merely regulate a pre-existing activity but constitute it, so that "violating" them makes the act not occur rather than making it occur badly.
7. Parasitic or hollow uses: the same sentence uttered on stage, in a joke, in a quotation, in a rehearsal, or in training data.

Be precise about which engineering object carries each piece of philosophical machinery. If an illocutionary act maps to something like an effectful, atomic, idempotent state transition committed under a protocol whose authority is externally grounded, say exactly that, and say which component carries which condition.

Second, the residue report. Give me a three-column table — translates cleanly, translates only under distortion, has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all — and for the middle column name the specific assumption you had to smuggle in to make the translation work, and for the right-hand column say whether the gap is a defect of the engineering vocabulary or evidence that the philosophical claim was never as crisp as it looks. I care more about this table than about the translation.

Third, apply the translated vocabulary to four cases I actually have to decide about:

(a) A stateless, temperature-sampled text endpoint with no durable identity, no persistence, and no stake emits "I'll have the report to you by Friday" to a human. Roughly 40% of re-samples produce the same string.
(b) The same sentence is emitted by an agent with a signed key, a spend capability with a hard ceiling, and a persistent ledger, addressed to another agent; the message is written to a tamper-evident log that both parties read.
(c) Case (b), but the exchange happens inside an evaluation sandbox: nothing downstream consumes the log, and both parties know this.
(d) An agent emits "the review panel accepts the proposal" where no panel exists, no institution recognizes the speaker, and no consumer of the log will change state on the basis of it — but a human reading the transcript later does change their plans.

For each: in the translated vocabulary, what happened? Was there an act, a misfire, a hollow success, a perlocutionary effect without an illocutionary act, or nothing at all? What should the protocol record?

Fourth, the design guidance. Which infelicities should a protocol fail closed on, and which should degrade gracefully? Which primitives should I add because the theory demands them — revocation, delegation, replay protection, standing-authority registries, explicit uptake acknowledgements, quorum requirements — and which of those does the theory have no vocabulary for but arguably should want?

Finally, one paragraph, taking a stand: can anything recognizably like obligation be derived from protocol correctness, or is the normativity of a promise irreducibly outside what I can specify? Answer from the residue you found, not from general impressions.
