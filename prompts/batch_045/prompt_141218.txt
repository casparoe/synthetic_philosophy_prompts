I'm the most junior member of my college's Core Curriculum Committee, and I've been asked to draft the recommendation memo that will go out before our meeting in three weeks, so I'd like it to read like something a committee can actually vote on. The case before us concerns our required first-year course, Freedom and Authority, which is built almost entirely around Mill's On Liberty and which also carries the college writing requirement: eight sections a year, most of them taught by adjuncts whose training is in ethics or ancient philosophy rather than nineteenth-century political thought. Two proposals are on the table, and a third has emerged in committee. The history department wants students to read a second Mill primary text alongside the familiar one — the chapter on the government of dependencies from his 1861 book on representative government, and, in the fuller version of their proposal, the memorandum he drafted in 1858 as an East India Company official arguing that British administration in India had improved over the preceding thirty years. Their claim is not the usual one that context is illuminating. It is that the civilizational distinction Mill draws — the aside in On Liberty to the effect that despotism is a legitimate mode of government in dealing with peoples he takes to be unfit for self-rule, and the corresponding case for ruling dependencies as a kind of trusteeship — is a premise doing load-bearing work in his liberalism rather than a blemish on its surface, so that teaching the doctrine while omitting the exception teaches students the conclusion and conceals the qualification that was meant to bound it. Our senior political theorist, backed by the alum whose gift funds the course, says the opposite: that this is a parenthetical tossed to Victorian readers, that there is a difference between an author's commitments and his argument's commitments, that Mill's writings on India were by contemporary standards reformist rather than merely justificatory, and that the real effect of the pairing will be to teach first-years to read by suspicion in a course whose purpose is to get them arguing about liberty at all. The third option, which I floated and which nobody has yet committed to, is procedural rather than curricular: adopt the pairing but make it turn on what we've been calling the deletion test — students run the argument of On Liberty with the civilizational distinction struck out and report what actually changes, and whether the harm principle, the case for free discussion, and the argument's account of progress survive the deletion — with the outcome of that exercise in the first two years determining whether the pairing becomes permanent or reverts to optional.

There are constraints that a good memo will have to respect rather than wave away. We have room for exactly one change to a syllabus that is already tight, and whatever we adopt has to be teachable by someone who will not have read the Indian writings before the week they teach them, which rules out any option that depends on a subtle reading being transmitted in section. It also has to survive two objections that keep getting raised in the corridor: that applied consistently this treatment would take Locke, Kant, and Aristotle off the syllabus with it, and that a course organized around whether its central author is trustworthy is a different and worse course than the one we have. And it has to say something about the paper assignments, since the course is where our writing requirement lives and the current second paper asks students to apply the harm principle to a contemporary case — a prompt that may or may not be able to carry the additional material.

Please draft the memo itself, not advice about how I might draft it: two pages at most, addressed to the committee, with a bottom line that picks one of the three courses of action or proposes a fourth of your own in equally concrete terms, a timeline running from a spring pilot in two sections to a college-wide decision next fall, a specification of what exactly appears on the syllabus and what the revised writing prompt would ask for, and one honest paragraph on the strongest consideration on the side you are rejecting. I want you to commit: give the recommendation flatly, with your reasons, and tell me where you think it might be wrong rather than laying out the options and leaving me to choose. One practical note, since I'm drafting this from home and can't get to the library: don't give me page citations or a reading list, and paraphrase in your own words anything you say Mill says, indicating roughly where it occurs, and I'll pin down the exact wording myself. Close instead with a short list of the specific claims about Mill's texts that your recommendation leans on, how confident you are in each, and what would change your mind.
