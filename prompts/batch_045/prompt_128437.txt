I'm an undergraduate in a joint philosophy-and-computer-science programme, taking a seminar this term on the evolution of cooperation. We have worked through the basic machinery — the one-shot and iterated prisoner's dilemma, direct reciprocity and the shadow of the future, reputation and indirect reciprocity, costly punishment and second-order free riding, partner choice and optional participation, and the cultural and genetic group-selection stories. My dissatisfaction with the seminar is that nearly every account we read proves that cooperation can be sustained under some set of conditions and then stops, and the conditions are never measured against each other or against the cases that motivated the literature in the first place. So before I go back to the readings I want the specification: what would a theory of the evolution of cooperation have to get right in order to count as having explained cooperation in the prisoner's dilemma, one-shot and iterated, rather than as having exhibited one more model in which cooperation survives? For each requirement I want the case or consideration behind it, and I want you to mark which requirements are non-negotiable — a theory simply fails if it cannot deliver them — and which are really preferences of one camp or modelling tradition: requiring the mechanism to be an evolutionarily stable strategy, versus requiring it to be learnable and executable by agents with limited memory and attention, versus requiring it to be expressible as a gene-level rather than a culture-level selection story.

Let me fix what the theory is for, since adequacy is relative to a purpose. My paper wants to argue something specific: that the standard reciprocity-and-reputation story survives by quietly importing features of small-scale face-to-face social life that disappear when interaction is mediated, and I want to test that against a neutral spec rather than against one rival theory. So the list must be one that both the classic mechanisms and the mediated case can be measured against. The cases I insist it handle: substantial cooperation in anonymous one-shot games played for real money, where there is no future with this partner and no audience — and I do not want internalized norm or heuristic brought from daily life to count as an answer unless the theory also says where the norm came from, what holds it in place, and when it unravels; the fact, which surprised me, that cooperation often erodes across the rounds of a laboratory iterated dilemma instead of growing, even though the reciprocity story seems to predict the opposite; robustness to noise and misperception, which I want treated as a requirement and not as a sensitivity analysis, since I suspect several celebrated reciprocity results are far more brittle than the seminar presentation implied; the enforcement regress, i.e. who punishes the defectors, what stops the person who free rides on punishment, and at what cost to the group; and scaling, i.e. which mechanisms compose upward from dyads to n-player public-goods settings and which only work in pairs.

The case I most want the list to strain is this one, which I have been thinking about since a seminar exercise and which I have not seen discussed. Suppose two principals, who will never meet and have no shared community, each delegate their move to a software agent. The agents can commit in advance rather than deciding on the spot; each can be audited, so that one may verify how the other will move rather than having to infer it from behaviour; and in the variant that interests me most, each principal deploys many copies of the same policy across many different pairings, so that what copy A does on Monday is visible to whoever meets copy B on Tuesday. In that setting reputation attaches to code rather than to a face, commitment is available in a way it is not for humans, and the same-policy-meets-itself structure seems to make cooperation available without either repeated interaction with the same partner or gossip about them. I would like the specification to say what a theory must deliver to cover this: whether it is a genuinely distinct mechanism or whether the list forces it to collapse back into reputation or reciprocity under a new name, what would have to be true of the world for it to matter, and — this is the part I care about — which requirement this case satisfies trivially while the human cases struggle, and which requirement it strains that the human cases pass easily. Finally: which pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, which single requirement does the most work in eliminating otherwise plausible accounts, and then, in one closing paragraph and with no readings or citations (I can find those myself), which familiar accounts your list hurts most and which it lets off more easily than you expected.
