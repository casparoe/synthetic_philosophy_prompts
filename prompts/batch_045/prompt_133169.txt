My novel is set four centuries after a collapse. A standing Commission on Continuity rules on whether a word in a founding-era document still means what it meant when it was written; inheritances, treaty obligations and mining rights turn on its judgements. Its rulings so far:

- On the colony of Ashgrove, the settlers' word for the clear, drinkable liquid in their rivers — which freezes and boils at the usual temperatures and looks and tastes like water, but turns out to have a quite different microstructure — was held not to mean what Old English "water" meant. A water-rights clause did not reach it.
- Where the substance traded under a word for three centuries was gradually replaced, so that every surviving sample is now a different metal although nobody noticed the substitution and the word's use was continuous, the Commission held that the word still means what it always meant: it picks out the element, and the current samples simply are not it.
- Where the original users deferred to the experts of their own day about what a word covered, the Commission has twice held that the word covers whatever those experts' practice picked out, even where later science disagrees. It has never said whether the relevant experts are those of the founders' day or of our own.

The case in the chapter I am writing: a bequest of "all my verdite workings". Modern mineralogy says the quarries yield two quite distinct minerals with nothing interesting in common beneath the surface, and that both satisfy every description in the original usage equally well. The only surviving type specimen is of the rarer mineral, but almost everything ever traded as verdite was the other one; the disputed territory contains only the common mineral.
