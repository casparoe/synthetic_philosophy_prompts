Two colleagues of mine in a biomedical ethics working group spent the better part of a seminar arguing past one another, and at the end they asked me to referee. I declined, because I genuinely could not tell which of them had the better of it, and I would be grateful if you would take it up instead. I have reconstructed their exchange below as faithfully as I can, and I have not indicated my own sympathies, since I have none.

The disputed thesis — call it tranquilism — was stipulated at the start of the session as follows: a person's welfare over an interval is fixed entirely by the extent to which, across that interval, they are free of craving and of suffering. Positive enjoyment, absorption, interest, and what we ordinarily call pleasure contribute nothing in their own right, though they may matter indirectly, by preventing later craving or by serving as evidence that craving is currently absent. Both participants accepted this statement of the view.

The case around which the argument turned was introduced by Nadia. It concerns a safe, cheap, fully reversible neural implant she called the governor, adjustable from 0 to 100, which scales down the motivational force of desire — anticipation, restlessness, frustration at delay, the sting of disappointment — while leaving sensory discrimination and the felt character of ongoing activity untouched. At high settings a person still enjoys dinner, conversation and music while having them, but does not want them beforehand, does not mind when they are cancelled, and does not grieve when they end. Both parties agreed that no third party is affected and that the only question is whether a person on a high setting is thereby better or worse off.

1. Nadia: Our recoil from the governor is not evidence about welfare. What we recoil from is the loss of something we value — striving, ambition, a recognizably human shape of life — and tranquilism is precisely the thesis that what we value and what benefits a person can come apart. There is independent support for it. Asked to choose between a life of calm contentment and a life of ecstatic highs and devastating lows, most people choose the calm one, and they do so even when the two lives are described so as to contain comparable totals of enjoyment by ordinary hedonic reckoning. The natural explanation is that the highs contribute nothing and the lows are doing all the negative work.

2. Paul: That comparison does not discriminate between the theories. The ecstatic life as described contains real suffering — crashes, anxiety about the next crash, dependency — and pleasures saturate while pains compound. Equalize for those and the intuition evaporates. Here is a cleaner argument, the argument from residual richness. Take two subjects, each wholly free of craving and suffering for one hour. One spends it absorbed in a late Beethoven quartet, noticing and savoring every phrase; the other spends it in flat, grey, neutral wakefulness with nothing going on. Hold the craving-and-suffering profile fixed — no frustration, no ache, and no boredom either — so that the entire difference lies on the positive side. Tranquilism must say the two hours are exactly equally good for their subjects. That is a consequence I reject, and it is not one you can soften.

3. Nadia: The argument from residual richness assumes the point at issue, namely that absorption is a separable positive ingredient rather than a pattern within the negative one. There is a credible alternative: what we call enjoyment is largely the phenomenology of relief — attention settling, a background restlessness lifting, a strain released. The quartet listener's hour is not the grey hour plus an additive good; it is a differently shaped relief-profile, and it presupposes a craving-free subject as its substrate. Notice how difficult it is to imagine pure positive enjoyment with no relief anywhere in it: every example one tries collapses either into relief or into blankness. That is at least some evidence that the two dimensions are not separable in experience.

4. Paul: Grant all of that for sensory pleasures. It does not touch achievement, knowledge, or love. Someone who spends a decade mastering a difficult language, at real cost in frustrated craving, acquires something that makes those years worth having, and not merely because the accomplishment wards off later craving. On your view the costs count and the accomplishment counts for nothing at all, which renders unintelligible the ordinary structure of prudential trade — paying in suffering for a good that is not itself an absence of suffering.

5. Nadia: I accept part of this. On my view the achievement has no weight as such; it matters only by keeping subsequent craving and suffering away, and I concede that this is a bullet to bite. But the bite is smaller than it looks, because tranquilism is a thesis about what benefits a person, not about what is admirable, worth doing, or worth choosing. We can praise striving, and even choose it for ourselves, while denying that it makes anyone's life go better for them.

6. Paul: The concession spreads to the governor, which is why it matters. Consider how the device would be evaluated in practice: ask people on a high setting whether they want it dialled down. But the implant also dampens the desire to alter the setting, so their answers are produced by the very thing under assessment and are worthless as evidence — a difficulty that does not arise for ordinary analgesics. And when people who fully understand the trade refuse the implant outright, the natural reading is that they are paying in craving for positive goods they expect to receive. If tranquilism cannot represent that trade, it cannot represent prudential reasoning at all.

7. Nadia: On unreliability: any intervention can reshape the desires you would use to judge it — a demanding career changes what one wants from a career — and we do not on that ground dismiss what people say about their own lives. On the refusals: what they show is that people dread the loss of striving, and dread is itself an anticipatory discomfort of the sort tranquilism instructs us to treat as evidence about the state the person is actually in, not about the state they are imagining.

8. Paul: That is the difficulty in one sentence. The person on the governor reports contentment, and you count it; the person offered the governor reports dread, and you discount it. A view that must sort first-person reports before it may use them is not so much a theory of welfare as a rule about whom to believe.

Two things struck me as bystander, and I mention them only because neither participant addressed them. First, they never settled whether craving was meant to include merely dispositional appetites — standing tendencies to want things, which can be present while the person feels nothing — or only occurrent felt urgings, and several of their claims seemed to me to slide between the two readings. Second, they never distinguished a person's welfare from what makes a life meaningful, though a good number of their remarks appeared to belong to the second question rather than the first.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the eight numbered contributions, say whether it is clearly mistaken, weak but fair, strong, or clearly right, and justify your rating briefly — including, where a move is strong, whether the other side had a good reply available that they did not make. Then state who has the better of the argument overall and why, and say what, if anything, both parties are missing. I would especially value your judgement on whether the verdict changes depending on how craving is understood, since that ambiguity seemed to me to be doing unacknowledged work. I am not looking for a survey of considerations on both sides; I want a verdict, with the reasoning that supports it.
