I write literary fiction, and the novel I am currently finishing is set largely inside a fictional economics department during a long, wet academic year in the late 1970s. Several chapters turn on a seminar in which a young philosopher attacks a senior theorist who has been using Harsanyi's aggregation theorem to argue that a government should rank policies by a weighted sum of individuals' expected utilities, with the weights carrying whatever concern for equality one happens to have. In trying to make those chapters something a specialist would not wince at, I have read my way into the surrounding argument, and I have come away with the impression of a dispute that has barely moved in decades: the result itself is conceded on all sides to be formally correct, and yet every critic says, in one form or another, that it does not show what its friends take it to show. The friends say: grant that each person's preferences satisfy the expected-utility axioms, grant that the social ranking satisfies those same axioms, grant a Pareto condition, and the weighted-sum form follows, so the only room left for a distinctively egalitarian view is in the weights. The critics say: the Pareto condition doing the work is an *ex ante* one, applied behind a veil in which nobody yet knows who they are, so the rule can recommend a prospect that, once the uncertainty resolves, everyone agrees is worse for an identifiable loser who is now known to be a loser; and the related move of imagining an equal chance of turning out to be any of the affected people only delivers a sum if one is risk-neutral about which person one becomes, which looks less like a neutral formal constraint than like utilitarianism in disguise.

My own hypothesis, such as it is, is that the impasse is not about the mathematics at all but about what a social welfare function is *for*: whether it is meant to be a ranking of outcomes by how good they are, or a decision rule for a chooser who must act under uncertainty, or a way of encoding what each person has reason to want from behind a veil. If that is right, then the two sides are answering different questions under one heading, and the arguments simply pass each other. But I am not confident, because some of the moves look genuinely substantive to me, and because a mathematician in my writing group has offered what he calls the long-run collapse reply, which I cannot evaluate: if the same society faces a long sequence of many independent, small-stakes distributive choices, then applying the ex ante rule over and over makes the realised sum concentrate around the expected sum, and the gap that the ex ante/ex post objection exploits becomes negligible; so, he says, the objection is a artefact of one-shot cases and dissolves in the limit. I suspect that reply either quietly assumes the very separability across decisions that is in question, or relocates the disagreement to the cases that are irreducibly one-off (a single indivisible transplant, a tiny probability of civilisational catastrophe, a variance that is borne within one lifetime rather than spread across many), but I cannot tell which.

So here is what I would like, in British English, and without any citations, a bibliography, or a reading list — I am writing in a cottage with no library access and would only be able to guess at references anyway. Give me a diagnosis of this debate rather than a verdict: is it verbal, a difference about which cases count as the relevant data, a difference about what a formal result in social choice is supposed to establish, a hidden empirical or formal question about separability or interpersonal comparison doing the work, or two questions being pursued under one name? Engage the actual positions as I have sketched them, including the long-run collapse reply, and say plainly if my hypothesis is wrong or only partly right. Finally, and most usefully for my purposes: given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, a class of case — and is there any reason to think the debate should simply be expected never to move? Please aim for roughly a thousand words of close argument rather than a survey.
