I'm recently retired — forty-odd years in statistical physics, mostly non-equilibrium stuff and a lot of time spent bullying renormalisation group methods into behaving on messy lattice models — and since I packed it in I've been going along to a philosophy reading group at the local university, which is how I ended up reading about this thing that people who write about AI call embedded agency. The short version as I understand it: the textbook picture of a rational agent has the agent on one side of a boundary and the environment on the other, observations coming in, actions going out, and a reward signal arriving from outside; but a real agent is itself a physical thing inside the environment, smaller than it, made of the same stuff, able to tinker with its own innards, and able to act on whatever channel the reward is supposed to arrive on. And apparently once you notice that, a whole pile of things come apart: the agent can't contain a complete model of itself, it can't be sure the model it learns is the true one, the 'what would happen if I did X' that decision theory wants isn't a clean intervention because the agent's own behaviour is correlated with the rest of the world, and so on.

The trouble is I have no intuition for any of it when it's put like that, and I do have intuition when it's put in the language I used for forty years. So translate it for me. Take the four or five distinct problems that get bundled together under embedded agency — the broken boundary between agent and environment, the self-model that can't fit inside the thing it's modelling, the reward channel turning out to be part of the system, the decision-theory one where the intervention you want to evaluate isn't available to you, and the worry that your learned model isn't guaranteed to converge on the truth — and restate each of them, one at a time, in the vocabulary of coarse-graining, effective theories, seperation of scales, mean-field and self-consistent field calculations, system versus reservoir, fixed points, and relevant versus irrelevant operators. I was fluent in that once and I still think in it, so don't water it down. And be honest: where the translation is exact say it's exact, and where you have to bend the physics to make the fit work, tell me exactly what you bent. A couple of paragraphs per problem is plenty.

Then the bit I actually care about, the residue. My working guess — and I want you to argue with it rather than agree politely — is that three of those five problems are just the self-consistency problem I spent my career on: you want a description of the whole, your only tool is a part of the whole, so you look for a fixed point of a map that has your own output as one of its inputs, and mostly you find one by iteration and mostly it's good enough at the length scales you care about. I've built myself a toy version to check: imagine a controller regulating a room's temperature where the controller's own heat output and its own wiring are part of the room, and its model of the room can only be built by running the room. Call it the mean-field thermostat. What I cant see is whether the AI people are describing something structurally different from that, or the same structure with more adjectives.

So tell me what doesn't survive into my vocabulary. Is there anything in embedded agency that my framework literally cannot express — and if so, is that because my framework is missing something (normativity? the fact that there's a should in there, a goal the agent is supposed to have, which no Hamiltonian has?), or because one word is being used for three different things? And a related worry I keep circling: in every self-consistent calculation I ever did, the person doing the calculating stood outside the system. Here the thing doing the modelling is inside it. Does that break the analogy at the root, or is it the same fixed point viewed from a worse seat?
