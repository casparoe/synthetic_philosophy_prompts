Last night I got into a long arguement with a friend about my job and I want it settled properly instead of by whoever talks louder. I'm on the model risk team at a mid-size lender. We review the scoring models that get used for underwriting and for a couple of other things — maybe a dozen to fifteen a year — and I get roughly one analyst-day per model. Whatever I write goes to our counsel and then to the chief risk officer, and it has to end in one of three verdicts: ship, ship with conditions, or don't ship. We can't ask for more data and we usualy couldn't get it anyway. My background is stats, not philosophy, and the people who sign off are a lawyer and a risk guy, so anything I hand them has to survive being read by someone who doesn't know the theory and doesn't want to.

The argument was this. My friend says: pick one fairness metric, write it into policy, check it, done. I said picking the metric is itself the moral call, and worse, the metrics fight each other — there's that well known result that when the outcome rates differ between two groups you can't have all of calibration-within-group, equal false-positive rates and equal false-negative rates at the same time except in degenerate cases. He said fine, then just pick the one that sounds nicest. We went nowhere, and the honest problem is that I don't have anything I could hand to a new analyst and expect them to reach the same verdict I would.

So: give me a procedure, not an essay. Questions in a fixed order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a hard no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. I care a lot about which distinctions have to survive the simplification and which can be dropped, because a checklist that tries to keep everything is a checklist nobody runs.

Three wrinkles that keep coming up and that a generic fairness checklist never handles:

One, deployment shape. The same kind of model is used as a threshold screen (approve/decline), as a ranking with a fixed budget (we have N slots and hand them to the top N), and as one input a human sees before deciding. I don't think error-rate parity means the same thing in those three, and I think in the fixed-budget case it might not be the right question at all. The procedure needs to branch on this.

Two, we don't collect race or sex of owners. Group membership is imputed from surname and census tract by a consultant's model, and we know it's noisy — call it right maybe three quarters of the time, and worse for one group than the other. Every parity number I have is therefore measured on estimated groups. I don't know whether that kills the whole exercise or just widens the error bars, and I don't know which direction it biases things.

Three, the label is a business choice. Default within 18 months, 90+ days past due, first missed payment — these give different models and different fairness numbers, and for applicants we declined we have no repayment outcome at all, so the vendor imputed those. My worry is that some of our numbers are unfalsifiable rather than merely noisy.

Run the procedure on these three, with numbers:

BizScore — a term-loan approval screen for small businesses, threshold decision. Label is 90+ days past due within 18 months, with imputed outcomes for the roughly 40 percent of the sample we'd declined. Equal false-negative rates across the two groups hold within a point. Equal false-positive rates don't: among businesses that would have repaid, one group is declined at about twice the rate of the other. Predicted-value parity fails too, and calibration looks fine for one group and not the other. Counsel wants a single number for the exam file.

HardshipRank — 500 counseling slots, 4,000 eligible customers, score ranks by predicted chance of missing a payment, top 500 get the slot. No threshold anywhere. Fixed pie. I want to know what the procedure even asks here, and I want it to say something about the option of equalizing some parity quantity by withholding help from the highest-scoring people — helping nobody so the spreadsheet looks better — because I think that is what pick a metric and check it quietly licenses.

AssistScore — shown to a loan officer as one input among several. Officers override around 30 percent of the time, and the overrides cluster by branch. My guess is the right verdict is that we're auditing the wrong object, but I want the procedure to force that verdict rather than me asserting it after the fact.

Include at least one case where the procedure gives me an answer I don't want — I'd rather see that now than in an exam. And tell me plainly what the procedure can't do: which questions it looks like it answers and doesn't, and where a value call has to be made and signed by a human.
