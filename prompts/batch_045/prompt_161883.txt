I run a small reading group — me, two PhD students, an ML engineer, and a guy who mostly just argues about this in rationalist blog comment sections — and we've spent about four months on Mary's room and gone precisely nowhere. Everyone knows all the moves by heart: Jackson's original set-up, the ability hypothesis (she learns how, not that), the phenomenal-concept strategy, acquaintance and ostension replies, the Dennett-style reply that a properly resourceful Mary would already have worked out what red looks like from the physical story alone, and the anti-physicalist reply that every one of those just builds the conclusion into its account of what knowing all the physical facts can deliver. Nobody's credences have moved more than a couple of percent in either direction, which feels weird given how hard the original intuition lands.

My hypothesis — and I'd rather you stress-tested it than confirmed it, because I've probably not been even-handed — is that the fight isn't really about consciousness at all. It looks to me like a fight about what a fact is and what it takes to know one: the physicalist side is implicitly running a coarse-grained story about fact-identity and propositional content, so that the what-it's-like fact just is some physical fact she already had, picked out under a phenomenal mode of presentation, while the anti-physicalist is running a fine-grained one where any difference in mode of presentation, or in the property being predicated, is enough to make it a new fact. If that's right, the consciousness stuff is decoration and the real disagreement lives in the theory of content, which would also explain why people keep telling me to taboo the words 'fact' and 'knows'. But maybe that's too glib: maybe there's a genuine substantive bet in there about whether a complete physical/functional description would let a suitably sophisticated agent predict its own first-person reports, or maybe it's bedrock intuition all the way down and no amount of machinery helps. So diagnose the thing rather than adjudicate it: verbal, substantive, or two questions sharing one name? Is a hidden question about content, about knowledge, or about counterfactual possibility — the inverted-Mary and twin-Mary variants — doing the actual work? And what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress?

I tried to build a progress-test with the group and I want to know if it's real or if it just begs the question. Call it the credence-partition test: before Mary leaves the room she writes down a partition of hypotheses, couched in physical and functional vocabulary only, that she regards as exhaustive over what her first colour experience will be like; then on release we ask which cells the experience rules out. If she can rule out none — if her credence function over her own pre-registered partition is untouched — the physicalist says she learned nothing, and the anti-physicalist says of course, the partition was drawn in the wrong vocabulary, the hypothesis she needed ('this is what red looks like') wasn't expressible in it. Is that a crux both camps would accept as bearing on the dispute, or is it just the original disagreement re-encoded in the language of probability? If it's the latter, give me what a real crux would be — a formal result, a distinction, a variant case, a change of question — and what each side should predict about it in advance; or tell me honestly that no progress should be expected here and why. A diagnosis with teeth, please, not a literature survey.
