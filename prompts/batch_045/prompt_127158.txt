I am finishing a doctorate in political theory on the democratic credentials of deliberative mini-publics, and I keep encountering the same dialectical move in my own field: whenever someone defends sortition, or the more ambitious lottocratic proposal of replacing elected legislatures with single-issue randomly selected assemblies, the Irish Citizens' Assembly of 2016 to 2018 and the referendum on the Eighth Amendment that followed are produced as the decisive proof that random selection works in practice. I want that move taken apart rather than repeated.

Treat the following as stipulated. The Assembly consisted of 99 citizens plus a chair, Ms Justice Mary Laffoy, a former Supreme Court judge; the members were drawn by a polling company from the electoral register and stratified by gender, age, location and social class, they served without payment beyond expenses, and no politicians sat among them, the government having promised a body without participation by politicians. Its mandate, set by resolution of the Oireachtas, covered the Eighth Amendment, fixed-term parliaments, the manner in which referenda are held, population ageing, and climate change. It discussed abortion across weekend sessions from late 2016 into 2017, heard experts and stakeholders, and voted to recommend replacing the 1983 constitutional guarantee of the equal right to life of the unborn with an explicit mandate for the Oireachtas to legislate, along with recommendations on the resulting legislation that were more liberal than commentators had expected. A special Oireachtas committee of 21 elected members then considered the report and reached broadly similar conclusions, though it differed on some specifics. A referendum on 25 May 2018 approved the change by about two to one, on turnout somewhat under two thirds. The government was required to respond to the Assembly's reports in the Oireachtas; on the other four topics little has come of the recommendations, so far as I can tell, and if I am wrong about that I would like to be told. There was also attrition and at least one irregularity in how replacements were recruited.

What I want is a case analysis running in both directions.

First, a careful mapping. On sortition theory's own terms, who are the actors and what are their options: the 99 members, the chair, the government that set the mandate and chose to hold the referendum, the Oireachtas committee, the electorate, the campaign organizations. What exactly did random selection change about the situation each of them faced, and what does the theory predict they should have done? Be disciplined here: where sortition theory has nothing to say about some actor or some feature of the episode, say that it is silent rather than manufacturing a prediction for it.

Second, separate three claims that the literature tends to run together, and tell me which ones this episode actually supports and what in the case would discriminate between them: that random selection combined with sustained deliberation yields epistemically better judgments; that it confers a permission or authorization that elected representatives lacked; and that its operative function was to insulate politicians from the electoral cost of a position many of them already held. The fact that a committee of elected politicians, working from much the same material, arrived at broadly similar recommendations seems to me to be a real problem for the first of these, and I want to know how much weight that observation can bear.

Third, the reverse direction: what the episode shows about sortition theory. I have two worries I want developed rather than merely conceded. The first is what I think of as ratification dependency: the Assembly's output mattered only because it was fed into a parliamentary process and then ratified by a popular vote, which are precisely the institutional mechanisms that lottocracy in its stronger form is meant to displace, so it is unclear that the case is evidence for that stronger form at all. Say what would have to be true of the case for it to count as evidence for lottocracy proper, and run the counterfactual in which an assembly of this kind decides the constitutional question on its own authority. The second worry is the leaky lot: given attrition, the dependence on volunteers willing to give up weekends unpaid, and the failure of random procedure in at least one round of replacements, the body that actually deliberated was not the body the lot selected, and I want an account of how much that matters to the arguments from descriptive representation and from the absence of electoral incentive.

Close by telling me whether this episode is a confirmation of sortition theory, an anomaly for it, or a case it was never designed to handle. I know the literature; I am not asking for a survey of it or for anything to look up. I want the argument.
