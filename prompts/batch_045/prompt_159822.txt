me and a friend have been going in circles about statutory interpretation for about a year now — he's clerking at a state supreme court and is a very hard textualist (ordinary meaning at the time of enactment, purpose only comes in as a tiebreaker when the text is genuinely ambiguous, legislative history mostly off limits, and if the literal text catches something the legislature didn't think about then that's the legislature's problem), and I'm the purposivist in the group (read the provision the way a reasonable legislator aiming at the mischief would want it read, and don't let sloppy drafting produce outcomes nobody voted for). We've agreed to stop arguing and produce instead an adversarial collaboration document we can both sign, and I want you to draft it. Please don't tell us who's right; we want the document.

Everything should hang off one made-up statute, so don't go looking for it — I wrote it: Section 7 of the Public Venue Safety Act says "No person shall bring, or cause to be brought, any weapon, firework, or other hazardous object into a public venue," and defines "hazardous object" as "any object capable of causing serious physical injury to another person." It was enacted by voter initiative after a riot at a stadium, so there are no committee reports, no floor statements, nothing but the text and the campaign pamphlet. The three cases we fight over: (1) a chef wheels a case of long knives into a city-owned hall for a televised cooking demonstration — literal text says yes, the mischief says obviously not, and I think we both acquit but by completely different routes, and I also think there's a version of his view that has to convict here and I want that version stated honestly; (2) an off-duty police officer carries her service weapon into the stadium, and the statute has no law-enforcement exception — maybe deliberately, maybe because the drafters forgot; (3) twelve years later, before any amendment, someone flies a heavy camera drone over a packed stadium on game day and it crashes into the crowd — he thinks a drone was never "brought into" the venue by somebody sitting in a parking lot two miles away, I think the case is squarely inside what the initiative was for. Here's the extra wrinkle: the state's drafting office is recodifying the Act and is thinking of adding a purposes clause ("The purpose of this Act is to protect the safety of persons in public venues"), and I want the document to say whether that helps, because I've started to suspect the clause is a trap — read as a literal directive it would criminalize anything risky at all, and it also seems to me that turning purpose into enacted text should make *his* camp happy and make *mine* nervous, which is backwards from what we both assumed.

Format: a numbered agreed-claims section first — the things we can both sign, including about the cases where we land in the same place, and for those, spell out each route separately, because that's the part that keeps tricking us into thinking we agree more than we do. Then a disagreements table: for each disagreement, my formulation in a wording I'd accept and his in a wording he'd accept, plus one line on why the exact wording does work. Then the cruxes — I think there are four, but add or merge as you see fit: whether "ordinary meaning" is an empirical fact about what readers at enactment would have understood or is partly something courts construct using rule-of-law values like notice and legislative supremacy; which approach actually produces fewer mistakes given how statutes really get made (staff drafting, omnibus bills, initiative texts); who the model reader is supposed to be, an ordinary citizen or a lawyer or an official; and what either of us is even allowed to do with an enactment that has no legislative history at all. For each crux, give one thing both of us would accept as bearing on it — a study, a kind of evidence, an argument, a survey — and then write down our predictions separately, his and mine, in advance, so neither of us can wriggle afterwards. Also: mark anywhere you think our fight is really about words rather than doctrine and say which part, tell us which single crux is doing the real work and which ones are noise we should drop, and say what each of us should count as evidence against our own position. Finish with a short drafter's appendix: the office has to decide what to put on the page next month and can't wait for any of this to be settled, so given unresolved cruxes, what should it actually do differently, and is there anything there both of us can endorse. And skip case citations and further reading — we're doing this on a train and can't check anything, so if you want to gesture at a real trend describe it without naming the case.
