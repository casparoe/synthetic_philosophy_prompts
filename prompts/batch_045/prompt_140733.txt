I teach a forty-five-minute discussion section in a first-year undergraduate course called "Minds, Machines, and Information." My students have had one week of introductory programming — enough to have met the idea of a file format and to have each written a small function that reads data out of a file — and no linguistics, no biology past high school, and no philosophy. Next week's section is on Hofstadter's distinction, in *Gödel, Escher, Bach*, between the frame message, the outer message, and the inner message of a message, and on the question those three levels are introduced to answer: where, exactly, the meaning of a message is located.

Do not write the explanation for me. Plan how I should build it.

First, the order in which the three notions have to arrive, with the dependency structure made explicit: which of the three cannot be understood until another is already in place, which distinction collapses into confusion if it is taken first, and what has to be on the board before I use any of the three words. Tell me also where in that sequence the answer to "where is the meaning?" should be stated — first, last, or never in so many words — and why there.

Second, one carrying example. It should meet three constraints and no more: it must be an object I can bring into the room and put in my students' hands, rather than a thought experiment or a diagram; its frame message must be something they can discover themselves in under three minutes of being led, rather than something I announce; and it must be the sort of object that yields a different inner message when only the outer message is changed, so that I can leave the object untouched on the desk, alter something outside it, and let them watch the content change. Name the example and say what each of the three levels is in it. Then give me one sentence each on why not the phonograph record and the record player, why not the artifact recovered from an alien civilization, and why not DNA as read by the machinery of a cell.

Third, the misconceptions my students will arrive with or form, in the order in which they will form them, together with the point in the sequence at which each has to be headed off rather than merely corrected afterwards. The ones I expect are these: that the frame message is simply a file header or a declared format, so that a machine can always be relied on to find it; that the inner message is the part that is really "in" the object, while the other two are only facts about us; and that the three levels are fixed properties of a thing, so that whatever is functioning as a frame must be a frame. I also expect my strongest student to conclude that if the meaning is not in the object, then any reading is as good as any other. Correct me if I have misdescribed any of these, add the one you think I have missed, and tell me which is the dangerous one — the one that will silently disable everything downstream if I let it stand until the end of the section.

Fourth, one question to end on that would show me the idea has landed, along with the wrong answers I should listen for and what each of them would tell me about what that student does and does not have.

Finally, tell me what I should openly decline to explain at this level, and how I should phrase the refusal. I have in mind Gödel's incompleteness theorems, information theory in Shannon's sense, and anything at all about strange loops. Say which of these is not merely too much but actively misleading at this stage, and why.
