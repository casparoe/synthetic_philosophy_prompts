For about a dozen years I have made my living subtitling films, which means watching the same three or four minutes of a scene anywhere from thirty to a hundred times, and for the last six of those years I have also helped run a discussion night at a public library where we screen a film and talk for an hour afterwards. So I have more exposure than most people both to the wearing-off and to the stubborn not-wearing-off of emotional responses to fiction, and I have started to notice a pattern I cannot name. It is not the puzzle I have seen raised about why anyone gets upset at characters they know do not exist; I have read a little about that, and it is not my question. I have no difficulty being moved by something I know to be invented, and I do not find it puzzling. What interests me is the opposite-looking case: a response that dies and cannot be brought back, even though none of my beliefs about the work seem to have changed in a way that should matter. First case: a few years ago we screened what we had announced as a documentary about a fishing town that had lost its fleet — plainly shot, no score, people talking to camera about their fathers. Half the room was visibly shaken. After the discussion, someone who knew the director mentioned that the film was staged: the crew were actors, the interviews were written, though drawn from real testimony and checked with the people who had given it. The room went cold and several people said they felt had. We showed it again a month later, honestly relabeled and with the staging explained in advance. The print was identical; everyone knew exactly what they were watching; and the response was markedly thinner. What struck me is that it did not come back even when I explained that the staging had been done at the request of the community itself, that they had wanted their story told that way and had seen and approved the script, and that nothing had been concealed from them. People accepted all of that, agreed that no one had been wronged, and still said, in so many words, that they knew it was fine but could not feel it the same way.

The second case is from my own work. There is a scene — a father saying goodbye to his daughter at a station, nothing more — that I found genuinely hard to sit through for the first several dozen passes. Then I read an interview in which the director explained how it was built: the child's line had been fed to her off-camera, she did not know what it referred to, and the reaction everyone reads as heartbreak was filmed before the actor was told what the scene was about. I have thought about that interview for years now, and I still think the procedure is entirely legitimate; I admire the same technique in a dozen other films and have argued with people who call it cheating. None of that has helped. The response is gone, and it stays gone, and the odd thing is that on the very same viewings the suspense still works, the flinch still works, the scene still holds me — so this is not numbness or boredom or having worn it out, and it is not that I have come to believe something false about the world, since I never believed the events happened. A friend I described this to said it sounded like the intentional fallacy, which did not seem right to me, because nothing about what the scene means or how I would describe it has changed; only my ability to feel it has. What I think I am looking at has five features: the feeling was real and not pretended; it never rested on a belief that the events occurred; a change in what I believe about the work's provenance or construction destroys it; withdrawing that belief does not restore it; and the thinner state does not present itself to me as a defect — it presents itself as more accurate, and the earlier response now seems slightly embarrassing, like something that happened to me rather than something I did. I am also unsure whether my two cases are the same phenomenon, since one involves a breach of trust about what kind of object I was being handed and the other involves only finding out how the effect was made; and I cannot tell whether a memoir exposed as fabricated, or a card trick once you know the method, belongs to this pattern or to something else entirely.

So: does this have a name? I want to be clear that a label is the least of it, and that I would rather have the carving than the word. Please do not give me a reading list or citations — I am writing this in a library with no database access and could not follow them up anyway, so if names of views or authors are unavoidable, describe them rather than pointing me at them. What I would like is: whether this is one thing or two; what the nearest established concepts are and precisely how my case differs from each of them, including the standard accounts of emotional response to fiction and any notion of an emotion that fails to track what the subject believes; which of them, if any, actually fits; whether the metaphor of hysteresis — a system that does not return to its earlier state when the cause is removed — is a helpful borrowing here or a misleading one; and, if nothing established covers it, a term you would propose, a definition tight enough to separate it from the neighbouring cases, and a couple of boundary cases that decide what does and does not count. Finally, if it can be said without sending me to a bibliography: is a pattern like this evidence for or against any of the standard stories about what these fictional emotions are?
