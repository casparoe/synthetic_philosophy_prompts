I should say at the outset that I have no academic background in philosophy — I have never studied it and have been reading and arguing about it on my own for a couple of years, mostly the material around Williams's integrity objection and the complaint that in some situations there is one thought too many. What I want is not an introduction to that debate but an audit of a package of views I have assembled out of it. Here is the set as I currently hold it:

1. Impartiality. No one's interests count for more just because they are mine or near to me. If duties to family, friends or colleagues are defensible at all, they are defensible by what honouring them does for everyone's welfare in the long run, not by the bare fact of the relationship.

2. Three case verdicts. (i) In the case of the traveller who is invited to shoot one of twenty captured villagers himself, on the understanding that the other nineteen will be released if he does, I think he should shoot. (ii) In the case of the unemployed chemist offered a post in a weapons laboratory, I think he may refuse — and I have come to think this is consistent with 1 rather than an exception to it, since if he refuses the post goes to someone keener than he is, so the impartial calculation returns refuse anyway. (iii) In a case of my own: someone who has spent twenty years single-handedly building an archive of a dying language, and who is offered work where her skills would do, by any impartial estimate, ten times as much good, may stay with the archive and let it die with her.

3. Integrity. When someone is required to act against a project or conviction that gives his life its shape, the loss is not adequately described as a cost to his welfare or as a disturbance of his feelings. A theory that can register it only in those terms has misdescribed what it is.

4. A principle of my own, which I will call deliberative transparency: if something is a genuine reason for you to act, you must be able to bring it before your own mind in first-personal deliberation and act on it, and the act of bringing it to mind must not be what makes the action worse; conversely, if consulting a consideration corrupts what you do, it was not a reason.

5. One thought too many. In the rescue case — I can pull my partner from the water or a stranger whom I have good reason to think will go on to save very many more lives — I think it is objectionable to stand there and run that comparison, and I mean that as a claim about what he has reason to do and about what is wrong with him if he does it, not as a remark about wasted seconds or about how it looks from the bank.

6. I do not think that complaint is peculiar to consequentialism. Someone who checks whether a favoured rule permits him to save his partner, rather than simply saving her, seems to me to have the same thing wrong with him — which is why I worry that 5 has stopped being an objection to anything in particular.

What I would like from you:

- Which of these are genuinely in tension, ranked by severity rather than all flagged as equally bad. For each real tension, say exactly what would have to be given up, and what the cheapest repair would cost me elsewhere in the set.
- Which pairs only look tense and survive inspection. I am moderately confident about (i) and (ii) in 2, because the asymmetry there seems to come from the facts about the replacement rather than from my principles; I am much less confident about 5 and 6.
- Then, the single revision that would tidy up the most.

My hunch is that 4 is the weak item, largely because it is the only one I invented rather than inherited from what I have been reading, but I do not trust that hunch, and I would much rather be told the set is incoherent than have it smoothed over.
