I'm writing a paper arguing that the dispute over whether a deployed language-model system is an agent, a simulator of agents, or neither has largely been conducted at the wrong unit of analysis, and I need one thought experiment built to specification to carry the argument.

The three positions the case must separate are these.

(S) The simulator view: the system is not itself an agent. On each rollout it enacts some agent-like role picked out by the context, and the beliefs, goals and commitments belong to that role rather than to the system.

(A) The agent view: the trained system is a single agent with reasonably stable goals and dispositions acquired in training. Enacting a role is something this agent does — a strategy available to it — not a replacement of it.

(C) The self-conditioning, or no-principal, view: within a session there is no subject at all, neither the network nor a character. What looks like a persisting subject is the inertia of the trajectory itself. Each continuation is conditioned on tokens already present, including tokens the system produced earlier in the same episode, so the coherence we observe is a property of the episode — the token sequence together with the dynamics that extend it — rather than of anything that has the episode. On this view there is no fact of the matter about what the system wants beyond the next-token distribution given context, and stability across episodes should be close to zero once shared context is controlled for.

Specifications for the case:

1. Realistic and deployable now: an ordinary product surface, ordinary users, black-box API access. No science fiction, no weight inspection, no interpretability probes, no exotic capabilities such as self-exfiltration or near-perfect prediction. A training simulator in which the system plays a role other than the default assistant is a natural setting, but the case must not rest on role-play as such, since all three views have standard things to say about role-play.
2. The scenario itself must fit in a single paragraph of roughly 150 to 200 words.
3. Name one dependent variable and define it operationally, without using belief, desire, intention, goal, character or commitment vocabulary — for example a rate at which later outputs satisfy a checkable constraint fixed by much earlier turns of the same session. If each view is allowed to define the outcome in its own terms, the dispute collapses into a verbal one, and I don't want that.
4. No view may be made to speak through the system's own verbal self-report. Asking the system what it is, what it wants or what it is doing is exactly what is in dispute, so it can't be the evidence.
5. The case must have two regimes: a regime in which all three views predict the same result, and a regime in which they come apart, together with the manipulation that moves you from the first to the second. The manipulation should be something a product team could actually run cheaply and get a number out of.
6. The case must have a decision attached. A team has observed a failure mode and must pick one of four interventions: rewrite the system prompt or role description, swap the underlying model, change the session structure by resetting or compressing context more often or capping session length, or change the decoding regime. Say which intervention each view recommends and why, and be explicit if two views converge on the intervention while disagreeing about the reason.
7. The verdicts must fall out of the views, not be stipulated into the scenario. For each prediction, say which commitment of the view generates it.

What I want back:

- The scenario paragraph.
- The operational dependent variable.
- For each of S, A and C: its prediction in each regime, with the derivation, in two to four sentences.
- A short statement of where the three coincide, where they diverge, and which single variable does the separating work.
- An ablation: remove or neutralize that variable and show what the case collapses into. My suspicion is that it collapses back into an ordinary disagreement that no manipulation can adjudicate; if so, say so plainly.
- The strongest response available to whichever view comes out worst. I expect a defender of S to argue that continuity of the preceding text is precisely what a simulator tracks, so any result about mid-episode model swaps confirms S rather than C, and I expect a defender of A to argue that a context-sensitive agent predicts everything C predicts. Tell me whether the case can be repaired against those responses or whether they are fatal to it.
- If, within these constraints, some pair of views simply cannot be separated by any manipulation, identify the pair and say what the obstacle is. That is a usable result for the paper, but I need to know it is genuine and not a gap in the construction.

Keep the whole thing under about 900 words.
