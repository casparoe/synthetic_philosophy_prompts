I'm a PhD student in intellectual history working on nineteenth-century moral psychology, and this term I'm running a graduate reading group on Darwin's Descent. A friend of mine — a competent science writer, not a specialist — is drafting a short piece on what Darwin did to ethics and religion, and she sent me the paragraph below to check before her editor sees it. I can see that it is written from a particular angle, but I'm too close to the material (and too unsure of myself outside it) to say where the angle has done damage, so I'd like a sentence-level audit rather than a general verdict.

Here is what she sent:

Darwin's great contribution to ethics was to show that conscience is a product of natural selection. Our ancestors lived in small bands, and individuals who cooperated, felt sympathy for others, and obeyed the group's rules left more offspring than selfish ones did, so selection built into us an instinct of approval and disapproval that we now experience as the inner voice of right and wrong. On this picture a moral judgement is really only a report on what the surrounding group happens to approve of: there is no independent standard of right and wrong for it to track, and if a community had evolved to admire cruelty, then cruelty would be right for them. Darwin saw this clearly, and it is where he broke with the utilitarians — the object of moral concern for him was the welfare of the community, not the greatest happiness, and moral progress is simply this same selective process continuing to widen the circle of our sympathies. The same reasoning demolished religious belief. Darwin treated religion as an adaptation that bound groups together, and once you see that God is a product of evolution rather than its author, the belief has nothing left to support it. He drew that conclusion himself, gave up Christianity, and lived out his life as a convinced atheist who thought the universe the product of blind chance.

Could you go through it more or less sentence by sentence and sort the claims into what a historian of Darwin (or of evolutionary ethics) would accept without fuss, what is a fair simplification, what is subtly wrong or anachronistic, and what is simply not Darwin? The particular things I can't settle: whether the individual-level mechanism she describes is Darwin's at all, or a modern reciprocity story quietly substituted for his account of tribal competition and of social instincts growing out of the parental and filial affections; whether his genealogy of the moral sense really carries the metaethical conclusion she draws, or whether that is a later debunking argument retrojected onto him (I half-remember a late letter in which he worries whether the convictions of a mind descended from the minds of lower animals can be trusted at all — if that is right, does it put him nearer her reading than I'd like, or is it a doubt he raises and then refuses to rest on?); whether he is genuinely breaking with the utilitarians, since the standard he appeals to in those chapters sounds consequentialist to me — something like the general good or welfare of the community — so that I can't locate the break except in their psychology of calculation; whether moral progress is for Darwin the same selective process still running, or something he thinks is largely checked in civilised societies and carried instead by reason, education, habit and the widening of sympathy; and, on religion, whether the group-cohesion adaptation story is his or the modern cultural-evolution literature about moralising gods imported into the Descent, and whether — setting the origin story aside — he actually infers from the origins of religious belief to its falsity.

The line I'm most confident is wrong is the last one: my impression is that he preferred to call himself an agnostic, that he had never denied the existence of God in the way "atheist" would suggest, that he continued to treat the origin of things as beyond our intellect, and that "chance" is not how he understood selection — but I'd be grateful to know which parts of that are right and how to compress them into half a sentence. Two further things: are the errors directional, in the sense that they all lean the same way, and if I correct them does the paragraph's polemical conclusion survive in a weakened form or does it collapse? And what is the minimum she would have to change for a specialist not to wince? She won't add citations, so I need replacement wording she can keep rather than references to chase up.
