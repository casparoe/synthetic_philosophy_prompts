So here's the background as I understand it, and then the idea. In the standard program-equilibrium setup for a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, each player submits a program that gets the other player's source code as input and outputs an action; the usual trick (some self-referential quining construction) lets you submit something like 'cooperate iff the other player's source is identical to mine', and then mutual cooperation is an equilibrium of the one-shot game even though defection is strictly dominant in the underlying game. But everyone also knows the embarrassment-of-riches objection: the equilibrium set is absurdly permissive, because programs can condition on arbitrary syntactic trivia — cooperate iff the opponent's source has an even number of characters, whatever — so cooperation gets 'supported' but so does nearly everything else, and the cooperative profile isn't focal, it's just one gadget among infinitely many. My thought is to attack the permissiveness not by shrinking the programming language or banning self-reference (which feels ad hoc and language-relative) but by putting a robustness requirement on the profile itself. Call a cooperative profile mutation-stable (to degree k) if each player's program still cooperates when fed any program obtainable from the partner's source by a handful of small edits — single-token insertions, deletions, substitutions. The 'cooperate iff identical' gadget should be maximally fragile under this: flip one character and mutual cooperation collapses to mutual defection. Whereas a tolerant program, one that simulates the opponent against a battery of probe programs and cooperates if it *behaves* like a cooperator, looks robust — but it presumably has to pay for that tolerance by being exploitable by mutants that behave cooperatively against the probe and then defect against you, and it also has to deal with the fact that simulating an arbitrary program may not halt, so realistically you bound the simulation by a step budget B and everything becomes approximate.

I already see two traps and I want the proposal to stare at them rather than hide them. First, unconditional defection is itself perfectly mutation-stable — mutate it however you like, it still defects — so robustness alone can't be the whole selection criterion, and I think the honest question has to be about a tradeoff: for programs that do cooperate against themselves and do defect against unconditional defectors, what's the achievable frontier between tolerance (fraction of near-mutants you still cooperate with) and exploitability (how much value adversarially-chosen near-mutants can extract), is that frontier monotone in the step budget B, and is the price of robustness bounded away from zero or does it vanish as B grows? Second, the whole thing might turn out to be a fact about whatever toy language I pick rather than a fact about anything conceptually interesting.
