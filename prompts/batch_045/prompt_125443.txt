I am drafting an internal position paper on occupational sex segregation by field of study, and before I circulate it I would like my own commitments audited for joint tenability. Below is the position as I currently hold it. I am not certain where the weaknesses are, and I would rather be told which pairs of commitments actually collide than be given a general diagnosis.

1. The empirical pattern. I take the so-called gender-equality paradox to be a real feature of the data rather than an artifact of how equality indices are built: in societies that rank higher on standard measures of gender equality and that provide stronger social insurance, fields of study are more sex-differentiated, not less — the share of women among engineering and computing graduates, for instance, tends to be smaller rather than larger. I intend to write as though this pattern holds.

2. The mechanism. My own gloss, which I have been calling the legibility-amplification account, runs as follows. Once higher education and occupational entry stop functioning as strategies for escaping poverty or securing mobility, they become acts of self-definition. In individualized, post-traditional societies gender remains one of the few widely legible, ready-made identity scripts, so choice gets routed through it; and because fields of study carry gendered cues about who is expected to belong there, small initial differences in anticipated belonging are amplified by peer sorting and by self-reinforcing expectations about who fits. On this account the paradox is produced by a social mechanism and gives no support to the inference that women have a natural aversion to technical work.

3. The background meta-view. Gender is socially constructed. There is no environment-independent, pre-social fact of the matter about what men or women really want to do; every observed distribution is the product of some social environment or another.

4. The normative claim. The resulting segregation is nonetheless a justice problem and not merely an aggregate of private lifestyle choices, because it shapes bargaining power within households, determines who designs the technologies and institutions everyone has to live under, and reproduces gender as a structure.

5. The policy constraint. The state may alter the choice situation — information, mentoring, visible role models, the ambient cues a field gives off, pay and working conditions within it — but may not override the choice itself. Reserved places in a field of study, or targets that operate as quotas on identifiable individuals, are ruled out.

6. The methodological commitment. I am evidence-first, and I distrust the maneuver of protecting a favored hypothesis by declaring inconvenient choices to be adaptive preferences formed under nonideal conditions. If people choose a given way under conditions of legal equality and material security, I want that counted as evidence rather than explained away.

7. Working verdicts. (a) I think a well-designed Norwegian upper-secondary programme that measurably shifts girls toward engineering is legitimate and worth public funding. (b) I find myself uneasy, in a way I cannot fully justify, about an otherwise identical programme that shifts boys toward nursing or early-childhood education. (c) I think a university that reserved a substantial fraction of its computer-science places for women would be acting unjustly, even though I accept the substance of (4) about that field's male dominance.

I also take for granted, without argument, the orthodox historical claim that patriarchal institutions and male-dominated family structures are the principal cause of gendered divisions of labour, and I do not read (1) as refuting that.

Three colleagues have already objected, and I cannot tell which of them is right. One says (1) and my background claim about patriarchy cannot both be true. A second says the verdicts in (7) do not follow from (1) through (6), and in particular that the asymmetry between (7a) and (7b) is not deliverable by anything I have said. A third says that (2) is doing exactly what (6) forbids, namely neutralizing inconvenient evidence.

Please audit the set. For each pair of commitments that genuinely conflicts, say how severe the conflict is, what the cheapest repair would be, and what that repair would cost me elsewhere in the set. For each apparent conflict that dissolves on inspection, explain why it only looks like one. Rank the real conflicts by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and flag any item in (7) that is not actually supported by (1) through (6). Do not advise me on sources or further reading; this is a consistency check on the package as stated, written on the assumption that no library or database is available to either of us.
