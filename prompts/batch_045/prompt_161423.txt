I teach the closing session of a final-year option on causation — twelve students, mostly joint-honours, ninety minutes, and a room that is always half-asleep by that point in the term — and I want to end it by handing round a single sheet with a poem on it and saying nothing at all. A colleague in the department maintains that a poem can only gesture at a philosophical view and never actually carry one. I would like to test that, and I would rather find out where she is right than come away with a pretty piece of verse.

The material is a case I have invented for the session, so please do not go looking for it in the literature. Nell has the night shift on a small reservoir above a village; on nights when frost is forecast, whoever is on duty has to open a bypass valve before midnight, or the village main freezes and bursts and the village loses its water. Osei, who works the day shift, would normally drive up and open the valve himself, except that he checks the night log first, and if the log shows that Nell has signed on he stays at home. On the night in question Nell signs the log, fully intends to open the valve, is distracted, and does not. The main bursts. Meanwhile, asleep in a bothy on the hill above the sluice, there is a backpacker who also does not open the valve — she does not know the valve exists, no one was relying on her, and no arrangement of duties had ever put her anywhere near it. Hers is the very same non-event as Nell's, and it is not a cause of anything. What the poem has to carry, in its own terms and without naming any of it: first, the ordinary shape of the thing, a not-doing that lets something happen which would not otherwise have happened; second, the two-step shape, in which Nell's route to the burst main runs through her having stopped Osei from stopping it — a prevention of a prevention, not merely a failure to prevent; third, the contrast with the backpacker, whose identical not-doing carries no weight at all; and fourth, the uncomfortable coda, that if the road up had been blocked by snow so that Osei could never have come anyway, then Nell's failure to open the valve looks causally idle and the whole weight shifts back onto her signing of the log — an act, not an absence. If something has to go to make the poem work, drop the fourth; the second and third are the ones I care about. Change valves, weather or names freely, provided those structures survive.

For form I would like a sestina: six unrhymed stanzas of six lines, the end-words rotating in the standard way — each stanza takes the previous stanza's end-words in the order 6-1-5-2-4-3 — closed by a three-line envoi in which all six words appear again, two of them as line-endings and the rest carried inside the lines. I would like the machinery to be doing the philosophy rather than decorating it. The same six words should keep turning up in a different order in every stanza, and one of the six should be an absence-word — "nothing", "no one", "never", whatever serves — so that across the poem it occupies every position in the stanza in turn: the thing that isn't there is never in one place, and yet it is in every part of the story. In expert hands each recurrence of an end-word changes its meaning slightly, and that is exactly the effect I am after, since the whole difficulty is that one and the same not-doing means one thing under Nell's arrangements and another under the backpacker's. I would like the speaker to be the backpacker, the one who got away with it, trying to say why the night belongs to Nell and not to her. "Log", "valve", "frost" and "nothing" would suit the six end-words if you can make them behave, but take yours where mine fight the poem. No jargon in the poem itself: no "omission", "counterfactual", "norm", "prevention", "overdetermination"; no epigraph, no footnotes, no prose before it, and no padding a line out just to land an end-word.

Two tests, one for each half of the room. Someone who works on causation should be able to tell from the poem alone which not-doing is meant to be doing causal work, and why the other one is not, with no gloss from me; someone who has never opened a philosophy book should still be able to read it as a poem about a cold night and a broken water main. After the poem, a note of no more than 200 words telling me where the piece takes liberties with the case or with the metaphysics, which of the four moves you think rests on the most contested ground and why, how confident you are — in plain words or percentages — that a philosopher of causation would not wince at it, and the single point in the whole thing you are least sure about, whether that is a line, a reading of the case, or a claim about what follows from it. Please write both the poem and the note in British English.
