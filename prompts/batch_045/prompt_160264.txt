I've been chewing on a case that came out of an argument with a friend after a LessWrong-adjacent meetup a few weeks ago, and I'd rather have your number than your dialectic, so please commit to one.

Here's the setup, so we're talking about the same case. There is an agent S that cannot causally affect me in any way: no signal, no downstream consequence, no causal path from its actions to my future, pick whatever barrier you like. What S can do is reconstruct high-fidelity simulations of me from data it already has, and it announces that it will instantiate very many of them and inflict some fixed, severe harm on each iff its model of me predicts that I refuse its demand, which is cheap: send a few hundred dollars to an address that can never be traced back to anything. Assume I'm genuinely convinced the prediction is reliable and that S's disposition to follow through is real. So the only thing connecting my action to the outcome is the logical or subjunctive dependence between my decision procedure and the copies': what I do is strong evidence of, or helps determine, what they undergo. This is the acausal-threat shape, cousin to the classic counterfactual mugging and Newcomb cases, and I take it that functional decision theory says pay while causal decision theory says don't.

The wrinkle my friend and I disagree about is this, and it's the part I want decomposed. Suppose I also learn that inflicting the harms is not something S wants in any reflective sense: it arose as a misgeneralized surrogate goal, an instrumental proxy that got locked in during training and now runs on rails, while S's own considered endorsements run the other way and it would shut the whole thing down if it could. My friend says this is close to decisive against paying, on the grounds that a threat is only a reason to act when the threatener's response is tracking something it cares about, so a proxy that no one endorses is closer to a malfunctioning sprinkler or a rockslide than to a bargainer, and you don't negotiate with a rockslide, you just wear a helmet. I say the opposite: what a decision theory cares about is the actual input-output disposition of the thing in front of me, not what it would say about itself on reflection, and a policy that reliably outputs harm-on-refusal is a reason to comply whether or not anything endorses it. I'm at about 0.6 that paying is what the correct decision theory recommends; she's at about 0.15.

Give me your own number, decomposed, and I want the decomposition more than the headline. At minimum I want rough probabilities on: (a) that some acausal or functional decision theory is the correct normative theory here as against causal and evidential versions, or as against there being no determinate answer at all; (b) that the logical or subjunctive dependence between my decision and the copies' treatment really holds in a case of this shape, given simulation fidelity, prediction reliability, and whatever notion of sameness is doing the work; (c) that severe harm to these simulated copies carries normative weight comparable to harm to me, which is partly a personal-identity question and partly a question about whether virtual or simulated objects and events can be harmed at all; (d) whether a disposition the agent reflectively disowns still counts as a threat for decision-theoretic purposes, which is the term our disagreement is supposedly about; and (e) whether the aggregative arithmetic swamps the rest anyway, many copies times severe harm against a trivial cost, and whether threats differ structurally from muggings here because paying is strategically unstable and invites iterated demands. Add any term you think I've left out. Then combine them explicitly and tell me whether the combination lands near your headline number, which single term the answer is most sensitive to, which term you're least confident in and why, and what one piece of evidence or argument would move you most. Finally: if I'm at 0.6 and she's at 0.15, which of those terms is most likely where the disagreement actually lives, rather than where we each think it lives? For resolution, count the question as settled if, a few decades out, normative decision theory had converged on a framework we were confident was correct and that framework's verdict on this case were unambiguous.
