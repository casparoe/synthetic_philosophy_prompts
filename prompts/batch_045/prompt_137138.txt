I serve on the Algorithmic Priorities Board of our city, a nine-member body that must approve, before each fiscal year, the objective that the Office of Data and Evaluation uses to rank capital projects — road resurfacing, sidewalk and transit-shelter accessibility work, tree-canopy and park renewal, stormwater retrofits — in the model that allocates the participatory capital budget across districts. Our vote is binding and must be recorded with written reasons.

For the coming fiscal year the Office has asked us to replace the current ranking rule, which weights residents' stated requests with a population adjustment, with a two-stage target its memo calls Community Volition. Stage 1 elicits substantive priorities from a stratified sample of residents. Stage 2 extrapolates them: a budget-literacy briefing corrects factual error about what the capital budget can legally be spent on; respondents then revise their rankings after reading anonymized arguments written by other respondents, which is meant to stand in for deliberation; and a vendor-supplied language model runs a coherence pass that identifies conflicts among a respondent's own answers and resolves them. Staff argue that raw responses encode residents' adaptation to long-standing underinvestment in some districts, misinformation about how much of the budget is already committed, and the turnout advantage of districts that already participate most.

The Board is split. Three of us hold that the extrapolated target is unfalsifiable in the specific sense that it lets staff and their vendor substitute their own judgment for residents' and describe the result as the residents' own. Two hold that any procedure which stops at stated preference simply launders the status quo through a survey. One member has circulated a note urging us to consider a narrower variant, fact-corrected stated preference, which would correct only demonstrable factual error about the budget and would attempt neither simulated deliberation nor coherence correction. I have undertaken to draft the decision memo the Board will vote on in three weeks, before the vendor contract's sixty-day renewal window closes.

Please write that memo, addressed to the Board.

It should state a bottom line — approve Community Volition, reject it, or approve it in a restricted form, and if the last, specify precisely which stage survives — and then defend it. Along the way it should deal with the following, which are the matters the Board is actually arguing about.

Staff's own pilot is the source of our difficulty rather than a resolution of it. Under their protocol the top-ranked district priority shifted from arterial road resurfacing to sidewalk and transit-shelter accessibility; staff attribute the shift to the briefing and the deliberation stage. But the pilot also showed that the ordering was unstable: a longer briefing and a stricter coherence rule each changed the ranking substantially, and where respondents proved to hold intransitive pairwise preferences the vendor's cycle-breaking rule did visible substantive work, in one case reversing a district's stated first and third priorities. I want the memo to say what the Board should require about the stability of the extrapolated target before it can be treated as the residents' will rather than as an artifact of the protocol the vendor happened to choose, and to say how stable is stable enough, given that we must decide in three weeks rather than in principle.

It should then address legitimacy rather than accuracy: whether a target that no resident can reconstruct or contest without a statistics team can ground a public and binding ranking, and whether anything short of abandoning extrapolation would repair that defect. One option some of us have discussed is a retrospective endorsement check, in which a fresh sample is shown both the stated-preference ordering and the extrapolated ordering, is told how each was produced, and is asked which it would endorse; the Board would then treat low endorsement of the extrapolated ordering as a reason not to deploy it. Say whether you recommend that, what threshold you would set, and why that test bears on the question we face rather than merely on whether residents like the outcome.

It should also settle a scope question we have been avoiding. The projects that the extrapolated target promotes are disproportionately ones whose beneficiaries are children, elderly residents, people with disabilities, and people who will move here later; the sample that generates the target is current adult residents. Say whether the relevant population for the extrapolation should include future residents, and whether that is a decision for the Board to make explicitly or one that may be left to the protocol.

Finally, give the strongest argument against your own recommendation a full paragraph, in the form the member who most disagrees with you would put it, and state what evidence, if it came back from the pilot, would lead you to change your position.

The memo is for the public record. It should rest on argument alone, cite no literature and recommend no reading, and be intelligible to a Board member who has never encountered the philosophical debate about idealized preferences; it may nonetheless engage the extrapolated-volition framing from AI-alignment work if doing so helps. Include a dated implementation schedule with the decision points between now and the fiscal year, and pre-registered criteria for counting the pilot a failure. Aim for roughly 1,200 words.
