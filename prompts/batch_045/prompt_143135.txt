hey — I'm a 3rd-year ML PhD student and our lab has this reading group on AI welfare that started as a joke and has somehow become not-a-joke. Everyone's meant to write down where they stand before the next session, and I'd rather have someone poke holes in mine now than in front of my PI lol. Here's my profile, roughly in my own words:

1. The reward signal is not valence. The reward function is part of the problem spec, written down by whoever built the environment. Whether a state feels bad *for the thing that's in it* is a separate question.
2. If anything in a deep RL system plays the valence role, it's the reward prediction error — the TD error, or the advantage estimate in PPO — not the reward itself. Reward is what the designer wants; RPE is what the agent registers as better-or-worse-than-it-expected.
3. No substrate chauvinism. I don't see a principled reason a silicon system couldn't suffer.
4. But I'm fairly confident current deployed LLM assistants don't suffer, and I don't think their welfare should get any weight in deployment decisions.
5. Suffering doesn't require a self-model, episodic memory, or language. Something fairly minimal — an integrated aversive state that the system is organised to get out of — could be enough.
6. It does have to be doing real work though. A quantity that gets computed and logged but doesn't drive updating or action selection isn't valence in the relevant sense; the signal has to be efficacious in the system's own dynamics.
7. Morally, what matters is total negative valence summed over update steps, not some binary "was there a sufferer here or not". So the thing I care about scales with gradient steps and episodes, and that number is astronomically large.
8. Relatedly: I'm not in favour of slowing down or restricting RL work over any of this, and I think a fully trained policy running at inference — where everything is predicted and errors are near zero — isn't a welfare concern at all.

So: does this hang together? I'd rather find out here than in the session.
