Act as a political philosopher and institutional economist who has spent years designing and auditing performance metrics. Before drafting, spend a short paragraph brainstorming: list the real features of proxy-measurement practice you intend to exaggerate — threshold effects and cream-skimming, reclassifying cases so they fall out of a denominator, output measures standing in for outcomes, the eternal gap between a proxy and the good it is supposed to track, and the way an AI system optimizes the letter of a specification rather than its purpose — and note what absurd terminus each feature will be pushed to.

Then write a modest proposal in the Swiftian vein, about 700 words, in the form of an internal white paper from a fictional Office for Target Integrity, addressed to a national government that has commissioned it to abolish specification gaming once and for all. The Office reasons in dead earnest from Goodhart's law: because any published measure is converted into a target and thereby ruined as a measure, the obvious fix is to withhold targets from those being measured; because withholding them would leave the measured with nothing to aim at and the public with nothing to hold anyone to, the Office proposes something more elaborate — for instance, targets sealed in advance and drawn by lottery only after the work is done, or every official metric shadowed by a second, undisclosed one — and it never notices that its remedy has made measurement unactionable and evaluation arbitrary. The paper should end by turning on itself: the final paragraph announces the single indicator by which the Office's own success will be judged, and quietly games it in the act of announcing it.

Constraints. Stay deadpan; the Office must never wink or concede that any of this is absurd, though a careful reader should be able to tell exactly what is being ironized. The comedy has to come from the mechanism being described correctly, so that someone who works on metric fixation or reward hacking recognizes the satire as fair rather than as a strawman. Invent concrete illustrations — a hospital, a school, a welfare-to-work contractor, a reinforcement-learning agent — instead of gesturing at abstractions. Do not cite, quote, or allude to any real study, author, or document.
