The topic is the non-identity problem: the class of choices in which, because the timing or manner of conception would have differed under the alternatives, the individuals who exist would also have differed, so that no one who is actually born can say that they would have been better off had we chosen otherwise. I will be defending the person-affecting restriction — the thought that a choice can be morally criticised, in the way that matters here, only if someone is made worse off.

The case at issue — call it the Substitution Protocol. A couple undergoing IVF has two viable embryos, M and H. Embryo M carries a pathogenic variant that will cause the resulting child to live with a significant but not catastrophic impairment: a life clearly worth living, but one with a markedly restricted range of opportunities. Embryo H carries no such variant. The clinic also has available, as a stipulation rather than a prediction, a technique that can correct the variant in M at the single-cell stage, restoring M's genome at that locus to the sequence that H carries; stipulate further that there are no off-target effects and that a child born from the repaired embryo would be healthy in exactly the respects in which a child born from H would be. Three options: (1) transfer M unedited; (2) transfer M repaired; (3) transfer H.

Two contrasts matter. (1)-versus-(3) is the standard non-identity contrast — a different individual exists, so nothing is worse for anyone. (1)-versus-(2) is the contrast I want you to work on: here the same zygote, the same continuously developing organism, is in question, and only its genome differs.

Your position, which you hold without hedging: choosing (1) is morally wrong, and its wrongness is not exhausted by anyone's being made worse off. You rest this on two claims.
