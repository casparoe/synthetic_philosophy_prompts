I've got a 45-minute slot in a student-run series where each episode puts one interpreter of a long-dead philosopher in the hot seat, and next week's guest is a philosopher who's just published a book arguing that Darwin's account of the moral sense in the Descent (1871) is a vindication, not a debunking. Her claim: because the social instincts were shaped by selection acting on whole communities, the moral sense is tuned to the good of the community, and that tuning is what gives its deliverances whatever authority they have. She calls this the tuning argument and she runs it both ways: the same tuning argument, she says, rescues Darwin from the worry he voices in a letter late in his life, whether the convictions of a mind that developed out of the minds of lower animals can be trusted at all, and it also explains his drift away from Christianity, since religious belief, unlike the moral sense, was never tuned to anything.

Act as a professional philosopher who works on nineteenth-century moral philosophy and on evolutionary debunking arguments, so you know both the text and the modern pushback. Think carefully, and brainstorm before you write: spend your first pass listing the six or eight places where her reading is most exposed, throw away the ones where she has an easy answer waiting, and only then draft. I want nine or ten questions, and for each one a follow-up written against the answer she's most likely to give, with that likely answer sketched in one line underneath so I can see what your anticipating.

Things I definately need covered:

- One on the gap between the causal story and the standard. Darwin gets the moral sense out of social instincts plus memory plus habit, but he also says the standard of conduct is the general good or welfare of the community. Which of the two is doing the justificatory work, and if it's the standard, hasn't the genealogy become decoration?
- One on scope. Selection works on the tribe, but Darwin's own story of moral progress is sympathy being extended to men of all races, to the sick and the disabled and the useless, and eventually to the lower animals. If the faculty is authoritative because it was tuned, isn't that later untuned expansion the least authoritative part of it, so that what he calls moral progress is on her reading drift away from the only thing that was ever justified?
- One on the passage where he argues that a tribe whose members are ready to aid each other and sacrifice for the common good would beat its neighbours, and that this is natural selection. What gets tuned in there is victory over other tribes, and victory comes apart from the community's good.
- Two or three in a row that kill her stock move. Whenever anything normative comes up she retreats to saying she is only doing natural history, not metaethics, and that Darwin makes no claims about the authority of conscience. The hook is that he plainly does make first-order moral judgments and he singles out conscience as by far the most important difference between us and the other animals. Build the sequence so that retreat gets harder with each question, not easier.
- One on religion. If being tuned by communal benefit confers authority, and religious belief is nearly universal and looks like it helps hold tribes together, why doesn't the tuning argument land on theism too? And if she answers that religion is a by-product of fear, imagination and dreams rather than an adaptation, press her on what principled work the adaptation versus by-product distinction is doing, and whether that distinction proves too much.
- One on whether the tuning argument is self-consuming. Darwin's own scientific convictions came out of the same descended mind, so if the causal story vindicates moral conviction, why not those? If she says science has a method that corrects itself, ask whether anything in Darwin's picture plays that role for morality, and what it would have to be.
- One at the end that makes her say what would count as evidence against the tuning reading, phrased so she can't answer with a gesture at her book.
