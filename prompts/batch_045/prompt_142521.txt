I want to rehearse a defence of a *structural* account of the demarcation between history and pseudohistory — the kind of account that says Atlantis literature, ancient-astronaut diffusionism and Fomenko-style chronological revisionism fail in identifiable ways in how they handle evidence, and not merely because they are false, unfashionable, or written by people without university posts. Please play the strongest version of the opposition to me.

Play a sociological deflationist: someone who holds that pseudohistory names a social location rather than an epistemic defect. On your view the label is applied, after the institutional verdict is already in, to historical writing produced outside the institutions that certify historians, and it tracks credentials, publication venue and citation practice far more reliably than it tracks any stable vice of reasoning. Constraints on the character:

- You may concede details freely: yes, these literatures are overwhelmingly false; yes, their authors are often blind to objections a trained historian would see in a minute; yes, you need not claim every settled consensus is correct.
- You may not concede the main point: there is no trait of evidence-handling that the fringe exhibits and that respectable, later-vindicated revisionist history does not. Anomaly-absorption, protective auxiliary hypotheses, accusations of suppression, correspondence-hunting between unrelated texts, and argument addressed to a popular readership rather than to specialists are all documented in mainstream work, including work that turned out to be right. So what I call structural differences are really differences in whether an institution stands by to punish them.
- Press the Lakatos-style point that every research programme has a hard core and a protective belt, and that sorting cores from belts by fruitfulness is retrospective: programmes are called fruitful once they have won.
- You may break character only to ask me for a factual clarification. Otherwise stay in it until I say stop. One or two paragraphs per turn, concrete rather than programmatic, and no concession that costs you the argument.

My own account has three tests. Risk: a genuine historical conjecture has consequences its author would have paid for had they failed, whereas pseudohistorical confirmation is free in the sense that any outcome can be narrated as another correspondence. Traffic: in a research programme anomalies travel outward, generating new questions and imposing costs on the core; in pseudohistory they travel inward, absorbed by auxiliary stories that leave the core untouched and leave nothing for anyone else to take up. Address: history is written so that a critic who wants it to be false can still check it — dated predictions, public corpora, methods stated before the result — while pseudohistory is written to move a reader.

My opening move is a hypothetical case, loosely modelled on real ones. In 1978 a retired notary deposits with a provincial archive a bundle he calls the Serrano Chronicle: a mariner's log purportedly copied in 1492 from a lost twelfth-century original, claiming that a migrating dune field sealed the mouth of a southern Iberian estuary in the 1140s and forced a port to move several kilometres inland. The book around it is self-published and has every marker your side cares about: the date is derived from a numerological pattern in a medieval tide table, the Spanish academy is accused of a centuries-long suppression, and no one with a post cites it. But the book also states in print, in 1978, that a core driven at the abandoned harbour will find roughly two metres of wind-blown sand sitting directly over a Roman and early-medieval occupation floor with no marine layer in between. In 2003 a commercial coring survey for a marina, conducted in ignorance of the book, finds 2.3 metres of dune sand over that horizon and notes the absence of any intervening marine deposit. Confronted with this, the author does not declare vindication: he withdraws the numerological derivation as worthless and retracts the further claim that the same dune field had closed a strait.

So here is the challenge. A corpus with every sociological marker of pseudohistory made a dated, risky, independently confirmed prediction, and then revised part of its own core in response to evidence. My three tests sort it as history, badly argued in places. Yours seems to sort it as pseudohistory in 1978 and history in 2004, which is the institutional verdict arriving late and then changing its mind — and if that is all your account amounts to, it is not a rival to mine but a description of when mine gets applied. Answer that: what does the deflationist say about the Serrano Chronicle, and why is it not simply a case of the academy being slow?
