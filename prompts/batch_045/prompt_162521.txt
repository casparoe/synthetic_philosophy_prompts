Hi — I'm a novelist, and I'm in the middle of an email argument with my German translator that I am definately losing, mostly because I keep saying things like it just has to feel the same and she keeps replying with very reasonable questions I can't answer. The problem is a poem in my new novel. It's a lullaby the narrator's dead sister supposedly wrote: three quatrains, four beats to a line, every rhyme landing on a feminine ending (the sort of -ée / schwa-tail thing you get in French and don't really get in English, let alone German), the first letter of each of the twelve lines spelling out HELENA if you read straight down, and a refrain that comes back wrong at the end. My translator wants to do it in free verse, drop the acrostic and put in an alliterative pattern on the H instead, and add a footnote explaining what the original did. My publisher thinks the footnote is a great idea. I think the whole book falls apart if the poem stops being a thing that has a shape — and I can't say why in a way that survives her next email.

There's a further wrinkle that's making me care about this more than I expected: the poem appears three times in the novel, and each time another line of it has gone missing, because the narrator is forgetting her. By the third appearance the final rhyme-word has been replaced by a dash. So whatever you think a translation preserves, it has to be the kind of thing that can be subtracted from in the same places: a version that only works if it's intact is no use to me, because the novel's whole point is that it comes apart. Oh, and the German publisher has now asked whether the lullaby can be made singable, since the audiobook is going to have a setting of it, and the Italian one is muttering about prose. I've been reading Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot: In Praise of the Music of Language (1997) in the mornings, which I love and which has also failed to settle anything, partly because his own answer seems to be to produce eighty versions and let the reader see the family resemblance — and partly because the title is itself a pun on le tombeau de Marot, tombeau in the old sense of a piece made in memory of someone dead, which is about as far from a decision procedure as you can get.

So what I actually want is the spec, not the survey. Write me the desiderata: what would a theory of translation under formal constraint have to deliver to be worth anything — which cases it has to get right, each with the case behind it, which distinctions it must respect rather than collapse, and which bits of what people demand are real requirements and which are just the prejudices of one camp. Things I want explicitly handled rather than gestured at: what the unit of translation even is (word? line? whole? the constraint set itself?); whether preserving a feature and compensating for it somewhere else are the same achievement or different ones; whether a translation that needs footnotes has failed; what counts as the same poem, i.e. whether my German lullaby is my lullaby, a new poem after mine, or an imitation of it; whether the reader the translation owes something to is the monolingual German reader, for whom this is the only version there is, or the bilingual one who can see what got lost; how you handle a formal feature that's doing semantic work, like the rhyme that goes missing when the narrator's memory does; forms that are structural rather than audible, like acrostics and fixed forms and poems whose shape on the page is the point; and the case where the target language simply has fewer or more resources for a given constraint. Mark which desiderata are non-negotiable and which are preferences, tell me which pairs you think can't be jointly satisfied — my suspicion is that something like preserve every formal feature / preserve every semantic feature / produce a poem that actually works in the target tradition is a trilemma, but you tell me — and say which single requirement does the most damage to the popular theories. Please don't just conclude that it's a trade-off between form and content, I've read that sentence a hundred times. You can score the usual stances against your list at the end — literalism, dynamic equivalence, imitation, thick translation with notes, the fluent-invisible-translator position, functionalist or skopos-style approaches — but keep it short and keep the weight on the criteria and the cases, since the criteria are the bit I can actually use in an argument with my publisher. Aim for roughly 1100-1300 words, prose rather than bullet soup, though a small table is fine if it earns its place; no bibliography needed, I can't chase references anyway. And please write it in British English — I'm in the UK and teh style sheet at my publisher is absurdly strict about -ise endings.
