I keep a list of verdicts about which physical systems have a mind, and I've finally noticed that they don't add up to anything I can state. I'd like a principle, or a short menu of rival principles, that I can hold up against them — and I'd like to be told honestly which verdicts I'd have to give up.

The case that got me started is Hofstadter's Aunt Hillary, the ant colony he converses with in the Ant Fugue: a system whose thoughts ride on the traffic of individual ants the way mine ride on the traffic of neurons, and which we can't really talk to because its thoughts run at the wrong speed for us. I find the example both persuasive and suspicious, and my other verdicts don't line up around it. Here they are.

1. Aunt Hillary herself: a mature colony, tens of thousands of workers, a queen, trail chemistry, collective regulation of foraging effort and nest temperature. Verdict: yes, a mind — an alien and very slow one. Note that I'm notably more confident that the colony has beliefs and goals (that it knows where the aphid herd is) than that there is something it is like to be it.

2. A single worker ant from that colony. Verdict: no. Not zero, perhaps, but below the line where the word earns anything.

3. A honeybee colony selecting a new nest site — scouts, waggle dances, a quorum before commitment. Verdict: yes, and for whatever reason I gave in case 1.

4. A slime mold grown across a maze, retracting tubes until the surviving network approximates the shortest route between two food sources. Verdict: no. This is the pair that worries me most: 4 is decentralized, adaptive, made of mindless parts, and solves a spatial problem, exactly like 1 and 3, and I say no. So decentralized adaptive system made of mindless parts is not the thing doing the work.

5. A large corporation, or a nation's foreign-policy apparatus across a decade. Verdict: beliefs and goals in a thin, useful, sometimes legally recognized sense; no mind. I will happily say that a firm decided to exit a market, and I will not say it suffered, deliberated, or understood anything. Relatedly, I'm comfortable saying that Germany feared encirclement before 1914 and uncomfortable saying it felt anything.

6. Craniopagus twins joined by a thalamic bridge — stipulate that each reliably reports the other's visual percepts and moods. Verdict: two minds, one shared stream. I resist one mind here about as hard as I resist two ordinary unrelated minds.

7. A commissurotomy patient whose hemispheres can no longer exchange information. Verdict: one person; I resist two minds. This is a second uncomfortable pair: 6 and 7 are both two semi-independent neural systems sharing a channel, and my answers differ. If the only difference is that 6 has two brainstems, I want that made explicit and defended; if it isn't the only difference, I want to know what else is doing the work.

8. Gradual replacement: neurons swapped one at a time for functionally identical silicon units, with no behavioral discontinuity. Verdict: the same mind throughout.

9. Restore-from-archive: the complete functionally relevant state of a colony, firm, or person is recorded, the original is destroyed, and an exact physical replica is built and started. Verdict: a replica, not a continuation. Third uncomfortable pair: 8 and 9 both preserve everything that matters functionally, and only one preserves the ongoing process. If I'm right about both, then which states are realized is not the whole story.

10. The speedup: suppose we could run the colony-level dynamics of case 1 a thousand times faster, so that Aunt Hillary's thoughts arrived at conversational speed while the underlying chemistry stayed the same in every structural respect. Verdict: my confidence that she has a mind goes up — and I count this as evidence against myself. A timescale shouldn't matter metaphysically, so if speeding her up changes my verdict, then the speed was doing cognitive work for me rather than metaphysical work, and I should discount it.

11. Fusion: two neighboring colonies of the same species are merged into one nest with one brood pile and one surviving queen. Verdict: one mind afterward, which I can identify with neither predecessor, and which I also resist describing as both predecessors surviving in merged form. And noticing that makes me less confident about 1 and 3, which is a sign that something is wrong.

12. A language-model agent with a long context, a persistent memory store, and tool use, deployed to pursue objectives over days. Verdict: I attribute beliefs far more easily than experience, and I can't tell whether that asymmetry tracks something real or is just my reluctance to multiply sufferers.

The tentative principle I've been carrying around: a mind lives at the highest level of description L of a system such that (a) L's states are coarse-grained relative to the system's parts; (b) L does causal work on the system's own future that the micro-level does not do better — there is an idea in the causal-emergence literature that a macro variable can carry more effective information about a system's future than the micro variables do, and I want that clause if it can be made precise; (c) L is homeostatic, in that states at L act to preserve the arrangement that realizes them; (d) L cannot be factorized into near-independent subprocesses without loss. Call it: the level that pushes itself around, or the level that fights for its own continuation.

What I want: test that against cases 1 through 12, tell me which clause is load-bearing and which is decoration, tell me which of my verdicts it cannot give me and whether any of them is simply confused (4 and 7 and 10 are my own suspects), and repair or replace the principle as needed — including, if you think it right, a version that makes mindhood a matter of degree and tells me what the degree buys. Then I want its verdicts on three cases I haven't listed: (i) a centaur, a specific human and a specific model instance who have worked together daily for years and complete each other's derivations, with the question being whether there is a mind at the level of the pair; (ii) the person produced by method 8 versus the person produced by method 9, where the two end states are functionally identical — say what, if anything, differs between them; (iii) two adult humans joined by a high-bandwidth bidirectional neural link that shares percepts and affect continuously, which should be compared to 6 and to 11.

Finally: is the resulting principle ontological, or is it only a heuristic about where to point the word? I've tried steelmanning the view that everything is a mind to some degree and bounced off it, because at that point the word stops paying rent; but if the honest answer is that my twelve verdicts are a set of habits with no joint, I'd rather be told that than handed a gerrymandered clause that saves each verdict separately.
