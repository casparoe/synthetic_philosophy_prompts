I convene a fortnightly reading group on formal epistemology and decision theory — six of us: two philosophers, an economist, two mathematicians, and a computer scientist — and we have now spent three sessions on the two-envelope paradox without anyone moving by so much as a degree. I don't want another solution. I want a diagnosis of why this particular dispute will not settle, and I want the diagnosis tied to what the positions actually claim, not to a survey of the positions.

Here is where we actually are. Everyone agrees about the closed case: before you open anything, symmetry gives you no reason to prefer either envelope, and the argument for switching, applied to that case, is simply invalid. Everyone also agrees about the open case under a proper, bounded distribution: once you see an amount, whether switching has positive expected value depends on where in the distribution you are — near the floor switching is good, near the ceiling it is bad, in between it can be a wash. The fracture is entirely about the open case with no known upper bound, and as far as I can tell we have sorted into three camps.

The first says the expected value of the other envelope is not finite and not defined, because there is no proper probability distribution over the amounts that makes the pair (x, 2x) behave as the argument requires; the argument therefore has no conclusion at all, and the real content of the episode is a lesson about decision-making under undefined or infinite expectation. The second says the argument makes an illegitimate step: it treats the amount you observe as if it were one and the same quantity across the two states, so that the other envelope is either double or half of *it*, when in fact which state you are in determines what the observed amount is; conditionalize properly and the apparent gain collapses, and where no proper prior exists the argument never gets off the ground. The third says the argument survives: in the unbounded setting the expectation really is infinite in both states, or undefined in a way that makes ordinary dominance reasoning inapplicable, so nothing has been shown to be invalid, and the lesson is that expected-utility maximization is an incomplete theory of rationality and the paradox is a datum against it rather than a mistake to be located.

Two things we tried, which I'd like you to use as probes rather than ignore. First, the capped variant: we replaced money with hours of relief from a migraine, with a hard and known ceiling of twenty-four hours and pairs of the form (h, 2h). Proper priors obviously exist here, so the first two camps should say the puzzle is dissolved and the correct answer is a straightforward calculation. Two members who grant every step of that still report the pull of the switching argument when they imagine seeing a mid-range value. I don't know what to make of this. Is their residual pull evidence that the formal diagnoses leave something out — a sign that the disagreement is partly about the phenomenology of the argument and not only its validity — or is it a psychological carry-over with no bearing on the normative question? If it is evidence, which camp does it favour?

Second, the iteration. If switching is indicated after seeing any amount, and switching changes nothing about your epistemic situation except which envelope you hold, then it is indicated again immediately, without looking again, and indefinitely. Some of us treat this as a reductio of the switching argument. Others point out that the combination of ex ante indifference with an ex post recommendation to switch, for every possible observation, is already the formal shape of the problem — a failure of the expectation to conglomerate — and that the iteration merely restates it rather than refuting it. Does the iteration discriminate between the three camps, or is it a symptom they all share, and therefore useless as a test?

My own hypothesis, offered as one, is that the stalemate has two components. One is verbal: *should switch* is being used to mean at least three different things — permitted, required, and there-is-a-reason-for — and the camps talk past each other accordingly. The other is substantive and, I suspect, load-bearing: a disagreement about what I'll call the completeness demand, that is, whether a theory of rationality owes a verdict at all in a case where the relevant expectation is undefined or infinite, or whether silence is an acceptable and indeed correct output there. I suspect most of the heat comes from people answering this differently without saying so.

So: what kind of stalemate is this — verbal, substantive, several questions under one name, or a hidden formal or empirical question doing the work? Which of the disagreements is load-bearing, and what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a theorem about conglomerability or about expectations, a sharpened account of what rationality demands when expectations are undefined, an empirical result about where the intuition comes from, an agreement to stop treating the bounded and unbounded cases as one problem? Or is there a good argument that no progress should be expected here, and why?

Two constraints on the answer. Please separate clearly what is settled — the mathematics, the closed case, the bounded case — from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested diagnosis as if it were the consensus. Where you are giving me your own bet rather than something the field has settled, say so, and say what would move you off it. And I don't need references, a reading list, or citations: we have the literature in front of us. I want the structure of the disagreement and the reasoning for your reading of it.
