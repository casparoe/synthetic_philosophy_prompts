Hi — I'm a journalist putting together a piece on self-control for a magazine that likes its ideas served with a bit of rigor, and I've gotten myself stuck on something I thought I understood until a behavioral economist I interviewed poked a hole in it. Hoping you can help me get it straight before I try to explain it to readers, and sorry in advance if this is a bit to in the weeds. The setup as I understood it: the standard story from behavioral economics is quasi-hyperbolic discounting, where a reward at delay t gets valued at βδ^t, with the extra β < 1 applied only to things that aren't available right now, so the immediate option is full-price and everything else is shaved. That neatly explains why on Sunday night I plan to go running Monday morning and then Monday morning I don't — from Sunday the cost isn't yet immediate, from Monday it is, and the ranking flips.

But she made two points I can't shake. First: at the moment of action, a hyperbolic discounter is doing exactly what she most prefers at that moment. The reversal is a fact about how preferences change across time, whereas the classic philosophical picture of akrasia is synchronic — right now I judge that I shouldn't have the second drink, and right now I have it anyway. So is the time-inconsistency model really describing weakness of will, or is it describing something adjacent that merely looks like weakness from the outside? Relatedly, a fully 'sophisticated' agent who anticipates her own reversals doesn't sound weak-willed at all, just strategic about precommitment devices — so where, in her case, is the weakness supposed to live?

Second, and this is where I want most of your words: she floated what she called the 'both-now' problem. The explanatory engine of quasi-hyperbolic discounting is the gap between now and later, so β only bites on delayed options — but plenty of the failures people actually flagellate themselves over don't have that shape. At 11pm I can go to bed or keep scrolling; both are available immediately, the present-bias terms cancel, and the model has nothing to say about why I keep scrolling while genuinely judging I should stop. (Same worry for two options that are both in the future: deciding on Monday whether to start a chapter Friday or Saturday.) Is the honest conclusion that temporal discounting covers one family of self-control failures, the ones with a now/later structure, and that something else is needed for the rest? And if we bolt on a second mechanism — she gestured at something like a small fixed cost for overriding whatever you're already doing, or a cost that scales with how much an option requires initiating rather than continuing, though I may be mangaling her idea — does the resulting hybrid still deserve to be called an account of akrasia in the philosophical sense, or has it quietly become a theory of attention, or of habit, or of something else entirely?

I'd like an intuitive, gut-level explanation rather than a proof, since I have to render this into a couple of readable paragraphs and my readers won't tolerate equations beyond maybe showing the βδ^t thing once. If it helps, use the running example of a writer who every evening plans to start a new novel at 7 the next morning and every morning doesn't. And please say plainly if you think the 'long-run self' is doing smuggled normative work here — i.e., if we're just stipulating that the patient perspective is the correct one — because that's the part I most suspect I'm not getting.
