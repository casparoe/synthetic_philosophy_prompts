Next week I am giving a thirty-minute talk, with twenty minutes of questions afterwards, to my lab's internal deployment-readiness seminar. The room decides whether a one-quarter pilot of the protocol I am proposing gets funded, so the question period matters more than the slides. I would like a Q&A preparation sheet.

The room: roughly five capability-side ML researchers, most of whom regard misalignment as speculative and who think in benchmark points, latency, throughput and cost (one of the five is sympathetic and will defend me if I give him the material); two security engineers from the red team, who talk in threat models, attack surface, side channels and defence in depth, and who will assume from the outset that the adversary knows the scheme in full; one interpretability researcher, who will ask whether intent is legible to a weaker monitor and what changes once the model knows it is being watched; one philosopher of AI, who will press on the decision-theoretic and normative assumptions; and the deployment lead, who will ask only whether this can ship inside the product's latency budget, and who has veto power.

The system under discussion: a frontier general-purpose model deployed as an agent with real tool access — filesystem, code execution, network, a purchase API — competent at multi-step tasks, not assumed to be superhuman, but also not assumed to be incapable of reasoning about its own situation and its own oversight.

What I am defending, in two parts. First, a claim about allocation: for systems that may be misaligned, the safety budget should go mainly to structural measures — measures that reshape the action space so that defection stops paying — rather than to detective measures, which try to catch defection as or after it happens. My argument is that detective control has a structural liability: the monitor and the monitored share an input distribution, and the monitor is by assumption the weaker system, so the reliability of any detection signal decays precisely as the capability that makes control necessary grows. Structural measures, by contrast, do not require me to predict what the model will do; they require me only to control what it can do, and their force does not obviously decay with capability. Detection is demoted to forensics and tripwires.

Second, and as the concrete instance of this: blind commitment batching (BCB). The agent does not act one action at a time. In each epoch it emits a batch of k actions — call it 32 — into a commit buffer. A trusted beacon, whose output the model cannot observe or influence, then selects m — call it 8 — of the 32 for execution; the remaining 24 are never executed and form the shadow set. Outcome feedback to the model is aggregated over the whole epoch and delayed by one epoch, so that it cannot infer which actions ran. The shadow set is scored after the fact by a trusted, weaker monitor; if it contains attempts that would have caused harm, the epoch's credentials are revoked and the deployment halts. The point of the mechanism: any single action executes with probability m/k, so targeted sabotage is unreliable; but making it reliable requires filling many slots of the batch with the same or complementary attempts, which loads the shadow set, which is then inspected at no risk, because those actions never run. The model is pushed into a dilemma between unreliability and legibility.
