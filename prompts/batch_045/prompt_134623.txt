I am finishing a novel whose middle third follows a retired punchcutter and lettering conservator, Ottoline March, as she adjudicates a tray of glyphs that my narrator puts in front of her one at a time, asking the same question each session: is this an a? She has a shelf-note in her studio about a project I half-remember from a magazine piece years ago — Hofstadter's Letter Spirit, I believe it was called — which tried to get a program to draw an alphabet in a coherent style, and she has a catchphrase my narrator keeps hearing: the gesture of the letter survives everything else. Her verdicts so far, which I have already drafted and would rather not rewrite: a double-storey a from an old-style face, yes; a single-storey a from a geometric sans, yes; that same single-storey shape when I told her it was taken from Cyrillic, no, on the grounds that it belongs to another alphabet; an a from a worn impression in which the stem has parted from the bowl by a hairline, yes, a damaged a is still an a; the same a with its counter filled solid by ink spread, no, that is a spot and not a letter; a double-storey a rotated through 180 degrees, no; an a drawn with so much aperture that it is nearly a u with a long right stem, yes, though she called it badly made; an a whose thick-and-thin is inverted relative to the rest of its typeface, yes as to identity but not the a of that face, which is how I discovered she separates whether a thing is an a from whether it belongs to the style it is sitting in; and, when my narrator showed her a Cyrillic shop sign as a joke, she read the two capitals that look exactly like our H and our B as n and v, on the grounds that what a letter is depends on the job it does in its own system.

The exhibit I have not written yet is this: a valley workshop kept pattern sheets for nine generations, from the 1730s to the early 1900s, each master copying the previous master's alphabet, so that the descent of every glyph is documented without a gap. By the last sheet the a has drifted into what any present-day reader shown it cold would call an o with a small tick at the lower right — the bowl has closed into a near-circle, the stem has shrunk to a stub — while the valley's o has drifted into an elongated hexagon, so the two are never confused in practice, the drifted glyph is the only one that ever appears where the local spelling has an a-sound, and the villagers read it as their a without hesitation. What I need from you is not whether it is really an a but which rule Ottoline is running: set out the two or three rules that fit her earlier verdicts equally well, say what each predicts about this exhibit and about the two axes she keeps apart, give me your best guess at her answer with the reasons, and tell me which further exhibit I should put on the tray in the last chapter to force the rules apart — I have two candidates, a glyph from a second valley whose descent goes back to an a but which a spelling reform has reassigned to the o-slot, and a glyph with no descent at all, drawn by a program or an untaught forger, that nonetheless does the a-job perfectly and sits in the grid as though it had always been there, and I would rather you told me which one does more work and why.
