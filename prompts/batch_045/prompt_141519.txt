Hey — I'm on the investment committee of a small liberal arts college's endowment (about $90M), and I've got to circulate a memo before next week's meeting. Can you write me a first pass? American English please.

We're deciding what to do with a one-time $6M bequest. Three options, five-year horizon, payoffs in today's dollars:

A. A locked-in $2.0M gain (bond ladder / guaranteed annuity thing).
B. 80% chance of +$3.0M, 20% chance of −$0.5M.
C. 60% chance of +$4.5M, 40% chance of −$1.5M.

Straight expected value ranks B ($2.3M) over C ($2.1M) over A ($2.0M), so B wins. But one trustee, Marla, keeps pushing a different procedure: rank the outcomes worst to best, then replace each probability p with its square before adding up, so the bad tail counts for more than its share. On her arithmetic the ordering flips and A comes out ahead by a decent margin. Separately, our committee has had an informal "floor rule" for years — never take an option with more than a 5% chance of losing more than 10% of the money at stake, whatever the upside — which knocks C out but leaves A and B standing.

The part I can't settle: our finance guy says we make twenty-ish calls like this a year across the portfolio, the tail risks are close to independent, so any weighting of the tails is just a taste that costs us money in the agregate; he also says Marla's rule, applied consistently, would make us prefer losing slowly over winning overall. Marla says that's fine for the portfolio but not here, because of our spending floor: one bad year means real cuts to financial aid, and the damage doesn't average out across years.

Draft the memo: recommend one option, and say whether we adopt Marla's rule as standing policy or only for this case. Give the finance guy's objection a real hearing instead of a strawman. Under 700 words, something I could actually put in front of five people with votes.
