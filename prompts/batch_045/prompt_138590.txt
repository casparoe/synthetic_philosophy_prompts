I'm writing a blog post on Nāgārjuna's tetralemma for readers who've done a bit of formal logic but know basically nothing about Buddhism, and I've hit a wall. Every time I try to explain it I end up saying something that either sounds like straight-up contradiction, or like nihilism ("nothing is true!"), or like mysticism ("logic stops here, woo"). I want an explanation that doesn't collapse into any of those three, and that a smart reader with just truth tables and quantifiers can actually follow.

Here's roughly how I've been setting it up. Take some question — say, wether the person who wrote this post survives their death. The tetralemma says: not (she survives), not (she doesn't survive), not (both), not (neither). I want to explain what the *point* of denying all four is.

Three things I keep getting stuck on, and they're really the whole post:

1. Why four corners rather than two? Once you've denied P and not-P, denying "both" and "neither" looks redundant — at least if the corners are ordinary truth-functional claims and the background logic is classical. So what are the corners, such that four of them are needed? Options I can think of: they're four independent metaphysical theses rather than truth-functional combinations; or they're four stances someone can take up (asserting, denying, hedging both ways, refusing) and what's being declined is the stance-taking rather than any proposition; or the background logic is meant to be non-classical in some way. Tell me which of these (or what else) actually makes the fourfold form earn its keep, and why the others don't.

2. The two-register worry. The usual saving move is to distinguish, for each corner, an "own-side" reading — true of the thing in itself, in virtue of its own intrinsic nature, independently of anything else — from a "courtesy" reading: true only by convention, dependently, relative to our practices. Then the denial targets only the own-side versions and the courtesy ones are happily kept. My worry: if that's all that's going on, the tetralemma looks like theater. It's not a denial of four corners, it's a disambiguation, and the real thesis is just "everything is dependently arisen," which you could have said in a sentence. Is that fair? Is there a version of the two-register move that isn't a dodge?

3. The grocery-list test. Run the same machinery on "there's milk in the fridge." Does the fourfold denial come out the same? If yes, hasn't the thesis been trivialized into "nothing has intrinsic nature" — a claim nobody was disputing about milk? If no, what's doing the work — some feature of the subject matter, or of how "serious" the terms are? The traditional examples are the stock set of questions the texts have the Buddha refuse to answer, and I don't care which exact list or which text, so don't pause on textual details and don't point me at anything to read. I just want to know whether the tetralemma is meant to apply only to a special class of questions or to everything, and how to make that difference intuitive to someone with no background.

Also: the denial of all four — is it a fifth corner in disguise? I've been playing with calling the two options here "self-consuming" (the tool cancels itself once used) and "self-idling" (it keeps running but stops telling you anything). Rename them if you like.

Sections, please, in this order: "Setup" (~80 words: what the reader needs in hand before the four corners show up), "The core move" (~350 words: the gut-level explanation, jargon kept to a minimum), "Why four and not two" (~250 words), "The two-register worry" (~300 words), "The grocery-list test" (~200 words), "The fifth corner" (~150 words, covering the self-consuming / self-idling fork and the emptiness-of-emptiness move), "What to cut" (~80 words: which tempting bits of the standard presentation I should drop so the post doesn't tip into woo). Use logic vocabulary freely, keep Sanskrit out of it as much as you can, and don't cite or recommend anything — I've got the reading, I want the explanation.
