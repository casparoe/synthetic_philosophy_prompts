I need you to draft the joint statement that two philosophers would put their names to after an adversarial collaboration on Church–Fitch — you know, the knowability argument: Fitch's Theorem 5 from his 1963 paper, the result that if every truth is possibly known then every truth is actually known (contrapositive: if some truth is unknown, some truth is unknowable). One of them, call her the Repairer, thinks the right response is to keep some knowability thesis but restrict its scope: the collapse only shows the unrestricted principle is false, and anti-realism can survive with a narrower one. The other, call him the Undertaker, thinks every restriction that blocks the proof is either ad hoc or else leaves you with a principle too weak to do the work knowability was supposed to do — ruling out evidence-transcendent truths, underwriting an epistemic account of truth, whatever you take the cash value of the thesis to be — so the honest move is to drop knowability entirely and say what anti-realism is without it.

What I want is the document, not a verdict on who wins. Start from what both can sign: the derivation itself and which assumptions it really needs (I take it the proof is extremely cheap — distribution of K over conjunction, factivity, a couple of modal moves — but have them say exactly which premise does the work and whether dropping factivity changes anything); that no sentence of the form p and not-known-p can be known by anybody; that this is a "choose your exit" reductio rather than a direct refutation of any single premise; and that the same style of argument can be run on other operators — provability, ideal warranted assertibility, could-be-rationally-believed-with-certainty — with the question left open which of those variants go through without factivity.

Then the disagreements, each stated in wording its own side would accept as a fair statement of their view. Then the cruxes. I have views about what some of them should be, so please use these rather than inventing vaguer ones.

(1) The ad-hocness crux. Say a class of propositions is Moorean-free if it contains no proposition of the form p and not-Kp; a restricted knowability principle survives Fitch only if its domain is Moorean-free, so the Repairer has to exhibit one. The Undertaker's proposed test: a restriction is principled only if it can be stated in a language with no epistemic vocabulary in it at all — no "known", no "knowable", nothing defined out of them — because "every truth except the Moorean ones" trivially passes the Moorean-free condition and obviously shouldn't count. Both sides should go on record about whether that test is fair, about which candidate restrictions they think pass it (restriction to non-modal truths, to observational truths, to truths with actual truthmakers, to truths statable in some base vocabulary — you pick the plausible ones), and about what they predict the test will say about each.

(2) What the possibility in "knowable" is. Does the world in which someone knows have to be reachable from ours — same laws, maybe shared history up to some point, knowers broadly like us — or is bare metaphysical possibility enough, so that a truth counts as knowable if there's some world anywhere, different physics, different creatures, where somebody knows it? Give me a test case both accept as bearing on this. I was thinking of a truth whose only possible evidence has been destroyed: the exact number of coins in a jar that was melted down before anyone counted it. Use a better case if you have one, and write down each side's prediction about whether the restricted principle looks defensible under each reading.

(3) Self-application. The knowability principle, restricted or not, is itself a candidate truth. Does the Repairer's principle have to apply to itself, and to the claim that it applies to itself, and so on up? Is there a stable stratified or typed version — knowability at level n for truths of epistemic depth n, or whatever you want to call it — or does the hierarchy either collapse back into the unrestricted thesis or become something you can't state in a single sentence? Predictions from both again.

(4) The work test, for the case where the Repairer's principle survives (1) and (3): does the surviving restricted principle still earn its keep, or has it become something a realist could happily sign too? Each side should state what would convince them they were wrong about this.

I also want each of them to say what they would count as evidence or argument against their own position — not just against the other's — and I want you to flag plainly any part of the row you think is merely verbal. I suspect some of the fight about what "in principle" and "can" mean is exactly that, and maybe the fight about how to read the quantifier over propositions (actualist versus possibilist) too, but you decide which parts and say why.

Two format things. Write the whole thing as one continuous piece of prose, like a long email to the editor of the volume that's publishing it: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold, no tables, no section breaks — just continuous prose, paragraphs only. And don't pad it with citations, references or names of papers beyond Fitch's own, since I'll be reading this without access to a library.
