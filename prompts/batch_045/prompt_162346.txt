In June I'm giving the commencement address at Marlowe College, my undergraduate alma mater — a liberal arts college of about nine hundred students, 214 of them graduating. Its seal reads *Truth sought together*; the president will open by reading from the seal, the seniors will have signed an honor code that asks them to seek the truth and to speak it, and the college chaplain will have given an invocation in which she means something quite specific and theological by the word. In the audience will be the class (four philosophy majors among them, one of whom wrote a senior thesis on the prosentential theory of truth and will be listening for tells), the faculty in regalia, several hundred parents and grandparents, my father — a retired judge with no patience for anything he would call clever — and the director of the college's new Center for Truth and Public Life, who invited me and who has twice said she hopes I will say something about truth. What she has in mind is the spring's academic-integrity scandal: a great many essays and at least two senior theses turned out to have been written by a language model, and the campus has spent the year using the word truth constantly while meaning a dozen different things by it.

The difficulty is that I am a philosopher, and I am a deflationist about truth. I do not think truth is a substantive property with a hidden nature that inquiry gradually homes in on; I think that *is true* is a thin device — for endorsing a claim without repeating it, for generalizing over everything someone said, for semantic ascent — and that the real work in that neighborhood is done by evidence, justification, care, and the norms of assertion. So I cannot, without mental reservation, give the speech everyone is expecting: truth as a mountain the graduates will spend their lives climbing, truth as correspondence to a mind-independent reality that explains why true beliefs serve us, Truth with a capital T as something to pledge one's life to, and a peroration sending the class out to serve it.

What I want from you is the address I would actually give. Concretely: twelve to fifteen minutes, spoken English, no named theorists and no jargon; the families and the chaplain have to be able to follow it and be moved by it, my father must not hear it as condescension, and the four philosophy majors should recognize a deflationist speaking rather than someone who lost their nerve. It cannot be a debunking lecture, and it cannot be a shrug. It cannot cheat either: the emotional climax must not be carried by exactly the robust-truth rhetoric the rest of the speech disavows. It has to do something with the scandal — the graduates should come away with something they can actually do about what they assert, what they pass along, and what they hand in under their own name. And it has to face rather than dodge one question in particular: if *true* is thin, what is the honor code asking these students to do when it asks them to seek the truth? My own guess is that the honest answer runs through the difference between being wrong and not caring whether one is wrong — the vice of indifference to how things actually are, which needs no metaphysics of truth in order to be a real vice — and through the fact that saying *what she told me is true* is a way of vouching for a person you cannot fully quote. But do not feel bound by that guess if you can do better.
