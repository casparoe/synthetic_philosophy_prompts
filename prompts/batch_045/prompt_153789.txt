i'm putting together a tiny zine of form-poems about arguments — one argument per poem, and the house rule is that the form has to be doing some of the philosophical work, not just decorating it. i've got most of them finished and i'm stuck on the coherence arguments for expected utility maximization, which is the one i care most about not getting wrong. would of written it myself but i'm a terrible poet.

what i want is a sestina. six sestets plus a three-line envoi, 39 lines, no rhyme needed. the six end words are:

better / coin / circle / trade / loss / home

and they rotate the standard way — if the first sestet ends 1-2-3-4-5-6, the second ends 6-1-5-2-4-3, then 3-6-4-1-2-5, 5-3-2-6-1-4, 4-5-1-3-6-2, 2-4-6-5-3-1. in the envoi all six come back (they can sit mid-line as well as at line end, that's traditional), and the very last word of the poem has to be "coin". the returning words should feel like the cycle closing in on itself rather than like homework. keep the speaker consistent: someone who is both inside the cycle — getting traded at, step by step — and able to step outside it and say what the argument has actually bought.

how the content should break down:

- first three stanzas: the naive picture. a chooser with cyclic preferences gets money-pumped, one swap at a time, every individual swap a good deal by its own lights, ending up holding what it started with and less money. but do not let it drift into being a dutch book: this is a sequence of trades, each one a sure loss at the moment it's accepted, not a set of bets that are separately acceptable and jointly guaranteed to lose. somebody who knows the literature should be able to tell from the poem which one you're doing and why they're not the same argument.
- the turn at stanza four: the reply i keep running into. the pump is supposed to work only on a myopic chooser; a chooser who can see the whole sequence coming just declines one of the swaps and no money leaves its pocket. so the argument shows at most that cyclic preferences are unliveable for someone who can't plan ahead. put that objection in — and put in what it costs, that the chooser's answer now depends on which step of the sequence it happens to be standing at, or on what it thinks is still coming, so the same pairwise comparison flips depending on where you catch it. don't resolve whether that's irrational.
- stanzas five and six: the narrowing, which is the bit i actually want the poem to land. even if you grant the whole thing, what the pump has bought you is acyclicity — and something like completeness — not expected utility maximization. getting all the way to EU maximization needs independence, and that's the axiom people actually break in Allais-style cases, and whether there's a pump-style pragmatic argument for it of the same force as the one for transitivity is exactly what people argue about. i want the poem to register that the argument arrives somewhere much smaller than the thing it's advertised as supporting, and to let that disagreement stay visible instead of smoothing it over.
- envoi: the bill. all six words, "coin" last.

constraints: no technical vocabulary inside the poem itself — no "acyclicity", no "independence axiom", no "dutch book", no "intransitive" — and no footnotes or explanation after it, the poem has to carry it. but a decision theorist should be able to point at a line and say which claim it's making and whether that claim is the strong one or the deflated one. and somebody who knows none of this should still think it's a good poem: real images, some sound-play, loose iambic pentameter that doesn't dogtrot. keep seperate the part that's the cycle itself and the part that's the commentary on what the cycle proves.
