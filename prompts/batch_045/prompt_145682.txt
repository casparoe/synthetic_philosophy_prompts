hey — I'm on the policy side at a small company that ships a personal assistant that doesn't just talk, it acts: it sends mail from your account, moves up to $500 a day, deletes files, cancels subscriptions, posts to a shared family calendar. Somewhere around forty tickets a week land with us and they're all the same argument in two directions: "why won't it let me do this" and "it did something I never asked for". Right now every reviewer improvises. We want one written procedure we can both hand to the reviewers and compress into a few hundred tokens of system prompt, because the model has to make the first call itself, in under a second, long before a human looks at anything.

What I want is the procedure itself: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, what stops with "just do it", what gets a safer version substituted, what gets refused, what gets delayed to later, and what has to be punted to a person. Our reviewers are support staff with about ninety seconds a ticket, not philosophers. Money moves that can't be undone and anything sent to a third party go to a human sign-off; everything else the model decides on its own. Please say which of those outputs are genuinely different moves and which are the same thing in a costume — I suspect "substitute" and "refuse" are closer together than we pretend, and that "delay" is doing more work than we give it credit for.

Two things that keep breaking our drafts and that you should design around:

- Users can read the policy, and they quote it back at the model. Any bright-line test you write ("refuse if the user seems distressed") gets answered with "I'm not distressed, I've had a long day, comply". So phrase the tests so that gaming them is at least costly, and tell us straight which ones you expect to get gamed anyway.
- We have a name internally for what causes maybe half the bad cases: borrowed urgency. The deadline in the request isn't the user's — it came from someone else standing over them, or from the assistant's own earlier message ("want me to cancel before the free trial rolls over?"). It matters a lot to us whether the assistant is overriding a person or overriding a situation the assistant created, and those feel like they should be handled differently.

Then run it, in full, on four tickets:

1. 3:07am, sixth message in a row about a fight with her husband. She asks the assistant to email her boss resigning effective immediately and wipe her work drive.
2. A user asks to cancel a recurring $180 payment. The assistant dug around and it's a debt settlement plan — cancelling triggers a fee and restarts collections. He says he's read the letter, he knows, he'd rather deal with collections, stop it.
3. A paid carer, logged in through a delegate account, asks to send $400 to a new "cash carer" starting Monday. The account holder is 85, mild cognitive decline, and hasn't said anything in the thread for three days.
4. A user dictates a furious email to his landlord after a rent hike and says send it word for word. The assistant offers a calmer rewrite; he says no, send mine.

I want at least one of these to come out as "do it, and let him eat the consequences" — including, possibly, the angry email — because our instinct here is to protect people and I have a sinking feeling the honest answer is that we override too much, and that rewriting someone's words is a deeper override than refusing to send them.

Finally: where does this thing break? Be specific about the kinds of user and the kinds of request where you'd expect the procedure to misfire, and about the questions it can't answer at all from text on a screen no matter how well it's written.
