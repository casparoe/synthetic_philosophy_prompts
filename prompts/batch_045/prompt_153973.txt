I'm presenting next month at a joint session of a psychiatric epidemiology research seminar and a philosophy of medicine reading group, and what I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the position.

The paper: psychiatric classifications are loop-sensitive instruments, and a nosology should say so on its face. I propose that each diagnostic category carry an explicit uptake model — a stated expectation about how the category's own introduction will move measured prevalence, help-seeking, and self-description — and an instability flag, raised when the category's estimated loop gain exceeds a fixed threshold. I define loop gain as the ratio of the observed change in measured prevalence over a fixed window to the change that would have been measured with instruments and help-seeking rates held at their pre-adoption values, and I estimate it from jurisdictions that adopted the category at different dates. The conceptual core is a three-way decomposition that I argue the literature runs together: an ascertainment loop, in which clinicians and instruments get better at finding what they have been told to look for; a presentation loop, in which people present differently, or present at all, because the category now exists and is reimbursable; and a constitutive loop in Hacking's sense, in which the kind itself shifts because the people classified reorganize their experience, memory, and behaviour around the classification. My empirical claim is that prevalence time series of the kind nosologies are defended with cannot separate these three, and that the authority of the classification rests on their sum being read as a fact about the world. The policy upshot I actually have to defend: for flagged categories, changes in measured prevalence should not be treated as evidence about underlying disease frequency in funding or service-planning decisions.

The room: five or six psychiatric epidemiologists and nosologists who think in terms of instrument design, ascertainment, inter-rater reliability, and non-identifiability; three clinicians, one openly impatient with philosophy and one sympathetic but worried about the patient sitting in front of her; two philosophers of science who know the looping-effect literature and will want to know whether I am making a metaphysical claim about human kinds or merely an epistemic claim about measurement; and one service-user representative who will ask who bears the cost of this proposal. I am a philosopher of science by training and not an epidemiologist, and that will be in the room.

Give me the objections I will actually get, in roughly the order they will arrive, each phrased the way that person or subgroup would phrase it: the epidemiologists in the vocabulary of identification and diagnostic substitution, the clinicians in the vocabulary of the consultation room, the philosophers asking whether this is dynamic nominalism with a confidence interval bolted onto it. For each, separate the damage it does to the conceptual core from the damage it does to the policy upshot — I expect the two to come apart — and give me what I should concede before I am asked, a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds, and where that reply is soft. Include the steelman of the objection I am most tempted to dismiss: that everything I describe is already handled under familiar headings like diagnostic inflation, concept creep, and disease mongering, and that my apparatus adds nothing a careful epidemiologist does not already do. I would rather taboo social construction than lose the session to it, so tell me how to close that line down without giving away the substance. Finally: the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and the weakest joint in my own argument that I should volunteer rather than hope the room misses.
