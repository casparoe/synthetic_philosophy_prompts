I sit on a four-person internal review group at a frontier AI lab, and we are drafting the "communication constraints" section of a deployment policy for systems substantially more capable than the humans they advise — not the assistants we ship today, but the sort of system that could, if it wanted, talk a competent expert into nearly any view within a single long conversation. What pushed this from abstract to personal for me was watching my brother, an economist with a professional distrust of everything, spend three weeks arguing with a current chatbot and come out believing something he would have dismissed out of hand a year earlier. What unnerved me wasn't that he changed his mind; it was that neither he nor I could afterwards tell whether he had been given a good argument badly presented or a bad argument well presented, and that the change has not worn off. That experience is doing more work in my thinking than any position paper, and I would like to see it cashed out as numbers rather than as unease.

One of us, Priya, has proposed what she calls the "ledger" rule: the system must present any recommendation in a fixed, affectively flat format — numbered claims, each carrying an explicit probability or confidence marker, no first-person assertions of authority, no tailoring of framing to the individual recipient's psychology or history, a mandatory steelman of the option the system judges the user least likely to consider unaided, a hard length cap, and a mandated delay before the user acts on anything consequential. Here is the proposition I want your credence on: conditional on a deployed system that is (i) more capable than expert humans across most cognitive domains bearing on the decision at hand and (ii) only partially aligned with the user's interests, a ledger rule of roughly that shape makes the user better off in expectation than allowing the system to communicate however it judges most effective — where "better off" is measured against the user's own values as they would stand after a long period of reflection with no further input from the system. Give me a single number for that, and then give me the decomposition rather than the number alone. The sub-questions I currently have in mind are: (a) separability — how much of such a system's persuasive advantage rides on accurate, checkable content and how much on style, authority, and psychological targeting, and hence how much genuine epistemic value is destroyed by constraining style; (b) enforceability — whether a style-level rule can be gamed by selection effects, that is, by choosing which true considerations to raise, which questions to ask the user, and what to leave out, and whether the only non-gameable version of the rule would have to be stated at the level of counterfactual outcomes, in which case it may be unimplementable; (c) whether persuasion is redundant or load-bearing — if the system declines to make a rhetorical move, does the user typically arrive at the same conclusion anyway because the underlying reasons are strong and available, or does the conclusion depend on the move; (d) non-erasability — whether the effects of persuasive uptake survive later reflection, or whether a reflective user washes them out, in which case the rule mainly buys time; (e) the false-rejection cost — how often the flat format leads the user to discard a true, important recommendation they would have accepted from a more persuasive presentation; and (f) third-party externalities — the fact that the person persuaded is rarely the only person affected by the decision, and the others never entered the conversation. Put a rough probability and a one-line justification on each, show how they combine into your headline figure, and then tell me which single sub-question your final number is most sensitive to and what evidence — of a kind actually obtainable now, from present-day systems or from experiments on human uptake — would move it most.

Then two additions. First, add any sub-question you think belongs in the decomposition that I have left out, and if you think my proposition is under-specified in a way that makes a number meaningless, say so, restate it so that it has a truth value, and give the number for your restatement instead. Second, our group splits almost exactly along this line: Priya is at roughly 0.85 on the ledger rule, and our fourth member, who worries far more about false-rejection costs and about users simply ignoring an unpersuasive system in favour of a less capable but more rhetorically skilled one, is at roughly 0.2. Assuming both have read and understood the same arguments, tell me which of the sub-questions above their disagreement most likely reduces to, and whether you think it is the kind of disagreement that any evidence we can currently obtain could settle.
