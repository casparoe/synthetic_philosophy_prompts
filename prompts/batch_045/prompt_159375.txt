I work at a place that refurbishes phones, and part of my job is grading trade-in screens as cracked or not cracked, so I end up staring at hairline fractures all day. A couple months ago a friend got me into that old heap / bald man puzzle and I've been chewing on it ever since, with no classes or nothing behind me. As I understand the two positions people actually fight about: one says there really is an exact total crack length at which "cracked" stops applying, we just can never find out where it is; the other says there are lots of acceptable ways of drawing the line, and around the middle a screen is neither cracked nor not-cracked. Neither one felt right to me, so I made up my own, which I call the drift view: there is always an exact line, but it isn't a fixed number of millimetres, its set by whatever few screens you've just had in your hands, so it's never hidden from you, you only have to ask what you were comparing against.

Last week my friend wrote out an argument that seems to kill all three options, including the ones he likes, and I can't locate the hole. (1) Take the first view: there is an exact cut-off in crack length, and no one could ever find out where, no matter how good their evidence got. (2) If a proposed difference could never show up in any possible experience, investigation, or decision, then there is no difference — the "two" states of the world are one situation described two ways. (3) So "the cut-off is at 3.72 mm" and "the cut-off is at 3.73 mm" describe the very same situation, and likewise for every neighbouring pair. (4) But if the boundary's being at 3.72 is the same situation as its being at 3.73, and so on down the line, then there is no exact cut-off at all, since a sharp boundary has to be at exactly one place and not at its neighbours. (5) So the sharp-cut-off view destroys itself. (6) The drift view goes the same way: the line is set by the comparison batch, but which batch was the relevant one — the last three screens, the last five, the whole morning's pile — is exactly the sort of thing no investigation could settle, so by (2) there's no fact about which line is in force and my view collapses into "no exact line" too. (7) And the many-sharpenings view goes as well: which sharpenings count as acceptable is also something no possible evidence settles, so by (2) there's no fact about which ones we're supposed to check the sentence against, and truth-on-all-of-them never gets defined. (8) So nothing on offer says what is going on in the middle region, which can't be right, because the puzzle is a puzzle about something. He did cheerfully admit the argument overreaches — by (2) there'd be no fact of the matter about whether some particular speck of dust sat on some particular rock four billion years ago, since nothing could ever tell us — and he says he's happy to bite that. I don't know whether that's where the trouble is or somewhere else in the chain.

Which step do you reject? Is the argument valid and (2) simply the thing to give up, or is there a bad move in the step from (3) to (4), or in how (6) and (7) describe the views they attack? Explain why the step looked fine, and then tell me what each of the three options — sharp cut-off, many sharpenings, my drift view — has to pay depending on which step we drop. If it matters what I'd rather keep: I'd rather keep my drift view than keep (2), and I'd rather keep "something is going on in the borderline region" than keep any particular theory of it. Lead with the one sentence saying which step you'd reject, then do the pricing; keep the whole thing under 600 words.
