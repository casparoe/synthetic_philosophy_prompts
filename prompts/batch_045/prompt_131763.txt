My father has recently discovered behavioral genetics and has started sending me articles. The last one was about the heritability of educational attainment, and his note said roughly this: if about 40 percent of who ends up with a degree is genetic, then about 40 percent of what we spend on closing attainment gaps is being spent on something that cannot be moved. My wife is five months pregnant, and I work in a field where people make预算 decisions on exactly this kind of evidence, so I sat down to write him a reply. The argument got away from me. As written, it seems to show that heritability estimates license no inference about intervention at all — which cannot be right, because a friend of mine is a dairy cattle geneticist and her office runs on predictions made with that very number. I would like you to tell me which step to give up.

(1) Heritability, in the quantitative-genetics sense, is a ratio of variances: narrow-sense h² is additive genetic variance over total phenotypic variance (V_A/V_P), computed in a specified population living in a specified range of environments. It is a property of a population–environment pair, not of a trait and not of a person.

(2) A ratio defined over a population is not a decomposition of any individual's outcome. Even if height is around 80 percent heritable in a population like ours, nothing follows about why my particular child is short; he may be short entirely because of illness or diet, with no relevant genetic difference at all.

(3) Nor does a within-population estimate license a conclusion about the cause of a difference between populations. Height rose substantially across twentieth-century generations with no change in the gene pool, and that is fully compatible with high heritability of height within each generation; likewise, the causes of a trait can be entirely genetic while the trait is entirely preventable environmentally, as with phenylketonuria.

(4) And heritability is compatible with arbitrarily large effects of changing the environment for everyone. Because interventions that help also tend to equalize, they shrink environmental variance — the denominator — and thereby raise heritability. Better and fairer conditions can improve a trait and increase its heritability at the same time.

(5) So a heritability estimate never underwrites a claim of the form: this fraction of the outcome is fixed by genes and only the remainder is available to us.

(6) My father's inference has exactly that form. So his conclusion does not follow.

(7) But the same number, measured in the same population, is the coefficient in the breeder's equation, R = h²S, which is what lets my friend predict how far the mean of a herd will move in one generation if she chooses which animals breed. Those predictions are tested every season and they work; they concern a population mean, not an individual, and not a comparison between populations. If (5) is right, that success is unexplained.

(8) Therefore (5) is too strong, and one of (2), (3), (4), (5) is doing work I did not notice.

Two complications I can see but cannot place. First, the figure in my father's article comes from twin studies, whereas the coefficient in my friend's equation is a narrow-sense estimate derived from relatives; I do not know whether that is where the trouble is. Second, I gather the breeder's equation carries assumptions — about the range of environments being held fixed, about genotype–environment correlation, and about applying to one generation at a time — and I do not know whether those assumptions are what separate (6) from (7), or what their failure would mean for my friend's numbers.

Please start with your bottom line in the first sentence — which step you reject, or whether the argument is valid and a premise must go — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question. Then: say precisely which inferences a heritability estimate in a given population does license and which it does not, including what it licenses about a mean versus an individual versus a gap between populations; tell me what rejecting each of the other candidate steps would cost me; and tell me what, if anything, my father is entitled to conclude. I am not willing to give up (7), and I am even less willing to give up (2) or (3), since conceding either hands him his original conclusion.
