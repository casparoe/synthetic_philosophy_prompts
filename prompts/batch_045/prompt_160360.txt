I am a statistician: I design and analyse large-scale online experiments for a living, and behind that I have a few years of recreational reading in epistemology. My working vocabulary is not justification, standards, and epistemic position but hypothesis space, prior, likelihood, posterior, Bayes factor, loss function, error probabilities and power, decision rule, pre-registered analysis plan, stopping rule, calibration, and the principal–agent relation between whoever commissions an analysis and whoever consumes it. Threshold values in my world are set by policy and then defended in a review meeting; they are not discovered.

Translate into that vocabulary the dispute between epistemic contextualism and pragmatic encroachment (interest-relative invariantism) about the predicate knows — both the substantial core the two views share and the exact point at which they diverge. I want the translation as faithful as you can make it: state, in my terms, what each view says has to be true of an agent's posterior, of her loss function, and of whoever is doing the attributing, for her to count as knowing.

Then give me the residue report, in three parts. First, what mapped cleanly — I expect the subject's practical stakes to translate into something very like the asymmetry of a loss function, and the contextualist's shifty standards into something very like a threshold fixed by the attributor's situation rather than the subject's. Second, what you had to distort in order to get it into my vocabulary, and exactly where you had to smuggle in an assumption of mine and call it a translation: I particularly want flagged any place where knowledge is doing normative work — as a norm of assertion, as what licenses treating a proposition as a reason in further reasoning, as what factivity is supposed to secure — for which my vocabulary has no slot, and any place where my framework merely stipulates a threshold by convention at the point where the philosophers think something is being determined. Third, what cannot be expressed in my terms at all.

Two cases from my side of the fence, which I do not think appear among the cases the philosophers use. Run each through the translation in both directions: state it in the philosophers' terms, translate their answers back into mine, and tell me whether either case is a genuine problem for either theory or merely an artefact of how my world is set up.

(i) Delegated stakes. I commission an analysis at low stakes, commit to a threshold in a pre-registered plan before I see any data, and certify a result as having cleared it. A downstream consumer then applies that certified result to a decision whose losses are a hundred times larger than mine, under a protocol I did not design and cannot see. My certificate does not obviously travel. Whose stakes, and whose threshold, fix whether I knew — and does the answer differ between the two theories in a way that matters to anyone holding the certificate?

(ii) Threshold-dependent stakes. In my world the loss function is not always exogenous to the inquiry: further evidence costs time, and the cost of delay is part of the loss, so the stakes of the decision depend on the stopping rule, and the stopping rule is set by the threshold I adopt. Raising my evidential standards raises the stakes, which raises my standards. Is there a determinate fact about what I know in such a case, or does the theory deliver an answer only at a fixed point of that loop — and what should either theorist say when there is none, or when there are several?

Finish with a verdict on the residue. Does what fails to translate point to something a decision-theoretic vocabulary like mine ought to have a slot for and lacks? Or does it suggest that the contextualist and the pragmatic encroacher are disagreeing about less than it looks like — say, about who gets to set the threshold, which in my world is a question of protocol design rather than a question of fact? If the honest answer is a mixture, tell me which parts of the dispute go which way, and why.
