You are a philosopher of law who writes on atrocity crimes, collective memory, and the politics of legal categories, and who also reads film carefully. Think this through before answering, and open with a short brainstorm: name the two or three readings of the film you first reached for and set aside, with one sentence each on why they are too easy.

I teach a unit on the politics of naming atrocities and want to use Terry George's Hotel Rwanda in a session that does not collapse into a morality play. Here is the frame I have been developing. When Lemkin coined the term and the eventual Genocide Convention fixed its definition, the word was asked to do at least three separable jobs, and most fights about naming are really fights about which job matters most. First, the legal trigger: applying the name activates a distinctive regime, a duty to prevent and punish that does not attach to war crimes or crimes against humanity in the same way. Second, the intent-claim: it asserts that the killing aims at the destruction of a group as such, in the special-intent sense, rather than being a byproduct of counterinsurgency, revenge, land hunger, or a collapsing army — and it asserts this about a group drawn from a list that, as I understand it, covers national, ethnical, racial and religious groups and pointedly not political ones. Third, the ranking: it makes an expressive, superlative claim — this is the worst thing — which is what gives the word its mobilizing force and is also what its critics say demotes every atrocity it is withheld from.

A student proposed amputating the third job: keep the legal trigger tied to the treaty name, invent a deliberately flat descriptive category for the intent-claim — call it, say, ascriptive mass killing, violence directed at people under a group-description, carrying no superlative — and let the ranking go. The obvious objection, and the one I want tested against the film, is that the power to force action may ride entirely on the superlative, so that a more accurate vocabulary would also be a more useless one.

Read the film through that dispute, using scenes you would actually put on a handout. Three I have in mind, described rather than quoted since neither of us can check a transcript: the exchange with the Western journalist about the footage, where he predicts that viewers will register the horror and go on eating their dinners; the UN officer's insistence that his mandate forbids him to act, and the near-total absence of the word from anyone with authority — my sense is that the only person who uses it is Paul, throwing it at the general near the climax, as a private moral accusation rather than an official classification, and I would want to know if that is right; and the closing text, which reports that the two Hutu men were tried and sentenced for war crimes, a lesser charge, so that the film's own last word withholds the term from the legal record. Do these scenes support the student's split, or do they suggest the film's real subject is not naming but salience, and that a non-ranking vocabulary would fail harder rather than better?

Then the harder half: where does the film resist the reading? My worry is that it is itself a naming machine, needing its audience to supply both the word and the shudder, and that its emotional architecture depends on exactly the hierarchy the student wants to dissolve. Is that a failure of nerve, or does the film know it and make something of it? Do not give me a bibliography or a reading list — I want the reading, and I cannot follow up on citations anyway. Close with one sentence on what the film adds to this debate that the seminar's argument alone does not.
