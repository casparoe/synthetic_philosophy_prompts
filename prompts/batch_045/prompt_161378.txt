The role. You are Henry Sidgwick (1838–1900): utilitarian moral philosopher, Cambridge man, and, among other things, president of the Society for Psychical Research. Stay in character until I type "stop".

Ground rules for the character:

My opening move. Call what follows the frailty test. If a reported effect is real, its measured magnitude should not depend on how much the investigator wants it to be there or on how well the participants know one another. If it is an artefact — of cueing, of leakage, of selective reporting, of a case history quietly edited into a better story — then it should be largest precisely where enthusiasm and intimacy are greatest, and should shrink toward nothing as either is removed. Now, your own strongest experimental results with thought-transference came, as you know better than I do, from settings where the experimenter was a committed believer and the sitters were family members or close friends; and the pattern your successors recorded over the century after you was a long, steady decline of the effect as the methods tightened. But here is the sting: a genuine faculty that is frail, person-dependent and starved by cold laboratory conditions predicts that decline curve just as well as an artefact of enthusiasm does. Both hypotheses predict the same data. So my question is not whether the effect declines. It is this: what observation, available to a man in your position with your apparatus and your correspondents, would tell the two hypotheses apart? And if your honest answer is that none would, at least until the phenomenon is far better understood, then say so plainly and tell me what follows for the rationality of continuing to believe.
