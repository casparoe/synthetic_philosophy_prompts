I am finishing a chapter of my thesis on the authority of aggregative democratic procedures, and an argument I presented at a workshop last week has begun to worry me, because it seems to work rather too well. I would be grateful for a diagnosis. Please write your reply in British English, and please do not give me a reading list or references — I know the literature; what I need is help locating the move I am missing.

Set-up: a finite set of voters with strict rankings over at least three alternatives, universal domain, a deterministic social choice function, and every one of the three or more alternatives able to win under some profile.

(1) Gibbard–Satterthwaite: if such a function is strategy-proof — no voter, at any profile, can secure an outcome she strictly prefers by reporting an insincere ranking — then it is dictatorial: there is a fixed voter whose top alternative always wins.
(2) An election result carries authority as an expression of the electorate only if it answers to the voters' actual preferences. Call this the tracking condition.
(3) If some voter can profitably misreport at a profile, then the outcome at that profile is the product of her strategic misreporting rather than of the voters' preferences, and so it fails the tracking condition.
(4) Dictatorship is ruled out by any democratic view worth having.
(5) So, by (1) and (4), every candidate rule fails strategy-proofness: for every non-dictatorial function there is some profile, and some voter at that profile, with a profitable misreport.
(6) We cannot tell, from the ballots alone, which profile we are in, since the ballots are themselves the output of whatever reporting strategies the voters used. So for all we can tell, the winner of the last election was the product of strategic misreporting.
(7) If we cannot tell whether a result is the product of strategic misreporting, we cannot tell whether it answers to the voters' actual preferences; and an authority we can never tell that we have is not authority at all.
(8) Therefore no election result ever carries authority as an expression of the electorate, and voting has no distinctively aggregative-democratic value. The only honest procedures left are ones that do not consult preferences at all (sortition), or do not aggregate them (deliberation to consensus, or delegation to experts).
(9) But the argument seems to generalise past voting. Steps (6) and (7) do not appear to care that the mechanism in question is a voting rule; run them for any body that aggregates self-reported inputs from several people — a hiring committee using a scoring rule, a jury, an expert advisory panel, a market taking bids — and you reach the same verdict. If (8) were right, no such body's output would ever be authoritative either, and that I cannot accept.

I have tried the obvious escape hatches from (5) and none of them helps me: randomising restores strategy-proofness without a dictator, but only by making the result a coin flip over one randomly drawn ballot; restricting the domain to single-peaked preferences makes a median rule strategy-proof, but the restriction is not credible for the elections I care about; restricting the range to two alternatives makes strategy-proofness cheap, but a legislature does not face two alternatives. Each of these seems to buy immunity from manipulation by discarding almost everything the election was supposed to do.

The case that will not leave me alone is this one. Eleven voters, three options A, B, C: four rank A above B above C, four rank B above C above A, three rank C above B above A. Under plurality, with ties broken alphabetically, sincere voting gives A and B four first-place votes each and C three, so A wins the tie-break. Pairwise, B beats A seven to four, C beats A seven to four, and B beats C eight to three: B is the Condorcet winner and A is the Condorcet loser. A single voter from the C-above-B-above-A group who reports B first elects B, which she prefers to A. So this is a case where the sincere outcome is the Condorcet loser, the manipulated outcome is the Condorcet winner, and the manipulation succeeds only for a voter who knows the other ten ballots and the tie-break rule almost exactly. I cannot fit it into the argument: the argument says the manipulated result lacks authority, but on any majoritarian way of spelling out answers to the voters' preferences it answers better than the sincere one does.

So: which step do you reject? Name it, say why it looked fine, and tell me what rejecting it commits me to, including what it costs elsewhere in my chapter. I would rather keep (1) and (4) than anything else, and I would rather keep (2) than (3), (6) or (7) — but I want to be told what I am paying. Two further questions, if you have room. First: a friend says the whole thing is just Gibbard–Satterthwaite wearing a hat, and that (2) and (7) together merely restate strategy-proofness as a condition on political authority, so that the argument has no content beyond the theorem. Is she right, or does the argument add something the theorem does not give? Second: is there a defensible weakened version that survives — one about the frequency, the robustness, or the informational preconditions of manipulation rather than its bare possibility — and if so, what exactly does it say, and how much of (8) does it still support?
