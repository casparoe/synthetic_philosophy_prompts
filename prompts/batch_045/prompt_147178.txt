hey — so I'm taking this "animal minds" seminar this semester and I also work about ten hours a week as the student assistant on my university's food and grounds purchasing group. We're the people who decide things like what protein goes in the dining hall, what the grounds crew sprays, whether the community garden gets a beehive. And honestly like half the proposals we now get involve insects somehow, and we have zero framework for them. I need you to build me one.

Some constraints so it's actually usable: we meet once a month, I get maybe two hours to prep each proposal, the output has to fit on one page, and it gets signed off by my boss — she's an ecologist, she's fine with numbers, she will absolutely not sit still for philosophy. Then it goes to student government, which means anything that sounds like "insects might matter morally" gets laughed at unless every step is spelled out.

Here's the part I want you to be careful with. The naive move is: probability that an insect is sentient is small but not zero, the numbers are astronomical, so expected welfare is enormous and insects dominate everything. My seminar gave me three reasons that's too fast, and I want the procedure to actually carry them rather than gesture at them.

One: it isn't a per-insect lottery. The evidence we have — nociception, whether they show protective or grooming behaviour toward injuries, whether they'll trade a cost to avoid a aversive stimulus, whether analgesia changes the response — is evidence about a whole lineage at once. So it's closer to one big shared lottery that we've all bet on together, not trillions of independent coin flips, and there's no diversification. What does that do to how I should weigh a tiny probability against a huge count?

Two: population counterfactuals. Insects are mostly the kind of animal that has tons of offspring and almost all of them die young anyway. If we kill a bunch, the population often just refills, so the "suffering prevented" we totalled up may not be prevented at all — we may just have changed who suffers, or shortened lives that were going to be short and bad anyway. I want a step that forces me to say what the counterfactual baseline is before I do any arithmetic.

Three: our choice changes the probability. Buying cricket flour creates the demand that makes more crickets get farmed; releasing ten thousand ladybugs into the greenhouse creates a lot of ladybugs that are then going to starve. The number of insects isn't fixed independently of what we do.

Also — and my professor kept pushing this — I don't want to settle on one probability. What I'd rather have is a kind of robustness test: a proposal only gets a green light if it looks defensible under three different assumptions — insects aren't sentient at all and only effects on vertebrates and ecosystems count; insects count for a little; insects count for a lot. Say clearly what happens when the three disagree, and be honest that picking the weight is a value call and not something a procedure can settle.

So: give me the ordered steps, what counts as a good enough answer at each one, where the procedure just stops and says no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person. Tell me which steps are really judgment calls wearing a procedure costume.

Then run it on three real proposals I've got on my desk:

1. Swap the whey in the dining hall protein bars for cricket powder, which the supplier says cuts the carbon footprint by a lot and lets us cut our chicken order.
2. Aphid outbreak in the teaching greenhouse. Options are neem oil, buying a box of ten thousand ladybugs to release, or doing nothing and losing the spring planting.
3. A student group wants to convert a mowed lawn to a wildflower meadow for pollinators, and separately wants to put two honeybee hives on it. I'm vegan and I'm pretty sure this one is the good guy, so please actually run it — and if the procedure spits out something I don't like, say so plainly rather than softening it.

Last thing: tell me what the procedure can't do. Where should I just not use it?
