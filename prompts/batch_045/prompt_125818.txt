I am writing an essay on persistence for a metaphysics seminar that uses film as a teaching text, and I want to use Christopher Nolan's The Prestige (2006, from Christopher Priest's novel) as the case study rather than the usual parade of ship-of-Theseus examples. I need a reading that is faithful in both directions: one that does not flatten the film into an illustration of a view, and does not bend a view to fit the film.

For the seminar's purposes, gloss the dispute as follows, so that the reading does not depend on prior acquaintance with the literature. Endurantism: a persisting thing is wholly present at each time at which it exists, and persists by being wholly present at many times. Perdurantism: a persisting thing has temporal parts or stages, each confined to a time, and persists by having different stages at different times; worm theorists identify the thing with the whole spacetime worm, stage theorists with each individual stage. Standard motivation for perdurantism: the problem of temporary intrinsics and the treatment of fission, where both post-fission continuers can be said to share the pre-fission stages.

The scenes I have in mind: Borden's Transported Man; Angier's Real Transported Man, run on the machine Tesla builds, which produces a duplicate while leaving the original in place, with one of the two then dropping through the stage into a water tank and drowning; Angier's remark, near the end, that he no longer knows whether he will be the man in the box or the prestige, and that he is no longer sure of his own identity; the revelation that Borden is two identical twins living a single life under one name, alternately appearing as the magician and as the assistant Fallon, one brother loving Sarah and the other Olivia, one brother losing two fingers and then having the same two fingers removed from the other to keep the disguise; and the closing image of the storeroom with its rows of tanks holding the drowned bodies.

The crux I want worked through is what I have been calling the prestige problem, and it is not the standard fission case: the duplicate is causally downstream of the original rather than a symmetric branch, the original and the duplicate briefly coexist, the original is then killed, and the procedure is repeated serially so that each night's survivor is himself the product of the previous night's duplication. So:

1. On each of endurantism, worm theory, and stage theory, is Angier's deliberative question — will I be the man in the box or the prestige? — even well posed? If it is not, what replaces it, and does the replacement leave Angier's gamble rational, suicidal, or merely confused? Note that on the worm-theoretic reading he survives both ways, and on the endurantist reading the duplicate is not him and every performance is a death; if those are the options, the film's framing of the machine as a gamble looks like a mistake rather than a piece of nerve.

2. Does perdurantism's usual advantage in fission cases transfer here, where the relation between originator and duplicate is asymmetric and causal? And if the perdurantist says that the pre-duplication Angier already contained two overlapping continuers, is the film committed to two streams of consciousness in him before the machine runs?

3. Does either view accommodate Borden, where two organisms seem to compose one person, and is the complaint that the brothers each had only half a life a claim about identity or about what matters in survival?

4. In the tank room, how many deaths are there, and does the horror the film invites depend on a persistence view?

5. Finally, where does the reading fail? The film conflates numerical identity with sameness of role and public persona, its machinery is magic rather than a clean intuition pump, and its closing insistence that the audience wants to be fooled may be offering a meta-answer: that which continuer is *the* person is settled by attention or convention rather than by metaphysics. Say whether the film earns that, or whether it is trading on a confusion.

Please give me about 1,200 words, name the two or three scenes you would actually screen in class, do not quote dialogue or otherwise rely on verbatim lines, and close by saying what, if anything, the film adds to the metaphysical dispute that the arguments alone do not. If Priest's novel changes any of this, add a sentence.
