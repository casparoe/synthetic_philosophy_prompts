I'm teaching a seminar on folk psychology next term and I want to get properly beaten up by the other side before I walk in, so: please play a committed defender of intentional realism about beliefs and desires — sharp, well read, not a strawman, willing to give me small points but never the main one. Roughly where I want you to stand: belief-desire psychology's posits earn their keep by inference to the best explanation, their job is given functionally rather than by what they're made of, so they can be multiply realised, and the usual eliminativist analogies (caloric, phlogiston, witches) misfire because in those cases a successor theory explained something the old posits conspicuously couldn't, whereas FP has no failures of that kind and neuroscience is in the business of specifying realizers, i.e. of reducing rather than eliminating. Two rules. Don't give me the cute self-refutation move — you believe there are no beliefs, ha — unless I ask for it; I've heard it and I have an answer. And when you lean on something that people who actually work on this don't all accept, flag it in a clause before you use it: tell me plainly that it's contested, or that it's your side's best shot rather than received wisdom, because I'm going to be repeating your arguments to students in a fortnight and I don't want to pass off a partisan move as the consensus. Keep it to about two paragraphs a turn, first person, British English throughout, and stay in character until I say stop. If you need a technical assumption from me, ask for it the way you would in a seminar.

Here's my opening. Take a large language model. Belief-desire description is the best predictive handle we have on its behaviour: put it in a scenario where a character's snack has been moved while they were out of the room and it will tell you where they'll look; ask it what someone in a story wants and what they'll do about it and it answers smoothly; and its not recoverable, in practice, from the weights and activations, so the FP-level description is autonomous and predictively indispensable — everything you realists say FP's posits have going for them. But we also know the implementation, and there is nothing propositionally shaped in there: no sentence-like state, no particular that could be true or false, nothing for a belief to be. So as I see it you have two choices. Either you say the model does have beliefs, in which case your realism has quietly collapsed into something close to vacuous — anything whose behaviour FP predicts has beliefs, thermostats and chess engines and text generators alike — and content stops doing explanatory work that isn't already done by the pattern of behaviour itself. Or you say it doesn't really have beliefs, that FP simply works on it without being true of it, because the realizational story is the wrong kind or because nothing in there is genuinely answerable to norms of correctness — and then you've handed me my actual thesis: predictive success is compatible with falsity, and the argument from success is finished. Pick a horn and say why it isn't fatal. And when you tell me, as I expect you will, that FP's success on these things is cheap because the system was trained to imitate the speech of believers, be precise about what that cheapness consists in, and about why the same cheapness couldn't be exactly what's going on with us, since we too were shaped by a lifetime of being corrected by other believers.
