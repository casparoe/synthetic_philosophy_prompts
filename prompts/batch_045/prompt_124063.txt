I'm a junior in an undergraduate political philosophy course on democracy and its critics, and I've been asked to lead one 60-minute discussion section for my class of 20 on direct democracy and referendums. I'd rather they collide with the problem than hear me announce it, so please design a simulation I can actually run with index cards and a projector and no real money: the class votes on a package of about five related spending measures for a fictional town, with a fixed budget and private preference cards that register how strongly each voter cares, not just yes or no.

Two design constraints matter to me. First, the bad outcome has to come out of sincere, well-informed, non-strategic voting — no villains, no hidden agenda, nobody ignorant or confused — so that students can each see that they voted sensibly and the group still produced something nobody wanted: ideally a package that is both over budget and one a clear majority would have rejected in favor of a feasible alternative. Second, I want a second round in which the same 20 voters, with the same preferences and the same measures, vote sequentially against a running budget, in an order set by one randomly chosen student playing town clerk, so the outcome visibly swings on that order alone.

Please give me: the measures, costs, budget and preference cards, built so that both pathologies actually fire at 20 voters (plus how to adjust if two or three students are out sick); the voting rules; a minute-by-minute timeline; whether to allow five minutes of open deliberation before round one and what that will do to the result; a small but real stake — I was thinking of letting the winning package decide something about our own final review session; and a debrief that maps each round onto a different claim about majority rule, without naming any paradox or theorem until after students have said what they noticed. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time (I expect one student to try to logroll and another to accuse me of stacking the cards), how to handle it, and which part of the case against multi-issue referendums the exercise cannot show and I will just have to state myself. Please answer in American English.
