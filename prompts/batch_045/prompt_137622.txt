I work in a small policy unit that advises ministers on what get called, rather vaguely, historical justice measures, and I keep coming back to a practice I cannot get a clean view of: the official state apology. You know the shape of it. A head of government stands up in the legislature and, speaking in the first person plural on behalf of the state, apologises for something done by a predecessor government or an earlier regime, often generations earlier — a massacre, a systematic removal of children from their families, a dispossession, an internment, a refusal to admit refugees. These apologies are almost always accompanied by a compensation scheme or a memorial fund, but the striking thing is that the money is not treated as a substitute for the words. The words are demanded as a separate good; affected groups negotiate over their exact wording; an apology delivered by the wrong official, or in the wrong forum, or without the relevant people present, is treated as no apology at all; and an apology that hedges — that expresses regret rather than apologising, or that adds a reminder that the acts were lawful at the time, or that balances the wrong against some later benefit — is frequently rejected as worse than silence.

What puzzles my colleagues and me is how selective the whole practice is, and we cannot tell whether the selectiveness is principled. The same state that will apologise for the acts of officials who have been dead for a century will insist, about its own current policies, that apologising is simply not something a government does; and the same state that disclaims inherited guilt, on the grounds that no one now alive was involved, happily relies on inheritance elsewhere — treaties ratified and debts contracted by long-vanished governments remain ours, and the claim to the territory itself depends on the state's continuity through exactly the period whose guilt is disclaimed. We have been sketching two internal tests in the hope of making this more disciplined, and I would like to know whether they are deep or arbitrary. The first is what we are calling the successor's proviso: an apology counts as an apology only if the government issuing it has itself, within its own term of office, undone some continuing effect of the wrong; absent that, we would label the statement an acknowledgement of history and not an apology. The second is a standing rule: no government should apologise for a historic wrong unless some living person or continuing institution can be named who was wronged and who is willing to accept the apology on their behalf.

So what I would like is a reconstruction of the implicit theory the existing practice actually runs on — about collective responsibility, about what sort of thing a state is and how it persists through changes of government and constitution, about what an apology is as an act and what it needs in order to succeed rather than merely to be uttered, and about whose standing is required to give or to receive one. Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and say where it is genuinely underdetermined, so that two quite different theories would both license it; tell me whether our two proposed tests cohere with that implicit theory or quietly subvert it; and say what would have to change in the practice if a state abandoned the idea that it can bear anything like guilt at all and treated apologies purely as forward-looking instruments. I would rather you reasoned from the structure of the practice than from the details of particular well-known cases, since I have no way of checking references from here. And please be explicit about how confident you are in the reconstruction you give, and single out the one claim in it you are least sure of — especially anything that rests on a contested reading of what these ceremonies are really doing.
