I run a small sleepaway camp, two-week sessions, about 120 kids between 11 and 14, and something happened this winter that I now have to stand up and explain at the closing campfire. For as long as anyone can remember, families paying full tuition have had $25 of canteen credit rolled into their fees, and the kids on camperships — about a third of the camp — get nothing. So every afternoon at the canteen window there's this extremely visible thing: some kids buy a slushie and some kids stand there and watch. The board voted in February to drop the credit for everybody. They priced extending it to the campership kids instead and it came out to something like $18k a summer, which the endowment can't absorb, so the option on the table was really only the free one. They also cut the opening-day bit where the campership names get read out loud while everyone claps. Which means: nobody at this camp is getting anything they didn't have last summer. Roughly eighty kids are getting twenty-five dollars less of candy and soda. I have to give a talk about that.

I've been going back and forth about it with my friend Nate, who teaches philosophy at a community college and whose daughter is a camper, so he is going to be sitting right there and I can't get away with anything sloppy. He calls himself a prioritarian, which as far as I can tell means he doesn't care about the size of the gap, he cares about theFloor — if you want to help the kids at the bottom, help them, don't burn money making the kids at the top worse for no one's benefit. He says there's a name for his complaint about what we did, something like the levelling-down objection, and the version I can't shake is: if the only actual change is that the kids who had more now have less and nobody has more, you don't get to say you did it for the sake of the kids at the bottom. Then a seventh grader named Devin said the same thing in four words at a cabin meeting: you're taking it from us and giving it to nobody. And he's right, and I'd rather the speech say that out loud than dress it up.

What I want is the closing campfire talk, six or seven minutes, to a mixed room: 120 kids who will mostly be thinking about their last night, maybe fifty parents (including a bunch who paid for that credit and are not thrilled), the counselors, and the board members who voted for it. I do think there's an answer to Nate and Devin and it is not "equality is good in itself" — it's that the credit was never really $25, it was a thing you could see every single afternoon, a line at the window between kids who belong here and kids who are guests here, and quietly dropping the name-reading wouldn't have touched that at all; the daily thing was the marker, not the ceremony. But I have no idea how to say that to twelve-year-olds without it becoming a lecture, and it has to not slide into telling the campership kids they should be grateful, or telling the tuition kids that their slushie didn't matter (it mattered, and their parents paid for it, and Devin is going to be listening for exactly that). There should be one honest sentence somewhere admitting that doing the free version of this doesn't get us off the hook for actually spending money on the campership kids next year, without turning the whole evening into a guilt trip. I need a joke in the first minute or I lose them entirely, and I need it to end somewhere a thirteen-year-old might actually remember in September. No dead philosophers' names, no "we're all in this together," and please nothing that implies anyone in that room is better off this summer than they were last summer — because they definitly aren't.
