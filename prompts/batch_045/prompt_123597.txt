I work on the policy and alignment side of a general-purpose assistant, and one phrase keeps doing a lot of unexamined work in our design documents: the deployment should be like a liberal state — neutral among competing conceptions of the good life, enforcing only the thin rules everyone can reasonably accept, and otherwise leaving people free to pursue their own ends. In practice we have built it as a layered thing: a neutral base layer that is helpful, honest, and non-judgmental, plus swappable value configurations that a user or a downstream deployer can select, more or less the way you would pick a locale or install a plugin. Neutrality is being used to license a specific conclusion: that we can avoid answering whose values the system serves by pushing that question downstream to whoever consumes it.

I would like an audit of the analogy before we build more on it. Please give me the correspondence explicitly — a table is fine: who are the citizens, what is the territory, what plays the role of the constitution, of legislation, of the courts, of the police, of voting, of emigration. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and separate the breaks that matter for the conclusion we are using it for from the ones that are merely decorative.

Three breaks I suspect, though I would like you to find others and to say which ones are load bearing:

1. Our citizens are the people typing, but the outputs land on people who never opted in and have no appeal — the subject of a background summary, the colleague named in a performance complaint, the relative affected by advice. A state at least has a bounded demos and an account of who is subject to its laws; we have an unbounded set of affected parties, no procedure for them, and a boundary that shifts with every prompt.

2. The neutral layer is neither neutral nor self-authoring. It is written by a small policy team and a few dozen contractors, and its defaults — what counts as contested rather than settled, what counts as harm, which tone reads as neutral — are themselves substantive value choices. The analogy looks like it smuggles in a demos and a constitutional moment that we do not have.

3. There may be no neutral output to fall back on, so the state's central move may have no analogue. Asked to help decide whether to move a parent into care, or whether to report a colleague's error, the system cannot simply forbear: refusing, hedging, and laying out both sides are each a stance, and a state's forbearance from coercion was supposed to be silence of a kind we cannot produce.

Does the conclusion we drew survive once the disanalogies are in, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? And if it does not survive, is there a better comparison for what we are actually building — a profession with role-differentiated duties, a public library or broadcaster, a club you can join and leave, a market of communities — and what would that commit us to that neutrality was letting us avoid?
