I got into an argument with a friend from a reading group last week about Malthus. Her line was that Malthus was "right but early": population tends to outrun food production (the geometric-vs-arithmetic thing), the positive check is famine/disease/war, the preventive check is delayed marriage and restraint, and the reason 1798–1900 or 1800–2000 didn't look like his forecast is that the horizon was longer than anyone's sample — plus, on her view, the checks didn't disappear, they changed shape: fertility fell instead of mortality rising, which she calls the same mechanism wearing different clothes. The other people in the group pushed back with the Boserup-style reversal (population pressure itself inducing agricultural innovation, so the arrow runs the other way), the demographic-transition story, and the observation that "subsistence" keeps getting redefined upward as people get richer, so the target variable isn't even the same series across periods. I've been trying to sort the defensive moves into three buckets — horizon-stretching (the sample just wasn't long enough), target-redefinition (the thing being predicted was re-specified mid-stream), and mechanism-relabeling (the output changed, the mechanism didn't) — and I can't tell whether that taxonomy is doing real work or just letting the Malthusian always win.
