I'm a junior at a large public university, and I'm the undergraduate member of the University Honors Council. This term I'm also taking an upper-level seminar in normative ethics, where we've been working through the debate about moral sainthood: whether a life wholly organized around the demands of morality is even a good life to want for oneself, let alone an ideal to hold up to others. The Council is now, unexpectedly, in exactly that territory, and I've been asked to circulate a draft decision memo before our meeting next Thursday. I'd be grateful for your help writing it.

The award is the Thorne Fellowship, the university's highest graduating-year honor. Its official criteria are "distinguished character and service to others," and the founding letter from the donor family adds that the recipient should exemplify "the examined life." This year we have two finalists, and the Council is split roughly into thirds.

Candidate A is close to what the literature calls a moral saint. She co-founded and now runs a free evening clinic in the city, donated a kidney as a non-directed living donor during her sophomore year, lives on roughly $18,000 a year and gives away the rest of her income, and — by her own account in the interview — sleeps about five hours a night, has no romantic relationships, no hobbies, and no plans to pursue the violin, which she played at a conservatory level before she quit in her first year because she judged it a poor use of her time.

Candidate B does serious but bounded work: fifteen hours a week at a legal-aid clinic, a senior thesis on bail reform that her department calls outstanding, ten percent of her income donated, a relationship she describes as central to her life, and a band she plays in on weekends. She plans to become a public defender.

The three camps on the Council: some hold that A is obviously the recipient and that anything else is a failure of nerve; some hold that honoring A would be honoring a life the university should not recommend, because the moral goods have crowded out friendship, cultivation, play, and reflection; and some hold that the question isn't which life is better but what the university does by honoring one of them.

Four complications have come up in our discussion, and the memo needs to deal with at least the ones that matter:

First, the exemplar effect. Honoring someone is a way of nominating a model, and models are copied badly. Students can't reproduce A's actual life; they'll reproduce a simplified public image of it — logged service hours, a pledge, a renunciation that looks impressive on a transcript — and the result may be credentialized altruism, burnout, or quiet resentment. A defender of A replies that this is a reason to explain the award better, not to give it to someone else. I don't know how to weigh that.

Second, contribution versus credit. A's kidney donation is the most visible item in her file, but she was one of a number of compatible non-directed donors in the pool that cycle, and it's not obvious the transplant wouldn't have happened without her. More generally, a lot of what makes A's record extraordinary depends on circumstances she didn't choose — her health, her family's financial safety net, the fact that she had a valuable skill to begin with. If some of this is luck, honoring it may honor luck rather than character, and the memo should say what the Council should do about that in a case where it can only see outputs and not the inner life: a sacrificial act done gladly and one done grimly are indistinguishable on paper.

Third, the examined-life clause. There's an argument that a total moral absorption is itself a form of unexamined life, because the person so organized has no reflective distance left from which to ask whether the pursuit is worth it — she has answered the question once and now just runs. I find that argument attractive and I'm not sure it's fair.

Fourth, a constraint I have to respect: the donor family has said informally that they read the founding letter as directing the fellowship to "the graduate who has done the most good," and they sit on the Council's advisory board. Any recommendation that withholds the award from A has to say why that reading doesn't settle it, or has to be framed so the criteria themselves are revised openly rather than quietly reinterpreted.

What I need is a memo the Council could actually act on, in American English, about 1,000 to 1,200 words, in memo form — header, recommendation up front, then reasoning. Specifically: a clear bottom line on this year's fellowship; a proposed standing interpretation of "character and service" that future cycles can apply, which I suspect should take the form of a demanding threshold plus some explicit room for self-directed projects rather than a maximizing rule; the strongest honest argument for the option you're rejecting, stated well enough that the losing side would recognize itself in it; and a short implementation section — what the Council communicates to each candidate and when, what goes in the minutes, and whether the criteria question should be put to the full Council or only to the advisory board. Please don't include citations or footnotes; I need argument and a decision, not sources.
