I have no academic background in philosophy — or, for that matter, in statistics. I taught myself probability because I needed it. For the past few years I have written and maintained the sensor-fusion code for a small autonomous-boat project: several cheap, badly calibrated sensors, a state estimate updated on every tick, particle filters, and a great deal of hand-tuned likelihood code. The vocabulary I actually think in is priors and posteriors, likelihoods, log-odds, observation noise, hard versus soft updates, and the occasional Bayesian network. Anything put in those terms I will follow; anything put in terms of propositions, possible worlds, or credences I will follow only with effort.

I recently went looking for something unrelated and came across the topic of Jeffrey conditioning and the problem of uncertain evidence. My current understanding, such as it is: the ordinary Bayesian rule tells you what to do when you learn something outright — you condition on it. But most things that happen to a person are not like that. You glance at a dim light in bad conditions and come away, say, 0.7 confident that it was red and 0.3 that it was green, and there is no hard fact you have learned that you could condition on. Jeffrey's rule is meant to cover this: you set your new probabilities over the partition directly and then adjust everything by holding fixed the ratios of probabilities within each cell. Around this there is apparently an argument about whether the rule is really a generalization of conditioning or a different kind of thing altogether, about a condition called rigidity, about whether you can be Dutch-booked or led into order-dependent answers, and about whether any of this bears on the older idea that what you are really certain of is your own experience.

What I would like is a translation, and then a report on what the translation lost.

First, restate the rule and the surrounding debate as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary — priors and likelihoods, soft evidence, virtual evidence, noisy observations, posterior over a partition — and say where each piece lands.

Second, settle a disagreement I have been having with a blog post. It claimed that what I already do with a noisy sensor, multiplying in a likelihood for the reading given the state, is Jeffrey conditioning under another name. I doubt this: in my code the reading is certain and the world is uncertain, whereas in these examples it seems to be the reading itself that is uncertain. But tell me whether I am wrong, and whether my framework is doing something that quietly presupposes the very condition the philosophers argue about.

Third, a rule I invented. When the light looks reddish, instead of assigning 0.7 and 0.3 to red and green and holding everything else in place, I move probability toward red and also, more weakly, toward neighbouring shades — orange, pink — according to a kernel over wavelength, with no clean partition anywhere in the picture. Is that a form of Jeffrey conditioning at all? Does it violate rigidity? Does it forfeit whatever makes Jeffrey's rule defensible — I have seen it described both as the update that moves your probabilities least and as the rule that behaves well under repetition, and I do not know whether either description is accurate?

Then the residue report: what mapped cleanly, what you had to bend to fit, and what has no expression at all in my vocabulary — with your view on whether that residue points to something my framework cannot represent or to something less substantial in the philosophical problem than it first appeared.

Please mark as you go which of your translation choices are the standard way of putting it and which are your own, and where philosophers genuinely disagree as opposed to where they mostly agree and only popular writing makes it sound contested. And if the request itself rests on a confusion — if the problem of uncertain evidence is not the same shape of problem as a noisy sensor, or if my made-up rule is not a variant of anything, or if translating it into my terms quietly assumes the answer — say so plainly rather than producing a translation that politely goes along with my framing.
