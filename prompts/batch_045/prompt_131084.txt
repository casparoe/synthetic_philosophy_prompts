I'm in a seminar on moral change right now (we did Appiah's book on honor codes and then the Bernard Williams argument about slavery — the bit where he says the ancients weren't missing any fact that we have, reflection just wrecked their conceptual scheme, and there's no guarantee that whatever replaces it is an improvement) and I also work about fifteen hours a week as a student campaigner for a small NGO that runs behaviour-change campaigns. We greenlight maybe four campaigns a year, each one costs a couple hundred thousand dollars, and our actual method so far is basically: decide the practice is bad, say on a poster that it's bad, measure nothing. I have to run a workshop for our country director and two community advisory board members next month on how we pick and design these things, and I'd rather bring them something usable than a summary of the readings.

So what I want is a procedure, not an essay. Take the distinction between norms held in place by argument, by interest, and by honour, and turn it into a decision procedure our team could actually run on a candidate practice in an afternoon workshop — the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops with a hard no, and where it has to hand the decision back to a human being because it's really a question about values. The people running it will be comms staff and one field officer, so nothing that needs philosophy vocabulary to apply. Things I think it has to handle, and I'm not sure how: how to work out who the relevant honour audience actually is (whose respect does the person doing the practice need, and can we reach them); whether the practice can even be made visible to that audience, since I suspect a lot of our campaigns target things nobody will admit to anyway; whether there's a face-saving exit ramp so people can stop without conceding they were wrong before — my guess is that's why the anti-dueling stuff worked and our stuff doesn't; and how to tell a case where people already privately agree with us but can't stop because of coordination or money, which I think no amount of honour or argument will fix. I also want it to be honest about the Williams worry my tutor keeps raising, which as I understand it is that a procedure like this can tell you whether you'd win but not whether you're right — if that's a real limit, mark exactly where it bites in the procedure.

Then run it on three cases. First: the initiation week for the sports clubs at my own university, which involves a lot of drinking and some genuinely nasty hazing that everyone knows about and nobody reports. Second: informal cash gifts that families give to nurses at a district hospital where our partner works — everyone knows it's against the rules, the nurses' salaries are months in arrears, and families who don't pay say they get ignored; I can't tell if this is an interests case, an honour case, or neither. Third, and this is the one where I expect an answer I won't like: our own fundraising imagery, i.e. photos of identifiable children in pretty dire situations that we send to donors because they outperform everything else we've tried. Please don't tell me to go read more — I need something I can put on one page and defend in the room.
