I've been chewing on the unilateralist's curse setup — n people, each of whom can unilaterally pull a lever that affects everyone, each with their own noisy estimate of whether pulling is net positive, and the lever gets pulled if anyone's estimate says go. So the group effectively acts on the max of a bunch of noisy estimates rather than the mean, it over-triggers systematically, and it gets worse the more conscientious members you add. I think it generalises much further than the usual framing suggests, and I can't tell whether that's a real result or whether I've broken something. Argument:

(1) Nothing in the maths requires the evaluators to be different people. What the curse needs is (a) a disjunctive trigger — any one evaluator can bring the action about — (b) errors that are roughly independent across evaluators, and (c) a default that only positive estimates move you away from.

(2) My own decisions come out of a disjunction of noisy sub-personal evaluators: the bit that runs the cost-effectiveness numbers, the bit that wants status, the bit that gets a gut sense that this is important, the bit that reads one more forum post and gets convinced. Each is a rough, partly independent estimate of the same quantity, and any one of them can get me to act.

(3) Same thing over time. I revisit a decision; each revisit is another draw; I act as soon as a draw says go. So the more conscientious I am — the more I re-examine — the likelier I am to end up doing something that is net negative in expectation.

(4) The effect is largest exactly for the decisions I care most about: fat right tail, nasty downside. Ambitious career moves, having kids, giving away a big chunk of income, moving country.

(5) So 'reflect carefully, then do what looks best' has negative expected value across a wide class of actions, and the curse isn't a point about institutional design at all — it's a point against individual judgement, including the judgement to act on the kind of estimates people on these blogs act on.

(6) Which is mad: taken seriously it says I should think about my decisions less, because thinking adds draws.

I'd much rather keep (1) than give it up — the formal version looks solid to me — but (2) is doing an awful lot of work and I genuinely can't tell if it's a substantive psychological claim or an equivocation about what 'aggregation' even means inside one head. When I put this to a mate he said fine, just precommit; but deciding to precommit is itself a decision of the same kind, so I dont see how that gets me out.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you? If your answer is that my sub-personal evaluators aren't independent enough, say how independent they'd have to be for the curse to bite and whether there's a way to tell. And if you reject (2), explain what the honest scope of the curse is — is it only ever a claim about how to design groups, or does some of it leak into being a solo agent?
