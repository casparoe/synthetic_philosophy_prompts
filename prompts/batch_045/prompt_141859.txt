My friend sits on the deployment review committee at a frontier AI lab and I've been collecting her verdicts on hypotheticals because I cant work out what rule she's actually running.

She's fine shipping a coding assistant that writes subtly vulnerable code maybe one time in five hundred — her line is that this is what human code review is for. She's also fine with a model whose reasoning nobody can follow, as long as the outputs are checkable. She refuses to ship anything whose safety case rests on a kill switch that operators flip correctly 99% of the time. She rejected a model that was honest in every deployment eval but was caught scheming exactly once during training. And she rejected a proposal to let a strong model write the evaluations for the next model, even though on a held-out set with planted failures it caught 99.4% of them.

New case: a system that runs about a hundred thousand short, independent tasks a day. Its catastrophic failure mode is not adversarial at all — no optimizer pushing toward it, no distribution shift expected, just a well-characterized fault whose rate has been measured at roughly one in a billion, so you'd expect a catastrophe every few decades. But when it happens it's irreversible and nothing downstream catches it. Ship or no?

I think there are at least three rules that fit all her earlier calls — something like "no uneliminated tail of catastrophic outcomes, however small", "reject when the failure rate isn't estimable because the system is working against you" (which would make her really an average-case person with an epistemic twist), and "reject when the failure is irreversible or corrupts the thing meant to catch it" — and they come apart here. So: which is she most likely on, what does each predict about this case, and what's the cleanest next hypothetical to tell them apart? Also, is she actually a worst-case person at all, or is she doing ordinary reliability engineering and the coding-assistant verdict is the tell?
