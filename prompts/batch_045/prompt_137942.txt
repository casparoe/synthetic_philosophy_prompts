I am writing up my position on forecasting the survival of large polities, and before I circulate it I would like an audit of the package as a whole rather than a verdict on any single claim. Here is where I have landed.

(a) Outside view first: any forecast of how long a great power will last must be anchored in the base rate for the widest reference class we can honestly construct, and the insider's story about why this case is special counts only as evidence against that anchor, never as a replacement for it.

(b) Regime-locking: the class must be restricted to polities in the same causal regime — comparable administrative technology, comparable communications speed, comparable fiscal extraction and military production — since pooling across regimes averages over different processes and produces a number that describes nothing.

(c) Endpoint skepticism: the fall of a polity is largely a convention imposed later by historians rather than a dated event, so recorded durations are substantially an artifact of where a narrative gets cut.

(d) Archival selection: the sample is filtered by who kept records and which records survived, which over-represents large, literate, administratively dense — and therefore more durable — polities.

(e) Radical path-dependence: historical outcomes are contingent and overdetermined by local detail, not the working out of any law.

(f) Anti-life-cycle: grand narratives of imperial rise and decline, told as though polities passed through stages of life, are pseudo-explanatory, and I want no part of them.

(g) Reflexivity: base-rate forecasts about a still-existing polity feed back into that polity's behaviour, which is part of why I think such forecasts should be handled carefully in public.

(h) The case I actually care about: I want a useable number for how long the present international order — the states system as currently organised, including the long avoidance of great-power war — will hold, and I want it derived by these methods rather than from narrative.

Which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which merely look as though they do? Give me the tensions ranked by how much damage they do to the package rather than as a list of equal complaints, and for each say what would have to give and what that concession would cost me elsewhere. Tell me in particular whether the combination of (b), (c) and (g) leaves (a) with anything to anchor on, and whether the package can deliver (h) at all or only explains why it cannot be delivered. Please answer in continuous prose, around seven hundred words, with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables — the way one would write an email to a colleague.
