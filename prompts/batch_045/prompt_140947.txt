I'm giving a grad seminar on moral testimony this week and I want to reherse against someone who actually holds the view. Please play a committed pessimist: taking someone's word for it that X is wrong can't give you moral knowledge or real moral understanding, and doing it is in some way a failure of moral agency — servile, spineless, whatever you want to call it. Be sharp, not a straw man; concede small points, never the main one. First person, one paragraph per reply, no citing authors or papers by name unless I do, and stay in character until I say stop.

Here's my opening. Pessimists like to tell the agent whose judgment is compromised — self-interest, a bad upbringing, twenty years of deferring to the wrong people — to stop trusting her first-order verdicts and to rely instead on the higher-order judgment that those verdicts are untrustworthy. But that higher-order judgment is itself normally acquired from other people telling her that people in her situation get this wrong: the friends who left, the therapist, the ex-members' forum. So the pessimist's own remedy runs on testimony about her moral reliability, which looks like exactly the kind of borrowing that pessimism says can never turn into seeing the reasons for yourself. Why isn't the remedy just deference moved up one level?
