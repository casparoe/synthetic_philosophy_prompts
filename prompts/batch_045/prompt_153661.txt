i'm TAing a final-year philosophy of science seminar, 14 students, one 80-minute slot, and I've got the session on scientific realism and theory change. I want them to feel the pull of structural realism before I name it — the thought that when a theory gets overthrown we keep the equations and lose the furniture: the ethers and the calorics go, but the pattern of relations they were standing in survives into the successor theory, so maybe that pattern was all we ever should have believed in. But I also want them to feel the thing that makes me suspicious of it. If all a theory ultimately says is "there are some things, call them #1, #2, #3, standing in such-and-such relations, and everything we observe follows from that" — the theoretical names crossed out and replaced with blanks — then it looks like you can make it true with basically any old junk, as long as there's enough of it and you can arrange it to match the pattern: the pens in the room, four people's left shoes, some prime numbers. Last year I tried a version of this with a toy theory about three invisible magnets and it fell flat, mostly because I explained the point first and then asked them to do the exercise, which killed it.

So: can you design the whole session for me — groups, materials (index cards and random junk, nothing that needs a projector), a timeline for the 80 minutes, and what exactly the facilitator says and doesn't say? My rough idea is that each group gets the "crossed-out" theory plus a pile of objects and has to build models that make it true, and then something turns on their noticing that they can do it again and again with completely different stuff, and that this is either the whole point or a disaster, without me having told them which. Two things I care about. First, the epistemological and the ontic versions shouldn't collapse into each other: "structure is all we can know" and "structure is all there is" have to come apart in the activity, and by the end students should be able to say which one the junk-model problem actually bites and which one it sort of doesn't. Second, I want a debrief that maps specific moments in the room onto specific claims, tells me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm guessing someone builds a model with the wrong number of objects, and someone else says "well obviously the theory also says the things are physical / causal / in spacetime" and short-circuits the whole thing before anyone feels it), and is honest about what the game can't deliver — I suspect it can't show the continuity-across-theory-change argument that made structural realism attractive in the first place, since my little toy theory has no history, and it probably can't reproduce whatever makes the triviality worry bite for infinite domains rather than for four pens. Oh, and please don't put the words "structural realism" or "Ramsey" or "Newman" on any handout that goes out before the debrief, and no looking things up — just build it from what the setup already gives you.
