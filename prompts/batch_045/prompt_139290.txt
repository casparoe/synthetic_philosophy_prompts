I'm writing a piece on the replication crisis, and I've been leaning on an analogy several statistician sources offered me: a null-hypothesis significance test is a diagnostic screening test. Power plays the role of sensitivity, α the false-positive rate, the prior probability that a hypothesis is true plays the role of disease prevalence, and the positive predictive value — the share of "significant" findings that are real — is what a reader of a paper actually cares about. Used this way it is meant to license two claims I would like to print: that the folk reading of p < 0.05 as "only a one-in-twenty chance this is noise" is a base-rate fallacy, and that in fields where most tested hypotheses are false, most published positives will be false however carefully each individual test is run.

One source says the analogy is worse than useless, and I want to know whether she is right. A screening test is applied repeatedly to a population with a stable, measurable prevalence and its error rates are calibrated against a gold-standard follow-up; research hypotheses are not sampled from any population, "true effect" is a matter of degree rather than a condition you either have or don't, and α is a long-run rate over a procedure that includes the analyst's unrecorded choices about which comparisons to make — none of which has a counterpart in the clinic.

Audit the analogy for me. Give the correspondence table explicitly, say what the screening frame quietly imports that the target lacks (it seems to smuggle a prior probability of truth into a machinery built to avoid one), and separate the disanalogies that matter for those two conclusions from the ones that don't. In particular: suppose a funder required pre-registration of every hypothesis tested, publication of every result, and a replication as the gold-standard follow-up. Would the analogy then become exact, or is there a mismatch left over? And does it point at the right remedy — it recommends raising positive predictive value by improving the prior or the specificity — compared with the proposals to lower the significance threshold or to increase power? If the two conclusions survive, tell me on what; if they don't, tell me what the analogy was carrying. If there is a better analogy for where the unreliability actually comes from, I'd like that too.
