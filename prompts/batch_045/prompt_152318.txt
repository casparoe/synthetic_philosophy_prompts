I am writing a short critical piece on Ursula K. Le Guin's 1973 story The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas for a reading group on the ethics of abolishing suffering, and I want to use the story as a test of a view rather than as a decorative illustration of it. So that the piece does not depend on the reader already knowing the view, I want to state it first, in my own words.

The abolitionist program about suffering — the one associated with David Pearce's The Hedonistic Imperative — seems to me to rest on three claims, and these are the ones I want held fixed:

(1) Suffering is not a necessity of nature and not the price of any greater good. It is a design feature of evolved nervous systems, retained because aversive states steered behaviour, and it is therefore in principle editable out by genetic engineering, pharmacology and neural redesign.

(2) What has to be preserved is not the horrible phenomenology but the job that phenomenology was doing: marking damage, motivating avoidance and repair, making outcomes matter to the agent. The abolitionist's own proposal, as I understand it, is that some other signal could carry that function without anyone undergoing anything unpleasant. Call that the signal.

(3) If the signal can be kept and the suffering removed, then every remaining instance of suffering is gratuitous in a strong sense: not merely outweighed or unjustified on balance, but unnecessary — something a technology could have prevented — and that is the morally salient thing about it.

One further thing I am unsure about, and I would like your reading to say how much turns on it: whether the program is best read as a negative one, on which the target is the elimination of suffering and nothing else has independent weight, or as a maximizing one, on which the aim is to replace the pain–pleasure axis with gradients of well-being and the total amount of well-being matters. My suspicion is that the story drives a wedge between these two readings and that most talk of the abolitionist project slides between them.

The story, as I have it: Omelas is a city of apparently unqualified happiness; its happiness is strictly conditional on the perpetual misery of one child kept in filth and darkness; citizens are brought to see the child once they are old enough to understand; the narrator is emphatic that the bargain cannot be renegotiated and that the child cannot be helped without everything else being destroyed. Most stay. Some walk away, alone, into a place the narrator cannot describe and says may not exist.

I have seen the story taught as a blunt anti-utilitarian parable — no aggregate of happiness justifies this — but that reading strikes me as both too easy and maybe not even available. Le Guin rigs the terms so that no trade-off is ever offered to anyone: nobody is asked to weigh the child against the city, everyone is asked to accept a world they did not choose and cannot alter. So the interesting question is not whether the exchange is acceptable, since no one can make the exchange.

Here, then, is the variant I want your reading to work on. Imagine a technologically advanced Omelas, with the abolitionist toolkit available to its citizens. Two options:

(i) Decouple. Leave the causal dependency intact, but let it run on the child's distress signal with nobody undergoing the distress: the full informational profile of agony — damage marked, avoidance primed, the city's prosperity keyed to it — and no subject for whom it is like anything. I will call that state an orphaned valence. Nothing is suffered anywhere, and everything else about Omelas is preserved.

(ii) Recalibrate wholesale. Abolish the child's suffering and the hidden dependency with it, and accept whatever this costs the happiness of the city.

My questions, in order of how much they matter to me:

Whether (i) is the completion of the abolitionist project or its betrayal. On claims (1) to (3) it looks like the cleanest possible victory: suffering is zero and nothing of value is lost. Yet the story's presentation of the child is insistently first-personal — the child is a someone, not a reading on an instrument — and I suspect Le Guin is staking out a kind of moral remainder that abolitionism has no vocabulary for. Say whether that suspicion is fair to both sides, and whether the suspicion is even coherent if the child, under (i), is not miserable.

What is wrong with Omelas after (i)? If the answer is nothing, then the horror the story invites seems to have been located in the wrong place all along, and I want to know whether that is a reductio of the abolitionist criterion or a consequence the abolitionist should simply accept. Relatedly: under (ii), the negative reading of the program seems to require destroying a great deal of happiness to remove one instance of suffering, while the maximizing reading may not — so does the story show that the two readings are not the same project after all?

What to make of walking away. It does not help the child and, as far as the story tells us, does not reduce anyone's suffering; on the abolitionist ledger it looks like pure loss, removing a contented person from a contented city and leaving the causal chain untouched. Le Guin nonetheless presents it as the only response with any dignity in it. Is the story thereby committed to something abolitionism denies — a value in refusing complicity that is not commensurable with suffering-minimization — or can the abolitionist honour the refusal without cost, and if so as what kind of act?

A few constraints. I do not want a survey of what critics have said about the story, and I do not need any part of it quoted back to me; I have the text. I want a reading that is faithful in both directions: it should not flatten the story into an example, and it should not bend the three claims to make the fit look tidier than it is. Where the lens illuminates a choice or a scene, say so; where the story resists the lens, say that plainly too; and end, if you can, by saying what the story contributes to this debate that the arguments by themselves do not — or tell me if it contributes nothing.

On the epistemic side: mark where you are confident and where you are extrapolating, and distinguish carefully between what follows from (1) to (3) as I have stated them and what depends on a contested version of abolitionism I may have mis-stated. And if my framing is confused, please say so directly rather than working politely around it — in particular if orphaned valence is incoherent as I have described it, if option (i) is not really available given how the story specifies the dependency, if my stipulation that the dependency tracks a state rather than the felt quality of that state quietly assumes what is at issue, or if the wholeExercise of reading Le Guin through this program is distorting one or the other. I would rather be told the piece cannot be written than be handed a version that works.
