i sit on our city's franchise commission — we decide who counts as a voter for municipal elections, and the charter lets us write our own rules, which already feels circular. Our track record:

- green card holders, 10+ years here, pay city taxes: yes
- commuters who work here and pay the city earnings tax but sleep in the suburbs: no
- absentee owners of apartment buildings here, living abroad: no
- people with no fixed address who sleep in city shelters: yes
- out-of-state students in dorms here nine months a year: yes, if they register here
- people held in the city jail, pre-trial or serving under a year: yes; a year or more: no
- farmworkers on five-month visas in employer housing: no
- 16 year olds: no

New case, hearing is next week: Riverside, an unincorporated pocket of about 1100 people that the city completely surrounds. The county sheriff polices them, but they buy city water, their kids attend city schools under a contract, and roughly 300 of them work here and pay the earnings tax. The november ballot has both an annexation measure that would make them part of the city and the mayoral race. They've petitioned to vote.

What should we do if we want to be consistent with ourselves — full ballot, annexation measure only, or nothing? Which of our precedents is actually the closest analog, and which rule are we implicitly running that fits all eight old verdicts: something like all-affected-interests, or being subject to the city's coercion, or being a stakeholder whose life is mainly lived here? Are there two rules that fit the old verdicts equally well but come apart on Riverside? Note that the "they pay the earnings tax" argument seems to be closed off by how we treated the commuters, unless there's a real difference there I'm missing. If we let them vote only on the annexation, are we committed to the demos being different for every single decision, and is that ad hoc or a defensible position? And what case should we put on the docket next to tell the surviving rules apart?
