so I've been going back and forth with someone from my old LW meetup group — lets call her D — about what counts as a real pattern and when a stance earns its keep. She's a pretty standard mild realist: a pattern is real if there's some description more compact than the bit map, the physical-stance and the intentional-stance description of a chess engine can both be real at once, greedy reductionism is a sin, that sort of package. But her individual verdicts cluster in a way I can't reverse-engineer, and I've got a case I actually care about now.

Her track record, as best I can reconstruct (I'm paraphrasing, she'd definately quibble with half of these):

1. A 1000x1000 bitmap where "alternating columns" compresses it by ~99%: real pattern, obviously, next question.
2. The same grid with 3% of the pixels flipped, so "alternating, with errors" still beats the bit map but only by maybe 1.5x: real, "but I'd want to see the next sample before I bet on it."
3. Orion: real pattern, "one that stops at the edge of the dataset." She won't say there's a thing, Orion, and she thinks it earns you no predictions past the stars you already looked at.
4. Thermostat: the design-stance story (set to hold 20 C) is a real pattern. Ascribing it a belief about the room's temperature is not — "that metaphor stops paying rent the moment you ask it a question the set point can't answer."
5. A chess engine not wanting to lose its queen: real pattern, and the transistor story is also a real pattern, and no, you don't have to pick one. Robust under intervention — change the board and the ascription retunes.
6. A hand-scripted RPG NPC whose dialogue is a lookup table that reads like belief-desire reasoning: not a real intentional pattern. The script already gets you everything; the beliefs are just a worse encoding of the script.
7. A self-fulfilling chart level — everyone watches the level, so the level holds: real, but "real the way a traffic jam is real, not the way a planet is real." Would evaporate if everyone stopped looking.
8. Someone's claimed encoding of a famous novel in market noise — compressible given an elaborate prior, zero grip on new data: not real, overfitting with a story stapled to it.
9. A crowd's mood that partly constitutes itself, because people glance around to see what the mood is: real. She wasn't bothered that the individuals' states are themselves just patterns; she thinks the regress is harmless.
10. Separately, and she insists this is empirical rather than metaphysical: no current AI system has beliefs.

Here's the case I care about. Say we train a language model with an auxiliary objective I'll call intentional-stance legibility training: on top of the usual objective, penalize it to the extent that its behavior isn't well predicted by the best belief-desire ascription a reference predictor can come up with. Stipulate:

- On held-out tasks the intentional ascription beats both a physical-stance simulation and the design-stance story (trained to maximize human approval) at predicting what it does.
- It's intervention-robust: feed it evidence and it revises the way the belief-desire story says, including in cases where the designers can't derive the prediction from the training setup.
- Counterfactually: without the legibility objective, the intentional stance buys nothing over the design stance. The pattern is there because we trained for it.
- Nobody installed any belief-shaped data structures. No one wrote belief: the user is testing me into it. It's gradient descent all the way down.
- The reference predictor used in training was itself a language model doing the ascribing, so the pattern in this model is in some sense inherited from a pattern in another model.

So what does D say? Is there a real pattern here? Does she end up saying the thing has beliefs? I'm less interested in the verdict than in the rule: what's the principle she's running that generates 1 through 10, what other principles fit those ten verdicts just as well while coming apart on this case, and what does each of them predict here? Give me your best guess with the reasons for preferring it. Then tell me which of my stipulations is actually doing the work — which one, if I flipped it, would move her verdict, and which ones are just decoration — and what further case would separate the rules you named. My own guess is that the discriminating case is a model legibilized for some stance nobody antecedently had, a stance we just invented, but you tell me. And be honest if you think the disagreement goes verbal somewhere in the middle.
