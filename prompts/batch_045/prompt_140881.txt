What I'm after: a case in which the same ISOTYPE-style chart gets two incompatible readings from two viewers, in a way that isolates the step Neurath's team called transformation — the business of turning messy source material into a sketch for a supposedly self-explanatory chart, the job Marie Reidemeister mostly did.

Constraints on the scenario:

- Realistic, no science fiction. So: no aliens with different visual systems, no brain lesions, no agnosia, no invented pictograms. Set it in the actual world of the thing — the Vienna museum, or the later Isotype Institute, or a straight modern analogue like a municipal housing office, a hospital waiting room, a school. One paragraph, 150 words max, because it has to survive being a slide.
- Both viewers are fully fluent in the conventions. They know the repeated-same-size rule, they know the pictogram vocabulary, neither of them is misperceiving anything or guessing. The disagreement has to be about what the chart says about the world — overcrowding, wages, infection rates, whatever — not about whether the chart is well made or whether the underlying numbers are in dispute.
- The divergence has to fall out of the transformation step specifically: the selection, simplification and abstraction involved in getting from source data to picture. Not from an unfamiliar symbol, not from somebody breaking the count-instead-of-scale rule, not from colour or perspective conventions, not from a caption in a language one of them doesn't read. Every design decision in the chart should be one Neurath would actually have signed off on. If the case only works because the chart is botched, it's useless to me.
- And this is the direction I keep failing to hit: adding a verbal caption or label in the shared language of both viewers should not fix the disagreement — it should leave it exactly where it was, or make it worse. That's the wrong way round from what you'd expect, given that he always insisted pictures come accompanied by words and are at best a supplement to them. And it shouldn't be because the caption is in the wrong language or uses a technical term nobody knows.
