I have to submit a three-year research agenda for a small group: myself and two postdocs, all trained in moral philosophy and epistemology, with no budget for original fieldwork but with access to existing cross-cultural datasets and an informal collaboration with one lab that can run short online studies cheaply. The theme I have been handed is what cross-cultural variation in folk moral judgment implies about the objectivity of morality. I do not want a survey of the state of the art or a reading list. I want a defended prioritization of lines of work.

To make the exercise concrete, stipulate the following finding as where the evidence is most likely to land by the end of the period: variation between populations is large and systematic for considerations of loyalty, authority, and purity; moderate but genuine for harm-based prohibitions; and small for the bare phenomenon of taking anything at all to be morally required of one. Assume further that the variation is not an artifact of translation or of stimulus comprehension.

Given that, here are the lines I can see, named partly with distinctions I find useful. First, separating what I will call construal variance from content variance: whether a population differs in what its members take a survey item to be asking — a request for their community's norms, say, versus a question about what is really required — or differs in the moral considerations they apply once the question is fixed. Second, separating noise-sensitivity from ecology-sensitivity: whether judgments move with features of the situation that look morally irrelevant, such as framing or order of presentation, or with features of the social environment, such as the degree of market integration, which might themselves be the kind of thing that could matter to what is owed to whom. Third, the status of the convergence expectation itself: whether objectivity in ethics should have been expected to produce cross-populational agreement at all, or whether the right analogy is with perception under varying conditions, where disagreement is handled by a theory of which conditions are epistemically better rather than by abandoning the domain. Fourth, the question of whose verdict is put in question: if the samples that moral philosophers have historically reasoned from are the outliers, then any debunking argument seems to cut against the intuitions used in academic normative theory at least as hard as against folk morality. Fifth, what any of this should change outside philosophy, for people who have to act on cross-cultural moral disagreement in international law, public-health policy, or the evaluation of AI systems across user populations.

Rank these, or replace them with better ones, and defend the ordering. I want the criteria made explicit and the trade-offs between them argued: how much other work depends on each question, how tractable it is for philosophers who cannot collect new data, how neglected it is, and how much its answer would actually change what someone does. Tell me which questions are upstream of which, which you would drop entirely, and what result over the next two or three years would most move a line up or down your list.
