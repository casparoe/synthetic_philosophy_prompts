Me and my friend Dev got into an argument a couple of weeks ago about a thing that happened with his aunt and we never really resolved it, I think partly because we were disagreeing about two different things without noticing. So here's the case. His aunt has mild dementia and she's enrolled in a sleep study at the local hospital; each visit involves a lumbar puncture. At the first visit she was having a good day, she signed the consent form herself after the nurse talked her through it. The form says the procedure involves "some discomfort," which is literally true for most people but doesn't say the specific thing, which is that roughly one person in twenty gets a spinal headache afterwards that can knock them out for days. Nobody lied exactly, it was just described in the vaguest way available. She gets driven to every visit by her daughter, who is also her full-time carer and who gets $200 per visit from the study to cover gas and her time, and the daughter is really invested in the study and keeps telling her mom that she's helping people and should keep going. At the second visit his aunt is having a foggy day. She doesn't seem to remember agreeing to anything, and when the tray comes out with the needle on it she says "get away from me" — but then the nurse talks to her for about thirty seconds, she calms down, and she lets them go ahead, and they do the second puncture.

Dev thinks her consent to that second one was valid, I don't. I'm at maybe 0.35 and he's at about 0.8, and I want to know where the disagreement actually lives. To make it a real question: by "valid" I mean the researchers were morally permitted to go ahead with the second procedure on the strength of her consent alone, meaning they didn't need someone else's permission and they weren't wronging her by proceeding. Please give me your own number, but mostly I want the decomposition, with a rough probability and one line of reasoning for each piece: how much rides on the disclosure problem (does an omission of a specific risk invalidate consent when what was said was technically true, and does the standard track what a reasonable patient would want to know or what this particular patient would care about, since she's the type who hates being kept in the dark); how much on competence (does she have to understand what's happening at the moment of the procedure, or is it enough that she understood and agreed on a lucid day earlier); how much on voluntariness (the daughter's $200 and her enthusiasm — I keep thinking of it as a "double agent" situation where her interests are partly her mom's and partly not); and how much on whether consent is even what's doing the moral work here, since the procedure is low-risk and the research has real value, so maybe they'd be permitted to go ahead anyway and the consent talk is mostly covering the hospital's back. Then say how the pieces combine — is it basically one dominant term, or do they multiply out — and tell me which single term your number is most sensitive to, and which one new fact would move you the most.

Two things I've been turning over that might be junk, tell me if they are. First, I've been calling it the "veto window": my idea that consent only stays valid if there's a live, usable way for someone to say no at the actual moment the thing happens, and that a confused "get away from me" is a veto even from someone who can't remember what they signed — so the thirty seconds of soothing from the nurse is the real problem, not the paperwork. Second, "ratification after the fact": if she's lucid the next morning and says she's glad they did it, does that reach backwards and make the previous afternoon's consent valid, or is that just a separate thing that makes everyone feel better? If you think I'm missing a sub-question entirely, add it, and at the end say which of me and Dev you think is more likely to be wrong and what exactly the other one is seeing that he or I isn't.
