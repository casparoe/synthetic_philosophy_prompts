I am a junior at a large public high school, and I have been asked to lead one forty-five-minute session for the new members of our debate club: about twenty-five ninth and tenth graders, almost none of whom have studied philosophy, with a whiteboard and no assigned reading. The request comes from a practical problem rather than a syllabus. In novice rounds, speakers constantly land a cutting or misleading claim and then, when challenged, retreat behind the fact that they never literally said it. The coach wants the newcomers to have a vocabulary for what a speaker manages to convey without having said it. I have been reading a little about Grice on speaker meaning and conversational implicature, and I have some half-formed ideas about how to use it, but what I need is a plan for teaching it rather than an explanation of it: the order the ideas must come in and why, the one example that should carry the session, the misconceptions to expect and where in the sequence to defuse each, a check at the end that would show the idea has landed, and what I should openly decline to cover.

Three things I have decided, which you should feel free to overturn. First, the carrying example should be a single utterance drawn from school life that is literally true in every detail and yet conveys something its speaker would disown, and it must have this property: changing one detail of the surrounding situation, while leaving the words untouched, switches the extra content off. I want that maneuver to be available mid-session so the group can see for themselves that the extra content is not sitting inside the sentence. Please do not use the philosophy-job recommendation letter; it is the only example I could find online, and my classmates have never been in anything like it.

Second, I want three cases, and I am unsure of their order: one whose extra content the entire room gets at once; one that only part of the room gets because it depends on background knowledge only some of them share, which I want to use to force the question of whether the conveyed content belongs to the sentence or to the utterance in its context; and one where the speaker wants the hearer to work the content out and also wants to be able to deny it afterwards.

Third, here is where I think my plan may collapse. I had intended to give them the cancellation test: if the speaker can append a denial of the extra content without contradicting the original sentence, then that content was only conveyed, not said. But a sharper student will notice that this lets every speaker off the hook, since the retreat to the literal content is always available — which is precisely the dodge the novices are abusing. If the test licenses that, the session will have handed them a more sophisticated version of the move I am trying to disarm. So I need to know where in the sequence to raise this, and how to frame the test so that it does not collapse into the conclusion that the speaker always wins.

The misconceptions I expect, which I would like placed in the sequence rather than merely listed: that the conveyed content is just the tone or attitude of the message; that it is the same as what the sentence implies or entails; that it is lying under another name; and that the maxims, if I name them at all, are rules of politeness that the speaker broke. Tell me what I have missed. I would also like your view on two smaller decisions: whether to make explicit the assumption that the speaker is still trying to be cooperative and informative, or to leave it implicit and let the cases do the work; and whether to introduce the word "implicature" at all before the final ten minutes. Finally, if you think the debate-club framing risks teaching them to treat conveyed content as a tactic rather than as an ordinary feature of talk, say so and tell me what to aim at instead. Please do not suggest readings or citations; they will not do any homework.
