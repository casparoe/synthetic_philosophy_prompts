I have been asked to give a seventy-five-minute guest session in the first-year letterform studio of a type design programme, and I want to use it to bring about eighteen students — who can draw a passable 'a', who can name bowl, stem, spur, terminal, counter and aperture, and who have had no philosophy — to the point where they feel the force of a single question: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? Not how one draws one, and not which 'a' is best, but what a mark has to be like in order to be that letter at all — given that a Bodoni 'a', a Futura 'a' and a single-storey 'ɑ' seem to share no measurable feature while remaining unmistakably the same letter, and that some marks much closer to the canonical 'a' than any of those are not one.

I will have a whiteboard, a stack of printed specimens, and no projector. Please do not answer the question for me: plan how I should spend the time. I want the order in which the pieces must be introduced and what depends on what — not a menu of things that could be said.

Three notions of my own that I would like the session to earn, and that you may take as given:

*latitude* — the set of shapes a letter may take across typefaces. My reason for thinking this is the hard part is that the latitude of 'a' looks disconnected: there seems to be no continuous deformation that takes a double-storey 'a' into a single-storey one while remaining an 'a' at every intermediate step, so the category cannot be a region of shape-space with a centre.

*contrast budget* — the pressure, within a single typeface, for 'a' to stay distinguishable from o, d, g, q, e, u and n at the sizes and in the media the face is actually used at, which suggests that much of what makes an 'a' an 'a' is not intrinsic to it but is its slot in a system of neighbours.

*the counterfeit* — a mark that satisfies every local criterion you could write down for an 'a' and reads correctly in isolation, but is visibly not the 'a' of the face it has been dropped into.

I have three candidate carrying examples: an *orphan sort*, in which each pair of students is handed twelve unlabelled glyphs cut from six typefaces — two from each — and asked to sort them into six piles without being told what any glyph says or whether any two come from the same face; the *counterfeit*; and the disconnectedness of latitude itself. Which should carry the whole session, what must it have and what must it lack, and why not the three I am otherwise tempted by — the Bodoni-beside-Futura comparison, the squint-or-blur test, and asking them to draw an 'a' from memory?

The misconceptions I expect, in no particular order: that the essence is a checklist of features you could tick off; that since no feature is shared by all 'a's, the letter is arbitrary convention and one could swap 'a' and 'o' throughout a typeface at no cost; that there is an ideal 'a' which real fonts approximate, the truest one being nearest to it; and that talk of a letter's spirit is mysticism, the real story being only what readers happen to recognise, which is a psychological matter and none of the designer's business. Put these in the order in which they will actually arise, and tell me the exact point in the sequence at which each should be defused — I would much rather pre-empt one than correct it, because in a studio a wrong framing reaches the far side of the room before I can answer it.

Two further things. First, the hinge I am least sure about: once the disconnectedness of latitude has landed, the session can collapse into "no answer, mere convention" — what must already be in place before that point so that the question survives it? Second, the end: I want one task whose results would let me tell, next morning, whether the question has landed or whether they have only learned to say "it's about the system, not the shape." What should that task be, what does an answer that has landed look like, and what does a fluent but empty one look like? And what should I openly decline to explain at this level — say, the philosophy-of-concepts literature, perception and letter recognition, or the history of the letter — and how should I mark the refusal so it reads as a decision rather than an omission?
