I run the clinical ethics consultation service at a large teaching hospital, and most of our consults are family disagreements about treatment for a patient who has lost capacity. Our standing protocol is autonomy-based: look for an advance directive, identify the designated surrogate, apply substituted judgment, fall back on best interests. It keeps producing recommendations that families experience as foreign — it insists on one person whose judgment counts, and it treats the patient as a self-contained chooser with a pre-existing will to be unearthed. I want a second procedure to run alongside it, built from Confucian role ethics, that begins from the patient as a node in a web of relationships and from obligations that attach to roles rather than to individuals.

Constraints on the design: it has to be runnable inside one thirty-minute family meeting by a rotating fellow with no philosophy training; it has to end in something the attending physician can sign and our legal office can defend, even though that office thinks exclusively in terms of individual autonomy and statutory surrogacy; and it has to be honest about where it stops.

So please write the procedure: the questions in order, what counts as an adequate answer to each, where it halts with a recommendation, and where it deliberately hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values or of institutional authority rather than something the framework can settle. Two features of our caseload must be handled explicitly. First, roles are frequently held by people who have not lived them — the son who is statutory next of kin but has not spoken to his father in twelve years, the stepdaughter who has done the daily caring for six. Say what your procedure does when role and enacted relationship diverge, and state plainly whether you are treating a role as conferred by social position or as constituted by the relationship actually being lived; I know this is contested, so commit to one and say what it costs you. Second, patients often arrive with a written advance directive that the family wants set aside. The written document is the paradigm object of the autonomy framework and has almost no place in a role-ethics one; do not quietly ignore it — say what the procedure does with it.

Then run the procedure on three recent cases: (1) a widowed father with advanced dementia and no directive, whose estranged son, the legal surrogate, wants a feeding tube and aggressive treatment, while the brother who has visited weekly for years says he was told repeatedly that he never wanted machines; (2) a man with pneumonia and a signed directive refusing antibiotics, whose wife and two adult children, all closely involved, insist he has changed his mind and want it overridden; (3) two daughters equally involved in their father's care, one arguing that filial responsiveness means honoring the wish he stated, the other that it means protecting him from a burdensome intervention. I expect at least one of these to come out somewhere I won't like; do not steer around that.

Finally, answer as a specialist in comparative moral philosophy who knows both Confucian ethics and clinical practice. Think the design through before you write — sketch at least one alternative structure and say why you rejected it. Flag where the translation does violence to the theory: which distinctions you had to drop to make it usable by a non-specialist in half an hour, which you refused to drop, and which step is most exposed to being gamed by an articulate family. Close with a confidence note on the procedure as a whole and on each step, and single out the one claim you are least sure of, including whether your uncertainty is about institutional design or about what the tradition actually commits you to.
