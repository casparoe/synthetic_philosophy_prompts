My flatmate and I had a three-hour argument last night about peer disagreement and I think we were talking past each other the whole time. He kept saying that if someone you regard as an epistemic peer disagrees with you, you obviously have to move toward them; I kept saying that depends on how carefully youve actually looked at the first-order evidence. I never managed to convice him, and I now think its because "do you defer to peers" isn't one question at all. So: build me a "which position on higher-order evidence do you actually hold?" quiz for the reading group I run. Eight questions.

What I need from it:

- Every question is a concrete case with two or three answer options, phrased so someone can answer without knowing what any position is called. No "do you trust experts?", and no question where one option is clearly the polite one. People should get sorted by what they say about cases, not by which label they like.
- These cases have to be in the mix: (1) the person disagreeing with you is you-last-week, when you were exhausted and in a foul mood; (2) you only found out about the disagreement because a journalist emailed you for comment — people who agree with you never bother emailing; (3) you later learn that during the whole period you were forming the view you were on a medication that quietly scrambles your sense of how confident you are (not what you believe, just the confidence); (4) the disagreement is about whether a long sum you did carefully twice by hand came out right, and you can't redo it now; (5) six peers disagree with you and you have no idea whether they talked to each other first; (6) your peer has a much better track record than you on this exact kind of question, but you've gone over the evidence for this particular one far more closely than she has.
- Build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of names: say which two or three axes actually generate the options (I'm guessing one is uniqueness vs. permissivism at the first-order level, one is whether higher-order evidence is just more evidence or a special kind of thing, one is whether you weight by track record or by peer status), lay out the combinations those allow, then drop the real views into the cells. These leaves have to show up: equal-weight conciliationism, steadfastness, the "higher-order evidence is just ordinary evidence, nothing special happens" view, a calibration/track-record view, and something where standing pat and budging are both fine. The quiz also has to say something about akrasia: which answer patterns land someone on "I still believe p, and I also believe my belief that p was formed badly", and whether that landing spot is coherent or just uncomfortable.
- For the empty cells: tell me which answer combinations nobody occupies and why — incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unwritten. And flag any two leaves the instrument can't actually separate rather than pretending the tree is finer than it is.
- Don't give me any authors, papers or citations; the group hasn't read the literature and would only argue about who said what.

Format: first a table with columns # | case (one sentence) | option A | option B | option C | axis this question separates. Then a table with columns answer pattern (like A,B,A,C,B,A,A,C) | position name | one sentence on what it commits you to | most awkward consequence. After that a JSON array where each element is {"combination": <answer pattern>, "status": "incoherent" | "unattractive" | "unexplored", "diagnosis": <one sentence>}, and finally a JSON object {"indistinguishable_pairs": [["<leaf>", "<leaf>", "<why the cases can't pry them apart>"]]}.
