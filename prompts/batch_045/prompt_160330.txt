I'm in a graduate reading group on agency and free will this term, and one member of it — a behavioral ecologist, the only person in the room who has ever actually watched an insect do anything — keeps a running document in which she enters every case we discuss with a one-word verdict: SPHEXISH or FLEXIBLE. Her track record so far: the digger wasp who, when the paralyzed cricket is moved a few inches while she inspects the burrow, drags it back and re-runs the whole delivery routine from the beginning — SPHEXISH ("the loop never closes," she said); the goose whose egg is lifted away mid-roll but who finishes the rolling motion against an invisible egg — SPHEXISH; the ant that repeatedly carries a living nestmate to the refuse pile after the nestmate has been dabbed with the corpse-marking chemical — SPHEXISH, even though she conceded the ant is responding exactly as it should to a real cue; the rat that, when its habitual alley is blocked, takes a shortcut it has never been reinforced for — FLEXIBLE ("it has a map, not a route"); a New Caledonian crow that solves a novel two-step puzzle box on its first exposure — FLEXIBLE; a patient with utilization behavior who, having been told explicitly not to touch anything, still picks up the comb and combs his hair, and does it again when the comb is put back — SPHEXISH ("nobody is in charge of that behavior"); a habitual smoker who lights up while absorbed in a phone call but who would put the cigarette out the instant you asked — FLEXIBLE, with the note "the person isn't sphexish, the behavior is"; a welding robot that runs its cycle over a misloaded part — SPHEXISH; a chess engine that abandons its plan after an unexpected move and re-searches — FLEXIBLE; a chatbot that, when told "step 4 of your own solution is wrong, check it," re-derives the step and corrects it — FLEXIBLE. Two slogans of hers pull in different directions and she has never reconciled them: "rigidity is a property of the loop, not of the behavior," and "you are flexible if the world can still get a word in." She has also once or twice muttered that the two labels may not be enough — something like "tracky but not up to me" — but she has never entered a third verdict in the document.

The case I picked for next week is a language-model agent wired into a loop and given a multi-step quantitative task. Change the numbers in the task and it produces the same solution shape with the new numbers substituted. Tell it that one of its own steps is wrong and it often finds and repairs the error. But if you quietly alter a single intermediate value in its own earlier output and then let it continue, it builds on the corrupted value without comment and reports a confident wrong answer; and nothing in the system consults the world or the task except as more text appended to the window. I have a small bet with her on the verdict, so I would like your reasoning rather than sources — I have all the case descriptions in front of me and do not need citations or references. Which verdict do you expect her to enter, and why? More usefully: what are the two or three candidate rules that fit her whole track record equally well, what does each of them predict about this case, and which of her precedents is the closest analog on each side? And if the track record genuinely underdetermines it, what further case — described concretely enough that I could actually put it to her — would force the rules apart, and would it finally make her open that third bucket?
