Hey — I run a tiny reading group with two friends (one historian, one econ-adjacent political scientist) on whether culture can be a cause, and I'm putting together a "where I stand" note for next week. Before I present it I'd like someone to audit it, because I have a feeling the package doesn't fit together as neatly as it feels like it does. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) I think the Protestant ethic thesis is right as a causal claim — something about ascetic Protestant ideas made a real difference to how early modern capitalism developed, and it isn't just a rationalization of an economic shift that had already happened. (2) I'm a methodological individualist: "Calvinism" doesn't do anything, Calvinists do, so when I say culture caused something I mean there's a story about individuals' beliefs, desires, opportunities and what they did. (3) I don't think a causal claim earns its keep unless you can sketch a mechanism; I'm suspicious of explanations that amount to "X came first and X and Y are robustly associated". (4) But I also think the interpretive stuff — Verstehen, ideal types, getting the meaning right — isn't just a heuristic for finding the real cause, it partly fixes what the cause even was, since the thing being explained is in part made of the meanings the actors attached to their own conduct. (5) And I hold a hard line on value-freedom: the investigator's values legitimately pick the topic (that kind of value-relevance is unavoidable) but shouldn't show up in the conclusions — while of course holding that other people's values are among the causes I study.

The one bit of scaffolding I've been using, and which I'd like you to take seriously rather than replace: I sort culture-causal claims into spark claims (an idea initiates a practice that wouldn't otherwise have existed), steering claims (the practice was coming anyway; the idea fixed which version got pursued and how hard), and ratchet claims (the idea neither started nor steered it, but locked in a pattern that would otherwise have decayed). My hunch is that reading the Protestant-ethic story as a ratchet claim makes it safer, and that's the reading I've been defending. Relatedly: when my historian friend points out that in some regions the economic practices arguably predate the religious ones, I notice I want to say two different things — "fine, it's a ratchet claim, not a spark claim" and "fine, but since meanings partly constitute what's being explained, the dating objection is close to a pseudo-problem" — and I can't tell whether those are the same move or two different moves, or whether they're compatible.

So: go through these and tell me which pairs actually fight, which only look like they fight and dissolve once something is spelled out, and for each real tension what the cheapest fix is and what it costs me — rank them by severity, don't list them as equally bad, and say explicitly what I'd have to give up in each case (and if the answer is "the ratchet reading itself is doing too much work," say so). Also flag anything that holds together only because of something I've left unsaid. Skip citations and reading lists, I know the literature well enough to fill those in. Write it in American English, and keep it an audit rather than an essay — I'd rather have the tensions in order with what gives than a lecture on Weber.
