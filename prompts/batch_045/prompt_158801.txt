A forecasting tournament has been running for six years. Several hundred forecasters, all with public track records, all reading one another's published reasoning, are forecasting one standing question: whether a system meeting a stated capability threshold will be demonstrated before 2040. They have now exchanged and revised their figures in eleven rounds, each revision posted to a public ledger available to everyone. The spread narrowed early from a very wide initial range to a stable band of roughly 0.30 to 0.75, and it has not narrowed any further across the last five rounds.

I want to know what Aumann's agreement theorem should lead me to conclude about that residual spread. As I see it, there are four ways its preconditions could fail here: the forecasters have no common prior, having come to the question with genuinely different background commitments; their posteriors are not common knowledge in the iterated sense the theorem requires, however public the ledger is; the proposition in dispute is not a single well-defined event in a shared partition, because what counts as meeting the threshold is itself partly settled by what gets built; or the forecasters are simply not the Bayesian agents the theorem describes, and the spread is noise, stakes, reputational incentive and bounded reasoning.

Give me your credence that more than half the residual spread is attributable to the first of these — genuine failure of the common prior — rather than to the others, and decompose it: a number and a one-line justification for each of the four explanations, a note on how they should be combined given that they are not mutually exclusive, and a clear statement of which one you would actually put money on. My own number is about 0.45, and I suspect I am under-weighting how much work the third explanation does.

Finally, tell me which term your number is most sensitive to, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail: what further observation about this tournament, or what argument, would move you by at least 0.15 in either direction.
