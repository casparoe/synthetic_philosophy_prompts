I have no academic background in philosophy — I work in insurance claims and do my reading on the train — but for about two years I have been part of a reading group of six that has ended up arguing, nearly every month, about a single question: whether thought experiments (I have seen them called intuition pumps) actually tell us anything, or whether they only move us. We have split three and three, we are all a little tired of repeating ourselves, and we agreed at our last meeting that we should try to write down what we genuinely agree on before we meet again. I keep the notes, so the drafting has come to me. I would like you to draft the document.

The two positions, as I would name them: the tracker side holds that a well-built case can be a source of evidence — it varies one feature while holding the rest steady, and our response to it is sensitive to something real, either about our concepts or about what is possible; the framer side holds that the pull of a case comes almost entirely from what the case stages and what it leaves the reader to supply, that the pull is a fact about presentation rather than about the subject matter, and that no case should be relied on until it has been replaced, or at least checked, by something that is not a case. Rename both sides if you can find fairer names; mine are loaded, and I know it.

Three things all six of us accept, and I think accept honestly. First, that every case says less than the reader imagines: the reader silently imports a whole background — that the strange stuff is drinkable, that the hospital is competent, that the speakers share a language and a world. Call this the specification gap. Second, that a case's staging has at least two separable parts: which details are mentioned, and in what order they are mentioned. Third, that we cannot settle anything by asking, case by case, whether the verdict "feels" right, so we need diagnostics. The three we have invented between us are the neutral paraphrase (strip the staging, restate the case in bare terms, see whether the verdict survives); the small-step walk (build a chain of cases from an ordinary one to the bizarre one and ask whether the verdict flips at a point where nothing in what is described changes); and the mirror case (keep the strangeness, delete the feature that is said to be doing the work, and see whether the verdict survives).

Against that background I want the joint statement. Give me the claims both camps can sign, and do not let them be vacuous: if a sentence would be signed by nobody who actually holds either view, or only by a fence-sitter who was invented for the purpose, leave it out. Then give each side its own position in that side's own words, in a formulation it would put its name to rather than one the other side would write for it. Then set out the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of us. I think there are three, but correct me: whether the pull of a case is evidence about the subject matter or only about us; whether our three diagnostics can be trusted, or whether testing a test is just question-begging in a new place; and what it shows when informed readers who understand a case disagree about it permanently and without heat. A fourth may be what a case could even be evidence for — our concepts, or the way the world could have been.

For each crux, give a case or argument both camps accept as bearing on it, and write down in advance what each camp predicts it will show, including what each would count as evidence against itself. I have made up one case for this purpose and would like it used as the shared test: for fifteen years a man has had software answering his correspondence in his name, trained on everything he has written, checked against a weekly digest he nearly always finds himself agreeing with. A former colleague writes to ask whether he remembers a fire in a storeroom they both worked in thirty years ago. The software, using a journal entry he wrote at the time, replies that he remembers it well. He does remember it — he was there — but he was not consulted and knows nothing of the exchange. Did he assert that he remembers it? Did he lie? Run all three diagnostics on it, say where in the small-step walk the verdict flips and whether any described fact flips with it, and record each camp's prediction before you say what you think the diagnostics actually deliver. Then do the same for two of the famous cases we have shouted about most — the Chinese Room and the fake-barn case; I do not remember who originated either and it does not matter, so please do not tell me.

Finally, tell me honestly which parts of our dispute are only about words — I suspect "evidence", "intuition", and "possible" are each doing more than one job — and if you think one of the two positions as I have described it is not a position anyone should hold, say so inside the document and tell me what the real disagreement behind it is. Please do not give me a reading list, citations, or suggestions for further reading: I cannot get to a library and in any case I want your own formulations. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, in plain words with no jargon you have not defined, as one would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, and roughly a thousand words.
