I am a novelist, and my current book is set largely in the transplant unit of a near-future teaching hospital. I want the ethical argument in it to be technically right, not merely plausible-sounding, so I need help from someone who can hold the philosophy and the clinical facts at once.

Please take the role of a philosopher of medicine who sits on a hospital ethics committee: precise about distinctions, unwilling to settle anything with a slogan, and willing to tell me when a character is scoring a rhetorical point rather than an argumentative one. Before you adjudicate, spend a short preliminary paragraph listing the distinctions you intend to rely on — organism-as-a-whole versus personhood or higher-brain accounts of death; discovery versus decision in the determination of death; the difference between a criterion and a test for it; and what the dead donor rule is supposed to protect — and say explicitly which of them each of my characters' moves is answerable to. Use no outside sources; reason from the concepts.

Here is the material. Two of my consultants — Dr. Vance, a neurointensivist, and Dr. Okoro, a philosopher of medicine — got into an argument about two scenes I showed them, and I have reproduced it as evenly as I can.

Scene one: Mira, thirty-four, was declared dead nine weeks ago by neurological criteria after a catastrophic brain haemorrhage. Because she was eleven weeks pregnant, the hospital kept her body on an experimental "somatic support protocol" — extracorporeal oxygenation plus hormonal replacement — in the hope of bringing the fetus to viability. Her body has maintained blood pressure and temperature, healed a surgical wound, and carried a pregnancy that is, on the scans, developing normally.

Scene two: Elias, fifty-eight, suffered a devastating stroke but was not brain dead. Life support was withdrawn at his family's request; his heart stopped; after the standard waiting period he was declared dead by circulatory criteria and the theatre prepared for organ recovery — at which point his heart resumed a spontaneous rhythm. The recovery was abandoned and he was readmitted to intensive care.

Their exchange, in seven moves:

1. Vance: Mira is dead, and there is nothing exotic about it. Death is the irreversible loss of the organism's capacity to function as an integrated whole; the brain is the integrator; without it you have tissues kept going locally, not a human organism. A pregnancy can continue because the uterus sustains the fetus — the fetus is a passenger, not a part integrated with Mira.

2. Okoro: Then explain nine weeks of wound healing, thermoregulation and a pregnancy carried toward viability. Those are achievements of the organism as a whole, not of an organ in a bath. If the body can still do this, what died was not the organism but the person — the subject of consciousness. So neurological criteria quietly rest on a higher-brain rationale that you never argued for, and Mira's body is a live counterexample to the rationale you did give.

3. Vance: Homeostasis driven by a machine that replaces the brain is not self-integration; stop the protocol and the body falls apart within hours. And nobody thinks a ventilated body is alive. Besides, if you go higher-brain you get results nobody will accept: permanently unconscious patients who breathe on their own would count as dead.

4. Okoro: The first half of that concedes too much: if dead means would disintegrate once the machines stop, then death becomes technology-relative, and a better protocol would make the very same body alive again. The second half is a prediction about public opinion, not an argument, and the unpalatability of the higher-brain view does not repair the organismic one. So let us be honest: neither rationale is doing the work. What does the work is a decision — we treat certain losses as death because of what we want to be able to do afterwards.

5. Vance: That is precisely why Elias is not a scandal. On circulatory criteria, irreversible means it will not reverse of its own accord and we have decided not to reverse it. Autoresuscitation is a rare failure of prediction, handled by revising the waiting period. That is a discovery that went wrong, not evidence that the criterion is a convention.

6. Okoro: But prediction and decision are entangled. Elias's waiting period was fixed partly by how long organs tolerate warm ischaemia, and it was a transplant team that was waiting. So the determination tracks our retrieval interests, and if that is acceptable — I think it can be — then the dead donor rule is not the deep moral constraint everyone pretends it is. What matters morally is that Elias consented, that he was beyond recovery, and that organ removal did not wrong him.

7. Vance: Say that in public and you destroy the practice; patients will conclude that signing a donor card licenses surgeons to kill them. The dead donor rule has to be upheld as a public standard even if its rationale is messier in a seminar than in the guidelines. And anyway Mira is dead under both ordinary criteria, so your scene can keep her declared dead and leave the pregnancy question open.

What I want: rate each of the seven moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with one short paragraph of justification each, distinguishing the parts of a move that succeed from the parts that do not (several moves are mixed). Then give me, in under 250 words, an overall verdict on who is more right about what, and — the part I care about most as a novelist — a note on what both of them are missing, and what the strongest unmade move on each side would be, so that I can end the scene honestly rather than letting the more fluent character win by volume.
