long time lurker, rare commenter. I've been chewing on herding and info cascades for a while now — partly the classic sequential setup (everyone queues up, each person gets one private hint and sees what everyone before them did, and after two or three people the thing locks in), partly watching group decisions where I actually live: funding calls, Slack polls, prediction markets where the price moves an hour before anybody says why. I finally tried to write down what I believe and it doesn't feel like it hangs together, but I can't find the seam. Audit please.

Roughly in the order I'd defend it:

1. The standard result is right and I believe it. A cascade can be perfectly rational one person at a time and still carry almost no information. Once the public history swamps one person's private hint, "seven smart people already did X" is near-zero evidence for X, and the group can lock onto the wrong option with probability bounded away from zero no matter how large it gets. I lean on this hard — I probably dismiss "everyone's doing it" faster than is socially comfortable.

2. And yet I defer to aggregates constantly. Forecasting tournaments, prediction markets, calibrated superforecasters, the plain wisdom-of-crowds stuff. On most questions I take the aggregate as better than my own guess and don't feel bad about it, for the boring reason: it's the same reason I'd defer to any Bayesian, other people have signal I don't, pooling is close to free accuracy.

3. When I disagree with people I roughly respect, I move toward them. Not full split-the-difference, I weight by track record, and I read persistent disagreement as at least weak evidence that somebody's process is broken, possibly mine.

4. I'm paranoid about double counting. If 200 people bought the thing I don't have 200 independentConfirmations, most of them read the same two write-ups or were looking at each other. So I discount. In practice discounting means I eyeball a number — eh, call it eight effectively independent people — because I have no principled way to set it.

5. Order independence is my main anti-suck check. If I'd have landed somewhere different having met the same arguments in a different order, something went wrong: anchoring, double counting, or I got had. I rerun big updates with the evidence scrambled to see whether the number holds.

6. I think way more people should be visibly willing to break a cascade — act on your own signal even though twelve people before you went the other way. Not because you're likely right, you're likely wrong, but because that action is what keeps the option alive downstream, and the alternative is everyone being agreeable while the group learns nothing. I've done it in real group decisions and would do it again.

7. Concretely, same thing: fourteen people have already voted A in the poll, my own read weakly favors B, and I think the right move is to vote B and say why in the thread, even though I also think A is more likely than B once I condition on everything.

8. Two mechanisms I've gotten kind of evangelical about. (a) Silent rounds — everyone commits privately before seeing anyone else, then reveal simultaneously. Cheap, and it looks to me like it kills the failure mode in 1 more or less completely. (b) A dissent bounty: the group pays people a small flat amount, no strings, to take the action the public record disfavors. My pitch is that this is nearly free information — you buy one person's private signal at the cost of one person's slightly-wrong action, and the group is way better off. I've floated (b) a handful of times and nobody has produced an objection I found real, which honestly makes me more suspicious of it rather than less.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? I have a vague feeling that 1 and 2 can't both be doing the work I want them to do, but I also suspect there's a distinction I'm failing to draw that makes it fine, and I want to know which of those it is. Same question for 5 and 6 — my anti-suck check and my cascade-breaking habit feel like they're aimed at the same thing from opposite ends.

Please rank whatever you find by how much it actually costs me, and for each tension say specifically what I'd have to drop, add, or qualify, and what that costs me elsewhere. I dont have a horse here, I genuinely don't know which of these I'd give up, so tell me rather than letting me rig it. And please separate what's settled — what follows from the model, what follows from Bayes — from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this: how much of real-world herding is informational versus reputational, whether actual markets do the thing where everyone reads the price so nobody bothers to gather the info the price is made of, how much of this survives contact with real humans who talk to each other between rounds. Don't hand me a consensus the field doesn't have. Also flag anywhere I'm using a word in a way that sneaks in a conclusion I haven't earned.
