I am a retired structural biologist — thirty years of protein crystallography, most of it on allosteric enzymes — who has lately been sitting in on a philosophy of biology reading group. For most of my career I disliked the way molecular biologists talk about DNA as a code, a program, or a set of instructions, and I am now trying to turn that dislike into a short essay of roughly 2,500 words for a scientifically literate general readership. I have never published outside my own field, and I would be grateful for your comments on the working notes below. They are more than an outline but not yet prose; I have kept them in the first person because that is how I think.

The thesis is that code-talk about DNA is not false but is a research stance rather than a discovery, and that the honest description of what DNA does is that it is a heritable, copyable, constraint-imposing physical particular — closer to a jig or a die than to a text. The notes develop this in four moves.

First, a distinction between three things people might mean. (i) The mapping sense: the codon-to-amino-acid table is a genuine empirical discovery, a many-one mapping that we can write down and use to translate. (ii) The interpretive sense: a code in the everyday sense needs a decoder whose conventions are external to the message, so that the same marks could have meant something else. (iii) The design sense: a code implies a sender and a purpose. My claim is that the mapping sense is unproblematic and useful, but that it gets its rhetorical force from illicitly borrowing the other two. In the cell, the decoding apparatus — transfer RNAs, the enzymes that charge them, the ribosome — is itself specified by the genome, and its specificity is a matter of shape, charge and free energy rather than convention. The reader of the description is part of what the description describes; it is a cookbook that also bakes the cook. Where the mapping is arbitrary, that arbitrariness is exactly what stands in need of explanation, either by stereochemistry or by the familiar suggestion that the code is a frozen accident; so arbitrariness cannot be the feature that makes the molecule linguistic, since it is the feature we are trying to explain.

Second, a thought experiment I call the swap test. Suppose we engineer a lineage of bacteria in which a particular sense codon is reassigned, by re-specifying its cognate transfer RNA and the enzyme that charges it, so that the codon now inserts a different amino acid. The lineage is viable; its proteome is chemically different but functionally adequate. Now move a gene from the standard organism into the engineered one. The same sequence now yields a different, possibly useless or toxic product. My question is whether the message carried by that sequence has changed. If the message is what we read off our standard table, it has. If the message is what the receiving cell's machinery produces, it has not — only our table has gone out of date. I take this to show that the coding relation is not a property of the sequence but of the pairing between a sequence and a particular decoding system, which makes the sequence much more like a key than like a sentence. And a key is not a code; it is a fit.

Third, some complications I want to be fair to. The mapping from DNA to protein does not even determine a unique product: alternative splicing, RNA editing, and the dependence of folding on translation speed mean that the same sequence can yield different chains in different contexts. Nor can we invert the relation: because the table is degenerate, a protein does not determine a sequence. So if encoding is supposed to be a recoverable, content-preserving mapping, DNA does not encode even proteins, let alone organisms. Moreover, the semantic content that would vindicate the design sense has to come from somewhere, and the only candidate is natural selection: a sequence is about whatever it was historically selected for. But then content depends on the environment and on the grain of the description, and two equally true descriptions of the selection history will assign different contents to the same molecule. This looks to me like a derived, interest-relative notion of meaning, which is fine, but it is not the robust propositional content that code-talk promises.

Fourth, the constructive part. What survives the criticism is that DNA is a structure that is copied with high fidelity and that reliably biases local chemistry in ways that feed back into its own copying. That is the notion of heritable constraint. Codes are read; jigs are used. I also want to argue that the metaphor's fruitfulness is explained by its being a stance rather than a truth: cracking something means finding a mapping that lets you predict and rewrite it, and the proliferation of code-talk across biology — histone, splicing, neural — is evidence that the word marks an investigative posture, not a natural kind. That, I think, is why the debate keeps restarting: both sides are right about different things.

My own doubts. One: I suspect I have simply reinvented, in clumsier terms, a position that professional philosophers of biology already hold, and I cannot judge how far behind the literature I am. Two: I am unsure whether I can state natural selection itself without information-talk, and developmental biology may be a harder case than the enzymology I know. Three: my jig and key analogies are presumably just as metaphorical as the thing I am attacking, so I may be guilty of the very fault I diagnose. Four: I do not know whether the swap test is decisive or merely restates my conclusion in a more vivid vocabulary.

What I would like from you, if you will:

Whether the threefold distinction is doing real work, or whether it is a piece of classificatory tidying that no one on either side would dispute.

Whether the swap test genuinely discriminates between the views I want to separate, or whether it presupposes the conclusion — in particular, whether an opponent can simply accept it and reinterpret it.

The strongest objection you can make to the whole line of argument, stated in your own terms. Please do not direct me to books, papers, or authors, and please do not give me references or a reading list: I have no access to a library and I want the objection in a form I can try to answer myself, not a citation to chase. If you think the position has a standard name, tell me what the position is, but do not tell me who to read.

Whether the jig/key alternative is genuinely less metaphorical than code, or whether I should drop it and rest the essay on the negative argument alone.

Where the essay is weakest, what I should cut, and whether the constructive proposal is worth the space or should be compressed into the final paragraph.

Finally, anything in the notes that would strike a working biologist as naive or factually wrong; I would rather be corrected now than in print.

Please reply in British English, and in the voice of a sympathetic but exacting referee: direct about what fails, generous about what might be salvaged. Prose rather than bullet points, and please keep it under about 900 words.
