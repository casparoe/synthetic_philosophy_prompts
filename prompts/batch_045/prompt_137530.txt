You are a philosopher of psychiatry who also designs active-learning modules for clinical psychology trainees: you have run this kind of exercise before and know where it stalls. Think carefully before you write. Spend your first pass brainstorming mechanisms by which a classification could change the people classified, then commit to one design and show that it delivers.

Design a 75-minute seminar activity for 14 to 18 upper-level students (mixed philosophy and psychology) that makes participants feel the force of Ian Hacking's looping effects for human kinds: sort people into a kind, and the people change, and so the kind changes, so that the category's apparent fit is partly manufactured by the practice of using it.

Specifications:

1. No lecture up front, and no one should hear the phrases looping effect, interactive kind, or social construction until the debrief. The mechanism has to be something the participants do to each other.

2. A repeated in-session measure. At the start, everyone completes a short self-rating (five to seven items about distractibility, rumination, mental re-reading of conversations, on 1 to 7 scales) and a five-minute monotonous sustained-attention task with optional breaks. Both repeat at the end. That pre/post gap is the data the debrief works on.

3. Three conditions, assigned by a subgroup of three or four students acting as clinicians who hold a deliberately vague checklist for an invented, obviously fictional syndrome. Give the syndrome a name, a one-paragraph description, and a patient leaflet. Conditions: LABELED — told they meet criteria, given the leaflet and a real accommodation (extended time or a break pass); UNLABELED — identical accommodation, no name and no leaflet, described only as being on a flexible-timing track; STIGMATIZED — labeled, leaflet, accommodation, plus told the category is one that employers and licensing boards are starting to screen for.

4. The clinicians re-apply the unchanged checklist at the end and report prevalence a second time.

Deliverables: a timeline in blocks of minutes; a materials list; for each condition the mechanism you predict and what you expect the pre/post ratings, break-taking, and re-diagnosis rates to do; a debrief that maps each observed shift onto a component of the looping story — self-ascription, the concept-dependence of looping, movement away from the description in the stigmatized group, the second-order loop in which classifiers revise criteria, and the contrast with indifferent kinds, since electrons do not read their leaflets; and an honest treatment of the blind-looping question. Condition UNLABELED is the novel case: if those participants shift too, differential treatment alone reshapes behavior and self-description with no concept of the kind in play. Say which outcome would support which answer about whether looping requires that the classified have some concept of the kind, and what would be needed to settle it.

Close with predictable failure modes — students guessing the setup, clinicians refusing to sort their peers, someone with a real diagnosis in the room — how you would handle each, and the limits of the exercise: what it cannot reproduce about timescale, institutional vectors, and life-course effects, and what you must therefore supply in words. End with the guardrail you would insist on in the debrief: that a category's being looped does not make it unreal, does not make the distress fake, and does not by itself tell us whether to keep using it.
