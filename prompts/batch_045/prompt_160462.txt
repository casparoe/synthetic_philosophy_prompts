i teach a 300-level moral psych seminar, 18 students, one 80-minute block, and we're on the guilt vs. shame question next week — which one is the better moral emotion to build a culture around, and whether a culture can even choose. I need an actual in-class activity: a game, with rounds and payoffs, that makes them run into the hard part themselves instead of me telling them.

The hard part as I see it is the audience problem. Shame does its work only when somebody is watching whose opinion you care about; guilt does its work precisely when nobody is watching. So a shame-run culture is fragile to anonymity, mobility and exit (leave the village, start over with a clean face), while a guilt-run culture needs an internal observer that you can't install by passing a rule — you have to grow one, slowly, and once grown it doesn't switch off just because you repeal the institutions that produced it. That lag is the thing I most want them to feel.

So: something like a little community simulation, 5–6 short rounds, materials no fancier than index cards, envelopes, and a whiteboard scoreboard. Every round there's an oportunity for a small fictional infraction — drawn from a deck, "short-changed the grain store," that sort of thing; nothing that draws on anything any real student has done, and no real wrongdoing anywhere in the room. The group scores separately for compliance-when-observed and compliance-when-unobserved. They get a fixed budget and a menu of institutions to buy: open-plan housing vs. walls, public reckoning vs. sealed confession, record-keeping vs. deliberate forgetting, a cheap exit option, a reintegration ritual, some kind of novice-training practice. Then the environment shifts between rounds — first someone can act unseen, then a neighboring community opens up and exit gets cheap, then they're allowed to repeal institutions and watch which effects fail to switch off. I'd like a hidden per-person "internalization" counter that builds up slowly under some institutions and decays slowly after they're repealed, so the group discovers they can change the rules fast but not the habit.

Payoffs in points, no money, and the scoring rule has to be simple enough for me to run live on the board. I don't want to name the distinction until after the last round.

Then a debrief that maps specific moments onto specific claims: where shame stopped working and why, where the guilt lag showed up, what the repeal round actually proved. I want both characteristic failure modes on the table by the end — shame's (hypocrisy, exit, exile that never really ends) and guilt's (scrupulosity, private self-punishment with no off-switch, harm done with nothing public repaired) — and an honest note on which part of the question the game can't touch: it can't make anyone actually feel either emotion on cue, it can't compress a childhood into 80 minutes, and it won't settle which is better, only show what each buys and what it costs. I'll have to supply the normative frame myself.

And please tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. My three worries: someone meta-gaming the point system instead of playing the community; the discussion collapsing into shame = embarrassment; and, most importantly, the activity accidentally producing real shame in the room. How should I rig the setup so that last one can't happen?

Also, don't build it around any outside reading — half of them won't have done it, and I don't want the thing to depend on a name or a citation.
