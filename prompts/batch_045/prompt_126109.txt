ok so I'm in a political philosophy seminar this term (distributive justice, mostly analytic stuff) and our TA keeps leaning on this one move that I genuinely can't tell is deep or is just doing all the work by being vivid. The move is the gambling-versus-lightning thing: in the Dworkin reading we got, the rough version is that a misfortune is either like losing a bet you chose to place, or like getting hit by lightning — if it's the first, it's your option luck and you've got no complaint against anyone, and if its the second it's brute luck and it's the kind of thing a just distribution should undo. He used it to shut down a whole discussion on tuesday: someone raised the case of a guy who left school at sixteen to take a job in a warehouse and now has a wrecked back, no savings and no prospects, and the TA basically said, well, he took the job, that's a gamble that didn't pay off, that's on him. Half the room nodded and I sort of wanted to nod too, but I couldn't say why it felt like something had been slipped past us.

The bit I keep chewing on — and this part I made up myself, so nobody has to be polite about it — is that real life seems to be full of what I've started calling mandatory gambles, ones where sitting out isn't on the menu. You have to put your savings somewhere, you have to pick some career or another, you have to decide whether to trust this employer or that one, and if declining the bet isn't available then 'you gambled and lost' threatens to cover basically every bad outcome there is, which can't have been what the distinction was for. Second wrinkle: the same nominal bet isn't the same bet for two people, because what matters is where it sits relative to your cushion. Losing five thousand means you eat differently for a month for one person and means you lose the house and then can't get the next loan for another; my flatmate does stats and kept muttering about absorbing barriers and gambler's ruin when I described this, so I may have mangled it, but the thought is that the person with the small stack is doomed by the sequence even if every individual bet is fair. So 'he chose to take the job' quietly hides that his stack was small and that the sequence was forced. And third, whether you even register something as a gamble depends on your information and on how good you are at that kind of weighing-up, and both of those look like they're themselves matters of luck rather than of choice.

So: audit the analogy. Give me the correspondence table — who's the gambler, what's the stake, what are the odds, who or what is the house, what plays the role of declining to bet, what's the payout, where does insurance fit in — and then tell me which of the disanalogies actually bite for the conclusion the TA wanted from it (that a lost gamble generates no claim on anyone, and that only the lightning cases are the state's business) and which are just colourful noise. I especially want to know what the picture smuggles in from actual gambling that the target lacks — that the odds were known and not set against you, that you could have walked away, that there's a discrete moment where you consented to this bet, that the only thing at stake is the payout rather than your future menu of bets, that the gamblers are all playing the same game — and whether, once you strip those out, there's anything left of the choice/circumstance cut at all, or whether 'you took a gamble' has quietly become just a vivid way of restating 'this one's your responsibility'. And if the analogy can't carry the weight, is there a better one that can — inherited stack plus a casino with different odds per table, a mandatory insurance pool, something from decision theory about sequential risk — or is the honest answer that no comparison is going to do the job the TA wants done?
