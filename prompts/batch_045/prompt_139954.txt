I have been keeping a list of my own verdicts on cases in which the assessment of an agent seems to depend on something beyond her control, and I want to know whether any single principle underwrites them. I am familiar with the standard taxonomy of such cases and with the usual dialectic around them, so please do not begin by restating either: assume them, and go to the cases. In each pair, hold fixed everything I do not mention — the agents are alike in character, motives, information and level of care.

1. Two drivers leave the same party at the same blood alcohol and drive the same route at the same speed. One meets an empty road; the other meets a child who runs out between parked cars too late for anyone to avoid. My verdict: the second driver has more to answer for. Not that he is a worse person, and not that he was more careless, but that there is now more that he owes and more that the child's parents may reasonably ask of him.

2. Two men, each intending to kill, fire at the same range in the same conditions. In one case the cartridge is defective — a defect present in roughly one cartridge in five hundred, unknown to the shooter — and the shot misfires. My verdict: no difference in blameworthiness at all, and nothing for the first man to answer for that the second does not. I register the tension with case 1: in both pairs the only difference is whether a risk the agent created materialized, and yet the outcome matters to me in one and not the other.

3. A nurse administers a tenfold overdose through a careless misreading of a chart, and the patient dies. The autopsy reveals an undiagnosed condition that would have killed the patient within a week anyway. My verdict: the carelessness is undiminished and she is plainly blameworthy, but the death is less hers than it would otherwise have been, and what she owes the family is smaller. Had the same misreading done no harm, I would not think her one bit less careless, but I would think her debt smaller still.

4. An engineer approves a braking specification. Four hundred engineers approve specifications of that type for other units, and the design is such that about one unit in four hundred fails in service. His unit fails; people die. My verdict: he is blameworthy, and the fact that some other unit would very likely have failed had his not does not excuse him. Yet when I consider the voter whose vote made no difference to an outcome she opposed, or the trader whose single order was absorbed by the market, I feel no blame at all — although each knowingly joined a process that produced the harm. I do not know what separates the engineer from the voter, and I want the principle to say.

5. The same engineer's specification error turns out, unforeseeably, to make the vehicles safer in a way that saves two lives a correct specification would have lost. My verdict: he gets no credit, and I resist the suggestion that he deserves any. But I also notice that I am running an asymmetry — bad outcomes raise what I blame, good outcomes do not raise what I credit. If a principle forces me to choose, tell me which of the two judgments it sacrifices and why that one.

6. (a) A man with an undiagnosed hormone-secreting tumour becomes explosively irritable and publicly humiliates a colleague. (b) A man raised by a brutal father becomes explosively irritable and does the same. (c) A man who took a drug purely for cosmetic reasons, knowing irritability to be a common side effect, does the same; at the moment of the outburst he is no more in control than the man in (a). My verdicts: the tumour mitigates substantially, the upbringing barely or not at all, the drug not at all. None of the three chose to be irritable, and whatever distinguishes (a) from (b) cannot simply be that (a) is a more proximate cause, since (c) is as proximate as (a).

7. Two employees steal the same sum from the same employer, in the same way, for the same reasons. One is detected only because an unrelated clerical error triggered an audit; the other never is. My verdict: the detected one is not more blameworthy, and yet there is now something he must do that the other need not — acknowledgment, restitution, exposure — and the relationship of trust has been ruptured in a way that depends on who happens to know. I do not want to say that the undetected one is blameworthy to a lesser degree; I want to say something about what blame does.

8. A surgeon recommends a risky operation that is, on the evidence available to her, the correct recommendation; the patient dies on the table. Before the outcome I say: recommend it. After the outcome I find myself wanting to say she should have waited. I take this to be a verdict of mine that a good principle will tells me to withdraw — but I want that shown rather than asserted, and I can imagine a principle that vindicates it instead, perhaps because the agent who now knows the outcome knows something relevant, or because there is a distinct later standpoint from which the judgment is not about her conduct at all.

9. Two officials with the same character and the same financial pressures: one is offered a bribe and takes it, the other is never offered one. My verdict: the second is not more virtuous for having declined nothing, and I would not praise her. But I would praise an official who, foreseeing that she would be offered bribes, arranged her affairs so as never to be in a position to be corrupted — even if, as it happens, she never was offered one. Whatever the difference is here, it does not seem to be a difference in outcomes produced.

The principle I have been working with, which I would like tested and repaired rather than replaced wholesale, distinguishes three things: fault, fixed by quality of will and by the risks the agent knowingly and unreasonably imposed, and therefore insensitive to outcomes; debt, fixed by what actually happened, because debts run to actual people and answer to what they suffered, and therefore sensitive even to whether the harm was going to occur anyway; and the fittingness of the agent's own feelings, which is sensitive to outcomes in a way fault is not, and permissibly so. To this I have bolted a tracing clause, so that impairments the agent brought on herself do not mitigate, and a difference-making clause, so that the many-hands case comes out differently from the engineer's.

Please do the following. First, test that principle against the nine cases, repair it where it fails, and state the best version precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without asking again — marking each clause with the case that forces it, and telling me if the three tracks collapse into two, or into one, or turn out to be one distinction relabeled. Second, give its verdicts on cases I have not listed: an agent whose bad outcome was produced by a third party's independent wrongdoing; an agent whose traits were shaped by her own choices over a decade; a team or a firm rather than an individual as the subject of assessment; and self-assessment — whether I may properly feel less guilty because it turned out fine. Say whether your principle makes luck in assessment a matter of degree or a matter of different kinds of judgment, and whether any part of the problem dissolves as merely verbal. Finally, state plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, which one or two you would have me withdraw, and what would have to be true for you to be wrong about that.
