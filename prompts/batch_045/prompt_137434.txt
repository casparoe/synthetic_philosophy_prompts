I am giving the toast at my brother's wedding in three weeks, and I need help writing it, because the circumstances have quietly made an ordinary wedding toast impossible.

Two years ago my brother and his partner were married in a small ceremony abroad by a friend who had been ordained by mail. The friend's credentials are not recognized where my brother lives, so the marriage was never registered: as far as their home jurisdiction is concerned the ceremony had no legal effect at all — not a defective marriage that someone could annul, just nothing. They found out this spring, when they tried to renew their daughter's passport. Last month they had a plain civil marriage at the courthouse; I was one of the two witnesses. The ceremony in three weeks is the public one, with the tents and the aunt who plays the accordion. They have told their parents and me, and they have asked me not to announce the courthouse wedding from the head of the room. Several people there do not know, including his partner's grandmother, who was a county clerk for thirty-one years, who cares a great deal about licenses and about being told things in the right order, and who is giving a reading.

The rest of the room: a philosopher friend of mine who will catch every reference I make and will be visibly annoyed if I get one wrong; a large group of the couple's friends who regard weddings as a party with better clothes; my brother's colleagues; several children under ten. Assume they know nothing about speech act theory.

There is also a complication of my own, which is why I have been reading about this rather than just deciding. I am being married in the autumn, in a ceremony we have also asked a friend to officiate, and since my brother's trouble I have become the person in the family who checks the paperwork. So I cannot write a toast that treats the paperwork as a mere formality, and I cannot write one that says the paperwork is the marriage.

The material I have been reading is Austin's account of performative utterances: felicity conditions, uptake, and his distinction between a misfire, where the procedure is not properly executed and the act simply does not happen, and an abuse, where the procedure is gone through correctly but hollowly or insincerely, and the act does happen. I want to add a third case, and I want the toast to be built around it, though obviously not named in it. Call it suspension: a case where the utterance neither takes effect nor is simply void, but is held over, with its felicity conditions either completed or left uncompleted by what is done later. On that way of seeing it, the ceremony abroad was not a party pretending to be a wedding and not nothing either; it was an utterance waiting, and the courthouse was the ratification. The question I keep turning over is whether ratification is retroactive — whether the courthouse made the earlier "I do" true after the fact, or whether the earlier "I do" stays broken and the courthouse is simply a different, later act. I know which one my brother and his partner believe, and I know which one the grandmother would believe, and I would like the toast to take a side without arguing for it.

What makes this hard, and what I want you to respect, is that the toast is itself a speech act with felicity conditions, and mine are awkward. I have no authority to marry anyone, so I cannot say the sentence that would make something true; I can only ask the room for its uptake, and I am not sure whether a room's cheers are really uptake or just noise. I also cannot say "today you become married," which would be false in a way the clerk would hear; I cannot say "you are already married," which would tell the room something the couple asked me not to tell; and I cannot say nothing about it, which would be the loudest possible way of saying it. The speech needs to be one that is true and loyal to the couple for every person in that room regardless of what they know — a sentence whose force does not change with what the hearer has been told.

So: write me a five-minute toast, roughly seven hundred words, for a mixed room of the kind described, spoken by a brother who is a witness to the courthouse marriage and who is himself getting married in the autumn to a friend-officiated ceremony he has started to worry about. Constraints. Do not explain any theory to the room and do not name Austin or anyone else; the references should be available to my philosopher friend and invisible to everyone else. Avoid "love is what really matters" (the grandmother will hear it as an insult) and avoid "the state recognizes your love" (my philosopher friend will hear it as an insult, and so will I). No quotations from anyone, no citations. Nothing that requires knowing the courthouse story to land, and nothing that would spoil it for someone who does not. It should be warm rather than clever, and it should not end in a way that leaves my brother looking like a man who filed a form.

Give me the toast, and then, separately, the single closing line the room raises its glasses to. I care most about that line, since it is the only part that is strictly speaking supposed to do something. If you think the suspension idea cannot survive being said out loud to this room — that there is no way to hint at it without either lying or telling — say so in two or three sentences and tell me what the toast should be built around instead.
