You are a professional political philosopher who specialises in structuring adversarial collaborations: you are the person brought in when two research programmes have spent two decades sniping at each other in footnotes, and your job is to produce a single document that both sides will actually sign. Take your time. Before you draft anything, work privately through three lists — for each camp: what it believes the other camp is committed to; what it is actually committed to; and what would make it change its mind. Only then write the document. Where you find that some apparent dispute dissolves into a dispute about words, say so plainly instead of manufacturing a crux around it.

The two camps you are mediating between, on the topic of intergenerational justice and climate change:

Camp A, the Custodianship camp. It holds that the relation between generations is not one of aggregated welfare trade-offs but of custody: each generation receives a world in trust and owes the next the delivery of a certain condition, and this duty is not discharged by compensating with other goods. It is unimpressed by arguments that trade present consumption against remote future consumption at a positive pure rate of time preference, and it thinks the discounting literature quietly assumes what needs to be argued — that there is one currency in which all intergenerational claims can be settled.

Camp B, the Discounted-Welfare camp. It holds that the only coherent way to compare policies whose costs are borne now and whose benefits accrue in a century is expected welfare aggregated across the whole affected future, discounted in the standard way (a Ramsey-style decomposition into a pure rate of time preference, a growth term, and a risk term). It thinks Camp A's thresholds are unfalsifiable moral intuitions wearing the costume of constraints, and that refusing to aggregate is a refusal to face scarcity.

To stop this from collapsing into a rehearsal of the usual exchange, the collaboration has agreed in advance to route everything through three stipulations that neither camp has previously used:

(1) Climatic option space. Define this as the number of distinct, viable social trajectories — combinations of settlement patterns, agricultural systems, and political arrangements that a society could still choose between — that remain open given the climate state it inherits. Camp A proposes that what is owed across generations is the preservation of some minimum of climatic option space; Camp B proposes to price its loss like any other loss. The parties agree to treat the definition above as fixed for the purposes of the document.

(2) The Conditional Legacy Bond. A stipulated institution: the present generation issues a bond whose redemption value, paid by the generation of 2150, is high if that generation judges itself to have inherited adequate option space and low if it does not. The bond therefore makes the present generation's successors the judges of its performance, and costs the present generation nothing now. The parties must say whether this mechanism creates, merely measures, or merely incentivises whatever duty is in question, and why.

(3) Three cases both accept as evidentially relevant: the Cheap Later case, in which abatement technology becomes roughly ten times cheaper in 2060 than today, so delay is cheap in consumption terms but locks in higher cumulative concentrations; the Irreversibility case, in which a threshold is crossed beyond which a large sea-level commitment unfolds over several centuries regardless of later emissions; and the Non-Identity case, in which a climate policy changes which particular people come to exist, while everyone who does exist has a life well worth living.

Produce the joint document in the following format.

I. A short joint preamble, one paragraph, stating what the collaboration did and did not attempt.

II. A numbered section of agreed claims, eight to twelve of them, each substantive enough that a reasonable member of each camp could dissent from it. They should cover at least: whether a policy can be wrongful if it makes no one worse off; whether extinction or civilisational-collapse risk can be folded into the discount rate or must be handled separately; whether the duty runs to future persons individually or to some transgenerational entity; whether losses to climatic option space can in principle be compensated by bequests of other kinds of capital; and whether it matters for policy whether climate damages shrink the level of future output or its growth rate.

III. A disagreements table with columns: the issue; Camp A's own preferred formulation, worded so that Camp A would sign it; Camp B's, worded so that Camp B would sign it; whether the disagreement is substantive or verbal; and what would settle it.

IV. A cruxes section, three or four cruxes. For each: the sub-question; the specific case or argument both camps accept as bearing on it; Camp A's prediction about what that case or argument will show, written down now; Camp B's prediction, written down now; and what each camp would concede if its prediction fails.

V. A short appendix listing any disputes you judged to be merely verbal, with the reason.

VI. Finally, one paragraph of joint advice to a finance ministry: the recommendation both camps can sign despite their residual disagreement, with a note on why the disagreement does not matter at that level of decision.

Constraints: do not cite, quote, or attribute anything, and do not supply a bibliography — the document must stand on its own for a reader with no library and no internet. Define every technical term on first use. Keep any illustrative figures clearly flagged as illustrative rather than as findings.
