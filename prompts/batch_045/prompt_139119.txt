I'd like a map of the exposure of a position I've held for a long time, because I've started to suspect I hold it out of loyalty to a framework rather than because I can say what would dislodge it.

The position, stated carefully: whether an agent knows that p is fixed by the agent's epistemic position with respect to p — their evidence, and on my preferred version whether their credence clears whatever threshold the concept of knowledge happens to set — and is not affected by how much is riding on p for that agent, for whoever is attributing knowledge to them, or for anyone else. Stakes, conversational context, and the cost of being wrong belong downstream, in the utility function and in norms of action and assertion, not in the epistemology. Call this purist invariantism.

I came to it the way most people with my reading history do: I internalized the picture in which there is one number for how likely something is and a separate function for how much you care, and the whole point of the architecture is that the first number doesn't drift when the second one changes. I also have a second, less-cited reason. Knowledge attributions look to me like they earn their keep by being stake-free states that can be passed between agents: if my saying that I know p is indexed to my stakes, then it can't do the coordinating work that a bare, portable epistemic status does, and I'd have to replace it with something like a calibrated confidence plus a note about my payoffs — which is just decision theory again. So my working hypothesis is that stake-sensitive knowledge duplicates machinery we already have.

The cases I actually test this against are ones I've constructed rather than inherited, and they're the ones I'd like you to work with:

1. A deployed classifier outputs a calibrated confidence of 0.97 that a scan is malignant. The same model, the same output, is reviewed by the same clinician under two counterbalanced briefings: in one, a false negative costs a patient their life; in the other, it costs a ten-dollar follow-up. The clinician is willing to say the system knows in the second briefing and not the first. Nothing about the evidence, the model, or the attributor differs. Is the right description of that shift semantic, pragmatic, or merely practical?

2. The handoff case: a remote consultant in a low-stakes setting and a surgeon in a high-stakes setting have byte-identical evidence and must produce one joint decision. The consultant says they know the lesion is benign; the surgeon says nobody knows. If your theory won't give a single answer about who knows, I want to know whether that's a feature or a cost, and what it costs.

3. The variant I find hardest: hold the stakes fixed and vary only how cheap further checking is, or how many live options the agent has. My sense is that a stake-based theory gets the wrong pattern here, but a theory on which knowledge erodes when inquiry is cheap and unexercised gets something right and is more dangerous to my view than anything about stakes.

What I want from you: what would count against purist invariantism, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Please separate the defeaters that work by way of pragmatic encroachment from those that work by way of epistemic contextualism, and tell me whether those are genuinely two defeaters or one defeater in two costumes. For each, say whether a committed purist should concede it or is entitled to dismiss it. In particular, I want a straight answer about my own escape route: I'm tempted to treat apparent stake-sensitivity in ordinary knowledge talk as a pragmatic impostor — the word being used to convey actionability — and I want to know whether that dismissal is principled or whether it's the sort of move that insulates the view against any data whatsoever, and, if it does insulate it, whether that is actually a problem for a view whose subject matter is a folk concept rather than a natural kind. I'd also like to know what would most efficiently move me: my guess is that only an argument showing that stake-sensitivity does explanatory work nothing else can do — about assertion, about the normativity of inquiry, about why we have the concept at all — would do it, and I'd like you to either produce that argument or explain why it can't be produced.

Do not give me a bibliography or reading list; I want the arguments, not the sources, and I'd rather you reason from the cases than from what you remember of the literature. Attach to each of your main claims a rough confidence, and single out the one point you are least sure of — including, where relevant, whether you take yourself to be reporting how the dialectic actually stands or reconstructing it from the concepts.
