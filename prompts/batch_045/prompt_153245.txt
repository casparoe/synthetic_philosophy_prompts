I have been reading about the difference between original and derived intentionality on my own — Searle's claim that some systems mean things in their own right while words, maps and diagrams only borrow their meaning from us, and Dennett's way of pushing back, asking us to consider extremely simple devices with only a couple of internal states and denying that there is any settled fact about what, if anything, those states are about. Before I decide what I think, I want the specification rather than another defence of a side: what would an account of that distinction have to be able to do to count as adequate?

The purpose I want it to serve: I want to use it to say whether the internal states of a large language model are about anything at all, and if they are, whether that aboutness is the borrowed kind that a sentence printed on a page has, or the kind that a person has. So it has to earn its keep on a case like that, not only on brains and ink.

The cases I suspect belong on the requirements list, though I am not confident all of them do: a naturally occurring pattern that nobody made and nobody designed to mean anything, such as tree rings or a footprint on an empty beach, which still seems to carry information; a state inside a machine that reliably tracks some feature of the world which no designer specified and nobody intended, discovered only by later analysis; a chain of copying or retelling that degrades gradually, where it ought to matter where the item stops being about what it began as; and a system whose behaviour is indistinguishable from one with the right history but which came by that organisation through accident rather than design or training. For each requirement worth keeping, give the case behind it and say whether it is non-negotiable or only a preference of one camp. Where two requirements cannot both be met, say so — that is the part I most want. Then, briefly, how a biological view like Searle's, an interpretationist view like Dennett's, and a historical or teleological view do against the list, and which single requirement does the most work in ruling candidates out. If the honest result is that nothing can satisfy the list and the distinction itself is what should be given up, say that plainly.
