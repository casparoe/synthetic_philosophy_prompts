I'm a philosophy PhD student working on late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century American philosophy, and I've been asked to run a graduate reading group next term on the pragmatist theories of truth. I wanted to open with something the non-specialists could follow, so I picked up a recent popular treatment of pragmatism. Its chapter on truth goes roughly like this:

The American pragmatists gave up the idea that a belief is true when it corresponds to reality, since we can never step outside our beliefs to compare them with the world. So they redefined truth in terms of usefulness. Peirce's version: the meaning of a claim is just the sum of the practical consequences of accepting it, and truth is simply whatever the community of inquirers ends up accepting at the end of investigation — he later renamed the doctrine pragmaticism mostly because he wanted a name ugly and precise enough to belong to him alone. James made the same point less technically: an idea is true just in case believing it is useful, truth is something that happens to an idea when it proves its cash value in experience, and that is why he thought religious belief could count as true if it makes a person's life go better. Bertrand Russell's objection settled the matter: a false belief — that your partner is faithful, that you will recover from an illness — is often far more useful than the true one, so any equation of truth with usefulness is refuted. Both men, then, made truth a matter of what works for us rather than of how things are.

I'm fairly confident the usefulness formulation is a strawman of James, and I had assumed the material on Peirce was basically sound, but I don't trust myself to say where. Please go through it claim by claim, separating what a sympathetic Peircean or Jamesian would accept, what they'd call a standard simplification, and what the view explicitly denies — and tell me whether the chapter's closing verdict survives once the misreadings are corrected.

The part I care about most, though, is this. The chapter presents the disagreement between Peirce and James as turning on whether the ideal limit of inquiry is ever actually reached, and that doesn't strike me as the real fault line. Consider claims inquiry could never converge on even in principle because whatever would settle them is gone: how many people were in a particular room in Alexandria in 415 CE, or what the sentences of a language with no surviving corpus meant. Both thinkers are fallibilists who reject crude verificationism, yet both tie truth to the practice of inquiry. On the corrected reading, which of the two has the harder time with permanently unsettledable claims of that kind, and is that a better axis for the contrast than the one the chapter offers?

For each verdict, say how confident you are in it, and single out the point you are least sure of.
