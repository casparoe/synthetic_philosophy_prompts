"Williams's integrity objection is the objection that utilitarianism is incompatible with integrity, and integrity here means roughly authenticity: acting from your own deepest commitments rather than from an impersonal calculation of what would be best overall. The mechanism is negative responsibility. Utilitarianism holds you responsible for everything you fail to prevent, so no undertaking of your own is ever safe: at any moment you may be required to abandon it in order to produce some marginally better outcome for other people, and in this way the theory alienates you from the very commitments that make you the person you are. The famous 'one thought too many' example makes the same point in miniature. A man can rescue only one of two people, and one of them is his wife. If he has to think 'saving her will maximize utility' before he can act, he is having one thought too many: the thought 'she is my wife' should already be enough to move him, and the fact that he needs something further shows that utilitarianism has removed him from his own life and turned him into a mere channel through which value is produced. So the argument refutes utilitarianism. Any view that makes impartial maximizing the test of right action will, if it is really believed and acted on, destroy the integrity of the person who believes it, and no moral theory can be acceptable if following it makes it impossible to remain the person you are."
