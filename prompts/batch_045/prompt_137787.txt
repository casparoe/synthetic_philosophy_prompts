Please reply as a professional moral philosopher who works on animal ethics and, more generally, on the question of what makes something a being that can be wronged — someone equally fluent in the equal-consideration-of-interests tradition, in the argument from marginal cases, and in relational, contractarian and capability-style accounts that deny sentience is the whole story. I'd like you to do real work here rather than return the first respectable-sounding thing: begin by brainstorming six to eight candidate principles, spend a sentence or two each on why most of them fail, and only then defend the two or three that survive. Where you think one of my verdicts is simply mistaken, say so plainly and tell me what giving it up costs me. No citations or reading list, please — I'll track down the literature myself.

Some background on where I'm coming from. I'm auditing a graduate seminar on moral status, I grew up around dairy and beef cattle, and I keep arguing with two people: my roommate, who holds that equal consideration of comparable interests settles everything and that whatever is left of my uneasiness is sentimental residue, and my uncle, a hunter, who holds that the only real duty is not to make an animal suffer and not to waste it, and that killing as such is never the problem. Neither seems to leave room for what I actually feel. So let me just report the verdicts and the comparisons that bother me. A pig raised well and killed painlessly at eighteen months: eating him is wrong, though far less bad than the same pig raised in a crate. My aunt's layer hen, good life, killed at three years when she stops laying: also wrong, and somehow a bit more wrong to me than the anonymous pig's death, even though everything I've been told says hens are cognitively simpler than pigs. A dog killed for meat: far worse than the pig, and I can't appeal to intelligence, since pigs outperform dogs on a lot of measures. A horse slaughtered for meat: much worse than a cow raised and killed in exactly the same way, and here I can't point to any capacity difference at all — it seems to be about what horses have meant to us and how we have lived with them, which is either a real moral factor or pure prejudice. Oysters: nothing at all, and still nothing if you tell me a thousand are being shucked, and still nothing when someone shows me ganglia under a microscope. A crab boiled alive: clearly wrong; the crab/oyster line is my sharpest threshold. Shrimp in a stir-fry: I do it, but uneasily, and I also think farming and boiling a million shrimp is much worse than boiling one — so aggregation works inside the probably-sentient group — while a million oysters still registers as nothing, which looks inconsistent to me. A house mouse in my pantry: killing it is permissible for the sake of a trivial interest of mine, though I dislike doing it; but killing the pig for bacon, an equally trivial interest of mine, is not permissible, and that pair is the one I keep failing to reconcile. Two hundred mice bred to develop a painful degenerative condition, in a research program very likely to save thousands of human children: permissible, maybe obligatory — yet each mouse suffers vastly more than my aunt's hen ever did, and I said the hen's painless death was wrong, so suffering seems to count for less than painless death in one verdict and more in the other. Ten thousand termites destroyed in my wall: no qualm at all, while the single mouse makes me hesitate, so number isn't doing the work I assumed. And a human in a permanent vegetative state — no cortex, no possibility of any experience, sustained by a machine — killed for organs: much worse than killing the pig or the hen, and the family's wishes clearly matter to me; but if death is bad because it cuts off a subject's future experience, there is nothing here to cut off, so something else is doing the work and I can't tell whether it is species membership, the relational and symbolic residue a human body carries, or duties to third parties.

Two more, which are invented rather than real, because I want the principle tested on things nobody has an entrenched opinion about. First, the "N-line" broiler: a commercial chicken line whose nociception has been engineered out, so that the birds show no distress at injury, withdraw normally, eat, dust-bathe, learn about places and food, and are in every other respect ordinary chickens — they simply cannot suffer. My roommate says eating them is obviously fine. I still think it is wrong to kill and eat them, though much less bad than eating an ordinary chicken, and I genuinely cannot tell whether that residue is a moral judgment or squeamishness about killing anything that walks around. Second, a case I have no verdict about at all: a colonial marine organism, the sort of thing that might be a million shrimp-grade units fused into one slowly integrating colony with a single twenty-year career of learning currents and feeding grounds — is destroying it one serious wrong or a million trivial ones, and should my principle even have to answer that?

Here is the tentative principle I've been toying with, which I'd like you to test and repair rather than just endorse. A creature can be wronged only if it has a point of view that can go better or worse for it. Below that threshold — my oysters, maybe my termites — moral status is zero and no amount of aggregation helps. Above it, the weight of a wrong is set by three things: how much the creature's death cuts off, which seems to require something like a psychologically extended self; what relation I already stand in to it, where dependence, cohabitation and being cared for by someone add weight; and a third factor I can't shake, how closely the creature resembles a paradigm person, which I suspect is speciesism in a lab coat. My own principle also can't obviously explain why the pantry mouse may be killed while the bacon pig may not, or why suffering counts for more than painless death with the mice and less with the hen.

So the deliverable: about 1,200 to 1,500 words with short headings, containing (1) two or three candidate principles — including a repaired version of mine — each stated precisely enough that a third party could apply it to a new case, with every clause tagged to the case that motivates it; (2) an honest table, in prose, of where each candidate collides with my verdicts and which verdicts I would have to abandon; (3) verdicts on cases I haven't listed, at least these: the N-line bird, the colonial organism, an uplifted pig with the cognitive profile of a four-year-old human child, a silicon-based system that matches a pig on every behavioural and functional test of sentience we know how to run, a wolf killing a fawn and a deer I could easily pull out of a frozen pond, and the discovery that oysters are neurally much more crab-like than anyone thought — plus one case of your own that you think discriminates best between the survivors; (4) a direct answer to whether any non-arbitrary version of speciesism comes out the other end, or whether speciesism is simply the wrong name for whatever is left; and (5) which single empirical discovery would move you most, and why.
