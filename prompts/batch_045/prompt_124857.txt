I want a map of the exposure of a position I rely on. Not a defense of it, and not a restatement of it in better words: what would have to be true for it to be false, and what would have to be true for it to be useless.

The position, and the argument it borrows from. In its home setting the argument runs like this. Take a two-sided, finite, perfect-information game that is symmetric between the players and admits no draw. Suppose the second player had a winning strategy. The first player then makes one arbitrary opening move and thereafter plays the second player's strategy against the second player, carrying the extra opening move around like an inert token. If an extra move can never hurt — if the game is monotone, in the way a board-filling game is, where an additional stone of your own colour on the board is never a liability — then the first player wins too, contradicting the supposition. So the second player has no winning strategy, and since draws are impossible, the first player wins. The conclusion is non-constructive: it says a winning first move exists, never which one. Call the generalised version of this the strategy-stealing assumption.

What I claim. I claim that the conclusion transfers with the structure. In any two-sided competitive setting that is symmetric, draw-free, and monotone in the sense that an extra action can be carried at no cost — call this *surplus-inertness*: having an additional action is never a liability — the side that moves first can in principle guarantee that it does at least a hair better than the other. The corollary I actually use is stronger-sounding: in such settings a persistent advantage on the follower's side is never explained by position; it must be a deficit of play. I lean on that corollary when I argue that a follower's lead in a capability race between two roughly symmetric actors cannot be structural, and I lean on the main claim when I argue that the initiative is not one advantage among others but the structural one.

What I want from you.

One. Take the conditions apart. Which of symmetry, draw-freeness, and surplus-inertness is load-bearing in the sense that its failure is fatal rather than merely awkward? Do the same for the conditions the argument needs that I have not listed: that a strategy specified for one seat is executable from the other, call it *role-symmetric simulability*; that the setting has determinacy at all, so that one side or the other has a winning strategy; and that the setting is genuinely two-sided rather than a many-sided setting with two salient participants. For each, sketch the smallest setting you can in which it fails, and say what the argument then delivers — does the conclusion degrade continuously, or does it collapse outright?

Two. The failure modes I want taken most seriously are the ones that do not look like failures. Three in particular. A surplus action that is materially harmless but informationally harmful: an extra move that changes what the other side can infer, so that the stolen strategy is now being run in an information environment it was never calibrated to. A setting with parity or counter effects, where inserting an extra move shifts every subsequent move number and breaks the simulation even though nothing material has changed. And a setting where the strategy to be stolen is a fitted response to the first mover's actual play rather than a strategy that wins against anything, so that stealing it is a fixed-point problem rather than a copy operation. For each, say whether it defeats the main claim, only the corollary, or neither, and whether a defender has a repair available.

Three. Build me the strongest single counterexample setting you can: two-sided, symmetric, draw-free, with an extra action available to the first mover, and in which the second mover nonetheless wins by structure rather than by the first mover's blunders. If no such setting is possible, say why — and say what that shows about whether my claim is an empirical claim or an analytic one.

Four. The corollary has an escape hatch I have become suspicious of: any observed follower advantage can be absorbed as a deficit of play. Is there any observable difference between the surplus was not really inert and the first mover played badly? If not, has the corollary been qualified into something that cannot explain anything? Alongside this, say what my position predicts that a follower-advantage or positional-luck rival does not, and whether that prediction is testable even in principle.

Five. Rank everything from fatal to merely awkward, and mark for each item whether a committed defender of my position would concede it as counting against them or would treat it as a misdescription of the view. I want the concessions, not the polemics.

Six. My own sense of where the weight falls: I find the informational and parity failure modes the most threatening, and I am unmoved by objections from bounded rationality, since my claim is explicitly in-principle and concerns the existence of a strategy rather than anyone's ability to find or execute it. Tell me whether that immunity claim is itself defensible, or whether the in-principle qualification is precisely what renders the position unfalsifiable — and whether, for a claim I want to use to argue that some actually observed advantage is not structural, that is a problem or merely a limitation.

Argue from the structure of the argument as I have set it out. I do not want citations, bibliographies, or reading pointers, and no appeal to who proved what about which board game; the prompt should be answerable by someone who has never encountered the argument before and has no access to a library.
