ive been going back and forth with a guy on a forum i read (neither of us has any philosophy or math training, we're just nerds who read about this stuff for fun) about the doomsday argument, and i want to get clear on the picture he keeps leaning on.

His argument goes: treat yourself as a random draw from an urn containing every human who will ever live. Call it a hundred billion of us so far, by the usual estimates. If humanity ends fairly soon and the total ends up around 200 billion, then finding yourself at around rank 100 billion is completely unsurprising — you'd expect to be somewhere in the bulk of the balls. If instead we spread out beyond Earth and the total is in the trillions, then being at rank 100 billion is freakishly early, like pulling ball 100 out of an urn with a trillion balls in it. So your birth rank is evidence against the big-urn hypothesis, and he says the numbers come out such that we're probably in the last stretch of the human story rather than the beginning.

He's using this to make actual decisions — he moved his giving away from long-term-future causes and toward nearer-term stuff, and he's been saying he's not sure he wants to have kids. I definately feel like the urn story is doing a lot of work, but I can't put my finger on what exactly its assuming.

Can you audit it properly? I want the correspondences laid out explicitly — urn = what, balls = what, the draw = what, the ordering/labelling = what, the two competing urn sizes = what — then where the mapping breaks, and then (the part I actually care about) which of those breaks matter for his conclusion and which are just nitpicks.

Two things I want squeezed specifically:

1. He also uses the same "I'm a random sample" logic somewhere else and I think it fights itself. He argues that because we've been around a couple hundred thousand years without anything wiping us out, and because no nuclear war has happened in the ~80 years we've had the bombs, catastrophes must be rarer than our guesses suggest — the idea being that observers only ever find themselves in the branches where nothing has killed them yet. That pushes toward "we'll probably last ages." Is that the same urn or a different one? Can one person consistently use both moves, or does the second one quietly cancel the first?

2. What's in the urn seems totally up for grabs. "All humans" gives one answer; "all beings with the kind of evidence I have" gives another; "everyone who will ever think about the doomsday argument" gives an obviously silly one, since that club is clearly growing and not nearly over. What's supposed to fix the contents of the urn, and does any of the ways of fixing it actually survive?

And finally: is there a better analogy than the urn, or is the urn fine and the problem is somewhere else in the reasoning?
