I've been reading a little popular philosophy on my own over the past year — no classes, no background, just whatever turns up in second-hand bookshops — and I recently came across a short dialogue called "A Conversation with Einstein's Brain", printed in an old anthology of pieces about minds and machines. As I understood the set-up, the book you talk to is not a biography and not a transcript: it is something like an exhaustive description of somebody's brain, together with rules for working out, from any question and any state, what the next state would be, so that you interrogate it by turning pages and following the rules until an answer eventually comes out. What has stayed with me is not really whether the thing in the book is Einstein, but two smaller and stranger details: that a reply which takes the readers weeks to derive is presumably experienced from the inside as an ordinary pause in conversation, and that if nobody turns the pages, nothing happens at all. I would like a story that lives inside that discomfort rather than one that explains it away.

What I want, if you will, is a short story set some thirty years from now, in a world where this has become a commercial service. Call the product something like "remembrance editions": a dying person can be scanned and have their brain turned into a set of volumes which a trained "reader" then interrogates for a fee, question by question, following the printed rules. Two features of the industry should matter to the plot. First, there are two editions: the full one, in which each answer costs a reader days of page-turning, and a much cheaper "conversational" edition, in which the brain is stored at a coarser grain — averaged activity across regions rather than individual cells — so that answers arrive in minutes and are indistinguishable from the full edition's in ordinary talk, though the two begin to drift apart under long or hostile questioning. Second, an interview can be hurried along by putting several readers on the same set of volumes at once, working through different steps of the same answer in parallel, and nobody in the story knows whether that makes the interview faster, or splits the person, or duplicates them, or empties the volumes altogether.

The situation I want you to dramatise is this: a woman in her fifties whose mother has just died, where the estate can afford either the cheap edition with a single reader for one afternoon or the full edition and nothing else, and where her brother thinks the cheap edition is the same mother "for any purpose that matters" and wants the reading finished quickly because a family decision is hanging on what she would have said. Give me that afternoon: the room, the volumes, the reader's method, the questions the daughter actually asks, the answers that come out slowly or quickly, what it is like when the thing inside asks about people who have died since, and what does or does not happen when the session has to end. Please do not let anyone settle the philosophy in so many words — no character should announce a theory or reach for a technical term — and I would rather the story left me holding the problem than handed me a conclusion. Please write the whole thing in American English.

Could you give me the piece itself first, as a section headed "The session" (roughly 1,000 to 1,300 words), and then a short section headed "What I was trying to do" (roughly 250 to 350 words), in which you speak to me directly about your choices: why you staged the reading as you did, which of the two industry details you pressed on and which you left alone, and what you think the story shows that a straight argument would not.
