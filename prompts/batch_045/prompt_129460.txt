Hey — picture this. You're advising a small but serious foundation that has maybe $40m to spend over five years on one thing: making the people who hold legislative power in a mid-sized parliamentary democracy less like a professional political class. They've decided up front to spend only on institutional design — the rules about who gets to sit in the legislature and for how long — not campaign finance, not media, not civic education. And they'd rather fund eight or ten people thinking hard for three years than run an advocacy campaign, because they think the advocacy is premature until the design questions are less murky. The boards patience is limited, so they want an agenda, not a survey.

Here's the menu they keep circling around:

1. Sortition: replace one chamber with a randomly selected assembly, redrawn regularly.
2. Hard term limits: two or three terms and you're out.
3. A third thing nobody has a settled name for, so call it lottery retirement: at each election a randomly chosen fifth of sitting members are barred from standing again. Being a career politician stays possible, but it becomes a gamble you can lose through no fault or failure of your own.
4. Cohort overlap: only half the sortitioned chamber is replaced each cycle, so there's some institutional memory — at the cost of some freshness.
5. Sortition with a secretariat behind it, where continuity comes from permanent expert staff who brief each new cohort, frame the options and draft the text. Depending on your view that's institutional wisdom without careerism, or careerism relocated to people nobody voted for and nobody sortitioned.

Assume, just for the sake of argument, something that seems roughly true of citizen assemblies where they've been tried: they do noticeably better than elected bodies on long-horizon stuff like pensions and climate, and noticeably worse at anything requiring them to say no to a loud, organized group right now — and members defer a lot to whoever briefs them.

So: what are the open questions here that actually deserve the money, in what order, and why? I care much more about the reasoning than the list. For each question tell me what else depends on it — which ones are upstream, e.g. do we need to settle what representation even is before we can say whether a random sample represents anybody? — how tractable it is for political theorists, economists, mechanism-design types and people who just think carefully about institutions, as opposed to empirical political scientists running field experiments, and what kind of finding over the next two years would move it up or down the ranking.

Two things I'd especially like your read on. First: which of the usual claims made for these reforms don't actually follow? The one I keep hearing is that random selection "fixes polarization" because you can't gerrymander a lottery, and I'm not convinced that's even the right mechanism to be looking at. Second: is the secretariat problem the crux that most of the menu implicitly depends on — the thing that decides whether sortition is worth pushing at all — or is it a distraction that only bites in an idealised version of sortition nobody is actually proposing? Relatedly, where does the expertise go under hard term limits (lobbyists, staff, agencies), and does that worry transfer to the lottery versions or not?

If you think the menu is missing something big, say what and why it should outrank the rest.
