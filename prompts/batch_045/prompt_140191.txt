I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a long feature about the Church–Turing thesis — the claim, roughly nine decades on from the 1936 papers, that everything we would count as an effective procedure is captured by Turing machines. One section of the piece will be a counterfactual, and I'd like your help with it: what would Kurt Gödel have made of today's machine-learning systems, had he lived to see them? Please respond as a historian of logic and philosopher of computation who also reads the modern technical literature — in particular the literature on non-uniform models of computation and on the behaviour of large networks trained by gradient descent. Before you write anything, do a short round of brainstorming: set out four or five genuinely distinct considerations that would have to enter a responsible reconstruction of a dead thinker's view on a question he never faced, then keep only the ones that survive your own scrutiny. Two house rules, please: no bibliography, footnotes, or page references (I have a research assistant for that, and I don't want you guessing at them), and no quotation marks around anything from the historical record — characterise what he argued in your own paraphrase, since neither of us should trust half-remembered wording.

Here is the material I want you to work with. First, the machines themselves: systems that produce plausible proof sketches in ordinary mathematical prose, formal provers and search systems that find constructions no human would have tried, and the fact that all of this is achieved by finite-parameter, finite-context, heuristic devices that are unreliable in ways humans are not — they are not sound proof procedures in any sense a logician would recognise. Second, two objections raised by people I've interviewed. A computer scientist put it this way: the thesis is close to vacuous as applied to any single trained network, because a fixed finite artifact is trivially simulable by a lookup table; the only interesting question is about a family of models and inference-time budgets indexed by the size of the input — a scale-relativised question — and once you reframe it that way, the thesis stops being a claim about machines and becomes a claim about a certain idealisation of them. A philosopher of mind added that Turing's route from the thesis to mechanism quietly assumes the mind can occupy only finitely many distinguishable states, whereas a mind that can take on ever-new concepts has no fixed finite state space; so the very idealisation that makes the thesis elegant is the one that makes it inapplicable to minds. I've been told that this is close to a complaint Gödel himself lodged against Turing in some late, posthumously published notes, and I'd like to know whether that attribution is secure enough to print — and, more importantly, whether the argument itself stands or falls with it. Third, two things Gödel did say that I think pull in opposite directions: the disjunction he sketched in his lecture of the early 1950s, to the effect that either the human mind outruns every finite machine or there are absolutely undecidable number-theoretic questions; and the 1956 letter to von Neumann in which he worried about how many steps it takes to decide whether a formula of a given length is valid, and observed that if the number grew only quadratically the mathematician's work could in principle be replaced by a machine. He was worrying about feasibility, not computability, well before complexity theory was codified — so would he have thought the Church–Turing thesis is simply the wrong object of reverence, and that the gap between the computable and the feasible is where the real action is? And one more scenario: suppose a physical network whose connection weights were arbitrary real numbers with unlimited precision were able to encode an oracle for some non-computable function. Gödel the Platonist about mathematical objects — would he have counted that as a genuine failure of the thesis, or as a cheat? Note that we could probably never tell empirically whether such a system was doing anything more than generating noise.

What I need is roughly 700 to 900 words of prose I could read aloud to my editor: a reconstruction of Gödel's likely verdict on whether contemporary AI bears on the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis and on the mechanist reading of his own incompleteness results, anchored in what he actually argued and explicit about the point where reconstruction turns into extrapolation. Then, in a short closing note, I want your calibration: which link in the chain rests on the thinnest textual evidence; what the strongest objection from a sceptical Gödel scholar would be, stated better than I could state it myself; and what would actually change your mind — which passage in the record, or which argument you haven't yet taken seriously, would lead you to a different reconstruction, and where your reasoning here is most likely to fail.
