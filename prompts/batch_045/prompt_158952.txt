I teach a graduate seminar on the intentional stance, and this term our reading group has included two colleagues from the machine learning group who keep returning to the same move. Whenever an interpretability paper says that the system believes that Paris is in France, or that it holds a false belief about its own circumstances, or that it is being deceptive, they say the attribution has gone to the wrong subject: the model is like an actor in a play, they say, and it is the character, not the actor, who believes that Paris is in France. The stronger version of the picture, which one of them prefers, is the séance: the model is like a medium who produces the utterances a departed person would produce and who need not know, in any sense, what is coming out of her mouth. Either way the analogy is being made to carry two conclusions: first, that the interpretability literature's ascriptions are category errors, so that talk of model beliefs, deception, or self-awareness should be reformulated as talk about a simulated persona; and second, that there is no further fact of the matter about what the system itself believes, so nothing of moral or epistemic importance turns on whether we adopt the stance toward the system rather than toward the character. A third member of the group says all of this is just Dennett's chess-playing computer again, that the intentional stance was always only a predictive strategy, and that the question of who really believes has no answer to be had. I am not satisfied with any of this, and before I commit myself in front of the seminar I would like the analogy audited properly.

What I want is the correspondence made explicit — who or what plays the role of the actor, the script, the character, the director, the audience, the improvisation — and then the places the mapping fails, with the failures that matter for those two conclusions separated from the ones that do not. I have four architectural facts I keep getting stuck on, and I would like each placed in the mapping. First: in the systems my colleagues actually work with, the character is not read off a script at inference time. It is specified in a system prompt during training and then trained into the weights, so that the persona persists when the prompt is removed and cannot be separated from the mechanism that produces the lines. There is, as far as I can tell, no actor left over once the character is subtracted. Second: the line that actually comes out is often selected, not merely generated — one component produces several candidate continuations and another, trained to predict which one human raters would have preferred, chooses among them. The audience, on this picture, is inside the production; if the analogy has a role for the audience, I want to know whether that makes belief attribution better off or worse off. Third: a colleague has built an ensemble in which the emitted text is an aggregate of several sub-networks that, run on their own, each produce a coherent continuation and disagree with one another about whether P, while a classifier trained on the internal activations of the aggregate predicts which way it will go. Is there one character here or several, and does the analogy entail that there is no fact of the matter, or that there are several facts? Fourth: the deployed systems keep a persistent memory across sessions, and the persona's earlier assertions are stored and then used as premises in later reasoning, so that what the character once said functions, within the system's own operation, as something it treats as settled. Does that give the character the kind of standpoint the analogy needs, or is it merely more lines?

I would be grateful for your own verdict, stated without retreating into a survey of what various parties might say. Does the actor analogy — or the séance variant, which I suspect is the more honest statement of what my colleagues actually think — survive these cases, and can it carry either of the conclusions it is used for? If it can carry one and not the other, say which, and why. If you think it fails, I would like a better analogy: my own candidate is that the system is like an improv ensemble of very many players, each of whom has read nearly everything ever written, with the next line a weighted compromise among what each of them would say, and I want to know whether that does better or merely relocates the difficulty — in particular whether it can make sense of the internal-activation evidence, which in the human case is roughly what distinguishes attributing a belief to an actor from attributing it to her character, and which in the model case has no obvious analogue. Please also mark explicitly which of your claims you hold with confidence and which you would abandon under pressure, and say what would count as pressure.
