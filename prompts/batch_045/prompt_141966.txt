I am writing a short paper, for a graduate seminar on ancient moral psychology, about the defense of supervised drinking parties that Plato puts in the mouth of the Athenian stranger in the Laws, and I have got stuck on a question about the architecture of the argument rather than about what it concludes. As I read the text, the defence proceeds on several apparently independent grounds at once: that well-run symposia give the lawgiver a cheap and comparatively safe way of discovering what his citizens are really like; that controlled exposure to the boldness and pleasure that wine produces is a form of training, the way one might train against fear — there is, as I remember it, a thought experiment about a drug that would induce terror so that citizens could be exercised against it, offered as the counterpart to what wine in fact does; that wine restores to the elderly something of the pliancy of youth, so that men who have gone hard and austere with age can be softened and made teachable again through song and dance; and, more quietly, that the institution lets Plato site his city between the total abstinence he associates with Carthaginians and Scythians and the licence he associates with Persians and Syracusans, while implicitly correcting Spartan and Cretan austerity. My supervisor adds a fifth strand that I am inclined to underrate: that wine deliberately takes ordinary self-command offline in a controlled setting, so that the only constraint still operating on the drinker is shame before others, which is exactly the motivation the city wants its citizens to run on.

What I would like from you is not a summary but a ranking of these five considerations by how load-bearing they are: give each one a score from 0 to 100 for how much of Plato's case for the institution would be left if that strand alone were removed or shown to be false, order them from most to least load-bearing, and give me one sentence of justification per item. For each item I would also like a short note on whether it helps explain a feature of the institution that any account has to explain: Plato's insistence that a drinking party be presided over by a sober, older and respectable symposiarch, with the young and the disorderly excluded or closely supervised. If a strand scores high on load-bearingness but leaves that requirement unmotivated, say so, because I take that to be a genuine mark against it. Finally, tell me which single strand you would call the keystone, in the sense that the others make most sense as elaborations of it rather than as independent supports — and if you think my five are really three considerations in different clothing, or that I have split one into two, say that instead.

The reason I am asking in these terms is that a visiting historian of Greek commensality in our seminar has urged us to stop describing these parties as education at all. On her reading the point is retrospective verification rather than formation or repair: the city already knows roughly what sort of person each citizen is, and the symposium is an instrument for auditing that judgement, so she wants us to call the practice the Dionysian audit. Score her audit reading from 0 to 100 as an interpretation of what Plato is up to, tell me which of the five strands it overweights and which of them it cannot accommodate without strain, and say whether adopting it would change the ranking you gave above. Please reason from the argument as I have sketched it and from Plato's psychology generally; I have the text in front of me and do not need citations, page references or suggestions for further reading.
