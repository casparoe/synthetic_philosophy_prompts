hey — we're doing aristotle's sea-battle example in my reading group on thursday (the one about whether its already true, tonight, that there will be a battle tomorrow, and whether that means the admiral's war council is kidding itself when it sits down to deliberate). i'd like to close the session with a poem instead of another half hour of argument, and i have a particular shape in mind.

this bit isn't from any text, i made it up, so don't try to match a source: the night before, the admiral writes two sealed orders and hands them to a clerk. one orders the fleet to engage at dawn, the other orders it not to. at dawn the clerk opens whichever one matches what happens. i want the poem to sit inside the question of what the status of the unopened order is, right now, while its still sealed.

and i definately don't want it to just go ooh, nobody knows yet, mystery of time. people have taken this in roughly three ways, and i want the poem to be working with one of them, consistently, and i want someone who knows the literature to be able to tell which one from how the poem handles the sealed order — without you ever naming the view:

- neither order is the true one tonight; there just isnt a fact of the matter yet, the future is genuinely open
- both have a truth value tonight, one true and one false, but the two branches are on a par — nothing tonight marks one of them as the real one, it's just whichever one in fact happens
- one of them is already the real one tonight; there's a single privileged future running ahead of us, we just can't read which it is

form: i want it as a pair. two versions of the same poem, in the clerk's voice (or moving between the clerk and the council), same length, same line breaks, same rhyme scheme — rhymed, please, i want it readable aloud — set out as if they were the two orders. they should differ in as few words as possible: one word a stanza would be great, and if you can get the whole difference down to a single word, better. that constraint is the point of the thing: the difference between the two tomorrows shouldn't be readable off the present, so neither version should give itself away. no breaking the seal at the end, no and then it happened.

if you can also get in the moment where the clerk realises that whatever is true tonight was already true last week, and the week before that, without it sliding into so it was all fated anyway — that's the line i'll remember. the council should be doing something that only makes sense if the outcome isn't settled (reading the wind, arguing about the shoals), and the poem should make that feel like it matters rather than like play-acting.

keep the whole thing under about 40 lines, twenty-ish a side, or less if less works. and go easy on the archaic pronouns — last time someone handed in a ballad full of thy oars and we all lost it.
