I should say at the outset who I am, since it bears on what I am asking for. I spent a career in solid-state chemistry, and for the last two years I have been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group. I do not want a survey of the literature or a list of names. I want a diagnosis of an argument that has outlasted my patience with it: which step is doing the illegitimate work, and what it costs to give that step up.

The argument is meant to establish that no scientific classification — the periodic table included — carves nature at its joints. I do not accept the conclusion, and I am confident I need not; but every time I locate the fault at one step, the argument seems to reassemble itself one step further along.

(1) A predicate marks a natural kind only if there are true generalizations holding of all and only its instances which are non-accidental and projectible — the sort of generalization one is prepared to call a law, or at any rate lawlike. Fit with the observed cases alone will not do: a generalization may hold of everything examined so far and still be an accident.

(2) Take any classification C of a domain, supported by whatever finite body of evidence you like. One can always construct a bent predicate: one which agrees with C on every case examined so far, and therefore accommodates the evidence exactly as well as C does, but which sorts the unexamined cases differently. The manoeuvre is the familiar one — build the divergence into the predicate by indexing it to something outside the evidence, a date, a place, a circumstance of examination.

(3) There is nothing to a generalization's being non-accidental and projectible over and above its accommodating the evidence and remaining serviceable in inference. Talk of what nature has singled out is not a report of some further fact available to us; it is a way of registering the success of the scheme in which the predicate does its work.

(4) So the bent predicate satisfies (1) as well as C does. And because it and C sort the unexamined cases differently, they are rival classifications of the same domain, each as joint-carving as the other.

(5) A joint, though, is where the thing itself divides. If two classifications divide a domain along different lines and neither has been shown to track a division the other misses, then neither has been shown to carve anything at all: we have two convenient catalogues and no verdict about which cuts deeper.

(6) Since (2) holds for any classification whatever, no classification carves nature at its joints. The chemical elements are a classification. So the chemical elements do not carve nature at its joints — and, by the same reasoning, neither does any other scheme the sciences have arrived at.

I cannot accept (6), and my reasons are not sentimental. When the ordering of the elements by atomic weight gave way to an ordering by atomic number, the change was not a change of convention: the older scheme put a few elements in the wrong order, and the new one put them right, and everyone in the field took this as a correction of an error rather than as a redecision. Again, the trade term covering the material we call jade turned out to comprise two minerals of quite different composition and crystal structure — a pyroxene at one end and an amphibole at the other — and that was a discovery about the world, not a stipulation about words. Contrast the plagioclase feldspars, which form a continuous series in composition between albite and anorthite, and which mineralogists divide into named intervals by convention, because there is nothing there to divide. My training tells me that the first two cases and the third are different in kind, and the argument above says they are not.

Of the premises, I should most like to keep (1) and the judgment I have just recorded; (2) strikes me as simply true, or true for any evidence we could actually possess. So the fault, if there is one, must lie in (3), (4) or (5), or in some equivocation I have not noticed in the phrases as well as and rival classification.

I should also report an attempt of my own at a repair, because I suspect it is either circular or delivers less than I want, and I would rather be told so plainly. The test I proposed to the group was this: a kind is natural to the degree that classifications drawn up independently, for unrelated practical purposes, and by inquirers working under different circumstances, coincide at its boundaries. The idea is that a bent predicate must get its grip from an index to some local circumstance, and that inquirers who differ in that circumstance will disagree; coinciding classificatory schemes, arrived at from different directions, would then be evidence that nothing local is doing the work. But two worries strike me. First, the test seems to presuppose precisely the distinction between real and conventional divisions that it was meant to establish — I say the inquirers coincided, but I can only recognise their coincidence as significant if I already assume that convergence tracks something. Second, and worse: an index need not be local. It could be shared by every inquirer we can ever compare notes with — a condition on the whole of our evidence, or a region no observation of ours reaches. Such a predicate would survive my test while being as bent as any in (2), and then all my test delivers is joint-carving-at-our-address, which is a weaker thing than I set out to defend and possibly no better than the convenient catalogue I was trying to escape.

There is a further pressure on (5) that I cannot adjudicate. The classification of matter by nuclide cross-cuts the classification by element, and neither looks like a mere convenience: they seem to me to be two real divisions of the same stuff, made for different purposes and at different scales. If that is right, then a domain can have joints in more than one direction, and (5) is simply false as stated. But if (5) is false, I do not see what work carving at the joints is doing any more, and the sceptic gets his conclusion by another route.

So, three questions. Which step do you reject? What does rejecting it commit me to, and is that commitment one a person in my position should find expensive? And is my convergence test a repair of the argument's fault, or only a restatement of my prejudices in more words? I would be grateful for a verdict on each, and for the reasoning that connects them.
