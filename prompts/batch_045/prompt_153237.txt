I'm a journalist working on a piece about misinformation, and a philosopher I interviewed sent this over as her "safe to print" summary of the testimony debate:

Reductionism says that when you believe something on someone's say-so, that belief is justified only if you independently have reasons of your own — your perception, your memory, your inferences — for thinking this speaker is reliable and sincere on this occasion. Anti-reductionism says that isn't how testimony works at all: it's a basic source of justification alongside perception and memory, so a hearer needs no reasons, and a speaker's word is enough until something specific counts against it. Basically, reductionists think the default should be doubt and anti-reductionists think it should be trust. And the anti-reductionists win going away: on the reductionist picture, any reason you had for trusting a speaker would itself have to come from perception, memory or inference, so you'd have to personally verify every claim you're ever told, which is impossible and which nobody actually does — so reductionism can't explain the enormous amount we learn from other people.
