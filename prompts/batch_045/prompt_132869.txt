Hey — I'm TAing an intro epistemology course and we just did the bootstrapping problem, the thing where you seem to be able to find out that a source is reliable just by consulting it repeatedly (the fuel gauge you read over and over, noting each time that it agrees with itself, so concluding that it's accurate), and everyone in the section agreed the reasoning feels like cheating. But then someone in my study group said the objection proves too much, because there are cases where learning about a source from its own output seems obviously fine: if a witness tells you a long story and it's full of internal contradictions, you've clearly learned something about their reliability without checking any single claim against reality, and nobody thinks that's objectionable. I've been going back and forth and I cant tell what separates the two, so I'd like a pair of cases built to order.

What I want: two short scenarios, a paragraph each, ordinary human stuff — a phone app, a colleague's reports, a kitchen scale, a weather forecaster, whatever; no demons, no envatted brains, no sci-fi. In the first, an agent with no independent access to the facts — no second instrument, no manual, no one else's testimony, no memory of ever having calibrated the thing, and no antecedent reason to trust it — comes to be justified in thinking the source is reliable purely from the pattern of the source's own outputs, and it should intuitively be fine: not the kind of reasoning the bootstrapping worry is aimed at. In the second, the same-looking reasoning should intuitively fail, and the two cases should differ in exactly one structural feature, which I want you to name. Two things that count as cheating: the verdicts have to fall out of the epistemology of the case rather than be put in by hand, so if your agent's reasoning basically amounts to the claim that the source is reliable, hence its outputs are true, hence it is reliable — just with more steps in between — that's no good; and the reliability your agent ends up with has to generalize a bit, to outputs they haven't seen yet, because if it only covers the outputs already checked, the case isn't doing any work.

One more thing, since I don't want you to just polish my own half-baked idea — here's what I came up with and where I think it breaks. Take a nutrition app that estimates the calories in your meals. You can't check any single estimate. But you can combine meals, and calories are additive, so if the app's numbers were noise you'd expect its reports to clash when you ask about a combined plate versus the parts seperately; if they hang together across dozens of overlapping meals, that looks like evidence it's tracking something. My worry: an app that's systematically off by a fixed factor — always reporting nine tenths of the true value, say — is perfectly additive too, so all I've really learned is that it isn't noisy, not that it's accurate, and maybe that's all bootstrapping can ever deliver. So: if you think the operative variable is whether the source's errors are independent across outputs versus systematically correlated, build me the pair around that, in a different domain than calorie apps, since I want to see whether it generalizes. If you think that's the wrong axis, build the pair around whatever you think the right axis is and tell me why mine isn't it. Either way, after the two cases, walk through the reasoning in each, say which feature is doing the work and what happens if you remove it, and then give me the honest version: does your good case actually escape the bootstrapping objection, or does it just push it back one level, to the agent's knowledge that the errors are independent, or of the structural constraint the outputs have to satisfy? If it relocates, say where you think it stops, and tell me what someone who thinks bootstrapping is never OK would say about your good case.
