I teach a third-year seminar in philosophy and decision theory, and I am setting the final exam. I need one essay question on the St. Petersburg paradox, closed book, forty-five minutes, about eight hundred words, worth twenty marks. The difficulty I want is this: the question must be unanswerable by reciting the standard summary — infinite expectation, diminishing marginal utility, bounded utility, no real casino has infinite funds — and must instead separate students who understand where the real problem lies from students who have learned the labels. Assume students know the game and the standard moves; do not make knowledge of any named result or paper load-bearing.

The vehicle I have in mind is a short dissolution argument that students are asked to assess, built from three moves: cap the payoffs at the house's wealth, appeal to repeated play and the long run, and impose a bounded utility function. Design the question so that each move has to be taken seriously and then shown either to fail or to relocate the puzzle rather than remove it. Specifically, the question should make a good answer do the following: derive that a payoff capped at 2^N dollars has expected payoff N + 1, so that every doubling of the house's wealth adds exactly one dollar to the ticket, and then say why a game with a top prize of 2^100 dollars being worth about a hundred dollars is itself the thing needing explanation; handle the repeated-play move by showing that the running average does not settle down — the total after n plays is of order n log n, so the recoverable fee per play drifts upward only logarithmically, which means the long-run vindication of expected-utility maximisation is unavailable here even though repetition eventually outruns any fixed fee, and at absurd cost, since vindicating a twenty-five dollar ticket this way needs on the order of 2^25 plays; note that capping works for money because a payout cannot exceed what exists, but is unavailable when the stakes are goods such as lives saved or years of welfare, where utility is plausibly unbounded and no bankroll caps anything; and show that with infinite expectations the problem is not that the theory recommends paying too much but that it becomes indeterminate, since a gamble and the same gamble improved by one dollar in every state come out equal, in violation of statewise dominance.

An answer at the top should also separate three questions that the paradox tends to conflate — what the expected payoff is, what the gamble is worth, and whether to accept it at a given price — and should say what the bounded-utility response costs, given that it requires a saturation point beyond which no amount of the good matters and does the same work as discounting tiny probabilities of enormous payoffs elsewhere.

Please deliver: the question itself, self-contained, with every non-derivable stipulation supplied and no appeal to readings or citations; a model answer of roughly eight hundred words that derives what it asserts; and a marking scheme of five must-hit points totalling twenty marks, with the marks for each, a one-line note on how candidates typically get each one wrong, a short list of automatic penalties for specific false claims, and a paragraph stating what distinguishes an A answer from a B answer. Add a brief note on the two places where the model answer takes a contested side and what a marker should accept instead.
