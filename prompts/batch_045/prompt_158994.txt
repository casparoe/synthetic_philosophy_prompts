Me and my housemate got into a proper long argument last night — she's an ML engineer, I'm a philosophy grad student — about whether a reinforcement learning agent can suffer. Her line was that reward is just a number the code uses to nudge weights around and that asking whether it feels like anything is no better than asking whether a thermostat suffers when the room is cold. My line was that the thermostat comparison is cheap, because in a thermostat the "error" isn't something the system represents or does anything about — it just is the state of the world — whereas a decent RL agent maintains its own running estimate of how things are going for it and changes what it does on the basis of the mismatch. Then she said something I couldn't answer: fine, but in most actual training setups that mismatch gets computed offline, off a replay buffer, by a critic that's frozen for thousands of steps, on trajectories collected by an older version of the policy, so whatever "live" connection you think is doing the philosophical work simply isn't there. We went round in circles for nearly an hour, she threw in something about dopamine tracking prediction error rather than pleasure (which I told her was a red herring for a conceptual project, since pointing at brains settles nothing about functional roles — but tell me in the proposal if I'm wrong about that), and we ended up agreeing only that the word "reward" is doing about four different jobs at once.

So I want to turn that into a proposal. It's for a small funded six-month, one-person project — I'd guess 8–10k words plus a bit of toy simulation work, nothing that needs a lab — and the question I want stated precisely is something like: does the case for taking negative valence in an RL agent seriously depend on the reward signal being a live control variable for the system that recieves it, i.e. on the agent's own error estimate being causally hooked up to what it is doing right now, rather than on the reward simply being low, or on the objective function containing negative numbers? My half-formed idea, which I'd like sharpened rather than politely accepted, is that we should stop asking "is reward pleasure?" and instead ask which of the several things a scalar signal can be inside a training loop could possibly play the valence role at all: the objective being optimised, the number actually fed into the update, the temporal-difference error, or the "how much should I change my mind" gain signal. I suspect the interesting answer is the last two and not the first two, which would mean the same reward function can support opposite verdicts depending on whether the loop is on-policy or off-policy, whether updates are batched and delayed, whether the reward is delivered by a separate learned proxy model, and whether the agent is ever deployed or only ever trained — and that most of the existing argument has been conducted at the wrong level of description. I'd also want it to say something about the frozen-policy case she raised, where a policy is updated but never actually acts in the world.
