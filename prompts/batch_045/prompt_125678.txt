I sit on the three-person progress review panel at Cedar Row, a six-month residential substance-use program that takes most of its residents by court referral. At the end of week six each resident presents a recovery plan to us, and we must then certify on a form that goes to the referring court either "engaged" or "not engaged." The program manual draws the distinction like this: compliance is doing what the schedule requires; engagement is "owning the plan as your own choice rather than as something the program requires of you." Our certification decides whether a resident keeps his court-ordered placement or is returned on the violation.

The resident in front of us, T.V., is thirty-four, eight weeks in, referred after a third OWI. He has no infractions, and the unit staff describe him as one of the harder workers they have had. His plan is, on the merits, the best the clinical team has seen this quarter: a housing application filed before discharge, an outpatient slot already held, a sponsor confirmed, and two named people he will call. He presented the whole thing in a flat, fatalistic register. "My father drank, my brother drank, I've been drinking since I was fifteen. I'm an addict. I'll do this plan because you need me to and because my PO needs it, and I'll probably drink again when I get out. That's just how it is with me."

Our clinical director reads this as exactly the failure the week-six presentation exists to detect. On her reading he has given his addiction the status of a fixed trait, described his own future as something that happens to him rather than something he does, and located every motive for the plan in other people's requirements — which is what the manual means by "not engaged." She wants him returned. She has also pointed out that the manual's own line, "Addiction is not an identity; it is a set of choices," is not decorative, and that certifying him as engaged while he says the opposite out loud makes a nullity of the item.

Our senior counselor — twelve years in recovery, and the staff member who knows him best — reads the same transcript the other way round. She said almost these words at her own week six, they were the truest thing she had said in years, and what helped her was not being made to disown them but being allowed to go on saying them while acting anyway. Her sharper point: if we require a resident to avow that he is choosing, we will get the avowal, because we are holding his liberty in our hands, and what we will have taught him is to narrate himself in whatever register the people with the clipboard reward — which is the opposite of what the manual is after.

Three further facts complicate it. First, when told his presentation was in question, T.V. offered to re-present and, in his words, "say it however you want it said" — which I take to damage both readings at once, since an avowal volunteered to a panel that can revoke your placement does not look like evidence of anything. Second, our two-year outcome file (41 discharges, small n) shows no relationship between a resident's score on the engagement item at week six and abstinence at six months; the item tracks something, but not that. Third, the counselor has floated a distinction I can't shake: that owning a plan and predicting you will keep to it are different attitudes, and that T.V. may be doing the first perfectly while making an honest, probably accurate, estimate about the second — in which case his fatalism is a forecast, not a refusal, and the manual's vocabulary has no clean way of saying so. One last constraint: the court form has two boxes and no field for conditions, so anything conditional we decide has to be enforced inside the house.

I have to file by Thursday. Please draft the panel's opinion memo, addressed to the clinical director and the counselor as joint readers — both of them will read it, and both of them have to keep working with this resident. Lead with the bottom line, and come down on one side: certify "engaged" outright, certify "engaged" subject to stated conditions, or certify "not engaged" with a specified re-review date. Use these sections, in this order:

1. "Bottom line" — 100–150 words: the certification and the concrete next step, with dates.
2. "The record" — about 150 words: only what the panel can actually assert.
3. "The two readings, at their strongest" — 250–300 words: reconstruct the director's case and the counselor's so that each would recognise it, including what each implies about what a recovery plan is for.
4. "What 'engagement' should be read to require" — about 300 words: how we should construe our own manual given the outcome data and given that the item is supposed to do work for a court.
5. "The case we are rejecting" — about 200 words: the strongest available version of whichever position you do not adopt, stated so that its advocate would not feel paraphrased unkindly, followed by why it still does not carry the decision.
6. "Disposition and dates" — about 150 words: who does what by when, what would count as satisfaction, and what would trigger a different certification at re-review.

Do not settle the case by restating the manual's definition; the manual is ours, and how to read it is part of what is in dispute. Keep the whole memo near 1,100 words.
