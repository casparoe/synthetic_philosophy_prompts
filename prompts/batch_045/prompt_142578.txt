I'm a junior at a large public high school, and I've been competing in policy and Lincoln-Douglas debate since freshman year, so I'm comfortable in that vocabulary: framework and value criteria, impacts and impact calculus, magnitude versus probability, comparative worlds, presumption and burden of proof, fiat and solvency, links and internal links, turns, uniqueness and non-uniqueness, and even-if arguments. I've also been reading about Pascal's wager on my own, and what I'd like from you is a translation, not an explanation: restate the wager and its main objections in the vocabulary I actually argue in, as faithfully as you can, and then give me a residue report on what the translation could not carry.

Please translate each of the following, one at a time, into terms that would make sense to a judge in a round:

1. The core argument, in its strongest form. That includes the claim that you are already in the game and cannot refuse to wager; the version that leans on infinite gain against a small finite stake; and the more interesting version that assigns no probabilities at all and simply says one option is no worse in every possible case and better in at least one, so no odds are needed.

2. The worry that expected value stops making sense once the payoffs are infinite: infinite gains cannot be scaled, added to, or traded off against finite costs in the usual way, so every nonzero chance of an infinite reward looks equally attractive and the reasoning cannot rank incompatible religions against one another.

3. The symmetry worry: that for any deity who rewards the person who wagered, one can describe a deity who rewards exactly those who refused to wager for prudential reasons, and nothing inside the argument itself excludes the second description.

4. The worry that belief is not the sort of thing you can decide to have, together with what I've been told is Pascal's own remedy for that: that you should perform the practices anyway, and that the performance is meant to work on you over time and produce the belief, rather than to express a belief you already have.

5. A variant of my own that I haven't been able to get anyone to answer, which I call the multiplication problem. The wager looks like a one-off bet, but it is really a standing rule for how to respond to a small probability of an enormous payoff; and once you adopt that rule you seem committed to accepting an endless series of further wagers, each with some real finite cost, each justified in exactly the same way, with nothing in the rule that ever tells you to stop and nothing that ever makes you revise. In debate terms: under this framework any number of competing wagers can be read against the original one and each is independently winning on the framework's own terms, so the framework collapses into whoever can name the largest consequence. That is very close to the kind of impact inflation that real judges punish, and I want to know whether the punishment is principled or just a tournament convention.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about:

For each of the five, say what translated cleanly, what you had to bend to fit, and what you could not say at all in debate vocabulary.

Where the debate framework appears to dissolve the problem by machinery rather than by argument — by demanding a link between a specified action and a specified outcome, by discounting very large or very speculative impacts, by requiring probability to be weighed and not just magnitude, by putting the burden of uniqueness on the affirmative, or by falling back on presumption when nothing is established — tell me whether that is a genuine answer to the wager or merely a way of changing the subject.

Tell me what has no counterpart on my side of the translation. My two guesses are these, and I want them checked rather than flattered. First, nothing in a round corresponds to the question of whether the judge would accept mere outward compliance, because in debate performing a position you do not hold is the normal case and no paradigm asks what you privately believe. Second, nothing in a round corresponds to the fact that a person deciding whether to believe might legitimately decline to decide at all, because a judge is required to issue a verdict and the framework never has the option of silence.

Finally, the question underneath all of this: does the leftover material point to something debate's weighing machinery genuinely cannot represent, or does it suggest that the wager was never as determinate an argument as it appears when it is written in the vocabulary of stakes, odds, and prizes?
