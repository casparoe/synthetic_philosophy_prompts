I'm writing a short piece for a philosophy-and-computation reading group, and I want to read Borges' "The Library of Babel" as a story about intractability rather than about infinity or skepticism. Two things Ive been chewing on that I want you to actually argue with, not just nod at:

(1) The librarians' problem isn't only that there are astronomically many books. Its that the Library gives them no neighborhood structure: the set of books is the same however you shelve them, but because each book sits beside ones that differ from it at random, nearness on the shelf carries no information about nearness in content, so there's no gradient to climb. Size alone doesn't make a search space hard; the access structure does. Two libraries with identical contents could differ between trivially searchable and hopeless, and I think that's the more interesting half of what Borges is doing.

(2) The usual NP picture says hard to find, easy to check. But checkability may be the part that breaks here. Since the Library also contains every one-character variation on any candidate book, the sects hunting the Crimson Hexagon, the perfect catalogue, or the Man of the Book can't trust what they're holding even when it looks right — and "is this the book that would justify a life?" isn't a decidable predicate anyway. So maybe the Library dramatizes something the complexity vocabulary has no clean name for.

So: where does the intractability lens actually earn its keep on the story — which scenes, which cults and their quests — and where does it break, or flatten Borges into an illustration? I suspect the religious and eschatological despair is doing work the lens can't see, and I'd rather you say that than force it.
