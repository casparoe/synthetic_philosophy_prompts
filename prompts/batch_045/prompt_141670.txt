I sit on the board of a small housing cooperative: twenty-two apartments owned and occupied by members, and four that the co-op owns and rents out. Next month the members will hold a binding vote on whether to convert those four rental units into owner-occupied shares, which would end the tenancies. Our bylaws are drafted on the assumption that there would always be renters and are silent about whether tenants may vote on anything, so the board has to decide the franchise. I would like your advice on that decision.

Two features of the case are making it harder than I expected. First, the vote does not merely change who lives here; it changes who the co-op is. If it passes, the four tenants leave and we become an all-owner building; if it fails, they stay and the co-op goes on being partly a landlord. So how much someone has at stake, and whether they will be bound by what we decide, both seem to depend on which way the vote goes — and the four tenants' ballots could easily decide it, since the owner-residents are split almost evenly. Second, the people with the most at stake are not the same people as the people who will live under the result: one owner-resident has told the board privately that she will sell and move out within the year whatever happens, while one tenant has lived in her unit for eleven years and would stay indefinitely if the conversion fails. Some of us want to let the tenants vote because no one is more affected; others say that only those who are permanently subject to the co-op's rules and carry its costs should have a say; and I keep noticing that each of us seems to favour whichever rule gives our own side the votes.

Please set out the strongest version of each position, tell me whether there is a non-arbitrary way to settle who votes — one that is not simply selecting the electorate that produces the outcome you prefer, and that does not quietly assume the very thing the decision is supposed to settle — and then tell me what we should actually do. Write your reply in continuous prose, in the manner of a considered letter or email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables.
