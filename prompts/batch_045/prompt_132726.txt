I'm writing a section of a paper on anthropics and I need a worked case, built to order, in which the self-sampling and self-indication assumptions do not merely differ in how confident they are but pick different winners.

Set-up, so we're using the terms the same way. SSA: treat yourself as a random draw from the observers in your reference class, so the anthropic likelihood of a hypothesis goes with the *fraction* of its observers who have evidence like yours, and the total headcount drops out. SIA: weight hypotheses by how many observers they contain before conditioning on your own existence, so *absolute* numbers survive the update. The presumptuous philosopher case is the familiar upshot: with the fractions equal, SSA rests at the prior while SIA leaps to the crowded world.

What I want is a case where the fractions are not equal, so that SSA and SIA come down on opposite sides — each by at least three orders of magnitude.

Constraints. One paragraph of scenario, in an ordinary empirical setting of your choosing — an astronomical survey, a demographic projection, an epidemiological cohort — and the indexical evidence must be something a real investigator could actually acquire by looking. No infinite populations, no simulations all the way down, no exact duplicates of the reasoner, no perfect predictors. The two hypotheses must be equally supported by everything non-indexical, with priors at 1:1 before the anthropic update, so that all the work is done by the self-locating evidence.

After the scenario, I need: the numbers and both calculations; a statement of which single structural feature produces the reversal (I expect it to be the gap between a fraction and an absolute count — under SIA the anthropic likelihood ratio is the SSA one multiplied by the ratio of total observer counts, which is why SIA is normally just a more confident version of SSA — but show it inside your case); what happens to both verdicts when that feature is removed, which should collapse the case back into the presumptuous-philosopher shape; whether the verdicts survive two different reference-class choices, all humans and all observers; and how a defender of whichever assumption loses would attack the case rather than accept it.

Finally, tell me whether the two assumptions also come apart over what to do — keep funding the survey, change the protocol, whatever fits your scenario — or only over what to believe. If the divergence is belief-only, say so plainly; the section is arguing that this choice has practical bite and I'd rather know now than build the argument on it.

And if the specifications can't be jointly met — if, given my constraints, the two assumptions provably cannot land on opposite sides, or if any case that appears to satisfy them is quietly smuggling in asymmetric non-indexical evidence or an infinite headcount — tell me that instead of handing me a fudged case.
