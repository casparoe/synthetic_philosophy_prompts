I run a fortnightly reading group for grad students and a couple of stubborn postdocs, and last week we did panpsychism, and specifically the combination problem. Two of them, Rupa and Eitan, went at it for about forty minutes after everyone else had gone to the pub, and they've both emailed me since asking me to settle it. I dont want to: I don't have a settled view, and they're both sharper on this than me anyway. So you do it. Here's roughly how it went, at least as well as I can reconstruct it. I've smoothed the wording but I think I've kept there order and substance.

E1. Eitan opens with the problem in its subject-summing form. Micro-level subjects, each with its own little experience, are supposed to constitute one macro subject with one unified conscious field. But putting many subjects together gives you a collection, a committee, a swarm: never a single subject. Numbers of subjects don't add into a subject the way lengths add into a length. So panpsychism cannot account for the unity of consciousness, which is arguably the central datum any theory here has to explain.

R1. Rupa says this is the hard problem wearing a new hat. Anyone who thinks macro-consciousness is constituted out of microphysical goings-on owes some account of how the many become one, and that includes the physicalist. If the objection is fatal for panpsychism it's fatal for everyone except brute emergence, which nobody wants. And the claim that aggregation of subjects can never yield a subject just assumes that subjects aren't the kind of thing that can have subjects as parts, which is precisely what's in dispute.

E2. Eitan grants that the problem is general but says it isn't equally bad for everyone, and then introduces what he called the crowding argument. If micro-subjects are genuine subjects with experiences of their own, a conscious human being is built out of an enormous number of distinct centres of experience. So you'd expect our phenomenology to be either crowded, shot through with a riot of alien mini-experiences we ought to be able to notice, or else gappy, with all that micro-phenomenality somehow switched off or hidden. Both need explaining, and the filtering or suppression mechanism the panpsychist needs looks ad hoc. Then a second version, the grain version: remove a single micro-subject, or add one. If the macro experience changes, our phenomenology is absurdly fine-grained and unstable. If it doesn't change, there's no point at which the macro experience could ever get going, since you can build up one addition at a time and nothing happens at any step.

R2. Rupa replies with what she called extractive combination, or phenomenal compression. The crowding argument assumes combination has to preserve micro-contents intact, like adding numbers. But combination could be lossy: the macro experience might encode only the large-scale structural or statistical features of the micro-experiences, the way a coarse-grained summary captures a mass of detail while discarding nearly all of it. On that picture there's no crowding. The micro-experiences are all still had, but what the macro subject enjoys is a compression of them, so of course introspection doesn't turn them up.

E3. Eitan says that isn't a version of panpsychism any more, it's a repackaging. A compression of an experience is a representation of it. If the macro experience compresses the micro ones, it represents them rather than being made out of them, and that slides into a higher-order or representational theory of consciousness, the very sort of thing panpsychists deploy against the physicalist. Worse, it re-imports the hard problem one level up: why is it like anything to have a summary? A hard drive holds summaries too, and nothing is going on for it.

R3. Rupa says fine, drop compression and just posit a primitive phenomenal bonding relation: when micro-subjects stand in the right relations, a further subject exists whose experience is grounded in theirs. This is no worse off than physics, which treats composition, or charge, or spacetime intervals as primitive and doesn't feel obliged to analyse them into something more basic. Primitive isn't the same as magical, and the demand that every relation be analysable is a philosopher's prejudice, not a requirement on a theory.

E4. Eitan says the physics primitives are cheap in a way this isn't. They're quantitative, they do predictive work, they're individually testable. A phenomenal bonding relation has no mathematics, no independent confirmation, and its only job is to make the combination problem go away. Calling it primitive is putting a name on the mystery and declaring the argument closed. And if the relation is brute, the panpsychist's advantage over the brute-emergence theorist evaporates, since that theorist's brute macro-facts are the same move made one level up.

R4. Rupa's last move was that we should expect the combination of subjects to be unimaginable from the inside. Our concept of a subject is formed by acquaintance with exactly one case, the ordinary human one, and there's no reason to think it tells us what happens when you take a great many subjects and fuse them. Eitan, she said, keeps treating subject as if it were a Lego brick whose behaviour under stacking we can just read off.

E5. Eitan said that's a conversation stopper: any view can protect itself from any objection by declaring the relevant case to be beyond our concepts, and if that move is available then the combination problem was never a real problem in the first place, so why did we just spend forty minutes on it.

Then they both turned to me and I pleaded the fifth.
