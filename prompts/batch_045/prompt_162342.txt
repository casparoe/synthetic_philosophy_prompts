I'm putting together the reading for a short undergrad module on gender and work — mixed politics in the room, about half of them arrive convinced the gender-equality paradox settles something and half convinced it's an artifact — and I want to give them a spec rather than another reading: what would a theory actually have to deliver to count as explaining it? The phenomenon I mean is the country-level pattern where societies that score high on gender equality and development show *larger* sex differences in vocational interests, some personality measures, and STEM uptake than poorer, less egalitarian ones, rather than smaller. I don't care about exact figures or which index, so please don't lean on any particular dataset, and no citations or reading lists — I'll find those myself. I also don't want the explanation for its own sake: I want one good enough to decide wether anything should be done about it, i.e. whether the pattern is an injustice, a loss of some other kind, or neither of those.

So: the list of requirements, each with the case or consideration behind it and why it's a requirement rather than a taste, and then a separate note on which ones are political prejudice in a lab coat — things one camp demands because it likes the conclusion — and which are genuine. A few candidates I can't sort out myself: it has to say whether it is explaining an individual-level or a country-level correlation and not slide between them; it has to survive the fact that "gender equality" indices partly bake in outcomes of the very sort being explained, and that equality co-varies with wealth and with post-materialist values so the three are hard to pull apart; it has to make a counterfactual prediction about what happens at the top of the scale instead of just fitting the curve we have; it has to handle that the pattern shows up in some measures and not others (interests yes, math achievement apparently not) and say what that difference tells us; and it has to respect that the differences are distributional, not individual — within-sex spread dwarfs between-sex spread, so any theory that needs a typical man and a typical woman is already out of business.

The part I'm stuck on, and the part I most want: I suspect two of the natural requirements are enemies. A theory should treat the choices people make in high-equality countries as genuinely theirs — not coerced, not false consciousness, the very thing we were trying to enable — and it should also explain why, once the pressure lifts, the aggregate lands on the same stereotype nearly every time instead of scattering or drifting whichever way local culture happens to push. The first pulls toward saying the outcome is fine and there is nothing left to explain; the second pulls toward saying something is still steering. If they can't both be met, say so, say which you'd drop, and say what dropping it costs, because I think the honest answer reorganises the whole module. Relatedly: is there a version of the paradox where the pattern is real, the choices are fully free, and it's still worth intervening on? That's the version I want to be able to either defend or give up. One more thing, and it may be junk: I've been using a made-up distinction to keep the families straight — call a difference *opportunity-sensitive* if it shrinks when you remove an external barrier (costs, gatekeeping, harassment) and *expression-sensitive* if it grows when you remove pressure to conform, so that the paradox is a trait that's expression-sensitive but not opportunity-sensitive, or sensitive to both in opposite directions. If that distinction carves anything, build the desiderata out of it and tell me what a third category would have to be; if it's jargon, say that. Finally, very briefly, two lines each and no paper or author names, how the main families do against your list — the "let people express themselves and the traditional preferences come back" story, the "internalized roles and norms" story, the "innate dispositions no longer suppressed" story, and the "measurement artifact / index construction" story — and which single requirement kills the most of them.
