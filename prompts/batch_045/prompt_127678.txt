I'm giving the only talk at my father's ash-scattering next Saturday. There was no funeral, at his request, and there will be no service: just a cold morning in October at the prairie restoration site where he volunteered for twenty years, a folding table under the picnic shelter, and maybe forty people. That includes his three children — I'm the eldest, I teach ethics at a community college, and I have not opened the Zhuangzi since my comprehensive exams — his brother Ray, who is a born-again Christian and has already emailed me a verse he would like worked in, six or seven of his former graduate students in Chinese philosophy who have read the texts properly and will hear instantly if I reach for a greeting-card version of them, and a couple of dozen neighbours and birders from the preserve. So: seven or eight minutes spoken aloud, roughly nine hundred words, and I need it to be true to him rather than merely consoling, without asking Ray or my sister to sit through something that uses their father as a prop for an argument.

Here is why this is not straightforward. My father spent his working life on the Zhuangzi's treatment of death, and the reading he held rules out most of what people say on occasions like this. He thought the popular framing — Zhuangzi teaches serenity in the face of death because the self dissolves back into the cosmic whole, so there is nothing to fear — was a Western mystical misreading, smuggled in partly through old Victorian translations (the copy on his shelf is one whose title calls Zhuangzi a "mystic"; he said that was the single worst word in it). His own position, as I can reconstruct it from arguments at the dinner table and from an essay of his that my siblings have never read, had three parts: first, that the transformation material should be read literally and materially — the body becomes soil, ants, crows, which is why he liked the story about not caring whether he was buried or left out for the birds — and not as a doctrine of the soul's return to a source; second, that the real work of those passages is to loosen our grip on the boundary between me and not-me, so that the question "what will happen to me when I die?" has no settled answer that could console anyone; third, that the response proper to all this is curiosity rather than serenity, which is why he thought the Zhuangzi is one of the funniest books ever written.

I want that reading to do structural work in the speech, not to decorate it. As I see it, I therefore cannot say that he is at peace, that he has returned to the source, that he is with us in spirit, or that he would not want us to be sad: on his own view there is no persisting subject to be in a state, and he thought sentences like those were tells about the needs of the living rather than claims about the dead. I also cannot turn him into a lesson — "Dad taught us that death is as natural as the seasons" — because my sister is still angry about exactly that move, and I think she is right to be. What I cannot work out is what remains once those are gone: what can honestly be said, in his own idiom, to a room half of which wants resurrection and half of which has read the texts? His practice when a friend died was to go out and look deliberately for some particular living thing — last spring it was a sedge wren — and I suspect the speech should end by asking the room to do a concrete thing rather than to feel a particular way, but I don't know how to make that gesture land for people who mean very different things by it. Two practical constraints: I am writing this in a motel room with none of his books and no usable connection, so give me nothing I would have to verify — no citations, no chapter numbers, no lines in quotation marks from a translation you cannot check; paraphrase anything from the Zhuangzi, don't invent a story you are not confident is really in there, and if a phrase is one you would want to hedge or footnote, leave it out. And it must not be solemn in the "timeless Eastern wisdom" register: he thought that tone betrayed texts that are full of jokes, mockery and absurdity, and I would like at least one moment of genuine humour.
