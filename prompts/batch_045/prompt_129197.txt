hey — I need help fixing (or replacing) a test I've been using on myself for years, because I've finally noticed it gives answers I don't believe.

The test: to decide whether some fact is really a reason *for someone*, I picture her idealized counterpart — her, but with all the true information, no factual mistakes, no akrasia, no slips in reasoning, and after vividly imagining what the action and its outcome would actually be like. Call her the council. If the council wouldn't be moved, I've been inclined to say there's no reason there. My tentative principle is: S has a reason to φ iff S's council would want S to φ — except for those reasons whose force doesn't depend at all on what S cares about, which S has whether the council likes it or not. I know that last clause is doing all the work and I have no idea how to write it properly.

Here are my verdicts, going by gut.

1. My flatmate won't eat sushi. He knows what it is, he's watched other people enjoy it, I've described the texture, he's not squeamish in general. Council: still unmoved. Verdict: fine — no reason for him to eat sushi (his reason to accept it at a dinner party is a different thing entirely).

2. A friend with pretty severe depression. Council, starting from where she actually is: nothing matters, no point, don't bother. Verdict: she totally has reasons — to text her sister back, to eat something, to see a doctor. Whatever the council says from inside that state is worthless to me.

3. My brother at 22. Knows the compound interest numbers, watched our gran get old, genuinely does not care what happens to him at 70. Council: spend it now. Verdict: he has a reason to save anyway. This feels like case 2 but I don't think it's the same failure — hers is that the motivational set is broken, his is that it's… thin? unexercised? I can't say what the difference is.

4. A guy at work who spends every weekend photographing level crossings. Cheap, hurts nobody, he's fully informed about how marginal it all is, and he'd keep going after imagining the whole thing clearly. Council: carry on. Verdict: I find it faintly sad, but I can't say he has no reason. I think I have to grant it.

5. A woman from a memoir I read who grew up in a household where it was simply axiomatic that a wife's good consists in serving her husband. She's never encountered anyone who thinks otherwise, so the "true information" available to her doesn't include any sense of wanting something else. Council: endorses it wholeheartedly. Verdict: she has reasons that don't wait around for her endorsement.

6. The fixed point. A friend who sincerely says: what I most want is to do whatever I most want to do. Her council has nothing to correct, nothing to add, and rubber-stamps everything. Verdict: the test hands her a clean pass on everything, which makes me think the test is empty in her case rather than that she's unusually well-ordered.

7. The pair I keep going back and forth on. (a) Someone raised from birth in a high-control group: give her all the true information and she still endorses staying, because the information just gets reinterpreted inside the framework. Verdict: no reason to stay, and reasons to get out that don't depend on her endorsing them. (b) A nun I know who came to her vocation slowly, counted the costs, has had doubts and come back to it: give her all the true information and she still endorses it. Verdict: that's a real reason for her, and it's *hers* in a way that's tied to her actual motives. But the shape of the output is the same in both cases — informed, coherent, stable endorsement. The whole difference is in how the motive got there. So does my principle need a history clause? What would it say?

8. And the thing that makes me suspect the whole setup: there are cases where you shouldn't deliberate like the council at all. Being a decent friend, or improvising in a band — the ideal-deliberation picture describes something you'd ruin the thing by doing. Someone told me that's just the difference between the standard you're answerable to and the one you should be aiming at, and that the standard applies to you "indirectly," but I can't tell whether that's a real distinction or a way of changing the subject when the account goes wrong.

So: give me a principle, or two candidates, stated precisely enough that I can run cases through it. Tell me which of my cases motivates each clause, which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and wants me to drop, and what it predicts for these two I haven't settled: (i) someone with no capacity for any affective response to music at all — does she have a reason to go hear a symphony? (ii) someone who deliberately trained himself over years to enjoy being cruel, and now does, stably and with full information. And say plainly whether your principle is meant as the thing agents should deliberate with or the thing they get assessed by, and what follows if it's only the second.
