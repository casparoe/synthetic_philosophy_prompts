I write literary fiction and, once or twice a year, teach a weekend workshop for novelists on character and decision. The next one has sixteen to eighteen adults, a single ninety-minute slot, and a room with moveable tables. I want an activity for it that does one specific job — and, because a novel I am drafting contains a scene in which a visitor at a writers' retreat runs a room through what looks like a parlour game and quietly dismantles a certain style of reasoning, I want the activity to be genuinely playable and not merely describable on the page. If it only works as prose, it is no use to me in either place.

The reasoning I want them to collide with is this. Two agents who reason identically, and know it, are not in the position the standard argument assumes when each faces a symmetric one-shot dilemma. The standard argument has each of them say: whatever she does, I do better by taking the sure advantage; her decision is already fixed and mine is not; so I take it. The rival thought — call it, in the debrief only, superrationality — says that if she is me in every respect that bears on the case, then her decision and mine are not two independent events, and the only outcomes genuinely available to me are the ones in which we do the same thing; and once the menu has been cut down to those, the sure advantage is no longer sure. I do not want the words game theory, prisoner's dilemma, cooperate, defect, Newcomb, evidential, or superrationality spoken in the room until the debrief, and I would rather the apparatus be dressed in novelists' vocabulary: sealed endings, a character who cannot hear you, the same story written twice.

What I need from you: rules and a minute-by-minute timeline for ninety minutes; materials I can carry in one bag; a payoff scheme with small but real stakes and no cash — the currency I can actually spend is my own working time and a few desirable objects, so please use exactly that (a one-to-one manuscript consultation, written notes on an excerpt, a good bottle, first refusal on something) and say where the stakes need to bite hardest; the roles or mechanics that make participants run into the conflict themselves rather than hear it named; and a debrief that maps particular moments in the room onto particular parts of the idea, in the order those moments are likely to occur.

Two mechanics I have in mind, which I would like you to build on, correct, or replace. First, a round of anonymous one-shot plays in pairs, run before any framing, so that the dominance argument comes out of their mouths before it comes out of mine. Second, a round in which each participant is paired with a counterpart who received a byte-identical briefing, who knows the participant received it, who knows that the participant knows, and so on — with the wrinkle that each participant must first write, seal, and hand in one sentence of advice to that counterpart, and that I read a sample of those sentences aloud, anonymously, before any choice is revealed. My hope is that the advice and the sealed choices visibly disagree, and that the disagreement is the thing they feel rather than the thing I announce. Tell me if the sequencing is wrong, or if the advice device corrupts the round it is meant to expose.

I would also like the group-level variant — the one where a single tempting object is privately offered to each person, and everyone receives something better but smaller only if nobody reaches for it — placed where it does the most work, with an honest account of what group size does to it and why it may pull against the pair case rather than reinforcing it. Then: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, above all anything that would lead participants to conclude the set-up was rigged or bluffed, since the second round depends entirely on their trusting the pairing; how to make that pairing verifiable without breaking the spell; which behaviours in the room count as evidence that the activity landed and which are noise, politeness, or showing off; and which part of the idea no game can stage, so that I know what I must say afterwards in plain words. Finally, tell me where in the debrief an intelligent participant could reasonably refuse the conclusion, and what refusing it there would cost them — I may yet want the character who runs the game to be wrong, or at least not clearly right.
