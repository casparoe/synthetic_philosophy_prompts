I'm a journalist who covers behavioral science and ideas, and a feature I filed last year on how much of the published moral-psychology literature rests on samples from Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic societies has left me stuck in an exchange I have not been able to bring to any conclusion. Three people I trust read the piece and then disagreed with one another, and after roughly a year of intermittent correspondence nobody has shifted position at all. The anthropologist says the variation is deep: the standard taxonomies — the familiar five-fold list of concerns like care, fairness, loyalty, authority, and purity — are themselves parochial, and what the psychologist codes as a difference in the *weight* a community puts on a shared concern is really a difference in which considerations count as reasons in the first place. The philosopher says the variation sits one level up, in inputs and weights: describe the non-moral facts the same way, hold fixed what everyone expects to happen, and the verdicts converge, because the groups were never disagreeing about what morality requires, only about what the world is like or how much a shared concern matters here. And a third interlocutor, who works on metaethics, says the whole descriptive argument is beside the point: massive, recalcitrant cross-cultural variation would no more refute moral objectivity than long-running disagreement about the shape of the earth refutes geography.

To make the problem vivid for the follow-up, I built a stylized case of my own, and each of the three reads it differently, which is why I'm writing. Two communities face the same situation: a young cultivator has a surplus, and both his own household and a senior kinsman's household have claims on it. The two groups agree on every non-moral fact I can think to specify — who labored, who went hungry last season, what each household will do with the grain, what everyone expects, what would happen to each household's standing. Both also assent, in the abstract, to the same pair of platitudes: help those who helped you, and see to your own. They nevertheless come down on opposite sides about what is owed here, and they keep doing so after each is given the other's full reasoning, translated as well as anyone can manage. I proposed a test for calling this kind of disagreement deep — call it *total transport*: the verdicts still diverge when each side has complete knowledge of the other's non-moral beliefs, full practical fluency in the other's evaluative vocabulary, and no pressure to defect. My philosopher says the test is well defined and never actually satisfied in the real cases. My anthropologist says it cannot be satisfied even in principle, and that this impossibility *is* the substantive claim rather than a skeptical aside. I can't tell whether we have discovered a real fault line or manufactured one out of a badly specified test.

So I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and I'd be grateful if you'd argue for one rather than canvass options. Is this a substantive disagreement or a verbal one about words like 'morality', 'fair', and 'objective'? Is a hidden empirical question doing the work — about whether the right descriptive taxonomy lumps or splits, or about what psychological architecture the variation is evidence for? Are the three of them simply answering different questions under one heading: a descriptive-psychological question about what is universal in our moral psychology, a semantic or interpretive question about whether the same concepts are in play across communities, and a metaethical question about what would make a moral claim correct? My own hypothesis is that the deep-variation claim borrows its apparent force from sliding between those questions, and that the disagreement about whether total transport is even specifiable has quietly migrated into a disagreement about concepts and interpretation that neither side signed up for — but I can't make that stick. Tell me whether it's right, what the best version of each position commits its holder to, and what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction I should draw, a formal result, an empirical finding of a specifiable kind, or a decision to stop asking the question this way. I'd like to keep the claim that at least some of the disagreement is substantive rather than merely verbal, since the piece rests on it; if that's the thing I have to give up, say so plainly and tell me what it costs. Please write in American English, since I'm filing with a US outlet, and don't give me citations or a reading list — I have access to the literature; what I need is the structure of the argument.
