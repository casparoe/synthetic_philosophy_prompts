Here is the position I want restated in my vocabulary. Scientific realism, in its standard motivation, leans on a no-miracles argument: the predictive and technological success of our best theories would be inexplicable unless those theories were at least approximately tracking how the world is. The main opposing pressure is the pessimistic meta-induction, which points at a long list of once-successful theories whose central theoretical claims we now treat as false, and infers that current ones will go the same way. Structural realism is the attempt to get the best of both: what is retained across theory change, it says, is not the ontology but the structure — the formal, mathematical, relational content that the discarded and the successor theory share. There is a modest version of this, on which structure is all we are entitled to believe in or claim to know, and an ambitious one, on which structure is all there is: objects are nothing over and above nodes in a relational structure, and it is a mistake to imagine a layer of intrinsic, non-relational individuals underneath it. The example everyone uses is the transition from Newtonian to relativistic mechanics: the ontology goes, the mathematical relations survive as a limiting case of the successor theory. And there is a standing objection I want carried along, which is that structure is too cheap: if a theory's content amounts only to the claim that there exists some domain of objects, of large enough cardinality, together with some assignment of relations that realizes the right abstract structure, then the claim looks close to vacuous, and the theory's empirical content threatens to collapse into mere adequacy to the observations.
