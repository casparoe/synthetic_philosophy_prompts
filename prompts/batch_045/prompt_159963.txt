I teach a 90-minute seminar for about fourteen upper-level undergraduates in cognitive science and philosophy. None of them has read anything on analogy-making or letter-string microdomains, and they arrive assuming that finding the rule in a puzzle is a matter of getting it right or wrong. I want an in-class activity, not a lecture, that makes them feel — before anyone names it — that a single worked example does not pin down a transformation, and that what they take themselves to be doing when they apply a rule is really a set of decisions about which descriptions stay rigid and which are allowed to slip.

The microdomain is a variant I have been using, and I would like the activity built around it rather than around the familiar roman-letter strings. There are five symbols: • ○ △ ✕ ★. Two relations on them are given on a handout and nothing else is given: a ring order, • → △ → ✕ → ★ → ○ → back to •, and a weight order, • < ○ < △ < ✕ < ★. Strings are short rows of these symbols, possibly containing runs, and a problem supplies one source-to-target pair and asks for the target of a new source. The point of the construction is that for △ and ✕ the ring-successor and the weight-successor coincide, so a pair like • △ → • ✕ looks entirely unambiguous; for • and ○ the two orderings diverge; and for ★ the weight-successor does not exist at all while the ring wraps round to ○. In the source pair the changed symbol is also simultaneously the rightmost one and the heaviest one in the string, which is where the trouble is stored up.

What I need from you:

- Rules, roles, and a minute-by-minute timeline, including an opening round that is genuinely trivial and scored, so that everyone is committed and on record before the ground moves under them. No explanation of the domain beyond the handout, and no naming of the phenomenon until the debrief.
- A concrete instance set: the actual strings, in the order I should hand them out, with a table giving for each item the answer predicted by each candidate description — ring-succession against weight-succession; rightmost symbol against heaviest symbol against the run as a whole; letter-level change against group-level change; wrapping against refusing to answer against importing the changed symbol from the example — and saying which pair of descriptions that item exists to separate. It should include at least one case that forces the letter-versus-group question and one that demands a successor exactly where no weight-successor exists.
- A payoff scheme needing no money that rewards teams for predicting the distribution of answers in the room rather than for being correct, so that the fact that answers cluster tightly — that the space of admissible answers is small and structured rather than anything-goes — shows up in the tally instead of in anything I say.
- A mid-session perturbation of the domain itself: a card that takes away one of the two orderings, or cuts the ring between two symbols, after which teams re-answer targets they have already answered. Design it so that what changes for them is which concepts have anything left to grip on.
- A closing round in which the teams build the trap themselves: each devises a source string that forces a team committed to a stated rule to slip, together with a rule about what counts as answering in the spirit of the rule rather than wriggling out of it.
- A debrief mapped onto moments in the session, naming conceptual slippage, levels of description, and the pressures that make some slips cheap while others feel like cheating, plus one final question I can ask to find out whether it landed.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle each: someone spotting the whole trick in round one and deflating it for everyone else; a team collapsing into the conclusion that there is simply no answer; the two-ordering setup being read as a gimmick rather than as the source of the pressure; the arithmetic of tallying eating the time I need for the debrief.
- An honest note on what the session cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself: that in the classical architecture for this microdomain, slips are subpersonal, graded by conceptual distance, and produced by a stochastic search under competing pressures, whereas my students will have chosen theirs deliberately and will be able to give me reasons for them.
