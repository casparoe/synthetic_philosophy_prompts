I have ended up holding a position about the old-evidence problem that I cannot get anyone to attack successfully, which makes me suspicious of it. I would like you to try to break it rather than to shore it up, and I would like you to reason from cases and from the structure of the probabilist framework rather than from the literature: I have no library access here, and a list of readings would be useless to me.

The position has three parts.

First, a claim about what the problem is a problem *of*. The usual statement is that evidence the agent already fully accepts cannot raise the probability of a hypothesis that predicts it, since the evidence has probability one and conditioning on a certainty changes nothing. I think this is a misdiagnosis: the pastness of the evidence is incidental. What the framework cannot represent is a certain *kind* of learning, namely coming to see a connection between propositions one already accepts. The textbook astronomical case is one species. Two others seem to me structurally identical: the case in which the hypothesis and the evidence are both long accepted and what is new is only a proof that the one entails the other; and the case in which the evidence was known but was not recognised as relevant, and the new item is a derivation of it from the hypothesis. In all three the agent's confidence in the hypothesis visibly rises, and nothing in a two-place confirmation relation registers the rise.

Second, a claim about where a repair must go. If the failure is a failure of the relata rather than of the credence function, then the popular repair — substitute, for the agent's actual priors, counterfactual or ur-priors describing what she would have believed had she not known the evidence — is mistaken in principle and not merely awkward. Either it permits a single piece of evidence to do confirmatory work twice, once when it was learned and again when the entailment is learned; or it evaluates the hypothesis against a credal state that no one could occupy, since expunging the evidence would require expunging everything that has been inferred from it over decades.

Third, a prescriptive claim: make confirmation a relation that takes the derivation as an argument, indexed to the agent's graded confidence in that derivation. This has consequences I accept. Two agents with identical credence functions over the same algebra can be differently confirmed by the same evidence. A machine-generated proof whose steps the agent cannot survey confirms less, or not at all. And confirmation accrues in the first instance to the hypothesis-plus-derivation pair, so some of it must be split off when the derivation is later found to be flawed.

So: what would count against this, ranked from fatal through serious to merely awkward? Please distinguish what a defender of my view should concede as counting against it from what such a defender would rightly dismiss, and say which of the three claims is load-bearing and which stand or falls with which. I am especially interested in four things.

One: whether the first claim is simply too cheap. If nobody denies that the difficulty generalises to new derivations, then the interesting content is all in the third claim, and I want to know whether the third claim is falsifiable or has been qualified into a research programme rather than a thesis — and if it has, whether that is a defect for a claim of this kind.

Two: whether the forgetting case is a defeater or a point in my favour. An agent who learned the evidence decades ago and has since forgotten it has a low probability for it, and the two-place machinery then permits re-learning the evidence to confirm the hypothesis. That looks like letting a verdict turn on a psychological accident of memory. My view is committed to saying that how you came to be ignorant is irrelevant, but I can imagine being told that this commitment is a cost, not a virtue.

Three: whether the move of relaxing logical omniscience so that the agent can *learn* that the hypothesis entails the evidence — the other standard family of repairs — refutes my second claim or merely relocates the dispute. If it succeeds, how would I tell whether it has succeeded against me rather than against the two-place view?

Four: what my view predicts that its rivals do not, and what observation would discriminate. My own sense is that the most decisive thing would be a case in which the two-place machinery gives the right answer and my three-place view gives a wrong one; the merely awkward thing would be an argument that agent-relativity makes the notion unusable for the purposes of philosophy of science. But tell me if I have that backwards.
