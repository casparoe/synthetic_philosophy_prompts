Ok so I think I've finally got the basic point that heritability is V_G/V_P and therefore a fact about a population in an environment rather than about a trait, but I keep hitting a case where that gives what feels like the wrong answer.

Take an inbred line where everyone is genotypically identical at all the loci that matter for trait T, but they're scattered across different environments. V_G = 0, so heritability = 0 — and sure, I get that we're supposed to read that as "none of the differences between these individuals trace back to genetic differences", not "genes don't matter for T". Its the same reason the heritability of having two eyes is ~0.

But now raise that same genotype in one single controlled environment. Now V_E = 0 too, so V_P = 0 and the ratio is 0/0. To me that's backwards: this is the case where T is about as "genetic" as it gets, and heritability hasn't gone up to 1, it's stopped being defined at all. Shouldn't the measure do something better here? My fix was to redefine heritability counterfactually — hold the environment fixed and ask how much variance you'd see if you randomized genotype instead, since the genes clearly determine the trait. But I cant make that precise and I'm not sure its even the right question. Is the 0/0 thing a real defect in the concept, or am I smuggling something in?
