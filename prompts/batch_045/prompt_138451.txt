i've been stuck on Hofstadter's MU puzzle (the one from GEB) for about a week and I think I've talked myself into a position that's wrong in several ways at once, so bear with me.

Setup as I understand it: you start from MI and you have four rules — stick a U on the end of any string ending in I; double everything after the M; replace any III with a U; delete any UU. The question is whether you can ever reach MU. The answer is supposed to be no, and the standard move is to notice that the number of I's is never divisible by 3 (you start with 1, doubling can't turn a non-multiple of 3 into a multiple, and the III-to-U rule only ever subtracts 3), while MU has zero I's and zero is a multiple of three.

Here's what I can't shake. I wrote a little breadth-first search from MI and ran it about 15 steps deep. It churned out tons of strings, including things like MUUUI (one I, three U's), but MU never showed up. Fine. But then:

(a) Isn't the I-count thing just a cheaper version of the same search? It looks like a filter that rejects candidate strings faster, not a different kind of move, so I dont get why people make such a big deal about it, or about jumping out of the system at all.

(b) I tried adding a fifth rule of my own — if a string ends in I, you may just delete that I — and MU falls right out: MI, MII, MIIII, MIII, MU. So if the impossibility evaporates the moment you tweak the rule set, then MU is underivable was never a fact about MU at all, only a fact about an arbitrary pile of rules, and arbitrary piles of rules don't tell you anything about anything.

(c) Which makes me think this is all just a cute version of Godel: any fixed formal system misses some truths, you can always patch one by adding the missing axiom, and that's basically the Lucas/Penrose point that a mind can see truths no formal system can reach.
