I serve as one of three members of the appeals panel of a large online gaming platform. Our rules provide for permanent bans on accounts that make threats of offline violence, and our guidance note insists that the question is never what a message describes but what it does: whether it constitutes an act of threatening rather than a statement about, or expression of a wish about, violence — and that a message's frightening its recipient is a consequence of such an act rather than a test for it. We have never settled whether our real test concerns what the speaker meant, what the words conventionally do in the setting they were uttered in, or what the message did to the person who received it.

Our precedents: (1) in post-match voice chat a player told an opponent he was going to kill him; neither knew who the other was and both were mid-match — not a threat, in-character trash talk. (2) A player who had obtained another player's real name and city messaged him that he was going to find him — threat. (3) A player posted a message naming no future action whatsoever, but listing the target's real name, home town and workplace, and adding that he had friends in that town — threat. (4) A player reported months of graphic violent messages from someone with whom he had freely traded such messages, conceding at the hearing that he had found them funny at the time, had sent worse, and reported them only after a falling-out — not a threat. (5) In a public forum a player wrote that someone should shoot the developers — not a threat; the post expressed a wish, named no target of the speaker's own, and committed him to nothing.

The new case: a streamer with a large and active audience, annoyed by another player, said on air that someone should find out where the player lives, adding that he was not telling anyone to do anything and only wanted the address. Viewers identified the player, posted his street and a photograph of his house, and a clip reached him; he and his family left home for a week. His lawyer argues that this is a distinct kind of act, which she calls a delegated threat: the speaker supplies the force and the audience supplies the capacity he lacks, so an utterance can be a threat from him even though he neither commits to acting nor could act himself, provided it reaches the target as one.

What should we decide if we are to be consistent with our own precedents? Set out the candidate rules our five verdicts are equally compatible with, what each predicts here, which precedent is the closest analogue, and which single further case I could put to the panel that would best tell the leading rules apart. I would also like your view on whether delegated threat is a coherent category at all, or collapses into something our existing vocabulary already covers — and if it is coherent, what further condition it needs in order not to make every angry remark about a person a threat.
