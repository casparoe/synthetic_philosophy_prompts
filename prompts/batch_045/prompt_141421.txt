THE QUESTION

Question 3 (20 marks). The "boundary problem" is the problem of who is entitled to be a member of the demos. It is standardly distinguished from the "scope problem": the problem of what the demos is entitled to decide over.

(a) Explain the difference between the two problems in your own words, and explain why the boundary problem generates a circularity that the scope problem does not. [5 marks]

(b) Two familiar proposed criteria for membership: the ALL-AFFECTED PRINCIPLE (the demos for a decision consists of all and only those whose interests are affected by it) and the ALL-SUBJECTED PRINCIPLE (the demos consists of all and only those who are subject to the coercion of the political order making the decision). Apply each to the following case: Ruritania, a wealthy state, decides to sharply restrict immigration from Belgravia, its much poorer neighbour. Which criterion gives the more determinate answer about who is in the demos, and why? [8 marks]

(c) A third proposal, which I'll call the NO-EXIT CRITERION: the demos for a decision consists of all and only those who cannot avoid the effects of that decision at reasonable cost by relocating to another jurisdiction. Does the no-exit criterion escape the central objection to the all-affected principle? Does it collapse into the all-subjected principle? Give at least one case where the no-exit criterion and the all-subjected principle come apart. [7 marks]

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER

(a) The boundary problem asks who the "we" is when we say that a decision was made democratically. The scope problem asks which decisions the "we" is entitled to take collectively in the first place, and at what level. In a sense the scope problem is the boundary problem applied to issues rather than persons. They're connected — the bigger the unit that decides, the more likely any given decision reaches people outside it — but they come apart: you can have a perfectly settled demos and still argue about whether schooling or transport is decided locally or nationally.

The boundary problem has a circularity that the scope problem doesn't. To hold a vote on who is to be in the demos you already need some group doing the voting, and the membership of that group is precisely what's in dispute, so you can never constitute the demos by democratic means. With scope there's no equivalent problem, because you can in principle let an already constituted demos vote on what it gets to decide over.

(b) The all-affected principle has a well-known difficulty: in a connected world almost everyone's interests are affected by almost every decision, so the principle threatens to make every demos global. In the Ruritania case this shows up right away. Ruritania's decision to close the border affects Belgravians who want to move far more than it affects most Ruritanians, so the principle seems to say that the people being excluded have to be included in the demos that decides on excluding them. There's also no natural stopping point: Ruritanian wages, house prices, and Belgravian wages are all affected too, so there's no non-arbitrary way of saying whose affected interests count.

The all-subjected principle is best understood as a narrower version of the same thought: coercion is just one way of being affected, so the demos is whoever is affected by being coerced. It looks better on this case, because whether you are subject to a legal order sounds like a yes-or-no matter rather than a matter of degree, which makes it more determinate than the all-affected principle. But I think it gives the same result here, and the same difficulty. Belgravians at the border are coerced by Ruritanian law — that is what border enforcement is — so they're in the demos; and anyone who hears about the policy and would like to come is deterred by the threat of force, which is arguably coercion as well. So the coercion principle also reaches the excluded, and it inherits the all-affected principle's over-inclusiveness.

That means it is committed to a conclusion about the case: if the people being kept out have to be part of the demos that decides whether to keep them out, then the exclusion can't be legitimate, since no group votes to exclude itself. Ruritania would have to open its borders. The two criteria are therefore less different than they look, though the all-subjected one is at least more determinate.

(c) The no-exit criterion does escape the worst version of the over-inclusion objection. Affectedness is everywhere, but being unable to leave is not: plenty of people are affected by Ruritanian policy and could relocate without much trouble, so they drop out of the demos. That yields a smaller and more stable demos than the all-affected principle, and it explains the intuition that the Belgravian with no alternative has a stronger claim to a say than the Ruritanian who is mildly inconvenienced.

It doesn't collapse into the all-subjected principle either. The two come apart in both directions. A tourist in Ruritania is fully subject to Ruritanian law for the two weeks she is there — she can be fined, arrested, deported — but she can leave whenever she likes, so the no-exit criterion leaves her out. Conversely, someone in a region the state has effectively abandoned, where nothing is enforced any more, may be unable to leave because of poverty or because no one else will take her, even though no coercion is being exercised over her at all. So the criteria track different properties: one is about the costs of leaving, the other about being governed.

One worry: the no-exit criterion makes membership depend on facts about other jurisdictions, since how costly leaving is depends on who else will admit you. Ruritania's demos would grow or shrink with Belgravia's and everyone else's policies, which seems odd. It also has the strange consequence that the rich, who can always leave, have the weakest claim to a vote.

But all three criteria are attempts to answer the same question, which is where the boundary should be drawn. Once we know who is in the demos we also know which territory the state may rightfully control, since the demos just is the people who live on the territory in question.
