I work as a dialysis technician, have no philosophy training, and got into this two winters ago: a podcast sent me to the old that-than-which-nothing-greater-can-be-conceived argument, I bought a secondhand modal logic textbook to understand the modern "positive property" reconstruction, and I have been chewing on it ever since. I now have two arguments I cannot get out of, and I want to know which line to reject and what rejecting it costs.

Here is the reconstruction as I have it. Call a property positive when it is better to have than to lack. (0) If a property is positive, then anything it necessarily brings with it is positive too. (1) For any property, exactly one of it and its negation is positive. (2) Every positive property is possibly instantiated — this is supposed to follow from (0) and (1), since a property that could not be instantiated would vacuously bring its own negation with it. (3) Positivity is rigid: if a property is positive, it is positive in every possible world. (4) Being godlike, that is, having every positive property, is itself positive. (5) Necessary existence is positive. (6) The modal logic is S5, in which whatever could have been necessary is necessary. From these: (7) necessarily, something godlike exists — because (2) and (4) make a godlike being possible, (0), (1) and (3) make godlikeness the essence of whatever has it, so that being has necessary existence wherever it is, its existence in one world would be existence in all of them, and (6) converts possibly-necessarily into necessarily. Now the trouble. Take any true proposition p; mine is that my daughter was born in 2011. Consider the property of being such that p. (8) By (1), either it or its negation is positive. (9) If its negation is positive, then by (3) it is positive in every world, so by (7) some being in every world has it; a thing has that property only if not-p; so necessarily not-p, hence not-p, contradicting the truth of p. So the negation is not positive. (10) Therefore, by (1), the property itself is positive, and by the same reasoning as in (9), necessarily p. (11) Since p was arbitrary, every truth is necessary: nothing could have been otherwise, nobody could have done otherwise, and the argument has destroyed the very modality it was built out of.

My friend Mira — she did one philosophy minor, which in our house counts as an authority — says this is the familiar overkill problem and that the way out is to restrict (1) so that positivity ranges only over purely qualitative properties, with no haecceities and no gerrymandered world-indexed properties like mine, which blocks (8). So here is my second argument, which uses only properties I think she will have to admit are qualitative and never mentions a world-indexed property. (a) Omniscience is positive. (b) So by (7), in every possible world there is a being that is godlike and therefore omniscient. (c) Let t be the true but contingent claim that my daughter was born in 2011; there is a possible world w in which she was born in 2012 instead. (d) The being that exists in w is omniscient, and an omniscient being knows every truth. (e) t is a truth. (f) So the being in w knows that my daughter was born in 2011. (g) Knowledge is factive: if someone knows that q, then q. (h) So in w my daughter was born in 2011, contradicting (c). (i) And w was arbitrary, so the same holds for every alternative to every contingent truth: there are no contingent truths. Mira says there is a scope error between (d) and (f), and that "knows every truth" may only mean knows every truth of the world it is in, so that the being in w knows the 2012 truth and never knows t at all. I do not find that satisfying: a being that exists in every world and is omniscient in every world ought, it seems to me, to know which world is the actual one and what happened in it — otherwise what, exactly, is it omniscient about?

So: which line goes? If it is (5), please tell me why that premise is more expensive than it looks, since to me it is nearly the most obvious one on the list — a being that cannot fail to exist seems greater than one that can. If it is (0) or (1) or (3) or (6), I want to see why, and what each of those costs the original argument. If the second argument is the broken one, name the broken step precisely, in the vocabulary of scope and necessity, tell me whether there is any way of stating the omniscience of a necessary being that really does deliver (f) — and if there is not, say whether that is an interesting fact about omniscience or merely a technical dodge. Is my second argument just the first one smuggled back in, reintroducing world-indexing through the content of what is known? And if the honest answer is that I should swallow the collapse and learn to live with a single possible world, tell me what that does to the argument itself: if there is only one world, does the step to (7) not become empty? I would most like to keep (6), (1) and the plain fact that my daughter might have been born in a different year. I am also not interested in the island-style parodies; I have read them and they do not touch the modal version.
