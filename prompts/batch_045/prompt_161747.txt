For the past year I have been reading, and re-reading, the argument from the incompleteness theorems against mechanism — the Lucas–Penrose argument and the replies to it — and what strikes me is not that the dispute is unresolved but that its *shape* has not changed in sixty years. The anti-mechanist says: take any consistent formal system strong enough for arithmetic that I can see to be sound; I can then see the truth of a sentence the system cannot prove; so my mathematical capacity outruns that system, and whatever I am, I am not it. The mechanist has three standing replies: I can never identify which system I am, so the premise never gets off the ground; I am not consistent in any case, so there is no Gödel sentence for me to see; and the premise that I *see* the truth quietly substitutes an idealized, infallible, indefinitely patient thinker for the finite and error-prone creature that was supposed to be the candidate mechanism. Both sides have said all of this many times, in nearly the same words. Nobody moves.

I do not want a verdict on mechanism, and I do not want a survey of the literature. I want a diagnosis of the impasse: is it substantive, is it in part verbal, are the two sides answering different questions under one heading, is a hidden formal question doing all the work, or is it a clash of starting intuitions about understanding and rule-following that neither side can argue for? Give me the diagnosis with reasons, and then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given it — or argue that no progress should be expected and say why.

I have been sorting the material with five distinctions, which I set out so that you can use them rather than reconstruct them, and so that you can tell me which of them are doing real work and which are decorations.

First, the **scope problem**. As far as I can tell there are three different theses called mechanism: (a) cognition is derivation of outputs from a fixed, finitely specified axiom system; (b) cognition is a mechanical procedure that may revise its own outputs — retract them finitely often, or converge on the right answer only in the limit, or rewrite its own rules; (c) cognition is whatever some Turing-computable function could produce, whether or not the procedure is surveyable, transparent to me, or specifiable by me. The diagonal construction bites (a) directly, bites (b) only if I am given a stable description of the procedure and its current state, and appears not to touch (c) at all. My suspicion is that a large share of the talking-past-each-other happens here.

Second, the **iteration game**. Read as a contest, the exchange is: the mechanist proposes a system M; the anti-mechanist produces its Gödel sentence; the mechanist extends to M′; and so on, possibly through a transfinite progression of such extensions. That is a formal question about whether either side has a winning strategy in a game of computable self-extension, not a question about minds. I would like to know whether such a question has a settled answer — whether, for instance, some computable iteration decides every Π₁ truth, or whether the completeness results for progressions of this kind require a choice of notation that no mechanical procedure can make. I am not asking for references: give me the *content* of any result you are confident about and argue from that content, and tell me plainly if you are not confident. If the answer cuts both ways, say so; that matters as much as a clean result would.

Third, the **self-application problem**, which I take to be the sharpest thing in the literature. For the argument to tell against *me*, the premise must hold for the system I am supposed to be: I must be able to see that system to be sound. But if that seeing were something I accomplish by carrying out the operations of the system under indictment, then by the second incompleteness theorem the premise is unavailable on the assumption that I am that system. So the anti-mechanist owes an account of seeing that is neither internal to the candidate system nor a restatement of the conclusion. Is this a fair statement of the bind, and is there a way out that is not simply the conclusion in different vocabulary?

Fourth, the **rule-relativity of the Gödel step**. Producing the Gödel sentence of S, given a description of S, is itself a computation; a machine can do it. So what is supposed to be non-mechanical is not the production but the seeing — and the argument then turns entirely on the gap between following a rule for generating G(S) and *recognizing* G(S) as true. That gap is where I suspect the bedrock lies.

Fifth, a caveat I cannot check: the Gödel sentence is defined relative to a chosen formula expressing provability, and the standard unprovability result requires that formula to satisfy the usual derivability conditions. I have a half-memory that with suitably deviant choices the fixed point can even come out provable. If that is right, does it threaten the argument's premise that there is a determinate sentence I can be described as seeing? If I have misremembered, say so and move on; do not build the diagnosis on it.

My own hypothesis, offered as one, is that the dispute is three questions under a single name — a formal question about the limits of computable iteration, an epistemic question about self-knowledge and warranted self-ascription of soundness, and a question about whether a third-personal, functionalist story can capture what the first-personal notion of *seeing* is meant to capture — and that the apparent standoff is the shadow of the fact that each side is confident about a different one of the three and infers from it to the others. There is also, I think, a symmetry worth noticing: the anti-mechanist's idealization of the thinker and the mechanist's retreat to procedures too opaque to be surveyed are both replacements of the actual human by something else, and the disagreement may be partly about which replacement is legitimate for this question. Tell me whether that is the right diagnosis, what it gets wrong, and which of the five points above is doing the most work. State your answer as a diagnosis with reasons; no bibliography, no authors, no reading list.
