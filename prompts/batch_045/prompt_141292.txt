I am writing the final examination for an upper-level ancient philosophy seminar — third-year students, no Greek required, who have read book V of the *Nicomachean Ethics* and book I of the *Politics* in translation. I need one essay question on Aristotle on money and exchange, worth roughly forty-five minutes of a three-hour paper, and I want it built around a crux rather than a topic: it should be unanswerable by reciting the standard three-part summary (that chreia is the real standard holding the exchange community together, that money conventionally represents it, and that money thereby makes unlike goods commensurable).

The crux I have in mind is the apparent tension between the conventionalism of the *Ethics* account — money exists by nomos rather than by nature and can be altered and made useless at will, and demand supplies only a measure good enough for practical purposes — and the teleology of the *Politics*, where wealth-getting has a natural limit and lending at interest is singled out as the most unnatural way of acquiring, on the ground that money was meant for exchange and breed­s no offspring. If money is a mere counter whose standing is wholly a matter of agreement, what content is left for the claim that some employment of it is contrary to nature? And if the answer is that money has a proper function, where in the *Ethics* account is that function supposed to come from?

To stop candidates from recycling a set piece, I want the question to include a short hypothetical case of my own devising: a city that strikes no precious metal but circulates sealed clay tokens, fixed in quantity by decree, neither exportable nor meltable, which in fact serve as the common measure and are accepted by everyone in the city. Candidates should be asked to say, first, how Aristotle's guarantee-of-future-exchange argument would explain the tokens working at all, and, second, whether a lender who charges for the use of those tokens is doing anything unnatural on Aristotle's terms.

Please supply: the question as it would appear on the paper, including the case; a model answer of about 700 to 800 words; and a twenty-mark scheme organized around five must-hit points with the marks for each, together with a one-line note on how candidates typically fail each point. I would also like the model answer to handle the circularity worry — whether prices measure demand or demand fixes prices — and to take a position on how chreia should be read, translations varying between need and demand.

Finally, close with a passage of about 150 words headed "What would change this answer's mind," naming the single textual or philosophical consideration most likely to overturn the reading the model answer gives, and flagging where that reading is most fragile — for instance, if the two books are treated as belonging to different strata of Aristotle's thought, or if the good-enough-for-practical-purposes remark is read as a concession rather than as a qualification of the whole measurement story.

Please write the question, model answer, and marking scheme in American English.
