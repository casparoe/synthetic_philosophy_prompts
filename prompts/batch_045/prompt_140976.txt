I work in pricing and reserving at a mid-size specialty insurer. Two or three times a month I am asked to put a probability on a quantity about which the only honest thing anyone can say is a range: what share of a claims cohort is fraudulent, when the only thing we know is that it sits somewhere between 5% and 40%; how long notification-to-first-payment will run, bounded between 3 and 40 days; whether a loss ratio on a product we have never written will clear a threshold. The number feeds a pricing or reserving decision, it is reviewed by an actuary who did not produce it, and it may end up in front of a regulator. I have no budget for formal expert elicitation, one hour per case, a spreadsheet, and analysts who cannot be assumed to know what a prior is.

The difficulty is not, I think, absence of data. It is that having no information does not determine a distribution. For a share bounded between 5% and 40%, being indifferent between equal-width subintervals of the share, between equal-width subintervals of the odds, and between equal-width subintervals of the log-odds all look like applications of the principle of indifference, and I don't think they give the same answer to the question I actually care about. A colleague tells me this is Bertrand's chord paradox in a business suit. I want a procedure, not a tutorial.

Write me a decision procedure my team can run on one of these bounded quantities and defend in writing. It should include at least the following, in the order an analyst would run them.

A gate, first: when indifference is the wrong instrument altogether rather than a risky one. I have in mind cases where a base rate or historical analogue is available, where the quantity is a count out of a known or knowable denominator, where the stated bounds are themselves soft estimates rather than hard constraints, and where we have already published a number for the same quantity. Say for each whether the procedure stops, downgrades its own output, or proceeds.

A mechanism step: before any distribution is chosen, the analyst must describe the process that would generate a value, and classify it. My rough sense is that a quantity produced by partitioning a finite stock or accumulating additive increments calls for one scale, one produced by compounding or by a ratio of two accumulating things calls for another, and that for any rate the reciprocal is a duration over which indifference is not preserved, so the procedure has to say which of the pair inherits the symmetric structure and why. Make that classification usable by someone without the theory, and say what the analyst is to do when the mechanism is genuinely unknown.

Symmetry tests that eliminate candidates rather than restate a preference. I have a suspicion that re-expressing the quantity as its complement refutes the odds parameterization while leaving the share and log-odds parameterizations standing, and therefore that the test narrows the field without closing it; settle that, and add whatever further tests actually discriminate. Note explicitly which re-expressions are related to one another by an affine map, since on those indifference is coherent and no conflict arises at all.

A step I have been toying with and cannot fully justify, which you should either make precise and place in the sequence with a stated scope, or explain as confused and drop, saying which you did: be indifferent on the scale on which the evidence you expect to gather later combines additively, on the grounds that the eventual sample average or the eventual model will report on that scale, so the prior and the estimator should not be at cross-purposes.

An output format with three parts: the number, a specification-sensitivity figure giving the spread across the parameterizations that survived, and a rule for when the spread straddles the decision threshold, in which case the correct output is not a number but a statement that the estimate is not decision-grade plus the cheapest information that would collapse the spread. Rank those information-gathering options by cost.

Then apply the procedure to three cases. First, the fraud share: bounds 5% to 40%, question is whether the probability that the share exceeds 25% is above or below 30%; give the number each surviving parameterization yields to two decimals. Second, the notification-to-first-payment interval: bounds 3 to 40 days, question is the probability of exceeding 14 days; run it twice, describing the generating mechanism in two different but equally plausible ways, and show the mechanism step producing two different answers, then say which description an actuary would be right to demand evidence for. Third, construct a case of your own in which the correct output is a refusal — realistic enough that I would actually bring it to you, with numbers that look reassuring, and not a strawman.

Finally: a one-line formal gloss per step, in a separate column or footnote, for the reviewer who signs off, since the operational wording will be plain throughout and must not require my analysts to learn any of this vocabulary. Mark every point at which judgment cannot be eliminated and someone has to sign their name. Say what the procedure cannot do. As a calibration check, run it on the classic chord problem and state which of the standard answers it licenses, or whether it licenses none. And tell me which single step does the most work, and what collapses if that step is removed.
