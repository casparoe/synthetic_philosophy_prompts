So I'm teaching the conservatism seminar again this spring and there's one question I keep failing to answer cleanly, so I want you to just take a swing at it.

Setup. Imagine a state government passes what everybody in the papers is calling the Reasons-First Act. Any standing rule, licensing requirement, or regulation that's been on the books more than thirty years gets repealed automatically in two years unless the agency that owns it files a short public statement — 200 words, say — naming the specific, presently-existing harm the rule prevents. Burden of proof on the keepers; no deference to the fact that it's been there a while; no grandfathering just because the original reasons have been forgotten.

The pitch is explicitly Chestertonian, and that's what I can't get around. The sponsors keep saying: we are not tearing down fences blindly, we are finally asking why the fence is there, and if nobody can say, that is itself evidence that there is nothing to say. And they add a second argument that I think is the real one: Chesterton's reformer couldn't find out why the fence was put up because back then nobody could run a randomized trial or a cost-benefit analysis or dig up thirty years of administrative data. Now we can actually go and look. So burden-shifting isn't iconoclasm, it's just epistemology catching up with the recieved wisdom.

What I want from you: what would Burke actually say about this thing? And I mean actually — not "he was a conservative, so he'd hate it." Where does the knife go in, precisely?

Four things I'd like you to get at, in whatever order serves the answer:

One, is Chesterton's fence even the same principle as Burke's, or is it a liberalization of it? Chesterton says: find out why it's there, and then you may remove it. My read of Burke is that a lot of what's load-bearing in an institution isn't the sort of thing anybody can state in 200 words even in principle — the reasons live in the habits of the people who work inside it, or in some equilibrium nobody ever chose and nobody can reconstruct. If that's right, the Act satisfies Chesterton's letter while gutting Burke's spirit, and the sponsors can honestly say they did what was asked of them. Tell me whether you buy that or whether I'm flattering myself.

Two, the evidence-based-policy move. Does cheap measurement and RCTs dissolve the Burkean worry, or just relocate it — now the unexamined fence is the choice of metric, the way the trial was powered, what counted as a harm in the first place? Is there a stopping point here or is it turtles all the way down?

Three, where would Burke have to concede? Some rules exist only because a guild wanted them and nobody defends them now. He isn't a nostalgist and he thought reform was sometimes the only way to preserve anything. So what does he grant the reformers, and what does he tell them to do instead of the 200-word filing?

Four, and this is the bit I care about most: the partnership of the living, the dead and the unborn. Does the Act wrong the unborn, or the dead, or neither — or is that whole bit of rhetoric doing no work in this particular case?

Six hundred words, tops. Lead with a single sentence that captures his verdict; no throat-clearing and no "it depends." Then spend the rest on the parts above, and finish with the one line from the reformers' side you think he'd find hardest to answer.
