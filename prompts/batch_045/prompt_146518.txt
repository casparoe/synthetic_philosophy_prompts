I am a PhD student in art history working on the provenance of collections assembled during the colonial period, and for the past two years I have sat as the graduate member of the Provenance and Restitution Committee of the university museum where my doctoral project is based — an encyclopedic collection of roughly ninety thousand objects, about a third of them acquired between 1880 and 1940 in territories then under British, French or German administration. I keep the minutes. The chair has asked me to draft the options paper for our next docket item, and before I write it I would like you to help me think it through.

Our terms of reference, which the board has never amended, permit us to recommend permanent transfer of an object out of the collection only where the acquisition involved force, fraud or duress; in every other case we are confined to loans, shared-stewardship agreements and collaborative research. Beyond that the committee has repeatedly declined to adopt a general policy and insists on deciding case by case, so the precedents are all we have. Here are the seven that bear on the new item:

1. Objects looted during a punitive expedition of the 1897 Benin pattern and claimed by the successor state's national museum: permanent transfer, unconditional. We also funded a storage wing at the receiving museum.
2. A marble frieze removed in the early nineteenth century under a written permit from the imperial authority then in force, claimed by the successor state: no permanent transfer. Rotating four-year renewable loan of part of the group, on the reasoning that the acquisition was lawful when made and that the museum's comparative display is itself a good.
3. Forty brass and ivory objects bought at a 1931 estate auction from a colonial officer's effects, with no field documentation and no source named: permanent transfer of thirty-seven. The minutes say the gap in the record is itself a product of the circumstances in which the objects left, and that the museum should bear the cost of its own ignorance. Three were retained for the teaching collection under a joint research agreement.
4. Ancestral remains taken without consent from a burial ground in a settler colony in 1908 and claimed by a descent community whose standing the national museum disputed: transferred directly to that community, which reburied them. This ran under our separate ancestral-remains policy, which requires transfer on request whatever the circumstances of acquisition, so I have never been sure whether it tells us anything about the rest of the docket.
5. Tools and a shrine figure sold in 1924 by a living maker to a travelling collector for what his grandson says was a fraction of their worth, with no threat documented: no permanent transfer. We offered a long loan and paid for scans and replicas for the family. The minutes say that inequality of bargaining power is not duress, and that unwinding the whole market in colonial-era ethnographica would be to decide by catalogue rather than by case.
6. Objects from a polity annexed in the 1890s whose territory is now divided between two states, both claiming, with no ceremonial claimant: deferred indefinitely and kept in store, on the ground that we will not make a transfer whose effect is to ratify a line drawn by the administration that took the objects.
7. Textiles claimed by a national museum whose storage our own conservators reported as inadequate and whose governing board had just been suspended after a coup: transfer deferred rather than refused, converted into a five-year shared-stewardship arrangement with a conservation grant and staff exchange. The minutes say conservation capacity is not a permanent bar but that we will not transfer into known risk.

The new item is a group of thirty-four objects — a beaded crown, two ivory armlets, four brass hip-masks, eleven ceremonial cloths, a staff, and associated pieces whose function we have never identified. They were bought by the museum in 1912 from a coastal trading company at a public auction in the port capital. The file holds the bill of sale, a valuation, and a short note from the district officer saying the objects came from the interior. The price was within the range comparable regalia fetched at port auctions in those years. In the eighteen months before the sale the title-holder had signed a protection treaty and been threatened with deposition in favour of a rival after a tax revolt; the threat was withdrawn two months before the auction. A colonial intelligence report says he sold regalia to meet the tax and purchase arms, and he sold further pieces to two other European buyers in the same period at similar prices, without recorded protest. Our 1912 correspondence was weeded in 1974 and we have never established the company's source.

There are two claimants. The National Museum of the successor state, independent since 1960, claims all thirty-four as national patrimony, has a climate-controlled store opened two years ago, and offers to display twenty permanently and lend material back to us. The council of title-holders at the ritual centre claims them as the insignia of a living office: the present title-holder cannot be enthroned without them, they must be kept in a shrine, handled only by initiated officials, not displayed to the uninitiated and not photographed beyond a condition record; the council will sign a conservation agreement and allow two condition checks a year. Two further complications: the crown and two cloths were conserved here in the 1970s with a synthetic consolidant and steel pins, which the shrine's protocol treats as spoiling them, though the council wants them regardless; and the beaded crown is separately claimed by the council of a neighbouring polity, which says it was taken from them in an 1880 raid, and which has an oral-history account and a missionary's 1882 description of the object in their shrine.

What I need is an options paper, not a polemic — the sort of thing I can put in front of a committee whose members disagree with each other and with me. Specifically: the two or three decision rules that fit all seven precedents as stated, with a note on which precedent each rule has to strain to accommodate; what each rule predicts on the three questions that actually matter here, namely transfer or no transfer, which claimant, and on what conditions; which precedent is the closest analog and which cuts the other way; your best guess at what this committee will do, set apart from what it should do if it wants to be consistent with itself; and an honest statement of where the precedents genuinely run out, naming the exact hinge. I would also like your view on whether the ancestral-remains decision tells us anything about the rest of the docket given that it went through a different policy, on which of my two complications actually changes the analysis and which is a distraction, and on the one further item I could put on the docket that would best separate the surviving rules from one another. I should say that I find the structural reading of duress attractive, so please give me the strongest available version of the opposite case as well — I have to write it anyway.
