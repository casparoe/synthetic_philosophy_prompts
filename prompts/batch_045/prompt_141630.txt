Two philosophers have agreed to an adversarial collaboration on Fitch's paradox of knowability, and I need the joint statement they would sign: a restrictionist, who holds that the paradox refutes only an unrestricted formulation of the knowability principle, and a realist, who holds that it is a sound reductio of any knowability principle worth wanting.

Shared background, which the document may rely on without re-arguing it: read knowability as the atemporal thesis that for every truth p it is possible that some agent knows p, with knowledge factive and distributing over conjunction, and with possibility treated as a normal modality ranging over consistent worlds, so that no contradiction is possible. Under that background the Fitch construction is valid: take any unknown truth p, form the proposition that p and that p is not known, and the principle renders a contradiction possible. Both parties accept this, and both accept that the live question is what a response would have to achieve, not whether the derivation works. The restrictionist's response confines the principle to propositions whose truth is compossible with their being known, the Cartesian restriction as it is usually called, which excludes the Fitch instance at the cost of narrowing the quantifier. The realist's response is to conclude that truth outruns possible knowledge: some truths are in principle unknowable, and any restriction that escapes the paradox is either specified by a criterion that already mentions knowledge or drawn just tightly enough to exclude the troublesome instances.

Three materials I want built into the document, because I think the usual framing ignores all three.

(a) Distinguish atemporal knowability from historicized knowability: for every truth p there is some time at or after the present, in the actual history, at which some actual or future inquirer knows p. My hunch is that historicizing blocks the original instance, since the proposition that p and that p is unknown at the present time can be known later without contradiction, but that the argument re-runs with an index-free ignorance clause, that p and that p is known by no one at any time, and collapses into the claim that every truth is eventually known, which is the same absurdity spread over time rather than removed. Check both derivations and correct me if the hunch is wrong.

(b) Take a Π1 statement of arithmetic that is true but unprovable in the standard axiomatization, and suppose for the sake of the argument that Goldbach's conjecture is such a statement. It is not Moorean: nothing in its content resists being known, so the Cartesian restriction does not exclude it, and the restrictionist is committed to calling it knowable while the realist is free to deny that. Say what coming to know such a proposition would have to consist in, and have both sides record what they take the case to show.

(c) The principle is wanted for anti-realist semantic work, not as a platitude: it is meant to underwrite the manifestation argument, on which understanding a sentence cannot outrun the ability to recognize that its truth conditions obtain. Test that with a sentence type the realist regards as recalcitrant, say a subjunctive conditional about an unactualized possibility such as that if this match had been struck it would have lit, and ask whether the restricted principle still carries the argument or whether it secures only a platitude.

Format: exactly twelve numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no preamble and no closing summary. Items 1 to 6 are the agreed claims, each of which both parties could sign, covering at least the shared formalism, what a satisfactory response would have to achieve, and what would count as merely relabelling the problem. Items 7 to 9 are the residual disagreements, flattened out of a table: for each, give the restrictionist's own formulation first, then the realist's, and say in the same item whether that disagreement is substantive or merely verbal. Items 10 to 12 are the cruxes: name the sub-question, give the derivation or case from (a), (b) or (c) that both accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance.
