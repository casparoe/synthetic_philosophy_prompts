I have been working through Kavka's toxin puzzle for a while and have ended up holding a package of views that I cannot tell is stable. Let me fix the setup I am reasoning about, since the variants below all depart from it.

The base case: at midnight tonight an eccentric billionaire will pay you a very large sum if, at exactly midnight, you have the intention of drinking at noon tomorrow a vial of a toxin that will make you thoroughly miserable for about a day. The money is paid for the intention alone. Nothing requires you to drink, and once midnight has passed you will have no reason to drink. The billionaire's machine reads your intention at midnight and cannot be fooled by pretending, by resolving to try, or by self-deception.

My commitments, in no particular order:

1. Kavka's claim about the base case is correct: at midnight you can genuinely form the intention to drink at noon, and thereby earn the money. The state you form is a real intention — not a wish, a desire, a fantasy, a pretense, or an intention to try to form an intention.

2. I accept a broadly planning-theoretic functional-role account of what intentions are. To intend to A is to be in a state whose role is to settle the deliberative question whether to A, to resist reconsideration absent new information, to guide and sustain action toward A, and to dispose one to A in the relevant circumstances. A state that plays none of this role is not an intention, whatever the agent is inclined to call it.

3. I think the popular escape hatch is a distraction and I want it closed off for the purposes of this discussion: read the case so that the agent is fully rational, has excellent knowledge of her own future reasons and conduct, and is as certain as anyone ever is that she will not drink. Do not let the puzzle be solved by the residual chance of a slip, akrasia, confusion, or a trembling hand. On that reading, I still say she can form the intention.

4. Settledness: once you intend to A, the question whether to A is closed for you. Reopening it without new information or a change in your beliefs about your reasons is a form of practical incoherence, not merely a change of mind you happen to be permitted.

5. No-execution: forming the intention generates no reason to drink and no requirement to drink. The agent who forms it at midnight and does not drink at noon has violated nothing, and is not akratic.

6. Reasons for intending can come entirely apart from reasons for acting: you can have decisive reason to be in the state of intending to A while having no reason at all to A. The billionaire's offer is the paradigm case.

7. There is a control asymmetry: intentions can be formed at will in a way that beliefs cannot. This is part of why the puzzle arises about intending rather than about believing, and I take the asymmetry to be real rather than apparent.

8. Variant, the no-reward control: same toxin, same self-knowledge, no payment and nothing else at stake. My verdict is that here you could not form the intention — the reward is not merely an incentive to make the attempt, it is what makes the intention available to you at all.

9. Variant, the commitment device: at midnight you may also, if you wish, sign an irrevocable contract that will compel you to drink at noon (or exact a large penalty if you do not). You need not sign it. My verdict is that with the device available, forming the intention is easy and indisputably yields a genuine intention; but I also hold that the device is unnecessary, and that the intention formed without it in the base case is the very same kind of state.

10. Variant, the amnesia: after the machine reads you at midnight you will be given a drug that erases every memory of the offer, the money and the intention; at noon you will be handed the vial with no reason to drink and will decline. My verdict is that this changes nothing — you can still form the intention at midnight, and it is no less an intention for being guaranteed to be downstream-inert.

11. Variant, the screened counterfactual: the billionaire announces that he does not care what you consciously avow at midnight; he will pay only if he judges, at noon, that you would have drunk had the money been made conditional on drinking. My verdict is that this version is genuinely harder, and I am inclined to say that here you cannot produce what is demanded of you.

12. Methodologically, I take the question to be first-personal and practical: what matters is what the agent at midnight can do and whether the state she forms is an intention, not whether any observer, machine, or billionaire could tell that she had formed one.

What I want from you is an audit of this set rather than a survey of positions on the puzzle. Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension with each other, and which only look as though they are? Order the real tensions by severity rather than listing them as equals. For each real tension, say precisely what would have to be given up, what the cheapest available revision would be, and what that revision costs elsewhere in the set. For the merely apparent ones, say why they dissolve. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most of the package at once, and whether my verdicts on variants 9 and 11 are doing work I have not noticed — in particular whether the contrast between them is itself an argument against holding 1 and 2 together. If any of my variants is underspecified in a way that changes the answer, say so instead of silently filling in the detail.
