I am revising the second chapter of a dissertation on individuality, and before I present the chapter at a work-in-progress session I would like an audit of the package I have ended up with. Below are the commitments as I would actually defend them. What I want is not a survey of the literature but a check on whether the set is jointly tenable: which items pull against which, which only look as though they do, and for each real tension what would have to go and what giving it up would cost me. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally serious, and tell me at the end which single revision would clean up the most.

(i) The identity of indiscernibles is false, and necessarily so. Black's two-sphere world — an otherwise empty universe containing exactly two perfectly similar iron spheres at some distance from one another — is a genuine metaphysical possibility, and in it there are two numerically distinct objects that share every purely qualitative monadic property. I also hold that this possibility is not itself a haecceitistic one: a world containing two things differs qualitatively from a world containing one, since number is not the sort of thing that has to be supplied by a thisness.

(ii) What survives is a weak principle: necessarily, any two distinct concrete objects are weakly discernible, that is, there is some irreflexive relation each bears to the other — a genuine one, not mere non-identity, since otherwise the principle is vacuous. In Black's world the distance relation does the work: each sphere stands one mile from a sphere, and neither stands one mile from itself.

(iii) There are no haecceities and no bare particulars; nothing non-qualitative individuates an object. I mean this as more than a negative thesis. My hope is that being the node it is in the total relational structure is what being that object consists in, since once (i) is granted I have nothing else to put in its place.

(iv) The anti-haecceitism extends to modality: a permutation of indiscernibles is not a difference. In Black's world there is no fact of the matter which sphere is which, and there are no possible worlds that differ only in that respect.

(v) I am a relationist about space: no substantival space, no bare positions, spatial facts are facts about relations among material objects. I arrived at this through the shift arguments and I would still appeal to them.

(vi) This is the case I have been relying on against my opponents, and I take it to improve on Black's. Take Black's two spheres, but suppose that one of them rotates about the axis joining their centres while the other does not. Stipulate that the spheres are perfectly rigid and perfectly homogeneous, so that the rotation brings with it no deformation, no equatorial bulge and no change in any mass distribution, and set aside relativistic complications. My verdict is that the spheres remain monadically indiscernible — each is an exactly similar iron sphere of the same mass, shape and composition — while the world is no longer the static, globally symmetric world Black described, and there is a real asymmetry in what each of them is doing. That, I argue, makes it a harder case for the defender of the identity of indiscernibles than the original: it cannot be waved off as an artefact of a frozen symmetric universe.

(vii) Finally, a verdict I would like to fall out of the package rather than be stipulated separately: two exactly similar spheres occupying exactly the same place at the same time are, I think, impossible.

Two pointers, so that you do not simply agree with me about where the trouble lies. A colleague insists that (ii) and (iv) cannot both be true, on the ground that weak discernibility ascribes to each sphere a feature the other lacks, which is only a thisness in disguise; I think that objection is confused and I would like to know whether I am right and why. My own suspicion, by contrast, is that the real strain is between (i) and (v): having given up the identity of indiscernibles, I may have disabled the very argument that led me to relationism in the first place. If I am wrong about where the trouble is, say so plainly, and say which horn of which dilemma does the damage. And if one of my cases is doing no dialectical work at all, I would rather be told that than have it repaired for me.
