i'm putting together a "where I stand" doc for a Rorty reading group we run on Sunday nights (we've done Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature and Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity, and we're now picking through the essay collections), and I want someone to stress-test it before the other four get their hands on it. Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. Antirepresentationalism, thin version: no vocabulary is answerable to reality as it is apart from some vocabulary or other, and the world can cause us to hold beliefs without ever being in a position to tell us which vocabulary to use. Justification is owed to audiences, not to the world.
2. Truth is not a goal of inquiry — inquiry aims at something more like coping, interestingness, or solidarity — though I'm happy to keep the word "true" hanging around as a cautionary flag meaning something like: I've checked and you should too.
3. Ethnocentrism: I can only justify my liberal scruples to people who already share enough of my final vocabulary. There is no neutral court of appeal, and looking for one is just theologizing in secular dress.
4. Cruelty is the worst thing we do, and the reason it's the worst is that pain and humiliation aren't linguistic or cultural artifacts — we share them with animals and pre-linguistic infants, which is what lets them do the work of a moral bedrock. Moral progress is the widening of the circle of people we count as one of us, and it happens through novels, journalism and photographs rather than through arguments.
5. Irony is a private virtue and a public vice. Holding your own final vocabulary lightly is fine in your reading life but corrosive in public, where we need shared, non-ironic premises to get anything done. (I also think this makes Rorty's own published irony a bit of a performative problem for him, but I don't think that sinks the position.)
6. The success of science and engineering needs no correspondence explanation. Newton handed us better tools than Aristotle did without being any nearer to nature's own language. And in practice I trust scientific findings completely — I take the antibiotics, I believe the climate data — and I don't think any of that collides with (1) or (2).
7. This one is my own addition, not something Rorty says, and it's the bit I argue about most. Call it the foothold condition: for something to count as a criticism of a vocabulary, it has to be statable partly in that vocabulary — it has to use some bit of the old vocabulary to show that the old vocabulary is getting in its own way. If you can't do that, you don't have criticism, you have replacement, and replacement isn't the kind of thing you can give a reason for; you just swap tools, and the swap is caused, not justified.
8. But I also want to say that genuinely new vocabularies — Galilean, Romantic, Freudian — can be radically discontinuous with what they replaced and could not have been argued for to the people who lived inside the old ones. Those people really couldn't be given a reason to switch.
9. And a verdict I want to hang onto: a vocabulary can become obsolete and be rightly abandoned, and that's more than a change of fashion.

Then there's a made-up case I keep coming back to. Suppose a community a few centuries out — call them the Tessellate — whose vocabulary has no true/false distinction at all, only "endorsed here and now" and "not endorsed," and who are stable, predictively successful, and not obviously more miserable than us. I want to say three things about them: they have lost something, it would be bad if we became like them, and we would be right to resist the change. But I can't see how to get any of those verdicts out of (1), (2) and (3).

So: audit the set. Which of (1) through (9) plus the Tessellate verdicts are genuinely in tension with each other, which pairs only look like they are, and for each real tension what would have to give and what giving it costs me elsewhere. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, say which single revision would clean up the most, and please be specific about where the Tessellate verdicts are actually coming from — I can't tell if they're smuggled in from somewhere I haven't admitted to holding. My hunch is the trouble is in (7), a friend says it's in (4), and we can't settle it.
