Short-answer question (15 points): In his essay on the balance of trade and his essay on money, David Hume criticizes the mercantilist view that a nation can enrich itself by securing a lasting favorable balance of trade and hoarding the resulting gold. (a) Explain Hume's price-specie flow mechanism, including why he thinks a favorable balance cannot be maintained indefinitely. (b) Explain Hume's point about the interval between the acquisition of money and the rise of prices, and say whether, for Hume, the resulting stimulus to industry is temporary or permanent. (c) Finally, say whether Hume's argument, as he gives it, depends on a commodity-money (gold-standard) setting, and explain why.

Here is the student's answer to grade:

Hume's central anti-mercantilist argument is the price-specie flow mechanism. He assumes a commodity money standard in which money is gold. If a country runs a trade surplus, gold flows in to pay for the net exports. This inflow raises the domestic quantity of money. Via the quantity theory of money, a larger money supply eventually raises domestic prices. Higher domestic prices make the country's exports dearer abroad and imports cheaper at home, so net exports fall. The trade surplus therefore tends to disappear automatically. Hence a country cannot permanently maintain a favorable balance of trade, and money cannot be the same thing as real wealth: real wealth is the stock of useful goods and the industry of the people, not the quantity of gold.

On the interval point: Hume notices that the adjustment is not instantaneous. In the interval between the arrival of new money and the full rise in prices, the extra money encourages industry and can have a good effect on output. But Hume is clear that this effect is temporary. Once prices have fully adjusted to the larger money stock, the stimulus vanishes, and in the long run the quantity of money does not affect real wealth. So Hume does not think that an influx of gold makes a country permanently richer.

Because he makes the stimulative effect turn on that interval, Hume also thought that a one-time increase in the money supply would permanently raise the country's real wealth, provided the increase was large enough to keep industry working at a higher level before prices caught up. This is why he rejected sudden inflations but endorsed gradual ones as a way of securing lasting economic growth.

Finally, Hume's argument does depend on a commodity-money setting. The mechanism works because an export surplus mechanically brings in gold and expands the money supply. Under a modern fiat system with floating exchange rates, the same self-correcting result would occur in exactly the same way: a trade surplus would raise domestic prices and eliminate the surplus, without any need for currency appreciation, because the real adjustment is always through price-level changes rather than exchange rates.
