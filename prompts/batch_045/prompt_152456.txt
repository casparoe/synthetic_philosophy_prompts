I've been reading the same small corner of the rationalist blogosphere for about ten years, mostly as a lurker, and an argument that came up in a comment thread there has been bothering me for months. I've rewritten it below in my own words, tried to steelman every step, and I still end up somewhere I can't accept. I'm not looking for reading recommendations or a bibliography — I want a diagnosis of the chain itself: which step do you reject, why did that step look compulsory, and what does rejecting it cost me?

The argument:

(1) Fix a speaker and a language. Everything an interpreter could in principle learn about what that speaker's sentences mean is exhausted by the speaker's dispositions to assent to and dissent from sentences under sensory stimulation. That is the whole evidence base; there is no further data of meaning for a hypothesis to answer to.

(2) Take the textbook case: a native says 'gavagai' in the presence of a rabbit. Manuals that render this as being about rabbits, about undetached rabbit-parts, about momentary rabbit-stages, and about the scattered fusion of all rabbits can each be fitted to every disposition to assent the speaker has or could have, provided you make compensating adjustments elsewhere in the grammar — in particular to how identity, counting and quantification get handled. Some candidates get unwieldy, but there is no possible behavioral finding that settles the matter, because any such finding would have to be put to the speaker in a sentence whose own translation is equally unsettled.

(3) Since the rival manuals are indistinguishable by any evidence there could be, there is no fact of the matter about which is correct. The indeterminacy is not our ignorance of a hidden fact; questions of meaning that go beyond the manuals are, as Quine puts the moral, simply not well defined.

(4) Now the twist my friend added. Nothing in (1)–(3) seems to depend on the language being exotic, and Quine himself is said to allow that the indeterminacy applies to speakers of one's own language and even to one's own past utterances. So run it at home: let the speaker be me and the language be present-day English. Define a one-to-one proxy map on everything there is — say, send each rabbit to a rabbit-stage-of-a-rabbit, and similarly for everything else — and reinterpret every predicate as true of the proxies of the things it was true of, permuting the account of what the stimulations are of alongside it. The result, call it schmenglish, leaves my entire pattern of assent and dissent untouched, is no less simple than the homophonic manual, and yet assigns my terms different referents. By (2) and (3), my own word 'rabbit' is as inscrutable in reference as 'gavagai' is.

(5) If the reference of my terms is inscrutable, then what I believe is indeterminate: there is no fact of the matter whether my belief is the belief that there are rabbits or the belief that there are schmabbits, since belief contents are built out of the references of the terms.

(6) Among the things I take myself to believe is the conjunction of (1), (2) and (3) — the indeterminacy thesis itself.

(7) If there is no fact of the matter about which proposition I am entertaining, then there is no fact of the matter about whether what I am asserting is true, and I cannot so much as determinately claim that translation is indeterminate. An assertion with indeterminate content is not one I can stand behind.

(8) So the indeterminacy thesis cannot be determinately asserted by me; and since (4) generalizes to every speaker, not by anyone. The thesis destroys its own assertability, and more generally it collapses into exactly the semantic skepticism its defenders say it does not give you.

I can't accept (8), and if I have to choose I would rather keep (1), (2), (3) and (4) than accept it — the gavagai point still looks right to me when it is aimed at a field linguist, and my friend's permutation version looks like the same point applied closer to home. Two replies in the thread struck me as too quick, though I couldn't say why at the time: one said the flaw is at (3), since underdetermination by evidence is not yet absence of a fact; the other said it is at (5), since inscrutability of reference is not indeterminacy of belief content. Neither felt like it explained why the step looked fine in the first place.

So: name the step you reject and explain what made it look safe. If your diagnosis turns on a difference between the original gavagai case and my friend's schmenglish case, say precisely which feature of the original fails to transfer. Then price the alternatives — for each other step you might have rejected instead, tell me what I would have to give up, and whether that cost is worse than the one you're asking me to pay. Argue it out; don't just tell me what the standard moves are called.
