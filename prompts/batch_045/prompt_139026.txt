I'm retired now — 34 years in maize breeding and quantitative genetics, mostly at a state ag station — and over the last couple of years I've been sitting in on a philosophy-of-biology reading group down at the university, and I've got rather more worked up about the units-of-selection business than is good for me. Everyone there is very fond of the selfish-gene style story and I keep wanting to resist it, but I can't tell whether my resistance is a real position or just professional habit. So: here are my verdicts case by case, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

[a] Sterile workers in an ant colony. I'm happy to say this is a relatedness story — Hamilton's rule, the relatedness asymmetry you get from haplodiploidy, whatever the current version of that is. What I resist is being told that "the colony does better" is a second, additional explanation sitting on top of it. I want to say: one process, described at one level.

[b] Cytoplasmic male sterility. A mitochondrial variant knocks out pollen production; the plant is, from the point of view of its own nuclear genes, damaged goods, but the mitochondrion gets carried in every seed. Then a nuclear restorer turns up and suppresses it. Verdict: two units in conflict, mitochondrial genome and nuclear genome, and the plant is the arena they fight in, not the beneficiary. I'm confident about this one.

[c] A driving allele — the classic segregation distorter that gets itself into more than half the gametes and pays for it with sick or sterile homozygotes. Verdict: gene-level again, straightforwardly. The individual organism is being exploited, not served.

[d] And here's the one that sits badly with [c]. An allele for better camouflage in a moth, or a thicker seed coat in one of my old lines — just a plain advantage in survival and fecundity. Here I dont want to say "really it's the gene that's being selected." I want to say the organism is. Which looks like nonsense, because the bookkeeping in [c] and [d] is the same — same covariance, same change in frequency. Yet my verdicts differ. Either [c] and [d] come apart and I owe you the difference, or they don't and I have to give up [d].

[e] Some sort of restraint trait — insects that lay fewer eggs than they could so the host isn't stripped, or that half-remembered chicken-breeding experiment where selecting whole cages for egg output beat selecting the best individuals. Verdict: I want to say this one needs genuine group selection, and I'm not persuaded by the "it's kin selection underneath" move. This is the verdict I suspect I'll have to surrender.

My own stab at a principle, which I'm sure is crude: a level is a unit of selection for a trait only if the trait's spread doesn't already disappear into the bookkeeping at the levels below it — you should be able to hold the lower-level stuff fixed and still have work left over at the higher level. Relatedly, I keep catching myself running together two quite seperate questions: "unit of selection" as the level at which you'd measure the selection differential to predict the response (which always hands the answer to the gene), and "unit of selection" as the thing whose interests the trait serves (which doesn't). Maybe those are two questions and I've been treating them as one.

So: is there a principle that gets [a] through [e], or close to it? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? And what does it say about the case I keep going back and forth on — genomic imprinting in maize kernels, where alleles from the pollen push for a bigger endosperm and alleles from the mother plant push back. Whose selection is that, and at which level?

Two practical things. No citations please — I've no library access from here, and I'd rather have your own formulation than a name to hang it on. And tell me how confident you are in what you're claiming, and single out the bit you're least sure of.

Give it back to me in five named sections: "The principle" (~150 words), "Where each clause comes from" (~250 words, each clause tied to one of my cases), "Verdicts I'd have to give up" (~150 words), "New cases" (~200 words — the imprinting one, plus at least one case of your own that would actually test the principle), and "Confidence" (~100 words).
