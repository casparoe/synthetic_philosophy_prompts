Hey — I need help turning a half-baked idea into a two-page research proposal, and I'm mostly stuck on the framing rather than the content.

Some context on why this suddenly feels personal: I'm in my second year of an ML PhD, and last summer I rotated through a lab that does shared-autonomy assistive robotics — think a wheelchair-mounted robotic arm that a person steers with a joystick or a head array. We were fitting reward models to a very small number of users with pretty restricted motor repertoires (standard inverse-RL machinery: Boltzmann-ish noisily-optimal demonstrators, max-ent over trajectories, a Bayesian variant with a prior over reward weights), so that when the user's input was wobbly or ambiguous the system could pick what to do on their behalf — bring the cup to his mouth, hand over the straw, reposition, or just wait. What I can't shake is a structural thing that nothing in the pipeline addressed: the behavior we fit on was produced by someone with one particular, fairly narrow action repertoire, and the thing we then used the fitted reward for was choosing actions out of a strictly bigger menu. Even if we'd fit the reward perfectly, in what sense does it tell us what he wanted rather than what he would have done, given what he was able to do? A person who always nudges a cup toward the edge of the table might be doing that because reaching is expensive for them, not because they want the cup near the edge. My half-formed thesis: the underdetermination everyone complains about in inverse RL isn't one problem with one size, and the reason it keeps feeling unresolvable is that we ask "is the reward identified?" as if identification were a property of the data alone. I want to say it's a property of the data together with the class of interventions you intend to deploy the reward under, and that the assistive case is just the sharp end of this, because there the intervention changes the agent's own action set and not merely the world's transition dynamics. Working labels: intervention-relative identification for the general idea, and "capacity-relative" for the special case where the demonstrator's abilities and the deploy-time agent's abilities differ.

What I want from you is the actual proposal document, and I need it to do a few specific things. First, state the question so it has a determinate answer rather than being a gesture — I'm imagining something like a taxonomy of intervention classes (plain in-distribution prediction; changing the environment's dynamics but not what the agent can do; expanding the agent's action set, as assistive tech does; changing the choice set a person faces, i.e. nudging or reranking; maybe changing what the person wants) and, for each, a characterization of the equivalence classes of rewards that are indistinguishable for that class of interventions but not for others — with the conjecture that these classes get finer as the intervention class gets richer, so that the classic many-rewards-fit-one-policy result is just the empty-intervention special case. Second, and this is the part I most need help with: explain why the existing ways of talking about this leave the question open. My sense is that the fights about priors and regularizers — max-ent versus a Bayesian prior over reward weights versus matching feature counts — are proxy fights about what the reward is going to be used for, and that once you say what the deployment intervention class is, several of those disputes either dissolve or become empirical. If that's wrong, say so in the proposal. Third, the approach: what I would literally do over the grant period, including a small empirical stress test (a gridworld and one continuous-control assistive-ish simulation with a deliberately capacity-limited demonstrator, where I fit rewards under the limited action set and then measure how much the reward-consistent models disagree once the action set is expanded — I have standard RL libraries and a decent workstation, but no new human data and no IRB, so everything has to be synthetic or already-public). Fourth, a deliverable for people who aren't me: I keep imagining a one-page checklist for a practitioner that says, given the intervention class you're deploying under, here's the ambiguity that remains in your inferred reward and here's the extra data that would actually shrink it — because in the lab nobody knew whether to collect more trajectories from the same user or to collect a few under a temporarily changed interface. And fifth, a blunt failure section: what would make this project have nothing to say (e.g. the equivalence-class hierarchy collapses and capacity changes turn out not to bite; or the taxonomy is just the existing identifiability literature relabeled — tell me how I'd know), plus a honest scope paragraph for a six-month pilot fellowship at maybe 60% time.
