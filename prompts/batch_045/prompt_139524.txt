Over dinner last night I had a long argument with an old friend that I have not been able to shake, and I would like your help mapping where I am actually exposed.

The subject was whether dreams are experiences. I hold that they are not. On my view, while a person sleeps there is no conscious goings-on for that person at all; what we get in the morning is not the recollection of something that happened to a subject during the night but a report whose causes are neural events that were never themselves an experience of anything. My friend holds the common-sense opposite and thinks my position survives only on a verificationist scruple that nobody should find persuasive any more.

One part of his attack I want you to take seriously, because I think it may be the strongest thing against me. He says my position is really two positions that its defenders slide between. The strong version, descending from Malcolm, says that dream statements are not reports of anything, that there is no experience to be had or lacked, and that the question of what one dreamt has no determinate answer independent of what one is disposed to say. The concessive version, associated with Dennett, grants that something dreamlike occurs but places its occurrence at or around the moment of awakening: the dream is composed then, so nothing that could be called the dream is located in the sleep period itself. My friend then argues that the concessive version collapses into the common-sense view the moment you admit that the composing is itself a conscious episode, since then there is an experience, merely a misdated one; and that the strong version collapses into it the moment you admit that a dreamer can be right or wrong about what went on.

So: what would count against the view I hold? I want a map of its exposure, not a catalogue of objections, and I want it ranked.

Please cover the following. First, the empirical defeaters, which I take to be the most dangerous: work in which trained lucid dreamers produce an agreed signal mid-sleep so that a report can be pinned to a time inside the sleep period; cases where sleepers physically act out what they later report; incorporation of stimuli delivered during sleep into the content of reports; and attempts to decode reported content from brain activity recorded during sleep. Say what each of these actually establishes, and which of them a defender of my position should concede as counting against it rather than wave away as evidence merely that events during sleep cause waking reports. I want the line between what defenders concede and what only critics allege drawn explicitly.

Second, two cases of my own, which I take to be new twists. (a) Suppose reports were collected under conditions that load working memory hard in the first minute after awakening, the way a demanding secondary task would. If the dream is composed at awakening, composition should degrade in a characteristic constructive way — losing temporal order, smoothing, confabulating — whereas if the report is retrieval of an experience it should degrade like memory instead: patchy, partial, sometimes verbatim. Is that asymmetry real, and does it discriminate? (b) Suppose decoding of sleep activity becomes reliable enough that we can read off a sequence of contents during sleep, and that intervening with a stimulus mid-sequence predictably alters both the recorded sequence and the later report. At what point does the causal chain from sleep state to waking composition become so content-preserving that calling the intervening state an experience is no longer a separate hypothesis from mine?

Third, the escape hatches. A defender of my position can always say that composition on awakening is instantaneous and sub-personal, and then no report whatever could refute them. Tell me honestly whether my view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a problem for a position of this kind or something it is entitled to — here compare it, if useful, to verificationist and private-language arguments, which I suspect share the structure. And tell me whether the whole dispute is at bottom verbal, about what is to count as an experience, and whether that would be a defeater for me or a dissolution of the question.

Finally, what would move me personally. I would be moved by a case in which the fine-grained content of a waking report is sensitive to a specific event during sleep in a way that no causal story from sleep state to composing mechanism could explain without itself attributing content to the sleep state. Tell me whether such a demonstration is even conceivable, or whether my position has ruled it out by construction, and give me your bottom line on how exposed I actually am.
