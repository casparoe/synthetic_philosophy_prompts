I'm writing a seminar paper on subsidiarity across four systems — the United States, Germany, Switzerland and the European Union — and I keep hitting the same obstruction. Every formulation of the principle I can find is procedural: it tells you who must justify what, to whom, and how clearly, but never which substantive wrong the principle is meant to prevent. So I want to approach it from the other side and treat the practices as the evidence. Four of them puzzle me. First, in Germany a federal statute that the Länder will have to execute, or that regulates their administrative procedure, cannot take effect without the consent of the Bundesrat, whose members are the Länder governments themselves: the Länder hold something close to a veto over federal legislation not because the subject matter is reserved to them but because they are the ones who will administer it. Yet the same Länder execute a great deal of EU law with, as far as I understand it, only a weaker right of participation in the making of that law — something approaching a veto only where their exclusive competences over schools, culture and broadcasting are touched. Second, in the Union the rule is stated as a comparative test — Article 5(3) of the Treaty on European Union says that in areas which do not fall within its exclusive competence, the Union shall act only if and in so far as the objectives of the proposed action cannot be sufficiently achieved by the Member States, either at central level or at regional and local level, but can rather, by reason of the scale or effects of the proposed action, be better achieved at Union level — and it is operationalised by the Commission giving reasons for its own proposals, by national parliaments (and, in some states, regional parliaments with legislative powers) sending reasoned opinions within a fixed period, and by a mechanism whereby objections reaching a threshold share of the votes allocated to those parliaments force the Commission to review and justify, while a larger majority lets the Council and Parliament send the proposal back. But the same member-state governments that sit in the Council adopt the measure, and the Court of Justice has rarely if ever annulled one on subsidiarity grounds. Third, American courts say that a federal statute will not be read to displace state law unless Congress has made its intention to do so unmistakably clear in the language of the statute — while the very same courts will invalidate a state statute under the dormant commerce clause, a doctrine inferred from a grant of power to a Congress that has said nothing at all. Fourth, in Switzerland no court can set aside a federal statute for exceeding federal competence, since the Federal Supreme Court is bound to apply federal statutes; boundary disputes are settled politically instead, through the optional referendum and through fiscal equalisation among the cantons.

What I would like from you is the implicit theory behind each of these, and the differences between them. My own attempt at a taxonomy gives three candidates, which you should feel free to replace or refine: (a) a reserved-sphere view, on which certain matters belong to a lower level as such and central action over them is a distinct kind of wrong; (b) a comparative-efficiency view, on which allocation is a single test about the achievement of a given objective plus a rule about who bears the burden of proof; and (c) a participation-or-consent view, on which the real wrong is not centralisation but being bound by a decision in the making of which you had no part, so that what is owed to the lower level is a voice or a veto rather than a domain. Then tell me, for each practice, what it strictly presupposes as opposed to what it merely fits well with, and where the practice is philosophically underdetermined because several quite different theories would license exactly what those systems do.

There are four cruxes I especially want addressed. Does the German consent requirement presuppose (a) or (c), and if (c), why is it not inconsistent to withhold the corresponding veto over EU law the Länder execute? Is a self-applied constraint — a legislature judging its own compliance, as the Union in effect does — a normative constraint at all, and how much does it matter whether the boundary question is ultimately settled by a court, by an electorate, or by the body whose power is in question? Does the American asymmetry between demanding clarity from Congress and demanding none from the states show that federalism there is protected as a matter of process rather than substance, or does it show something about who is thought to be the beneficiary of the principle? And is there any upward principle anywhere in this material: none of these rules says that a level must act, or must defer, when another level's failure harms outsiders, and yet Germany's Basic Law also authorises federal legislation where it is required to establish equivalent living conditions across the country, and the Union counts cohesion among member states among its aims — so is that a rival principle, a corollary, or already implicit in the phrase about what cannot be sufficiently achieved by the member states? Finally, say in concrete terms what would have to change in each system if the theory you reconstruct were replaced by one of the others — which existing rule becomes indefensible, and which rule now looks like it was doing no work at all. Please don't give me a bibliography, case citations or a reading list; I have those, and I want the reconstruction itself, roughly 800 to 1000 words, with your distinctions explained in your own terms rather than merely labelled.
