my translation reading group is doing one last session on constrained translation — that enormous Hofstadter book where he keeps re-rendering a short French Renaissance love poem in English while holding on to the rhyme scheme and the beat, and the claim that the scaffolding can survive intact even when almost none of the words do. I want to close the session with a poem instead of a summary, so I need something that demonstrates the argument rather than states it.

The fight I keep having is over a distinction I made up myself, so theres no literature to appeal to, which is why I want a poem to test it: I call a rendering vessel-faithful when the frame survives — rhyme scheme, meter, stanza shape, line count — and the words are just whatever had to be poured in to fill it; and cargo-faithful when the images and the sense survive and the form is whatever will carry them. My friend says the distinction is bogus, because the frame was never neutral packaging: in a poem the rhyme scheme is already doing semantic work, so same form, different words isn't preserving something, it's swapping one lot of meaning for another and then calling the container identical. I think shes mostly right and I still think the distinction earns its keep, and I'd like the poem to be the place where that gets settled — or at least made audible.

So: a villanelle. The two refrains should be the two positions, and since a villanelle's refrains rhyme with each other anyway they'll be locked into the same sound, which is the friends point in miniature. The lines in between should drift — start as attempts at rendering one particular untranslatable line from the source and get looser and further from it stanza by stanza, until in the last quatrain, where the refrains finally arrive side by side, an unchanged refrain reads as a different claim than it did in the first stanza. Don't give the source line and don't quote it: the poem has to work for someone in the room who has never seen the original, and I don't want it leaning on a French phrase to do its work. Don't use the words vessel or cargo in it and don't explain the distinction; someone who has actually thought about formal constraint in translation should be able to point at the line where the drift turns into a reversal and say what was traded for what, and someone who hasn't should just have heard a good villanelle. Keep the refrains short — under eight syllables, they have to survive being said five times — and please no rhymes on -ation or -ity, I've sat through to many translations that lean on those. If a sestina does the same-six-words-rearranged thing better for you, fine, but then the note has to say why. After the poem, in two or three sentences: where the turn is, and which of us it comes out vindicating, or whether it refuses to.
