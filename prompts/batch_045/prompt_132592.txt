I sit on the security review board of a mid-sized payments company, and we are deciding whether to fund a migration of our public-key infrastructure away from RSA and Diffie–Hellman. I have been arguing that we can safely wait, and I would like to know whether my reasoning holds up.

As I understand it, the security of these systems just is the NP-hardness of inverting them: an attacker must recover a private key or find a factorisation, and recovering a solution to a hard search problem is precisely what NP-hardness rules out. Quantum computers, as far as anyone can tell, do not solve NP-complete problems in polynomial time, so the hardness we depend on should survive their arrival. The only quantum result I can see biting is Grover's quadratic speedup for unstructured search, and that is handled by doubling key lengths.

What I cannot square is why the proposed replacements are supposed to be better. The leading candidates are lattice-based, and their selling point is a reduction to the worst-case hardness of certain lattice problems, which sounds, if anything, like a weaker guarantee than NP-hardness. So: am I right that a large enough quantum computer leaves the complexity assumptions behind our public-key systems untouched, that migrating must therefore be justified on grounds other than complexity theory, and that the real risk we are running is a proof of P = NP, against which no choice of algorithm would help us anyway?
