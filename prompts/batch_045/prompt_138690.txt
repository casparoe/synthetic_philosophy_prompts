I convene a monthly philosophy-and-film series, and before each screening I want the twenty-odd regulars sorted into discussion groups by what they actually believe about why anyone voluntarily seeks out art that makes them sad. The aim is that the people across the table from each other should disagree for structural reasons rather than temperamental ones, so the sorting has to go by what they say about cases, never by which explanation they would sign up to if asked. What I need from you is a classification instrument — a decision tree over the logical space of positions, plus a short quiz with a scoring key — not an essay on the problem.

**1. The space before the names.** Before any questions, fix the three or four axes that generate the space, and state them as yes/no or small-menu distinctions rather than as a list of views: for example, whether there is a genuinely unpleasant affective episode at all, whether there is genuine enjoyment, what the enjoyment is directed at (the events, the representation, the emotion itself, or one's own response), and whether the explanation requires the object to be fictional or the work to be good. Every leaf must then be given coordinates on those axes. If you think an axis I would reach for is spurious, say so and say what replacing it costs.

**2. The tree.** Six to eight branch nodes, eight to ten leaves. Each node is a single question phrased so that a filmgoer who has read no aesthetics can answer it, and so that it does not advertise where it leads. Each leaf carries: a name where one is standard and "unnamed" otherwise; one sentence stating the position; its most awkward commitment, phrased as something a holder of the view would rather not concede; and its verdict on each of the four mandatory probes in §3. Your leaves should include distinct cells for at least these, described by mechanism rather than by label: the sadness is merely tolerated as the price of other artistic or cognitive goods; the unpleasantness is defused by distance or by the frame while the affect is actively savoured; the emotion is not really felt but only undergone in imagination; the same passion is turned into pleasure by the manner of its presentation; what is enjoyed is one's own responding rather than what one responds to; the unpleasant episode is a constituent of an overall valuable experience and there is no conflict to resolve; the good is a piece of understanding the work affords, with the sadness as its vehicle or its felt recognition; and the good is a homeostatic release or settling after tension. Add up to two leaves of your own where the space has room, and if you think two of mine ought to be merged, merge them and say what the merged leaf can no longer explain.

**3. Four probes the tree must be able to discriminate.** Each leaf states its verdict on all four, and each appears as a quiz item. (a) A wordless instrumental adagio reliably produces the same lump-in-the-throat, sweet-sorrow state as the saddest scene in a narrative film: no characters, no represented suffering, no fictional world. (b) A nonfiction film of real suffering, formally indistinguishable from fiction and equally well made, produces the same emotional profile; does anything about the enjoyment change. (c) Two versions of the same film, identical craft and identical running time, one ending in devastation and one in a mild bittersweet loss: which does the respondent choose, and does the choice shift with how the week has gone. (d) The same sadness produced by a film the respondent judges artistically cheap and manipulative; is the resulting state equally worth seeking out. Probe (c) is also where I want the dose-response question handled explicitly: whether more sadness is more rewarding, rewarding only up to a ceiling, or irrelevant to the value.

**4. The quiz.** Ten items, three options each, administerable in about ten minutes, in the vocabulary of someone who watches films and not in the vocabulary of philosophy: no item may name a theorist, a school, or a doctrine, and no item may ask the respondent to state the explanation or to pick a label. Every item is a concrete case or a forced comparison; at most one may be a bare self-report. Each option should be something a thoughtful person could actually choose — if an option reads as a strawman, rewrite it rather than leaving it as a foil. Six items beyond the four probes are yours to invent; they should do real discriminating work rather than re-ask a probe in different words.

**5. The scoring key.** Map answer patterns onto leaves rather than onto single questions, with explicit tie-breaking rules and a note on what to do with inconsistent patterns. Give me at least three named impure profiles for combinations that cut across the tree — the person who denies enjoying sad art yet always chooses the devastating version, for instance, or the one who endorses both the insight story and the enjoyment-of-one's-own-response story — since I expect those to be common in the room. Tell me which two leaves you expect to be crowded in practice, which you expect to be empty, and why.

**6. Diagnostics.** Note the unoccupied cells of the space and briefly diagnose each: incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate instead of pretending the questions pull them apart. Identify the single node doing the most discriminating work and the one carrying the least weight, and state the background assumption on which the whole branching structure rests, together with what would show that assumption to be false.

**7. House rules.** No bibliography, no further reading, no citations. Where a position has a standard name or a canonical historical holder you may label it, but if you are unsure of an attribution, describe the position without one; I will check names myself, so do not hedge in the body. Assume the reader has seen a few canonical tragedies and read nothing about them. Keep the whole thing as compact as the constraints allow — if something has to be cut, cut exposition about the views and keep the diagnostic machinery.
