I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, though I've written Monte Carlo simulators for bet sizing and I know the basics of the Kelly criterion and the geometric-rationality arguments, so please don't re-explain them to me. What I want is the 1998 collapse of Long-Term Capital Management analyzed through that lens, in both directions: fund launched in 1994 by John Meriwether with Myron Scholes and Robert Merton among the principals, specializing in convergence and relative-value trades — small mispricings between closely related securities, each worth a few basis points — financed with borrowings, so that their positions were many times the size of their equity (I've seen roughly 25 to 1; if that figure is wrong, work with the order of magnitude) and larger still in derivative notional; in 1998 they lost about $4.6 billion in a few months, and in late September a group of banks put in $3.65 billion in a recapitalization brokered by the New York Fed, with the fund wound down by 2000.

Three things I especially want pinned down. First, the mapping: what exactly is the wager Kelly is supposed to be sizing here — is it each spread trade, or one aggregated bet — what are the odds, the payoff shape, and the bankroll, and what fraction does the criterion actually prescribe for a trade with an edge of a few basis points? Second, the independence assumption: their book contained hundreds of nominally distinct positions, but nearly all were wagers on spreads reverting while markets stayed liquid and financing stayed available, so they moved together in 1998. If I treat the book as a single correlated bet instead of many independent ones, what does the correct aggregate fraction become, and does that alone explain the outcome or is it a side issue? Third, the direction I haven't seen discussed: Kelly takes the probabilities as given, but these came out of models, and the fund's positions were marked to market and could be forcibly closed by counterparties long before convergence. How badly does overbetting hurt when the edge is overestimated, is there a fractional or worst-case variant of the criterion that would have said not to put the trade on at all, and does the criterion have anything to say about a bet you may not be allowed to hold to resolution — or is path-dependent financing outside what the framework can even represent?

Finally, tell me what the episode does to the theory. Is this a clean confirmation of geometric rationality — positive expected value, negative expected log growth, therefore don't take it, no matter how many laureates are on the board — or does it expose limits: a dominance argument that is asymptotic being applied to a fund with a four-year life, ruin for an institution rather than for the people running it, and the question of whose wealth is supposed to be maximized anyway? I'd rather you say where the criterion is silent than stretch it to cover, and if you think Kelly is simply the wrong tool and something else diagnoses the case better, say so and show why.
