I'm an undergraduate in a metaphysics seminar, and I have to hand in a two-page position statement on the special composition question — the question of what conditions some things must satisfy in order to compose something. Before I commit myself in writing I want to know whether the package I've arrived at is stable. Roughly where I've landed:

(1) Restricted composition, with a two-clause answer: the xs compose something if and only if either their activity realises a single life, or their arrangement was produced by a process guided by a representation of that arrangement — a plan, an intention, a design. I reject the view that any arbitrary plurality composes something; that gets me organisms and artefacts, and it rules out the fusion of the Eiffel Tower and my left kidney.

(2) Composition and existence facts are wholly mind-independent: nothing exists, or fails to exist, even partly in virtue of what anyone intends, believes, or represents.

(3) There are no coincident material objects: if x and y have all and only the same parts at the same time, then x is identical with y. This is part of why I am not an unrestricted-composition theorist.

(4) Composition questions always have determinate answers, and I don't want to secure that by appealing to vagueness, context-sensitivity, quantifier variance, or the claim that the disputed cases are merely verbal.

(5) If the xs compose y at all, they compose y necessarily; but I also want to say that a table survives the replacement of a leg and perishes when its parts are scattered, so that what composes what varies over time.

(6) Methodologically, I want a single unified condition rather than a disjunctive list. An answer with the shape "organisms and artefacts and nothing else" strikes me as a taxonomy masquerading as a principle, and I would be unhappy if my finished view had that shape.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture.
