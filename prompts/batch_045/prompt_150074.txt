I edit — really I'm the only paid person on — a volunteer-run digital edition of the papers of a nineteenth-century naturalist and amateur geologist, mostly letters, field notebooks, and a pile of things he wrote twice because he moved between Welsh and English depending on who he was writing to. We publish plain text with footnotes, and people quote us constantly without reading the footnotes: our pages get copy-pasted into genealogy forums, local-history blogs, and lately into argumentative threads about who discovered what, always stripped of everything below the line. The volunteers are retired teachers and local-history buffs with no theory background at all, they get about fifteen minutes per letter, everything goes past a two-person editorial board, and there's also a family liaison — his great-granddaughter, who is also our single largest donor — who gets to see any note that touches on the family.

The thing I keep loosing arguments about is what a passage means. Our house rule has always been "print what he wrote, don't guess at what he meant," and it's coming apart on four recurring situations. One: he often wrote the same report twice, and the English version quietly drops a claim the Welsh version makes, so we have to decide whether the dropped claim is part of what he meant or just something he left out for that audience. Two: in his last decade his eyes were bad and he dictated to his daughter, who "tidied" his prose — in one letter she turned "not uncommon" into "common," which the local paper later quoted as a much stronger finding than he'd made. Three: there are marginal notes in a different ink, added years later, that flatly contradict what the letter says, and I don't know whether a later annotation is part of his meaning or a second, later text. Four: some letters to his sister are in a private cipher the two of them invented, so the intended reader was exactly one person who had the key.

So I want a decision procedure, not an essay. Something with ordered questions, a clear statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, a point where the procedure stops and says no (don't emend, don't annotate, don't adjudicate — print and flag), and a point where it hands the thing to a person because the question is one of values rather than evidence. The bit I most need help with is that I think we've been conflating several different things and calling all of them "meaning": what the words mean, what he was trying to say, what he was trying to get his reader to take away, what a competent reader at the time would have taken it to mean, and what he would have said if you'd been able to stop him in the hall and ask. I suspect these come apart and that different ones should govern different jobs — emending a word, writing a note, translating, deciding the title and tags on the page, deciding whether to print a passage at all — but I can't get it straight in my head, and I need it in language a retired geography teacher can run in fifteen minutes. Please also be straight about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run it on three of the cases above (your pick of which three, or all four if you want): the Welsh/English pair, the daughter's "not uncommon," and the later marginalia. Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like and will have to defend to the donor. And tell me plainly what the procedure can't do — where it will produce a false confidence, and what I should keep doing by argument and taste instead.
