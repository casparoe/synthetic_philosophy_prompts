Four of us meet on Sunday afternoons for a self-study group: a retired engineer, a nurse, a web developer, and me (I do logistics planning for a warehouse). None of us has any training in philosophy or economics. We arrived at this topic from a popular book on decision theory and have been arguing about money pumps for about a year, and I have noticed that we are not really disagreeing so much as talking past one another: two of us think the pump just *is* the argument, one thinks it proves nothing at all about what counts as rational, and one thinks the real question is whether you can bind yourself to a plan. What I want for our next session is not another explanation of the topic but a sorting instrument that separates us by what we say about cases, before any of us gets to use a label.

The deliverable: a decision tree that starts from the following commitment, which all four of us accept, and leads to a named position:

*There is nothing irrational about my preferences changing over time, or about my preferring A to B, B to C, and C to A. The fact that someone could offer me a sequence of swaps that leaves me poorer does not, by itself, show that anything is wrong with me.*

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of views: first give the branch questions and say which combinations of answers they permit, flagging any combination that is ruled out on purely logical grounds, and only then place the actual positions into the space. Every branch node should be phrased as a question a non-specialist could answer without knowing where it leads.

The nodes must include, and I want each of these cases doing real separating work:

- the ordinary cyclic pump, shown whole and in advance, where each swap is one you are happy to make at the time;
- my *blind pump*: the same swaps, but offered one at a time by someone who does not announce the sequence, so that no amount of foresight lets you anticipate it;
- my *forgiving pump*: a friend runs the sequence and quietly returns the money at the end, so you finish with what you started with and were never exploited;
- my *counter-pump*: a sequence that would leave you poorer but costs the exploiter more than it gains, so no one with any incentive would ever run it — does a pump that is possible but never profitable still tell against you?;
- my *fountain*: with cyclic preferences there is also a sequence that leaves you *richer*, if someone patient pays you to move round your own cycle in the direction you dislike. Does the existence of the fountain cancel the pump, or does it only show that whether you gain or lose depends on who approaches you?
- the *self-pump*, where what exploits you is not a bookie but your own future self's impatience;
- and one node on whether any of this carries over from preferences to opinions, i.e. to an updating habit that does not line up with the probability calculus, or whether the argument only ever concerned desires.

Leaves should be named in plain language, and these must all appear: judging each swap on its own plus a consistency requirement on your preferences at a time; judging whole sequences and revising your plan while treating your future selves as part of the situation you are planning around; judging whole sequences and sticking to your plan even when, at the later moment, breaking it looks better; planning as permitted but not required of you; the view that pump arguments show nothing about rationality; the view that rationality is entirely local to a time slice and makes no demands across times; and a view on which the lesson of the pump is about foresight, self-knowledge, or not being exploitable rather than about the shape of your preferences. The governing distinction for my purposes is between *the pump shows my preferences are defective* and *the pump shows something about my situation, my foresight, or my capacity to commit* — I care much more about that than about the standard planning-versus-binding split, so please let it structure the tree. If my fountain observation does not in fact track a distinct position, say so plainly and put it where it belongs — as a tie-breaker, or as a red herring — rather than inventing a leaf to flatter me.

For the combinations that nobody occupies, give a one-line diagnosis each: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored because nobody found it interesting. Flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely cannot separate, and supply three tie-breaker questions for those. Give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Keep it to six to eight branch questions and at most nine leaves.

Two constraints. First, assume we know what a money pump is; do not re-explain the topic except inside the case descriptions, and make every case self-contained so that nobody needs to look anything up. Second, no citations, authors, papers, dates, or reading lists — I cannot follow them up and we do not want them. If one of these positions has a standard one-word label I would recognise, put it in parentheses and nothing more. Finally, add a short paragraph on how four people should actually use the handout so that everyone commits to a case before anyone reads the leaf names, and tell me honestly which of our disagreements the instrument cannot settle.
