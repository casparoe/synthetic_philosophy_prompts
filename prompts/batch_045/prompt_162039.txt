I'm putting together a graduate seminar session on archives and power — who gets recorded, what survives, and what the silences in a source base do to the accounts that can be written from it — and I want to close it with a film rather than another methodological essay, because I think a worked case does something the conceptual literature struggles to do. My candidate is Welles's Citizen Kane (1941). The picture is already an archival inquiry: a reporter is sent to find out what Kane's last word meant, and what we watch is more or less the dossier he assembles — a newsreel obituary, a dead banker's private papers, the recollections of a business partner, a friend, a second wife and a butler, and finally a warehouse of crated objects that no one has catalogued and no one is looking at.

I'd like a reading of the film as a study in archival power, but I don't want it flattened into the claim that the archive is simply partial and partisan, and I don't want a reading that turns the film into an illustration. What I need is criticism. Five things in particular:

1. A taxonomy I can put on the board. I have been working with four kinds of silence: the record that was never generated; the record that was generated and then destroyed; the record that survives but is never consulted, because no one recognises it as evidence; and the record that survives and is consulted but is illegible to the researcher — present in front of him, answering nothing. Tell me whether the film supplies an example of each, whether the same item moves between categories as the inquiry proceeds, and whether the film has a fifth kind I am missing.

2. A distinction I want to try out on the seminar and am not confident about: the difference between a gap in the archive and a blindness in the archivist. The usual story about power and the archive is a story about destruction and exclusion — what was burned, whom nobody wrote down. But it seems to me that much of what defeats the inquiry in this film is not absent from the dossier at all; it is in the room, filmed, held for a moment by the camera, and thrown into the furnace while the researcher stands in the same frame giving up. Call this, if it is a real category, survival without legibility: material that power had no interest in suppressing and that therefore survived, and that survives precisely as noise. Is that genuinely a fifth kind of silence, or is it only my fourth kind dramatised? And if it is real, does it cut against the literature's instinct to locate archival power in suppression?

3. The document with false provenance. There is a scene in which a notice of Susan's operatic debut is published under a friend's name although the friend did not finish writing it, and Kane completed it himself. We saw how the document was made; the archive cannot say so. What does a record whose stated author is not its author do to an archival reading — is this closer to destruction than to bias, and does the film mean us to notice that the provenance of a source can be irretrievable even when the source is intact?

4. An inversion I want pressed. Kane is not one of the unrecorded; he is one of the great producers of public record — a press baron, a publisher of declarations of principle, a man who built a monument that is also a warehouse. So this is the uncommon case of power archiving itself at vast expense, rather than power archiving the powerless. Does the film suggest that volume and self-authorship buy no legibility? What, concretely, would have had to be added to the dossier for the inquiry to succeed — and is the film's answer that nothing could have been added, because what was wanted was not information that went unrecorded but a significance that only the subject could have attached and that he never attached for anyone?

5. The constraint of format. The inquiry is commissioned by a newsreel and needs what a newsreel needs: a single key that explains a man. Thompson's closing admission that no one word can explain a man's life is, I take it, the film's own verdict on that. Is the failure the film depicts a failure of the archive, or of the question put to it — and does the film distinguish the two cleanly enough for me to teach the difference?

Then two checks. Where does this reading overreach — which scene or character cannot be made to carry archival weight without damage to the film, and where would an honest critic say the lens is being imposed rather than found? And what, if anything, does the film add to the philosophical discussion that argument alone does not: is there something about silence that can only be shown?

I don't want footnotes, further reading or scholarly apparatus; I want the reading itself, keyed to the four or five scenes I should screen for the group.
