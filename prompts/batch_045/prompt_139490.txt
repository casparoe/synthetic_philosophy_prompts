You are a professional philosopher who works on conservative political thought and the epistemology of reform, and who also reads fiction attentively. Think carefully and take your time: before you answer, brainstorm the several different jobs a maxim like Chesterton's fence might be doing — a claim about evidence, a claim about who bears the burden of proof, a claim about the value of inherited arrangements as such, a claim about the cognitive limits of any single mind, a claim about diffuse and invisible causal effects — and keep that list in front of you, because I suspect my argument is quietly trading between them. I don't want a reading list, a bibliography, or citations. I want the argument taken apart and put back together.

I write historical fiction, and my current novel is set in an invented valley that runs its common granary under an arrangement I call the Double-Keep: the granary has two locks, the bailiff holds one key, and the second key is held by a person drawn by lot each year from among the households that own no grain of their own. Nothing leaves the granary unless both keys turn. My new bailiff, arriving from the capital, wants the second lock struck off: it is slow, it is absurd, and in the winter of the book it is the reason the valley nearly starves. In the argument scene I gave the granary's warden what I took to be the strongest available objection, and it has got away from me. As written, it proves that no inherited institution can ever be abolished, which cannot be right and which makes a boring book besides. Here it is.

(1) The fence rule: a person may not remove an inherited arrangement unless that person can state what it was for. Chesterton's version is the parable of the reformer who comes on a fence or a gate standing across a road, cannot see why anyone put it there, and proposes to have it taken down; the reply is that you may not remove it until you can say why it was put up, and that the inability to see a reason is not evidence that there is none.

(2) Latent wisdom: what holds for a fence holds for every long-lived institution. The reasons an old arrangement is still standing are largely tacit, dispersed across many minds and many small local adjustments, and no inquirer, however diligent, can recover more than a fraction of them. This is the Burkean claim that an established arrangement contains more reason than any one generation can produce or audit. It is the premise I most want to keep. My warden is a competent, hard-headed woman, not a mystic, and the whole point of the scene is that she is right about something.

(3) So the condition in (1) cannot be met by any reformer for any long-lived institution: (1) asks for knowledge, and (2) says the knowledge is not available.

(4) A requirement that cannot be met is not a requirement, it is a prohibition. So no long-lived institution may ever be removed by anyone, however bad it looks and however confident the reformers are.

(5) But my bailiff strikes off the second lock, and I want the reader to be glad he did, and in our own world long-lived institutions have been abolished that almost nobody now regrets losing.

(6) So (1) and (2) cannot both stand as I have stated them, and yet each of them seems to me to be exactly what a conservative ought to say.

Worse, my warden has an answer to (5), and I cannot get around it. In my draft she concedes that the bailiff can state the original reason for the Double-Keep — the second lock was added after a plague year, so that the landless households could stop the grain being sold off over their heads — and she says only: "That is what it was built for. You do not know what it has been doing for two hundred years." Which gives a stronger version of the argument:

(1′) The condition is not knowledge of the purpose it was built for but knowledge of the work it is doing now.

(2′) Present work is harder to establish than original purpose, because a function is a matter of diffuse and counterfactual effects: to know what an arrangement is doing you would have to know what would have happened had it not been there.

(3′) So the condition in (1′) is harder still to satisfy, and the prohibition in (4) follows a fortiori.

(4′) Yet in the novel the Double-Keep's present work turns out to be something no one intended and no one would defend: it lets the grain merchant play the two keyholders against each other, so that the granary is never opened at all, and the winter stores rot in the dark. My bailiff states exactly this, correctly, produces the ledger that proves it — and it still isn't enough, because Marthe can always say that the function he has found is one among many, and that the ones he cannot see are the ones that matter.

(5′) And there is a detail I wrote in and then could not take out: a generation after the removal, the landless households stop coming to the moot, and within twenty years the bailiff's inventory is quietly falsified again. Something the Double-Keep was doing was real, and it was not what it was built for, and nobody noticed until it was gone.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? Please do the following, in this order. Name the step — a single step, not a general suspicion — and say why it looked sound enough to get past me. Then price the alternatives: for each other step I might give up instead, tell me what I would have to accept if I gave it up, and whether it costs me more than the one you rejected. I would rather keep (2) or (2′) than (1), and I would rather keep the judgment that the second lock came off rightly than either; apart from that I have no stake, so don't spare a premise to protect a verdict of mine. Then give me a restated version of the fence rule that Marthe can actually use in the argument scene: something that is not a veto, that is not the platitude that one should weigh consequences before acting, that can register a cost which is real but diffuse and arrives a generation late, and that does not let her answer every reformer with the claim that the important functions are the invisible ones. If your restatement needs her to lose the scene in some respect, say which respect and why that is the right price.

Finally, a note on your own confidence. At the end, tell me what would change your mind: which further fact about the Double-Keep, or which consideration I have not supplied, would make you abandon the step you named in favour of a different one; and say where your diagnosis is most likely to be wrong, and what a defender of the step you rejected would say to put it back.
