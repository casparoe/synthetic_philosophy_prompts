I cover science and ideas for a national newspaper, and our numbers desk has an editor who rules on which comparison we may lead with when a study's topline and its subgroups disagree. Her record over the last three years: a public university's admissions figures, where women were admitted at a lower rate overall but the gap vanished or reversed department by department — she ruled the topline the artifact and had us lead with department-level rates; the kidney-stone trial, where the pooled success rates favor one procedure and the ranking flips inside both the small-stone and large-stone groups — same ruling, stratify by size; a randomized trial of two antihypertensives, where she let us lead with effects estimated within baseline-risk quartiles rather than the single overall average, saying there was nothing wrong with cutting a randomized comparison on a variable fixed before randomization; an observational study of two stroke treatments that divided patients by disability grade at discharge, where she refused the split because the grade is written after the outcome we were reporting; and a comparison of two chemotherapy regimens split into molecular subtypes whose smallest cell held nine patients, where she refused the split and ran the topline.

The case now on her desk: a large randomized trial of an escalation protocol for suspected sepsis on general wards. Topline 90-day mortality is 21% under the new protocol versus 18% under usual care. But patients are also classified by whether they were moved to a high-dependency bed within 24 hours, which the protocol itself makes far more likely — 48% of the protocol group versus 25% of the usual-care group — and within both the moved group (34% versus 39%) and the not-moved group (9% versus 11%) the new protocol looks better, with thousands of patients in every cell. The journal's abstract calls the topline an artifact of Simpson's paradox and concludes the protocol saves lives; the trial's statistician replies that the topline is the only number the randomization licenses and that the within-stratum comparison is not a comparison of like with like.

What will she rule, and which of her own rulings is the closest analog? More useful to me: the two or three rules her record is equally consistent with — cut as finely as the data allow provided the cut is fixed before outcomes are known and the cells are big enough; cut only on variables that lie upstream of the treatment; something else — what each predicts here, which one you think she is actually running and why, and the single further case I could bring her that would separate them. Give me too the sentence we would print under each ruling, since that is the only part readers will see.
