I sit on the board of a small coastal reserve — I was a limnologist for 35 years and I've only started reading philosophy since I retired, so forgive me if I mangle the vocabulary. A tech company has offered to install, free for two years, an autonomous system that runs our water control structures: it sets the tide gates and the pump schedule to hold the impoundment's salinity and level inside a band our restoration plan specifies, under a standing instruction to "reach the target while keeping everything else as close as possible to how it would have been without you." Two of us think that second clause is the whole ballgame; two think its marketing copy.

What I want the memo to face is the baseline problem: what would everything else have done anyway? Right now our warden works the gates by hand on judgment he can't fully spell out; before 2010 they were mostly left shut; before the causeway went in there was no impoundment at all. Pick any one of those as "without you" and the same physical action becomes free or expensive, so the penalty can't be doing the work we want it to do. I've been toying with a different penalty instead: charge the system for actions that shrink what we can still do afterward — gate settings it can't un-set, birds that nest somewhere they then can't be moved from, seed banks flushed out — regardless of how far the visible state moved.

Draft me a short memo to circulate before the September meeting with an actual recommendation: sign, don't sign, or sign with conditions I could write into the contract. Does my irreversibility penalty escape the baseline problem or just relocate it (what counts as an option, whose, over what horizon)? And if it does relocate it, is there any version of "keep side effects small" that doesn't secretly require us to have already specified everything we value about the marsh — because if that's the price, I'd rather say so out loud and vote no. Please also give the strongest version of the sign-it-as-is case; two of my colleagues will make it and they aren't fools.
