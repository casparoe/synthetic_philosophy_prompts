I'm in a seminar on perception right now, and our discussion of cognitive penetration keeps dying in the same place: someone offers a case — the Müller-Lyer, an ambiguous figure seen as a duck, a radiologist whose tumour pops out for her and not for me, a phone in a stranger's hand read as a gun — and someone else replies that this is attention, or memory, or judgement, or a change in which part of the scene you're looking at rather than a change in what you see. Nobody has said what would settle it, so before I read further into the literature I want the specification. Write the desiderata an adequate account of cognitive penetration has to meet: what a theory must be able to say about an arbitrary case in order to determine whether penetration occurred there; which distinctions it must be able to draw and must not collapse; the cases it has to get right, including at least one genuine perception that merely looks penetrated and one piece of mere cognitive influence that must not count as penetration; and the constraint that keeps the notion from collapsing into "any causal influence of cognition on experience" while still leaving penetration able to matter epistemically.

Two things I want handled head-on, because I suspect they do most of the work. First, the conflict between the "same proximal stimulus, different experience" test and the "difference-maker in the best causal-explanatory story" test: these seem to come apart in cases where a cognitive state changes which features the visual system bothers to resolve, and I want to know which test a theory should be built around and what it would have to give up on the other. Second, I want the list to separate penetration of the sensory processing from penetration of the content perceived (which property the scene is represented as having) from penetration of phenomenal character from penetration of how the experience is taken up in judgement and report — and to say whether these are one relation that must travel together or several that can dissociate, since a theory that treats them as one will look refuted by cases that only touch one.

Please also do three things with the list: mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp (I'd guess the demand that a penetrated perception never be able to confirm the belief that penetrated it belongs in the second category, but I want your reasoning rather than my guess); flag any pair of requirements you think cannot be jointly satisfied, since the interesting result would be that the notion cannot do everything people want it to do; and name the single requirement that excludes the most accounts, with the case that makes it bite.
