I'm in the final year of my philosophy degree, taking a paper on social ontology and the philosophy of action, and I have a supervision in a fortnight on shared intention. There is a distinction I keep failing to state cleanly, and I should be grateful for an explanation that gets at the conceptual structure rather than a run-through of the rival positions.

The easy end of the contrast I think I have. In the paradigm case — two of us shifting a heavy table together, or singing a duet — each of us intends not merely that I perform my part but that we do the thing; each of us knows the other intends it too; and all of this is out in the open between us. I also take the point that this is meant to differ from a merely aggregate pattern, as when everyone in a city drives on the same side of the road, or a crowd converges on a shop during a sale: each person acts for his or her own reason, and the pattern is an unintended sum. I do not need that contrast set out again at length.

What I cannot get clear about is the middle range. Consider the unloading of a container ship, the discharge pathway of a large hospital, or the working of an air-traffic system. Dozens of people each execute a role specified by a protocol, and the collective end is genuinely realised — the ship is unloaded, the patient is discharged — and realised reliably rather than by accident. But the end need not be represented by any participant. Several of them could not say what the end of the whole operation is; some may not know that there is a whole operation. Call these opaque collective actions: the end is realised systematically while going unrepresented by, and in some cases being unrepresentable by, every individual participant.

Two moves I have been toying with seem to me to be where the real issue lies, and I am unsure whether either is legitimate.

First, deferential authorship. Perhaps the individual's intention has a role-specified but content-underspecified object: roughly, that I do my part in whatever it is this organisation is doing here, whatever that turns out to be. Does this recover a genuine we-intention on the part of each participant, or is it really only an intention to comply with a role, plus a true belief that one's compliance is useful — an individual intention in collective clothing?

Second, the substitution test. Suppose we replace one participant with someone who produces exactly the same role-behaviour but lacks the deferential intention, acting purely for a wage. Does anything change in the description of what the group is doing? If not, does that show the deferential intention was doing no work in the first place?

So here is what I want explained. What exactly is supposed to be lost if I say that in the opaque case there is no shared intention at all, only interlocking individual intentions plus a structure that guarantees they mesh — with the we being no more than a manner of speaking? And what is lost on the other side, if I say there is a shared intention, but located in something other than a person: in the group, understood as something over and above the participants, with its intentions fixed by its decision procedure? In particular, is the difference between the transparent and the opaque cases best understood as one of degree — how much of the collective end each participant represents — or as one of kind, a difference in where the intention is located at all? And a related puzzle: mutual responsiveness is often said to be the mark of acting together, yet in the opaque case participants respond to the protocol rather than to one another, so is responsiveness to a shared procedure a species of mutual responsiveness, or a different thing that merely mimics it?

Please say which of these ways of going you think does least violence to the concept of intending, and why. But what I most want to know is what hangs on the choice: if in my essay I commit myself to one of these accounts rather than another, what else am I thereby committed to — about what it is to act for a reason, and about how responsibility for a collective outcome is meant to distribute itself over the people who produced it?
