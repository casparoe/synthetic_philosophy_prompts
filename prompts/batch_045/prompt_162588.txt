I think it quietly imports a bunch of things a legal order doesn't have: that replacing a plank is a causal operation rather than a norm-governed one, that nothing in the boat plays the role of a rule about what counts as a valid plank, that the sea is holding you up by buoyancy rather than by validity, and that there's no boat-officer deciding whether the swap was done properly. but I can't tell which of those disanalogies matter for her conclusion and which are just decoration.

three cases I keep getting stuck on, which I don't think the boat can even represent:

1. the package repeal. order L has rule A (say, a tax ceiling), rule B (A can only be amended by two-thirds plus a referendum), and rule C (B can be amended by simple majority). the assembly passes one instrument, by simple majority, repealing B and raising the ceiling in the same act. plank-by-plank it's fine; as one act it looks like the A-part is done under a rule that no longer exists by the time it bites. does the image have any resources for the order of operations, or does it just stipulate that there is one?
2. the upward ratchet. suppose instead they want to entrench A, raising the threshold from simple majority to two-thirds plus referendum. the entrenching act is authorised by the old, lower rule, but it purports to withdraw from future majorities the very power that authorised it. loosening is trivially fine; tightening looks like it needs a source of authority the old rule doesn't supply. call it the ratchet asymmetry. is that real, or am I inventing a problem?
3. the unamendable plank. a clause saying this clause may never be amended. in the boat that's just a plank with a label on it, and nothing enforces labels. in a legal order there's a court, and the court's jurisdiction to strike things down is itself a plank that can be swapped.
