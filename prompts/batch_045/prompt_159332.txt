I'm a journalist, and I'm finishing a long piece on how institutions decide who gets helped when they can't help everyone: a hospital committee allocating a scarce drug, a foundation's grant meeting, a triage protocol drafted during a pandemic. I've come out of the reporting with a set of verdicts that I can't compress into one honest sentence, and I'd be grateful if you'd tell me what principle, if any, they add up to, and what holding it would cost me.

1. One dose of a drug. Patient A is bedbound, in constant pain, with roughly two years of life left. Patient B is comfortable, mildly limited by a chronic condition, with decades ahead. The dose buys A about ten months of decent life and B about twelve. I give it to A. I keep giving it to A as the ratio worsens — I still choose A when B would get twenty months — and I switch to B somewhere around forty, though I couldn't defend the number.

2. A regulator can block a new diagnostic device that would be available only to the well-off. Blocking it does nothing for the badly-off: no price effects, no spillovers, no redistribution, just a smaller gap. I don't think blocking it is an improvement, and I'd say so in print.

3. Yet I do think abolishing a VIP fast-track — a separate, shorter queue for the well-off, or a hereditary title — is an improvement, even though it leaves everyone's material well-being exactly as it was and doesn't lengthen a single badly-off life.

4. Triage: one very badly-off patient versus five comfortably-off patients, same benefit per patient. I take the five. One very badly-off patient versus two comfortably-off patients. I take the one.

5. A hundred people in destitution, each lifted a long way, versus ten million comfortable people each getting a gain so small that none of them would notice it, though the arithmetic total is far larger. I take the hundred, and I can't talk myself out of it.

6. Two candidates for a scarce bed, equally badly off now, identical expected benefit: one is badly off through a voluntary, well-informed, thoroughly enjoyable risk; the other through bad luck. I give real priority to the unlucky one — while conceding that the benefit would do exactly as much good in either life.

7. The one I go back and forth on: a preventive programme for people who are comfortable now but at high risk of being very badly off later, versus treatment for people who are badly off now. On Tuesday I fund the prevention, on Thursday the treatment. I can't tell whether priority should track someone's expected lifetime position or the position they occupy when the help arrives.

My working sentence is this: weight a benefit more heavily the worse off its recipient is, otherwise just add the benefits up, and never make anyone worse off merely to make things more equal. Test it against the seven cases and repair it where it breaks. Tell me which verdicts it cannot deliver and which one I should give up, whether my refusal in case 2 rules out whatever case 3 requires, and whether cases 1, 4 and 5 can be held by one weighting at all or force me toward something less smooth. Then tell me what the repaired principle says about two cases I haven't settled: destitute people alive today versus people who will exist two centuries from now and be far better off than anyone living, and animals whose range of well-being is much narrower than ours. Finally, give me the one sentence I can print that a hostile reader can't break with an obvious counterexample.
