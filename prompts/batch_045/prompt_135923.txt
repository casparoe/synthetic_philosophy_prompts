I work on replication and consensus for a distributed database, and I want to know whether a literature outside my own has something to tell me about a problem I keep meeting in different clothes. I would like you to restate that literature in the vocabulary I actually use, as faithfully as you can, and then tell me precisely what the translation fails to carry.

For calibration: I am fluent in the following and you can use it without explaining it — quorum reads and writes; CRDTs and the algebraic properties we demand of a merge (commutative, associative, idempotent: a join on a semilattice); leader election and Raft-style totally ordered logs, and the difference between a leader-based and a leaderless design; base tables versus materialised views, and the whole business of incremental view maintenance — when it is safe to update a derived value from a delta and when you have to recompute it from the base data; immediate versus deferred constraint checking; hard integrity constraints as opposed to soft signals such as a learned fraud score or an anomaly detector's confidence; database repairs and consistent query answering over a database that violates its own constraints; and end-to-end evaluation of a pipeline against held-out ground truth. I have no formal logic beyond what an engineer picks up and no philosophy.

The material I want translated is the cluster of results around the discursive dilemma. Its standard illustration is a three-member appellate panel deciding whether a defendant is liable, where the applicable rule makes liability hold if and only if two conditions are each met. Two judges accept the first condition and reject the second; one accepts both. A majority therefore rejects each condition, and so rejects liability if the panel reasons from the conditions to the verdict; yet a majority of the panel accepts liability if the judges simply vote on the verdict. Please translate, at minimum: that divergence; the contrast between premise-based and conclusion-based aggregation; the general impossibility result for aggregation rules that satisfy universal domain (any combination of individually consistent inputs is allowed), collective rationality (the aggregate output is itself consistent), anonymity (no input is privileged) and independence — often called systematicity — (the aggregate's verdict on any one proposition depends only on the individual verdicts on that proposition, and the same rule is used for every proposition); and the families of escape routes that drop one of those conditions, including the one that keeps majority verdicts proposition by proposition and then repairs the result to the nearest consistent output, measuring distance by the number of propositions on which the two differ.

There is one case I care about more than the standard one, and I do not think it is discussed in the form I need. In my system the analogues of the premises do not entail the analogue of the conclusion: they are evidence for it. Each node reports, for each of three evidence items, a hard boolean for whether it observed that item, and the decision is a policy that fires when the evidence clears a bar, though any single item can be defeated by an exception. Separately, one of my merge functions *has* to be row-wise and field-local: if the merge had to read across fields to decide a field, it would no longer be a CRDT and I would lose convergence guarantees. So for me the analogue of independence is not a normative option but an architectural constraint, and the analogue of collective rationality has nothing to bite on once the link between evidence and decision is defeasible rather than deductive. I want to know whether the dilemma in my case is the same shape as the original, a degenerate case of it, or something the framework cannot see at all.
