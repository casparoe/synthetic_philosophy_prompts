ok so my school rolled out this thing this year where if your grades drop and you keep showing up late, they pull you into a "check-in" with the dean before you've actually done anything wrong, and my ethics teacher made us argue about whether that's just punishing people in advance with nicer words. that spiraled into a huge argument with my friend Marco about whether anyone ever really *deserves* to get in trouble for anything, and I realized I have a pile of gut reactions that probably don't fit together. so here they are, and I want to know what principle, if any, actually captures them. Marcus copies some answers off his phone during the AP bio test, has literally never cheated before, panics, and confesses the same day — I think he should get a zero on it, and "he deserves that" feels true to me, even though I also think the zero is mostly there so the rest of us don't try it. A junior who's been caught stealing from lockers four times gets expelled — I'm way less bothered by that than by Marcus's zero, which seems backwards if desert is about what you actually did. A kid I know through soccer got raised in a genuinely awful house and got expelled for starting a fight that put someone in the nurse's office: my gut says both "yeah he has to face consequences, he hurt someone" and "honestly he couldn't have done much differently," and I say both in the same conversation without noticing. Then there's a story I read about a guy whose personality totally changed after a brain tumor and started doing creepy illegal stuff — there I don't feel like he *deserves* anything, I just want him stopped, and the two feelings come apart really cleanly in a way they don't for the soccer kid. My dog pees on the rug and I yell at him: I don't think the dog deserves anything, but the yelling still seems fine, like punishment without desert is just a normal thing. And my friend who said something super cruel at a party while wasted and doesn't remember it at all — I still think he owes an apology and I think it's appropriate that people were mad at him, but if you asked me "does he deserve to suffer for it" I kind of freeze.

so: is there one principle that gets all of that, or close? I want it stated precisely enough that I could argue with it, with the case that motivates each clause, and I especially want you to tell me which one or two of my verdicts have to go — my guess is the soccer kid and the tumor guy are the pair that can't both stay, but you tell me. I've been reading a bit about this and it seems like there are two big camps: people like Dennett who think desert is a real thing agents can genuinely earn by being the kind of thing that responds to reasons, and people like Caruso who think nobody is ever deserving in that deep sense and consequences can only be justified by what they do going forward. Tell me which camp the principle you land on puts me in, or whether it sneaks between them.

and then run it on the cases I haven't decided: the dean's pre-crime check-ins, a group project where the thing breaks and nobody will admit whose fault it was so the teacher fails everyone, and whether a teacher who gives a detention for deterrence reasons should still tell the kid "you deserve this" — I feel like the answer to that last one is yes and also that it's a lie, and I dont know what to do with that.
