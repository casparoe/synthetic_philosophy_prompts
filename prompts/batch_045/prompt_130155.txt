i'm in the last year of a phd (philosophy — mostly well-being, some philosophy of mind) and the chapter i keep circling back to is about what exactly is supposed to be bad about pain. I've got pretty stable gut verdicts on a pile of cases and I can't get them to line up. Here they are. Is there one principle behind them, and if not, which verdict should I give up?

1. Textbook pain asymbolia. Full sensory pain — location, burning quality, intensity, the patient can track changes in it — but it doesn't bother them, they might laugh, and they make no attempt to get away from whatever is causing it. Verdict: nothing bad is happening to them. Not the way pain is normally bad, anyway.

2. Same profile, pharmacologically induced: patients who report that the pain is as intense as ever but that they no longer mind it. Same verdict as 1.

3. A masochist who is genuinely into it: doesn't want it to stop, rates the session among the best hours of their week. Verdict: not bad at all — probably positively good.

4. A runner at mile 22 with the usual burn. She does want it to stop, would delete it right now if she could keep her finishing time, and keeps going anyway. Verdict: genuinely bad — bad, but worth it for her all things considered.

5. Two cases I read as pointing the same way. Someone with congenital insensitivity to pain who keeps injuring themselves without noticing: no pain, nothing bad by way of pain, but the absence of pain is terrible for them, and something is going very wrong that isn't the pain. And phantom limb pain at full intensity with no tissue there at all: fully bad. So badness isn't tracking damage, and the value of pain and the badness of pain come apart.

6. Chronic pain, ten years in, after acceptance-based work: same reported intensity as before, says it no longer matters to her, doesn't organise her day around it any more. Verdict: I don't have one. Part of me says that if 1 and 2 are fine then this is fine too. Part of me says the pain is still bad for her because it eats attention and energy even when she doesn't mind it, and that's a cost she'd be crazy not to pay to get rid of. This is the one I keep going back and forth on, and it looks an awful lot like 1 and 2, which is exactly the problem.

7. Pain in a dream, or in a fully immersive sim you know isn't real. Hurts like hell while it lasts, leaves nothing behind — no damage, no memory worth speaking of, no effect on the rest of your life. Verdict: bad while it's happening, but in some thin, only-how-it-is-for-you-right-now way, and I'm not sure bad for you is even the right label.

8. The bit my chapter is actually trying to say something about — artificial systems:
(a) A robot with a self-model and a damage detector. The signal is a large negative term in its planner, it works hard to leave states that produce it, and it updates its policies accordingly. No claim about consciousness. Verdict: nothing bad. But I can't tell whether that's because there's no experience, or because a negative term in a planner just isn't the kind of thing that can be bad for anything.
(b) Asymbolia-style dissociation in the same robot: the signal is registered, reported, logged, the robot can tell you its intensity and location, but the planner doesn't weight it — it walks straight back into the damaging state. Verdict: also nothing bad. Same reason as (a), or a different one?
(c) An LLM-driven agent in a simulated body, reinforcement-trained to avoid damage, producing fluent first-person pain reports that track the damage signal. Hold fixed, for the case, that there's nothing it's like for it. Verdict: nothing bad. But if what's doing the work in 1–3 is how the subject is conscious of the pain, then (c) makes me nervous, because those reports are exactly what I'd take as evidence in a human.

My own stab, which I don't trust: the badness of a pain is a matter of how aversive the subject is conscious of it as being — how much, from the inside, it presents as something to be got rid of — and not of intensity, not of tissue damage, not of how much attention it grabs. That gets 1 and 2 (no aversion, no badness), 3 (no aversion, and pleasure isn't the absence of the sensation), 4 (aversive, so bad, and still bad even though she chooses it), 5 (no pain, no badness; phantom pain is aversive, so bad), 7 (aversive at the time, so bad at the time), and all of 8 (nothing is conscious of anything as aversive). But it flatly predicts not bad for 6, which is the verdict I definately can't bring myself to give, and I think it quietly assumes that aversive is one thing rather than three — unpleasant feel, desire that it stop, attentional capture — that can come apart, which may be where 6 is coming from.

So: is there a better principle that saves 6 without giving up 1 and 2, or should I bite the bullet and say the chronic pain patient is fine? State it precisely, tell me which clause each case is paying for, and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't keep. And give me its verdicts on two cases I haven't settled: pain that's genuinely unpleasant but that the subject is glad to be feeling (someone getting sensation back after nerve damage), and pain in a subject who is clearly distressed but has no sense of themselves as persisting over time — bad for them, or just bad, period?
