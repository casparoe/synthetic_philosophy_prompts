You are a senior researcher working at the intersection of the philosophy of measurement, welfare economics, and public policy evaluation; you have written funded proposals before and you know what review committees actually read for. Please think carefully before drafting, and begin with a short brainstorming paragraph (150 words or so) in which you sketch two or three candidate framings of the problem below, note what each would cost you, and say which one gives the sharpest question. Then write the proposal.

The setting: I work in a small policy unit attached to a national statistics office. A minister has asked whether the government should adopt an explicit national target of placing in the top ten of the annual world happiness rankings within ten years, and my unit has been asked to produce an internal decision memo on the concept rather than on the politics. I have a half-formed idea I want turned into a real proposal: that the debate splits into a measurement question and a targeting question that people keep running together, and that the interesting part is the feedback between them. On the measurement side, the familiar rankings rest on self-reported life evaluation, which is not the same thing as experienced affect, and reported numbers move with things like response styles, aspiration levels, and the order and framing of survey items, so it is not obvious that a rise in a country's score means a rise in anything we independently care about. On the targeting side, a ranking is ordinal and positional: only one country can be at the top, so if many governments each optimise their own rank, the total effort is large and the total improvement in human wellbeing could be nil, and the ranking would at the same time become less informative because the measured quantity is now being produced partly by the measurement. My suspicion is that there is a principled case — not just a prudential one — for a state to commit to steering by something it does not publish or does not target directly, and I would like the project to take seriously the option of a deliberately opaque or lagged wellbeing indicator as a policy instrument.

Write a two-page research proposal for a six-month, one-investigator project with a part-time research assistant, to be read by a mixed committee of economists, one philosopher, and two senior civil servants, so keep it free of unnecessary jargon. It should contain: a precise statement of the question, narrow enough that it has a determinate answer within six months; an explanation of why the two standard postures — that the existing measures are good enough to steer by, and that interpersonal comparisons of happiness are too shaky to steer by at all — both leave the question open; the method, month by month, meaning what I would actually do and why that approach rather than the obvious alternatives of commissioning a new survey, running a cross-country regression, or writing a conceptual overview; the range of possible conclusions and what considerations would decide between them; a concrete description of the deliverable for someone who has to advise a minister on whether to set the target; and an honest closing section on what would count as the project having failed and what would make it too big for six months. Do not include a bibliography, a literature review organised by citation, or any references section; refer to positions and arguments in your own words and I will add the citations myself.
