I'm putting together a two-page handout for an advanced undergraduate seminar on whether the numbers should count in rescue cases, and I want it built as a decision tree rather than a list of named views. Begin from the logical space: which questions genuinely separate the positions? My candidate axes are whether the fact that there are more of them is ever a reason at all, as opposed to facts about who is worse off or who has more at stake; whether the greater number can make it permissible to save the many when the one has an equally strong claim; whether it can also make it required; and, in the gap between permitted and required, whether the tie may be broken at all and by what — a coin flip, the bare fact of the majority, or nothing. Turn these into six to eight branch nodes, each phrased as a concrete case a student can answer without knowing what label it leads to, including at least: a bare five-versus-one with equal stakes and equal claims; a case where the one is far worse off than any of the many; a case where the number saved is enormous but each individual loss is trivial; a case aggregating many small benefits to a single person versus the same total spread across persons; and a case where the many can be saved only by imposing a harm on the one as a side effect. Each leaf should carry the usual name where there is one, or be marked as barely defended. Required leaves: a Taurek-style permissive no-numbers leaf, a may-but-need-not leaf, a strict-requirement leaf, an equal-chances leaf, and a leaf on which numbers start counting only past some ratio threshold — a student proposed that last one and I suspect it is unstable, so tell me whether the combination is really empty and, if so, whether because it is incoherent or merely unattractive. Close by flagging any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate; I expect the no-numbers and equal-chances views to collide somewhere, and I would rather the handout say so than quietly pick between them.
