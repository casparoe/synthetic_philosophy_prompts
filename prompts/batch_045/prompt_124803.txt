I'm giving the toast at my PhD advisors retirement dinner next week (his idea, not mine—he says if he's being forced out he at least wants a captive audiance). He spent forty years defending the F-twist—the methodological claim, as Samuelson called it, that a model's assumptions don't have to be realistic to be good; what matters is predictive success, and in fact the more significant the theory, the more unrealistic its assumptions tend to be. He wasn't just an economist about it, though: he thought the same went for people. A person's self-conception, on his view, could be a wildly unrealistic "as if" model, and as long as it got them to act well it wasn't a lie or a failure.

The room is going to be split. Half are his ex-students and econ/philosophy colleagues who know the F-twist and the standard objection to it: unrealistic assumptions are only okay inside the domain where they've actually been tested, and once you leave that domain the model can break without giving you any warning. The other half are his wife, his two daughters, his brother who runs a bait shop, and a bunch of grandkids. They mostly know him as the guy who after a mild stroke last spring kept saying "I'm fine, the model still runs," and they can't tell if that's courage, denial, or a joke in very poor taste.

It got personally relevant for me because I've been the one driving him to appointments and cleaning out his office. I used to think the F-twist was just an excuse for lazy modeling. Then I watched him use it to stay himself while his short-term memory got weird. He'd forget where he parked and then give me a ten-minute explanation of why, by his own standards, that didn't refute his self-model—it just meant the domain had shifted. Last month he got turned around walking back from the mailbox. He laughed and said his "assumptions" about his own navigation were "wildly unrealistic now, but check the predictions." I have no idea if that was wisdom or denial, and I'm giving the toast without pretending to know.

So the idea has to do structural work in what I can say. By his own F-twist, I can't stand up there and say "he was finally forced to face reality" or "his assumptions about himself were unrealistic, so he was wrong." Saying his assumptions were unrealistic is not an insult in his framework—it's basically the entry fee for a good model, and the experts would roll their eyes. But I also can't say "his unrealistic assumptions were harmless, so it was all fine," because by his own standard unrealistic assumptions are only as good as their predictions, and the predictions that he could keep teaching and driving have now definately failed. The family knows he's not fine, and the speech can't gaslight them. He also had a distinction he used in his methods seminar: "as-if scaffolding" (a deliberately false assumption you keep only as long as it pays for itself, and drop the moment it stops) versus "model idolatry" (keeping the false assumption after the predictions break down because you've fallen in love with the story, which turns the model into a cage). He was always teaching us to tell those two apart. The hard part of the toast is that for maybe the first time, he's the one who might not be able to tell whether he's still scaffolding or slipping into idolatry—and I have to acknowledge that worry without diagnosing him, because you can't run someone else's self-model for them.

If you want a concrete detail: he loved to repeat a line from an old article on business-cycle theory that Friedman had quoted as an example of fuzzy realist thinking: “economic phenomena are varied and complex, so any comprehensive theory of the business cycle that can apply closely to reality must be very complicated.” He thought it was funny because he thought the whole point of science was the opposite—find a simple, wildly unrealistic model that cuts through the noise. If the speech uses that, the economists will grin, but you have to translate it in about one sentence so his brother and grandkids get why it's kindly meant.

Can you write the toast? It should be about five minutes when read aloud, affectionate rather than a lecture or a roast, and it has to land for both halves of the room. It can't be a philosophy seminar, and it can't say anything he would have considered false—he would have considered it false to tell people to just "get realistic" about themselves, and equally false to say that because assumptions don't need to be realistic, it doesn't matter that he's sick or retiring. Don't give me a bibliography or citations; I just need the speech itself. Please write it in American English.
