I sit on our hospital's ethics consult service and I'm also trying to draft my own advance directive, and going case by case I've noticed that my gut verdicts about when an earlier self gets to bind a later one don't add up to any principle I can actually state. Here they are:

1. A woman with advanced Alzheimer's wrote, while clearly capacitated, that she wanted no antibiotics if she ever stopped recognizing her family. She now has pneumonia, seems content, recognizes no one, and would probably resist treatment if it were explained to her. I think we honor the directive and let her die.

2. A healthy 22-year-old writes that he wants no ventilator if he is ever permanently paralyzed and dependent on others. At 30 he is a C3 quadriplegic, practices law, and says his life is good. I think the directive has no force. But his earlier self was as ignorant of what quadriplegia is like from the inside as the woman in (1) was of dementia — and there I deferred to that ignorance, here I don't.

3. A man with bipolar disorder, euthymic, signs a psychiatric advance directive authorizing ECT if he later refuses it during a depressive episode, and adds that his refusal is to count as a genuine change of mind only if he has been euthymic for thirty days. He later refuses during a severe episode and meets ordinary clinical standards of competence. I think the earlier self wins, and I also think his clause about which refusals count is legitimate: an earlier self may stipulate the conditions under which the later self's word counts as a revision.

4. A woman with a strong family history of dementia writes the same kind of clause, except that hers says no revocation counts unless she passes a standardized capacity screening — which she will by construction fail once the dementia arrives. My gut says this one should not be honored, that an earlier self shouldn't be able to make itself permanently unreachable. But I can't say what distinguishes it from (3) except that it is more effective, and greater effectiveness shouldn't be what discredits it.

5. A secular man becomes devout after a near-death experience during cardiac arrest and refuses a blood transfusion; I think his refusal binds. A devout woman with a written refusal of blood products becomes irreligious and emotionally flattened after a stroke to the frontal lobes and now asks for the transfusion; I think the directive still binds. Both are abrupt value changes following a brain event, and I go opposite ways.

My tentative principle: the earlier self governs just in case the later self's divergence is explained either by an impairment of the capacities needed to revise one's values or by the earlier self's ignorance of what the condition is like from the inside; where the divergence is the later self's own informed and unimpaired revision, the later self governs. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the principle, or the candidates you can't choose between — then give the reasoning with no preamble and no restatement of my question. Test it against all five cases, say which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, and state what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: a directive written by someone who has since ended up, by ordinary standards, better off than they expected to be. And tell me whether the principle is supposed to travel outside medicine at all — to a lifetime vow, or an irrevocable precommitment contract signed at 25 and regretted at 45. Please write in American English.
