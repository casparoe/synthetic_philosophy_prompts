**Question.** Suppose a field tests hypotheses with two-sided tests at α = .05. Assume that the fraction of tested hypotheses that are genuinely non-null is π = 0.20, that every study has power 0.80 when the hypothesis is non-null, and that separate studies are independent. Ignore p-hacking, measurement error, heterogeneity across labs, and publication bias except where you explicitly say you are relaxing them.

A committee proposes a reform called dual-run confirmation: a finding may be published only if an original study and an exact, pre-registered replication of it both return significance at .05 *and* in the same direction.

(a) Under the status quo (single run), what proportion of statistically significant findings come from null hypotheses? What is the corresponding figure under dual-run confirmation? Show your work and state any approximation you make.

(b) The committee reasons as follows: dual-run confirmation cuts the false discovery rate by more than an order of magnitude, so the crisis of replication is at bottom a problem about the logic of significance testing, and the fix must be a change in the inferential rule rather than a change in how researchers are incentivised. Discuss this argument. In doing so, say what does the real work in the arithmetic of part (a) — a stricter effective threshold, or the accumulation of evidential support across two studies — and whether a comparable reduction could be obtained within single-run testing, for instance by lowering α or by raising power through larger samples. Finally, comment briefly on one way in which the idealising assumptions at the top of the question make the arithmetic misleading about real fields.

**Student answer.**

(a) Single run: non-null hypotheses yield significance at rate 0.20 × 0.80 = 0.16 of all tested hypotheses; nulls yield significance at rate 0.80 × 0.05 = 0.04. So 0.20 of tested hypotheses come out significant, and 0.04 / 0.20 = 20% of significant findings are null.

Dual run: for a null hypothesis, both studies must be significant *and* in the same direction, so 2 × 0.025² = 0.00125; weighted by the 0.80 of hypotheses that are null, that is 0.001. For a non-null hypothesis the chance both studies are significant in the same direction is 0.80 × 0.80 = 0.64, since the chance that both come out significant in the wrong direction is Φ(−1.96 − 2.80) ≈ 10⁻⁶ and can be ignored; weighted by 0.20 that gives 0.128. Total published findings: 0.129, of which nulls make up 0.001 / 0.129 ≈ 0.8%. So the false discovery rate falls from 20% to under 1%, a drop of about twenty-five fold.

(b) The twenty-five-fold drop happens because the second run multiplies the evidence: finding the same hypothesis supported twice is a Bayesian update, and the posterior probability that the hypothesis is true goes up by roughly the product of the two likelihood ratios. That is why the reform works, and it shows that what really matters is evidence rather than error rates. Lowering α in a single study would not do the same job: α is a property of the test under the null, and cutting it merely cuts the false positive rate proportionally, whereas the dual-run rule changes how much support the data provide. Nor would raising power help much, since power concerns misses rather than false alarms. The long-run error rates that Neyman and Pearson cared about are only a stand-in for the quantity we actually want, the probability that the hypothesis is true given the data; p-values never deliver that, which is why significance testing has produced a literature full of findings that do not hold up. Fisher's own framework already contains the idea that a test has power and that α should be fixed by weighing the costs of false positives against those of false negatives, but even he could not get from a p-value to the probability that a hypothesis is true. If the field reported Bayes factors, or posterior probabilities computed from an explicit prior, replication rates would improve even without pre-registration and without any change to publication practices, since the stopping rule principle means Bayesian inference is unaffected by the researcher's decision about when to stop collecting data, and a Bayesian simply conditions on all the data.

Two caveats about the assumptions. First, they make the arithmetic misleading, because in real fields p-hacking pushes the effective α well above .05, and because publication bias means π cannot be estimated from the published literature at all. Second, dual-run confirmation does not touch what one might call the quiet file drawer: a pre-registered study that comes out null is simply never written up, so the reform relocates the selection rather than removing it. There is also a cost — sensitivity falls from 80% to 64%, so the reform trades false discoveries for missed ones — and a failure to replicate is not the same thing as a false positive, since the original effect may be real but smaller than the significance filter led people to believe, or may not survive in a different population.
