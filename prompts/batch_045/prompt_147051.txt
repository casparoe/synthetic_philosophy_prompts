I've got a question about Galen Strawson's Basic Argument that I keep going back and forth on, and I'd rather have someone pull it apart than nod along.

I listened to a philosophy podcast episode on free will last week and the host summarized Strawson's Basic Argument roughly like this: you do what you do because of the way you are — your temperament, desires, character. Nobody chooses their character, and you couldn't, because any choice you made would itself come out of the character you already had. So to be really responsible for what you do, you'd have to be responsible for the way you are, and that would mean creating yourself out of nothing, which is impossible. Free will is therefore an illusion: given determinism, every decision was already fixed by your genes and upbringing long before you made it, and even if you bring in quantum randomness it doesn't help, because random isn't the same as up to you. The upshot is that nobody is ever morally responsible in the desert-entailing sense, so blame and punishment are never fair and we ought to give them up — though the host allowed that Strawson himself says we can't actually stop blaming people. Then he said the argument is basically just the old regress worry that Leibniz and Reid already disposed of, so most philosophers consider it dealt with and we can all go home.

I'm only a philosophy minor, so I can tell *something* is off — determinism seems to be doing a lot of work inside a summary of an argument I keep being told is neutral between determinism and indeterminism — but I can't tell which of the other bits are load-bearing and which are just a popularizer being breezy. Is this a fair summary?

And here's the bit I actually care about, because I've been turning it over since. It occurred to me that the regress might not have the shape Strawson needs it to have. He seems to picture it as a ladder: to be responsible for the self you are now you'd need a prior self that formed it, and a prior self before that, forever. But what if it's a loop instead? Say the way I am at thirty is substantially the product of deliberate choices I made at twenty, which came out of the way I was at twenty, which was itself partly shaped by choices I made at fifteen, and so on back — so that the adult I am is genuinely the product of my own earlier agency over time, with feedback at every stage, rather than of one unchosen starting point. Causal loops aren't vicious in general. So does the Basic Argument have anything to say to a circular self-formation story, or does it just assume the regress has to be linear and terminating? And if Strawson *does* have an answer to it, does that answer quietly rely on determinism (or on indeterminism) being true, which would be strange for an argument that's supposed to work either way? I half suspect I've just reinvented compatibilism and that Strawson would say my loop bottoms out in stuff I didn't choose, but I can't see exactly where the reply is supposed to bite, or whether it bites any harder than it does against the ordinary reasons-responsive compatibilist.
