Last night a friend and I spent over an hour arguing about a one-sentence reference letter and got nowhere, so I would like an outside verdict. The letter was written for our friend Marta by a senior colleague she had asked for a reference; it confirmed only the dates of her post in his group and contained no assessment of her work at all. Theo and I then disputed what the writer had actually done.

1. Theo: there is no implicature here. Gricean implicature is calculated from what is said, and what is said is true and in itself complete; the damaging reading comes from an omission plus Marta's knowledge of what such letters normally contain, so it is an inference from silence rather than something the writer meant.
2. I replied that the first Quantity maxim is what does the work: the point of the exchange fixes how informative he is required to be, and by saying only that he manifestly falls short, so the hearer recovers a further content on the assumption that he is still being cooperative — he would have said more if he had anything good to say.
3. Theo: Grice distinguishes flouting a maxim from quietly violating it, and only the blatant case generates an implicature; a terse letter is not blatant in the way an obviously false or absurdly roundabout remark is, and a recipient might simply take him for a busy man. He added a test: a genuine implicature should survive being addressed to a reader with no knowledge of the genre, so if the reading depends on local expectations about what such letters contain, it is a convention of the genre rather than an implicature.
4. I said the calculation tolerates indeterminacy — the content need only be that he has nothing positive to say, with Marta's step to 'he thinks I am weak' being her own further inference — and that non-detachability supports me: every way of saying only that carries the reading, while the same sentence sent to a body that had asked for nothing but dates carries nothing, which is just what a particularized implicature is.
5. Theo: then suppose he thought well of her and stayed quiet out of pique; on your own account he has still conveyed something false, and if implicatures are part of what a speaker means, calling that merely misleading is a technicality.
6. I said the distinction is real: lying requires asserting what you believe to be false, whereas misleading through what one implicates can consist in conveying something true, and it is the mechanism that separates the two wrongs.
7. Theo: Grice's own account of speaker meaning — getting someone to believe something by means of their recognition that you intend them to — fits what he did exactly, since he knew she would read the letter in that light; so the mechanism does no moral work, and the only question that matters to Marta is whether to ask him again.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the seven from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each, then say who is more right overall and what both of us were missing. Three points in particular: whether the flout/violate distinction can bear the weight I put on it; whether an implicature can be generated by omission alone, or whether the genre-knowledge test shows this case to be conventional rather than conversational; and whether 'he misled but did not lie' is a distinction with a difference here. I have tried to state Theo's moves as strongly as I can, but I am the one defending the implicature claim, so do not take my framing of the dispute for granted.
