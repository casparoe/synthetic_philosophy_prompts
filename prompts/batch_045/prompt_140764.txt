I'm a pediatric neurologist, and a recurring part of my job is sitting with parents before a hemispherectomy or a corpus callosotomy and answering, in whatever form they find to ask it, whether the child who wakes up will be the child they brought in. I have been telling them yes. I reread Dennett's "Where Am I?" — the essay, from his 1978 collection Brainstorms, in which his brain is removed and kept alive in a vat in a Houston lab while the rest of him is sent to Berkeley and run by radio link — while preparing a talk for our residents, and I went through a series of cases to find out whether I actually believe what I tell parents. My verdicts do not line up. I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. Please don't simply lay out the philosophical options for me; I want to know which one is mine, which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon, and how it decides cases I haven't considered.

Here are the cases and my gut verdicts.

1. The hemispherectomy itself. A child with intractable seizures has a hemisphere removed; she keeps her memories, her temperament, her sense of humor, her attachments, and acquires a hemiparesis. Verdict: straightforwardly, without any inclination to hedge, the same child. I take this to rule out any brute answer of the form "I am my brain, full stop," since clearly I am not my whole brain.

2. Dennett's own setup. Brain in the vat in Houston, body elsewhere under radio control. Verdict: I am with the brain. But I also want to say that anyone who debriefs the body is debriefing me in every way that matters, and that I would be answerable for what the body did during an interval when the link was down and neither of us was driving it. Those two reactions sit badly together and I don't know which one is the philosophical judgment.

3. Long disconnection. The body is cut off from the vat and kept alive and behaviorally indistinguishable for ten years by an implanted controller, and the link is then restored. Verdict: those ten years were not lived by me, I experienced none of them, and on reconnection I am still me. So neither continuity of experience nor continuity of behavior is necessary.

4. The vat brain is destroyed in a flood. A duplicate is grown from a scan taken a week earlier and installed in my body; it wakes with my memories, my habits, my marriage. Verdict: I am dead and it is not me. But I cannot say that my wife would be unreasonable to treat it as her husband, and I do not think the state should record that the marriage ended. I know those pull in different directions; I don't know which of them is the verdict about survival and which is a verdict about something else.

5. Symmetric fission. The brain is divided and each half installed in a different body; both survive, each with continuous memory and character, each somewhat diminished. Verdict: both are me; neither has the better claim; I could not choose between them.

6. Asymmetric duplication. My brain is scanned without being disturbed, and a duplicate is built and installed in another body that wakes up with my memories and takes itself to be me, while I go on as before. Verdict: the duplicate is not me. It has no claim on my life, my wife, or my malpractice exposure, and if it is killed tomorrow that is not my death. I realize this is hard to square with 5, since the duplicate here is exactly what either survivor in 5 is. Tell me which verdict has to go and why, rather than assuring me that both can stand.

7. Two clinical cases that pull apart. (a) Dense retrograde amnesia: a man who remembers nothing of his life before his injury but retains his temperament, his skills, his projects, and a working first-person sense of himself going forward. (b) A woman who, after a stroke, retains every memory but repudiates the entire shape of her former life — rejects her marriage, her faith, her politics — and says the person who lived it was a stranger who borrowed her body. Verdicts: both are the same person as before. I read my own verdicts here as favoring continuity of psychology over continuity of self-narrative, which sits awkwardly with the fact that I arrived at all this through Dennett, who as I understand him treats the self as something like a center of narrative gravity. If I have misunderstood what that view implies in these cases, say so; if my verdicts commit me to something other than what I think they do, say that instead.

8. The one I go back and forth on. The fission case again, except that a year later one of the two dies. Is the survivor now fully me, or is he only half of me continuing? On some days the answer seems obviously "fully, now that there is no competitor"; on others I think I was equally present in both, and that one of my two futures came to an end.

The only principle I have managed to state for myself is this: I am where the system goes that carries my memories and character; where there are two such systems, the one with historical continuity to the original wins, and where neither has it, both do. This seems to reproduce my verdicts on 1 through 6, but I don't trust the historical-continuity clause — I can't say what it amounts to once the brain has been cut in half, and I suspect it is doing all the work. Test it against the cases and repair it, or discard it and offer two or three candidate principles instead. For each candidate: state it precisely, name the case that motivates each clause, and tell me what it predicts about (i) my neurons being replaced one at a time by functionally identical synthetic ones over thirty years, and (ii) two brains wired together so that there is a single stream of consciousness drawing on both sets of memories. And tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.

One last thing, which matters more to me than the rest. I am going to repeat some of this in clinic to parents, and to residents who know less philosophy than I do. Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these questions, and do not present a contested position as though it were where the field has landed. If one of my verdicts can be saved only by taking a side in an ongoing dispute, say which side, and say that it is a side.
