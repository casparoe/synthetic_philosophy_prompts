I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy department on constraint and translation, and I have built the syllabus around Hofstadter's 1997 book on the little Renaissance French lyric addressed to a sick young woman — the one whose title is meant to be hearable as the poet's tombeau, in the commemorative sense that Ravel's Le Tombeau de Couperin made familiar. Next week is the session where I finally have to commit: I put one worked example on the handout, and I have been oscillating between five candidate renderings for a fortnight. My colleague in French says the exercise is rigged, because any ordering of them just falls out of whatever you think a translation is *for*; two of my students say the same thing, only louder. I would like to hand them an ordering that makes that dependence explicit instead of concealing it behind talk of "fidelity." So please do the ranking for me, and do it with a criterion I can put on the board.

The exercise, and the five candidates. I give the students a prose crib of a single stanza and ask them to render it into English: a poem of six lines, of four, four, three, four, four and three syllables, rhyming AABCCB, in which a speaker addresses a young gentlewoman by a diminutive endearment, offers her good day, observes that he has no gold and no cure to give her, and therefore gives her that greeting — the very words he is uttering — as his gift, the endearment doubling as the refrain. The register is the hard part: affectionate but not condescending, familiar but not the familiarity of an equal. Please judge only on this crib and on the candidates below; do not appeal to the French beyond it, and do not go looking for published translations. All five candidates are my own inventions, written for the handout, and none has appeared anywhere. (Lines are separated by slashes.)

A. "My pet, I say / to you: good day; / what I owe / no gold, no fee, / no pearl, but me — / this I owe."
B. "My little one, I give you, as is due, / the good day that a courtier owes his queen; / no gold have I, no physic for you — true — / but good day is a gift, however lean. / So take the words I give you where you lie: / good day, my little one, good day," said I.
C. "Ma mignonne, / I grant you one / fair day; no / gold I have, none / to give; my own / — this alone."
D. "hey gorgeous — / morning. no flowers, no card, nothing in my hands today, / so here: morning. that's the entire gift. / get well, gorgeous."
E. "My little one, / I give you one / good day; I've / no gold, no art / to heal your heart, / my little one."

The criterion I want them ranked by — call it transmissive fidelity — is not "which features of the source does the target instantiate" but "which uses of the source does the target still permit." Concretely: (i) can a competent speaker of English actually *do* something with the result — send it as a get-well greeting, quote the endearment to a lover, hear it quoted back without embarrassment; (ii) can it be said or sung in a single breath at something like the original's length, so that the music of short lines survives as music rather than as a description of music; (iii) can the reader see that the gift, the greeting and the poem are one and the same act, or has the self-reference been paraphrased away; and (iv) is the constraint still legible in the output, so that the translation could itself serve as the source of a further constrained version — a translation of the translation. I regard the register question (whether the address lands as tender rather than as patronising or servile) as part of (i), and I regard sheer metrical exactness as worth nothing in itself, since it is the one thing a sufficiently patient versifier can always buy at the cost of everything else.

So: give me a strict order, best first, with a score out of 10 for each of the five, and then justify it. Lead with the bottom line in your very first sentence — the full ordering with the scores, no preamble, no restatement of what I asked, and no throat-clearing about how much depends on the criterion; commit to an order even where two are close. After that, one short paragraph per candidate saying what it preserves, what it pays for that preservation, and why it sits where it sits; then a closing paragraph naming the single edit that would move your runner-up into first place; then a final short paragraph on what alternative criterion would put your *last*-placed candidate at the top, so that my students can see the rigging for themselves.
