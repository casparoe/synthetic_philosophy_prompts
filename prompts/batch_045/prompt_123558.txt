I have no formal training in philosophy or classics, but over the past couple of years I have been trying, entirely on my own, to work out a principled distinction between manipulation and legitimate persuasion. The rough test I have arrived at I call the traceable-redescription condition. The idea is that a persuasion episode is legitimate if the causal story of how the hearer came to believe the conclusion can be redescribed, without loss, as a chain of reasons that connects the conclusion to premises the hearer antecedently accepted or would accept once made explicit. It is manipulative, on this test, if the causal story contains a step that cannot be so redescribed: a step the hearer would repudiate as a reason if it were dragged into the open, as in "I accepted it because the speaker made me afraid" or "because I mistook his confident manner for knowledge." I am not at all confident that this test is coherent, and I have no idea whether it resembles anything that has been said before; I am simply trying to think it through.

Recently I came across the following passage in Book I, chapter 2 of Aristotle's Rhetoric, in an old English translation, and I would like help understanding it. The passage reads:

"Now the proofs furnished by the speech are of three kinds. The first depends upon the moral character of the speaker, the second upon putting the hearer into a certain frame of mind, the third upon the speech itself, in so far as it proves or seems to prove. The orator persuades by moral character when his speech is delivered in such a manner as to render him worthy of confidence; for we feel confidence in a greater degree and more readily in persons of worth in regard to everything in general, but where there is no certainty and there is room for doubt, our confidence is absolute. But this confidence must be due to the speech itself, not to any preconceived idea of the speaker's character; for it is not the case, as some writers of rhetorical treatises lay down in their “Art,” that the worth of the orator in no way contributes to his powers of persuasion; on the contrary, moral character, so to say, constitutes the most effective means of proof. The orator persuades by means of his hearers, when they are roused to emotion by his speech; for the judgements we deliver are not the same when we are influenced by joy or sorrow, love or hate; and it is to this alone that, as we have said, the present-day writers of treatises endeavor to devote their attention. （We will discuss these matters in detail when we come to speak of the emotions.） Lastly, persuasion is produced by the speech itself, when we establish the true or apparently true from the means of persuasion applicable to each individual subject."

I find the passage confusing in several ways, and I would be grateful for help with it rather than for any scholarly apparatus. Please do not give me citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I only want help understanding the passage and my own distinction.

First, I do not understand what Aristotle is counting as a "proof furnished by the speech." Does "furnished by the speech" mean that all three kinds are supposed to be produced by the words the speaker actually uses, as opposed to relying on the speaker's prior reputation or on documents and witnesses that are not created by the speech? If so, then even the first kind—the one depending on the speaker's moral character—is not simply the speaker's pre-existing character, but a character that the speech itself creates or displays, since the passage insists that the confidence "must be due to the speech itself, not to any preconceived idea of the speaker's character." Is that the right way to read it? If it is, then my assumption that character-based persuasion is automatically manipulative looks mistaken, because on this reading the speech is still doing the work.

Second, Aristotle calls all three of these "proofs," and he even says that moral character "constitutes the most effective means of proof." That makes me wonder whether his notion of proof is much broader than my notion of a reason. If "proof" here just means whatever reliably produces conviction, then it seems to include exactly the kind of non-rational influence I was trying to isolate as manipulation, and my manipulation/legitimate-persuasion distinction would not line up with his three kinds at all. On the other hand, the third kind is said to prove or "seem to prove," and to establish what is "true or apparently true," which suggests that within the third kind there can be a deceptive imitation of proof. So my question is: is Aristotle's classification orthogonal to my distinction, or does it cut across it? More concretely, can each of his three kinds be divided into a manipulative and a legitimate version under my traceable-redescription test, or does the test really apply only within the third kind, between what really proves and what merely seems to prove?

Third, I would find it helpful if you could describe two miniature examples: one that fits one of Aristotle's three kinds but would count as legitimate under my traceable-redescription test, and one that fits one of the three kinds but would count as manipulative under it. I am less interested in being told which answer is historically correct about Aristotle than in seeing how the threefold classification and my homemade test interact, and where the test breaks down. If the passage exposes a confusion in the test, please explain what that confusion is, using the passage's own distinctions.
