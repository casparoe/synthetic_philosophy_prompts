Suppose you are advising the finance minister of a small, labour-rich but cash-poor commonwealth who is considering replacing metallic coinage with a non-convertible, tax-backed 'employment scrip': the government would issue the scrip only to pay for domestic labour and staple goods, would withdraw the old coins, and would manage the total issue so as to employ all willing workers rather than to hit an inflation target. Berkeley, in The Querist, asks 'Whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?' and 'And whether gold, silver, and paper are not tickets or counters for reckoning, recording, and transferring thereof?'; he adds that money is useful only as it 'stirreth up industry, enabling men mutually to participate the fruits of each other's labour,' that 'the denominations being retained, although the bullion were gone, things might not nevertheless be rated, bought, and sold, industry promoted, and a circulation of commerce maintained,' and that 'the wealth be not proportioned to the industry, and this to the circulation of credit, be the credit circulated or transferred by what marks or tokens soever?' Which implications of this Berkeleyan account for the minister's scrip proposal matter most, and which alleged implications are overrated or do not follow from the quoted claims at all? Give me a defended ranking: say for each candidate implication how directly it follows from those claims, how much it should change the minister's decision, how assessable it is without new empirical data, and what would move it up or down the list; do not survey Berkeley scholarship or provide a reading list, and treat the scrip proposal as I have defined it.
