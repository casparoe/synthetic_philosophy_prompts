I have no formal training in any of this. I've been reading popular accounts of the South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission, the Argentine trials and amnesties, and Rwanda's gacaca courts on my own, and I attend a monthly discussion group where a single sentence gets used to end arguments: truth is the road to reconciliation. It was last used to settle two separate disputes, and I want to know what it would have to mean in order to settle either of them.

The first use was to defend the amnesty-for-testimony arrangement. Perpetrators receive relief from prosecution in exchange for a full account of what they did, and the argument was that a society which knows what happened can mend while a society that punishes in silence cannot. The second use was to argue against checking victims' testimony against police files, forensic evidence, and perpetrators' contradictory statements, on the grounds that treating testimony as something to be verified puts truth on trial and re-traumatizes the people who came forward. I would like to know which precise claim about truth, if any, supports each of these uses, and whether the two uses need the same claim or different ones.

Three distinctions I arrived at on my own, which I would like the answer to treat as tests rather than set aside.

One. I think there are two different kinds of not-knowing, and the slogan may be sliding between them. Sometimes the gap is informational: nobody knows where the body is, who gave the order, or whether the person is dead at all. Sometimes the gap is official: the perpetrators know, the neighbours know, the file exists, and what is missing is only that the state now says so. Call these unrecovered truth and denied truth. My suspicion is that most of what these commissions actually recover is the second kind, and that the two kinds might justify quite different institutions.

Two. Amnesty in exchange for testimony looks to me as though it buys a biased sample. The people who come forward are those with the most to gain, and what they describe will tend to be what is cheapest for them to admit: the acts already partly documented, or the acts in which they can present themselves as a minor figure obeying orders. The resulting record would then be systematically skewed, not merely incomplete. If that is right, it seems to cut against the first use of the slogan in a way that it does not cut against the second, and I would like that asymmetry made explicit.

Three. The trade-off framing assumes that justice is genuinely being given up in exchange for truth. But if the same perpetrators would in any case have escaped punishment, because the courts were gutted, the archives destroyed, or a blanket amnesty was already on the table, then the truth was not bought with justice; it was bought with something that was never going to be spent. I cannot tell whether this makes the arrangement better or worse, and I suspect the answer depends on which reading of the slogan is in play.

A fourth worry, briefly. Being listened to by an official body is a relief, and participants in these processes do report feeling vindicated. But a sympathetic interviewer or a radio phone-in might produce much the same relief. If what is doing the work is the experience of being heard, the slogan may be recommending something with nothing specifically to do with truth, and the way to check this is to ask what we would say about a commission that affirmed every account brought to it without ever testing one.

Please give me the disambiguation as exactly five numbered readings of the sentence, ordered from the thinnest and most nearly trivial to the strongest and most doubtful, each in one or two sentences. For each reading, state the precise claim it makes, say whether it is trivial, defensible but contested, or probably false, and say whether it would actually support the first use, the second use, both, or neither. No introduction and no conclusion.
