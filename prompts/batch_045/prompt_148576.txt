I write about science and ideas for a national magazine, and I am working on a long feature about the hinge-of-history hypothesis: roughly, the claim that we occupy an unusual and extremely narrow window in which the choices of a comparatively tiny present generation can determine the character of an astronomically much larger future, either by cutting that future off or by locking in values and institutions that then persist for as long as the future lasts. I have read enough of the surrounding literature that I do not need the standard exposition repeated back to me: I know the expected-value arithmetic, the point that the size of the future swamps near-term considerations, time-of-perils reasoning, and the argument that faster technological development is not always better. What I cannot get from the literature is the thing my editor keeps demanding of me, which is whether the hypothesis does any real work when aimed at an actual case.

The case I keep returning to is the recombinant DNA conference at Asilomar, and I will supply the facts so that you can take them as given rather than reconstruct them. In 1974 a group of prominent molecular biologists wrote to the president of the National Academy of Sciences asking for a committee to assess the hazards of the new technique of combining DNA from different organisms; the committee recommended an international meeting and asked researchers to halt such experiments in the meantime. The meeting took place in February 1975 at the Asilomar conference grounds on the Monterey Peninsula, with roughly 140 participants, mostly biologists but with some lawyers and physicians, and it produced voluntary guidelines rather than law: containment was to be built into experimental design and calibrated to estimated risk, using both biological barriers such as disabled bacterial hosts unable to survive outside a laboratory and physical measures such as hoods, restricted access, and negative-pressure rooms, together with training and good microbiological practice. Research then resumed under those rules, and the rules were relaxed over the following years as the feared hazards failed to materialize.

Here is what I want from you, and I would rather have arguments I can put in my own voice than citations or a reading list. First, a disciplined mapping: on the hypothesis's own terms, who were the relevant agents, what were their actual options, and what were the stakes once the long-run is included? Be explicit about where the hypothesis is silent — it does not tell you which technology is pivotal, or who has standing to decide for a future that cannot consent. Second, what would it have prescribed at Asilomar: a hard moratorium, or differential technological development, that is, steering which capabilities arrive first rather than trying to slow everything down? And how is one supposed to weigh the cost of the delay against a future whose value is astronomical? My editor's objection is that the hypothesis can be deployed to justify either answer and so predicts nothing; I would like you to take that seriously rather than simply rebut it. Third, what does the hypothesis explain about Asilomar and what does it misdescribe? The participants were worried about near-term, local, and personally salient harms, above all a cancer risk to laboratory workers and their families; the remedy was cheap relative to the size of the field; and the community was small, cohesive, and able to see itself in the same room. Those are not features of the hypothesis's canonical cases, and the fact that any one lab could have defected and captured most of the early gains raises the unilateralist's curse, where the option of acting first makes the collective outcome worse. Does that make Asilomar a case the hypothesis was never built for? Fourth, run the analysis in the other direction: what does the episode show about the theory? In particular, apply the counterfactual test I intend to use in the piece — if the delegates had explicitly reasoned as hinge-of-history theorists, what would they have decided differently? If the honest answer is nothing, then crediting the outcome to that style of reasoning looks like retrospective cherry-picking from a small set of celebrated successes, and I want to know whether that charge lands or whether there is a defensible reply. Finally, a short coda: the Asilomar-as-model claim is now invoked constantly in present-day arguments about governing transformative technologies, and in 2018 a researcher announced the birth of gene-edited babies to near-universal condemnation, which is often described as that model failing. Say what that later episode does and does not establish about self-governance by a research community, and about whether the hypothesis's prescriptions are enforceable at all.
