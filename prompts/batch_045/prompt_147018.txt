I coordinate a community advisory committee for a regional transit authority — fourteen volunteer members, one ninety-minute meeting a month, a staff analytics team that's genuinely good but has its own incentives, and an appointed board that can veto anything we recommend and is impatient with us. Proposals land on us constantly: fare restructuring, a bus lane, a fare-free pilot on two routes, cutting a late-night route that a neighborhood association hates, putting cameras on buses, an algorithmic scheduling tool that staff says will shave 8% off operating cost. Right now we lurch between two failure modes. Either the analysts show us a model and we rubber-stamp "the data says," or else we just take a straw vote and it becomes whoever showed up and complained loudest. I've been reading some Dewey — the bit where democracy isn't just a voting mechanism but a way of doing inquiry together, and where a policy is supposed to be treated as an experiment with consequences you actually go back and look at — and it felt like it was describing exactly our problem, but I can't figure out how to actually run it in a room. So: give me a procedure, something a facilitator can work through out loud in about twenty minutes per proposal, with a one-page version I can tape to my notebook. Triage questions in order, what counts as a good-enough answer to each, where the procedure just stops and says no, and where it explicitly hands the decision to a human because the question is one of values rather than facts. A few things I keep tripping on and want the procedure to handle, using whatever vocabulary works — I've been calling them my own names, don't feel obliged to keep them: the "shadow public," people who are definately affected but will never be in that room (night-shift workers, people currently priced off the system, the town next door that gets our parking spillover, riders who don't exist yet); the "feedback horizon," meaning whether the people who eat the costs find out and get to respond before the thing is locked in, or whether the bill comes due after the board that authorized it has turned over; "counterfactual visibility," whether the losers can even tell what they'd otherwise have had; and the fact that some decisions are just irreversible and therefore can't honestly be called experiments at all.

Please apply it to three real-shaped cases so I can see it work. One: staff proposes replacing the flat fare with income-based discounted fares, and they have a revenue model. Two: the algorithmic scheduling tool, which the board already loves and which I wanted to propose as a one-year pilot on a single depot — my guess is your procedure is going to tell me something I don't want to hear about that one, and I want you to say it plainly rather than soften it. Three: the late-night route with dismal ridership that a well-organized neighborhood association wants gone. For that third one I especially need the "minimum we must do before we're allowed to vote" answer, and I need a sentence or two I can literally read out loud to the room when I have to tell fourteen people we're not voting today.

And be honest at the end about what the procedure can't do. My worry is that anything built around testable consequences and reversible pilots will systematically favor decisions that are legible and quick to evaluate, and quietly push the slow, structural, hard-to-measure stuff — the kind of thing that probably matters most to the people who never show up — into the "not decidable by us" bucket. If that's a real flaw and not just me being paranoid, say so, and tell me what I'd have to add to catch those cases. Constraints worth knowing: my committee members have no statistics background and will not read a methodology appendix, staff will push back if the procedure lets us override their numbers on vibes alone, and whatever I hand the board has to be short enough to survive being skimmed.
