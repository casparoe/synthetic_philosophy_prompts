Two of us on the LessWrong-adjacent Discord have been going back and forth for about a week on whether a trained net "really has" features or whether features are just a coordinate system we lay over it, like latitude and longitude. He wrote out an argument for the anti-realist side. I cant find the hole in it, but the conclusion makes me want to bite a bullet I really don't want to bite. Here it is, cleaned up a bit:

(1) No difference in representation without a difference in causal role. If two systems agree on every causal fact about their internals — for every intervention you could perform on one there's an intervention on the other with the same downstream consequences, and vice versa — then they represent the same things. [Otherwise interpretability couldnt be right or wrong about anything, only more or less convenient for us, and nobody actually believes that: the whole selling point of mech interp is that the lookup-table net and the genuine bridge-detector net differ in a way that matters even though they score the same.]

(2) Behaviour doesn't fix features. Two nets can agree on every input and still differ in what they represent: one computes the answer from a real "is a bridge mentioned here" quantity, the other keys off some spurious correlate or just memorises. Everyone agrees on this, and it's consistent with (1), because (1) is about causal role, not about input–output behaviour.

(3) Smearing. Take any trained net N and any invertible linear map A on its residual stream at some layer. Build N_A by applying A straight after that layer and folding A^{-1} into the next block's weights. N_A computes exactly the same function as N on every input, on-distribution or off, and every intervention has a counterpart: we checked it numerically on a toy MLP — setting N's activations at that layer to v gives the same output as setting N_A's to Av. [We argued for ages about whether LayerNorm and Adam break the construction, since neither is rotation-equivariant, and we decided it doesn't matter because we're comparing two trained nets, not two training runs. I'm not sure we were right to decide that.]

(4) But N and N_A differ in what they represent. The intermediate quantity at that layer in N_A is A h(x), not h(x), so the things its downstream circuit operates on are smeared mixtures of N's features — 0.7 bridge + 0.3 France, that sort of thing. Call it the gruesome basis. If N's features are bridge, France, anger, then N_A's are mixtures of them.

(5) By (1), since N and N_A agree on every causal fact, they represent the same things. By (4), they don't. So there's no fact of the matter about which decomposition is "the" features: a feature set is a basis we pick, and an SAE dictionary is a projection, not a discovery.

(6) And that's where I get off, because it seems to prove too much. Run the same argument on variables in an ordinary Python program — alpha-rename everything, or refactor into an equivalent program whose variables hold mixtures of the originals — and out pops "no fact of the matter about what this variable holds", which is nonsense, the password is in that variable whatever you call it. It also eats (2): if feature talk is coordinate-relative, then the lookup-table net and the bridge-detector net don't differ representationally either, and "did it learn the feature or did it memorise" collapses into "which description do I find more legible". And then the stuff where a direction found in one model transfers to another trained on different data, and where clamping a feature way outside its training range still does something coherent, is a miracle.

I'd most like to keep (1) and (2) — they're the two I'd actually defend down the pub — and I'd much rather give up (5) than (6), but I can't tell what's wrong with (3) or (4), or whether the trouble is the step from them to (5). So: which step do you reject, why did it look fine when he wrote it down, and what does rejecting it cost me? If there's more than one way out, price them. And if some version of realism survives, I want to know what it says makes one basis privileged over the gruesome one — I keep seeing people reach for "the net's own downstream computation is sparse in that basis", for "gradient descent wouldn't ever have found the smeared one", and for "the features are whichever ones survive causal scrubbing", and I can't tell whether those are three answers or one answer in three costumes.
