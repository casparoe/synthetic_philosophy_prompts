You are a philosopher of mind who has published on the architecture of perception and on the epistemology of experience. Please think carefully, and before committing to an answer, spend a few lines brainstorming candidate correspondences and objections that you then prune.

In my graduate seminar the same argument recurs in defence of cognitive penetration. It runs like this: since Helmholtz we are entitled to model perception as inference — the visual system is like an investigator working from a degraded signal, combining that signal with priors to arrive at a hypothesis about the scene. But inference, properly so called, draws on whatever else the reasoner believes; that is what makes it ampliative rather than a reflex. So if perception really is inference, it is unremarkable, and in a sense predicted, that what a subject believes, fears, or has been told should show up in how things look, and the impenetrabilist is left defending an encapsulation that no inferential system could possibly have. A second student replies that the argument works only because the one word is doing two jobs: an inference is something an agent performs for reasons, revisable in light of arbitrary new evidence, whereas the visual system's transition from stimulation to perceptual state is subpersonal, hard-won, and developmentally fixed, and its priors are not the subject's beliefs and are not answerable to her testimony.

I want an audit of the analogy, not a verdict on the literature. Give me an explicit table of what plays the role of the evidence, the hypothesis space, the prior, the inferrer, the conclusion, and the revisability. Then separate the disanalogies that bear on the penetration argument from the ones that are merely cosmetic, and say what the analogy quietly imports into perception that a merely prior-guided, encapsulated system need not have: personal-level reasons, propositionally structured premises, diachronic revisability, sensitivity to just any consideration the subject can entertain.

To keep the audit honest, apply it to a case I stipulate rather than cite: subjects are trained that a faint chromatic tint in the left hemifield predicts a mild shock; after training the tint both looks different and elicits an autonomic response, but the effect appears already in the earliest retinotopic component of the visual evoked response, and it persists undiminished in subjects who have forgotten the training and now sincerely deny the association. Does the inference model have resources to describe this as a belief serving as a premise? If not, was the analogy ever carrying the penetration claim, or only the word?

Finally, is there a better analogy — perception as measurement, as a fixed-lag filter, as a language faculty parsing speech — and does it do the impenetrabilist's work without stipulating encapsulation as a premise?
