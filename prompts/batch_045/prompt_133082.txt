Background, as my teacher sets it out (I'm a first-year M.Phil. student in Sanskrit and my grasp is shaky, so correct me if I have garbled the framework). A pramā is a cognition that is true and that is a presentational awareness: an occurrent awareness (anubhava) as opposed to a mere re-running of something already cognised, i.e. as opposed to memory (smṛti). A pramāṇa is the instrument (karaṇa) that produces it. He recognises four: perception (pratyakṣa — produced by contact between a sense and its object), inference (anumāna — produced by a cognition of a pervasion, vyāpti, together with the sight of a mark, liṅga), comparison (upamāna — produced by a cognition of similarity together with what a trustworthy speaker said), and testimony (śabda — the utterance of a trustworthy speaker). He resists adding further pramāṇas.

I have been asking him to rule on individual cognitions — pramā or not, and if pramā, by which instrument — and his track record is harder to extrapolate from than I expected:

1. First sight of a clay pot on the courtyard floor at noon, in good light → pramā, pratyakṣa.
2. The same pot, deliberately looked at again the next morning, nothing about it or you having changed → pramā, pratyakṣa. He waved away the objection that nothing was newly learnt.
3. A coil of rope taken for a snake in the dusk → not pramā.
4. A white conch cognised as yellow by a jaundiced eye → not pramā.
5. Truly remembering, the next morning, the shelf where you left your spectacles → not pramā, though the content is true and wholly confident.
6. Seeing a man across the street and judging 'this is the Devadatta I met in Varanasi last year' → pramā, pratyakṣa; recognition is not a fifth pramāṇa, memory being merely an auxiliary here.
7. Seeing smoke on the far hill with the pervasion 'wherever there is smoke, there is fire' in mind, and judging 'there is fire on the hill' → pramā, anumāna.
8. Devadatta is fat and is never seen eating by day; judging 'he eats at night' → pramā, anumāna. Postulation (arthāpatti) is not a fifth pramāṇa.
9. Told by a forester that a gavaya is a wild animal somewhat like a cow; days later in the forest, seeing an unfamiliar animal and judging 'so this is what gavaya denotes' → pramā, upamāna. Here he does allow a distinct instrument, and insists this is not just perception plus testimony plus memory.
10. Told by a trustworthy villager that there is a ford a mile downstream, unseen by you and unverifiable from where you stand → pramā, śabda. He gives the same verdict where the content is something no sense of yours could ever check, e.g. that a rite correctly performed produces its stated result.
11. Looking at a stretch of bare floor where you had expected a pot, and judging 'there is no pot here' → pramā, pratyakṣa. Non-apprehension (anupalabdhi) is not a fifth pramāṇa — and, he added, this is not inference either.
12. Unable to tell whether the shape ahead is a man or a tree stump, wavering between the two → not pramā, it being doubt.
13. Dreaming that your brother has arrived; he does in fact arrive that day; on waking you are entirely convinced → not pramā.
