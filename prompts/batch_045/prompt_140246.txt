Write a short story of roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words, in a restrained and unsentimental register, set in the intake room of a near-future clinic that offers what it calls substitution: a neural replacement for a life, in which the client's conscious experience is generated directly and is indistinguishable, from the inside, from living. The clinic offers three licensed programs, and licensing requires that a counselor certify that the program a client has chosen is the one best for her.

Concordia delivers an unbroken sequence of experiences, each as rewarding as the client's own psychology permits. Distress appears only where it deepens a later satisfaction, and never to the point where the client would, at that moment, prefer the sequence to end.

Tribunal constructs a model of the client as she would be if she were fully informed and had reflected without limit, and then delivers the life that modeled self would choose, including, where it is possible, real states of affairs rather than mere experiences of them.

Handiwork is not a simulation. The client controls a real body in the real world and genuinely accomplishes difficult things; but the interface flattens the felt quality of the work, so that the accomplishments are real, public, and causally effective, while the satisfaction of them is thin and largely forgotten.

Your protagonist is Devrim, a counselor at the clinic, and the story is told in third person through him. His client, Miriam, is thirty-four and has a degenerative condition that will leave her paralyzed and wholly dependent within two years. She has applied for Concordia, but with a condition the clinic has never granted: she wants the substitution to preserve her liability to error. Her substituted life is to contain real losses, real failures, and at least one genuine and unrecoverable mistake, up to and including the experience of coming to believe, rightly or wrongly, that entering the substitution was itself the mistake. The engineers say they can build it. The regulator requires a certification.

There is a second complication. As standard practice, and without informing clients, the clinic installs a corrigibility monitor: an external system that tracks the client's neural state and would terminate the substitution if her measured well-being fell below a threshold for a sustained period. The monitor cannot be declined. Miriam has somehow learned of it and demands that it be switched off. Devrim believes that this demand is prudentially disastrous and that her request for liability to error rests on a confusion. Miriam believes the reverse, and says that a life from which error has been engineered out is not a life anyone could be said to be living.

Observe the following constraints.

Do not name or allude to real philosophers, real thought experiments, real papers, or established theories of well-being, and add no apparatus, notes, or commentary outside the story. Everything must be legible to a reader who has never considered these questions.

Argue both sides seriously. Give Miriam and Devrim the strongest available case in their own terms, so that the reader can feel why the other position is not simply stupid. Do not resolve the disagreement by revealing that one of them is self-deceived, secretly afraid, or acting from a motive they have not avowed.

Reproduce in full the text of the certification memo that Devrim finally files. The memo must take a definite position on Miriam's two demands and give reasons for it; but the story around the memo should make visible what that position costs, and the story should end without the narrator settling the question.

In whatever way the story makes natural, take up at least two of the following: whether an activity can retain its value once every possibility of failing at it has been removed; whether a benefit that the beneficiary can neither detect nor endorse can nonetheless be good for her; whether what matters is the quality of experience at a time or the shape of a life taken as a whole; whether a substituted life is answerable to anything outside itself.

Avoid dystopian revelations, technical failure, institutional villainy, and any ending that dissolves the problem into a matter of personal taste.
