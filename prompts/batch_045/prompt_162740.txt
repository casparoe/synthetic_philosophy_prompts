I'm a senior at a large public high school. This year I've been on the debate team and, separately, doing an independent reading project on assisted dying, which has meant arguing both sides of the question for most of eight months. I keep noticing one shape of argument and I don't have a word for it. I'd be glad of the name if there is one, but what I actually want is to know exactly what I'm looking at, so please don't stop at a label.

Here is the pattern. Take the number of people who actually use the law once it exists. When that number is small, supporters say: this is mostly reassurance, the safeguards are working, the alarm was overblown. Opponents say: a practice used this rarely cannot be worth the risk of a single mistake. When the number rises, supporters say: at last the people who were suffering in silence are getting what they asked for, and the earlier low figures only showed how many obstacles stood in the way. Opponents say: this is exactly what we warned about, the practice is spreading beyond the group it was supposed to serve. One quantity, moving in either direction, and both sides come away confirmed.

Second instance, different quantity: how many people withdraw a request for assisted death after they get good palliative care. A high rate means, to opponents, that such requests are unstable and that we would be killing people who would have changed their minds; to supporters, it means the screening is doing its job and those who remain are the settled ones. A low rate means, to supporters, that requests are stable and considered; to opponents, that the screening is a rubber stamp. Again, both directions get claimed.

Third instance, which I suspect is a different animal: the number of deaths that do not go as intended, where the person takes far longer than expected. Opponents treat anything above zero as decisive. Supporters say the number is too small to carry weight and that we cannot observe the person's experience anyway. Here the disagreement is not about which way the number points but about whether it can be taken at all.

Last semester I tried to fix this. Before looking at any figures, I asked people on both sides which way the number would have to come out for them to reconsider. Almost no one could answer — not "it would have to be enormous," but no direction at all.

So my questions. Does this configuration have an established name: an indicator that both sides agree in advance is relevant and that then supports both sides whichever way it comes out? I know about confirmation bias and motivated reasoning and I don't think that is quite it, since those are claims about how people reason toward a conclusion they already hold, whereas what I am describing looks like a property of the number itself — as though, given how loose the surrounding story is, both readings are reasonable. But then I notice the same people praising low numbers in one year and rising numbers years later, which makes me think it is spin after all. Is it a double standard or special pleading, or is that the wrong frame? How is it different from an ordinary disagreement about what follows from a fact, from a slippery-slope argument, and from a claim that is simply unfalsifiable? Is the defect in the reasoners or in the evidence? What property would an indicator have to have in order not to be claimable by both sides, and does the inability to name a direction that would count against you show anything, or only that someone lacks imagination? Please also give me the edges: two cases that look like this one but aren't, and one that is an instance but doesn't look like one. If there is no established term, coin one and define it. Please write in American English.
