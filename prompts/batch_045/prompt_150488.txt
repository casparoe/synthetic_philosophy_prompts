I am a retired isotope geochemist — thirty-five years of using conservative tracers, a bromide spike or an isotopic label, to infer flow paths in fractured rock — and for the past two years I have been attending a philosophy of mind reading group in the university's continuing-education programme. I am the person in the room who keeps asking what would count as evidence, which I am told is both my main contribution and my main limitation.

Last week the topic was epiphenomenalism, and the passage we were given to discuss comes from a bestselling popular book on consciousness. I have reconstructed it below from my notes; the wording is close to the original, but it is my reconstruction, not a transcription:

"Epiphenomenalism says that conscious experience is a by-product of brain activity: the neural states do all the causal work, and experiences ride along on top of them without pushing anything around — Huxley compared consciousness to the steam whistle on a locomotive, which makes a noise but does nothing to drive the engine. The standard objection is that the view refutes itself. If my experiences cause nothing, then my saying 'I am in pain' is not caused by my pain; so my belief about my own experience is unexplained, and an unexplained belief is not knowledge. If epiphenomenalism were true I could not know it — indeed I could not even think it, since a thought about consciousness must owe its existence to consciousness. The epiphenomenalist's reply is decisive, however: the very brain state that gives rise to the experience also causes the belief 'I am now in pain', so the belief is reliably correlated with the experience and therefore qualifies as knowledge. Knowledge does not require that the fact known should cause the belief, any more than a barometer's reading needs to be caused by the storm in order for the barometer to be a good guide to it. Once this is seen, the self-refutation objection collapses, and epiphenomenalism is left untouched."

The convenor called this a fair sketch. I am not convinced, and I cannot tell whether my doubts are real complaints or the reflexes of a man who spent a career being told that an uncalibrated correlation is not evidence of anything.

First, the barometer. In my working life that analogy would not have survived the first seminar. A barometer can be checked because both relata — the reading and the storm — are independently observable, by other instruments, from outside the system. If I have understood the position, no third-person data available to a neuroscientist contains the experience; the only public items are the neural states and the reports. So the "reliable correlation" the book relies on seems to be a correlation that nobody could ever have established except by trusting the very reports whose reliability is what is in question. Is that a fair statement of the residual difficulty, or does the epiphenomenalist have a way of establishing the correlation that does not already assume the point at issue?

Second, the word "untouched". Even granting the correlation reply, it seems to me to secure reliability only — true belief at a high rate — and to say nothing about whether my belief is answerable to, or genuinely about, the experience it concerns, nor about how the word "pain" in my judgment comes to refer to pain rather than to whatever neural state did the causing. My impression from the discussion was that defenders of the objection are making a demand of the second kind — acquaintance, or grounding, or a causal/explanatory condition on knowledge — and that the book's reply quietly answers a different question. But I also had the sense in the seminar that "knowledge" was being used in three different ways, and I may be manufacturing a disagreement out of a verbal difference. If so, say so plainly.

Third, my own analogy, which I offer because it is the only hold I have on the material. A conservative tracer injected into a flow is carried by the fluid, carries information about where the fluid has been, and exerts no back-reaction on it at all. That is, as far as I can see, precisely the structural relation epiphenomenalism postulates between experience and the brain: transported, informative, inert. The analogy initially looked friendly to the epiphenomenalist, since inference from tracers is perfectly good science; no hydrologist thinks a tracer's causal idleness undermines what we infer from it. But then I noticed that our confidence in a tracer study rests on having characterised the flow independently — head measurements, pumping tests, a second tracer — and on having calibrated the tracer against that independent characterisation. Remove the independent characterisation and the tracer tells you nothing about the flow; it is dye in water. So I want to know whether my analogy really is friendly to the epiphenomenalist, or whether, followed honestly, it is the barometer objection in a more flattering costume — and, if the latter, whether the epiphenomenalist's best answer is that here the correlation is not contingent at all but guaranteed by supervenience, and whether that answer is worth anything.

One further thing was said in the seminar that I could not place: that if the view is not confined to phenomenal properties but covers mental events generally, then no belief of mine is ever caused by the reasons for it, which would be a different and more alarming problem than the one about pain. Is that a separate objection, or the same one in a wider hat?
