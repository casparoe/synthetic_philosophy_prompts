I'm a third-year undergraduate in a seminar on moral conflict and practical reason, and each of us has to run one 55-minute discussion section for our group of sixteen students; the instructor doesn't attend. Mine falls on the question of whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible — that is, whether there are cases in which an agent must do one of two things, each of which is morally required of her, and in which, whichever she does, she does something morally wrong and incurs a remainder that no later action settles. I'd like to design something that makes the section run into the structure of that dispute rather than listen to me summarise it, and I'd appreciate help building it.

The setting and constraints:

- One room, 55 minutes, 16 students (I'd split them into four groups of four). No technology beyond index cards, markers and a whiteboard. No grades or money at stake, though I can offer a trivial stake: coffee vouchers, or control of the one-page summary that goes out to the whole class afterwards.
- The course has already covered prima facie duties, the contrast between a hard choice that has a right answer with heavy costs attached and a conflict that seems to go deeper than that, and the notion of agent-regret. So I want no lecture up front, and I want the vocabulary to arrive after the experience rather than before it.
- I'd also rather the activity not rest on any famous case — no Sophie's choice, no Sartre's student — because half the group will have met them before and will recite a position instead of thinking. My idea is that the groups build their own cases from a deck of claim-types (a promise to a friend, the rescue of a stranger, reparative justice owed to someone you previously harmed, loyalty to a colleague who has taken a risk for you, distributive fairness across people not in the room, fidelity to a long-term project of your own) and are tasked with constructing an exactly symmetric pair whose conflict cannot be dissolved by splitting the difference or by an appeal to consequences.

The core device I have in mind: each group writes a tiny "codebook" for its own case — a short list of verdicts of the form ought(φ) — and a referee (me or a rotating student) enforces two rules on every codebook: no verdict may require what cannot be done, and verdicts agglomerate, so that a codebook containing ought(A) and ought(B) is committed to ought(A and B). Any codebook violating either rule is stamped and returned for repair. What I want the groups to discover is that the thing they naturally want to say about their own construction — both claims bind me, and yet only one of them can be done — cannot be written down in a codebook governed by those rules, and that every repair available to them quietly drops something they were trying to record. Please help me stage this so the discovery actually lands: the exact wording of the two rules, how the referee's challenge should be phrased when a group hands in a codebook that says both, and the three or four repair options groups will realistically try (denying one claim, downgrading one to a mere cost, denying agglomeration, retreating to ignorance about which claim is stronger) together with how the referee should respond to each without giving the game away.

Two distinctions I'd like operationalised as concrete moves inside the activity rather than explained at the end:

1. The creditor test. Whenever a group records a residue following a verdict, they must also record to whom it is owed: to the agent's own welfare, to an affected bystander, or specifically to the person whose claim lost. My hypothesis is that groups treat plain costs as owed to themselves and remainders as owed to the losing claimant, and that this pattern of address is doing much of the work in the intuition that something obligation-shaped survived the choice. I want that pattern visible on the board, produced by them.
2. Dischargeable versus sealed residue. Some remainder can be settled by a later act — an apology, compensation, an explanation, a public acknowledgement — call that dischargeable. Some cannot: nothing the agent can subsequently do marks the matter as settled, call that sealed. Each group should be required, for its own case, either to write a discharge script or to state explicitly that no discharge script exists, and then to face the question of whether sealed residue is evidence of an obligation left unfulfilled (the dilemma theorist's reading) or only evidence of what loss feels like from the inside (the anti-dilemma theorist's reading).

I also want a re-choice move near the end: each group is asked whether, returned to the moment of choice with everything they now know, they would choose the same way, and then asked whether that settles it. My expectation is that for a plain cost case re-choosing washes everything out, whereas for the constructed conflict a group will both re-choose identically and refuse to call the matter closed — and that this signature should be on the board under a column heading before anyone names it.

What I'm asking for: a runnable plan — materials, the rules and the referee's script, a minute-by-minute timeline for 55 minutes, the claim deck, the repair options to expect and how to meet each, and a debrief that maps particular moments in the session onto particular parts of the dispute (the argument against dilemmas that the codebook rules encode, the reply that ought does not agglomerate across conflicts, the difference between an epistemic quandary and a putative conflict in the obligations themselves, residue versus regret, and the escape-by-inventing-a-third-option response). I'd like a mechanism that makes escape clauses costly: any third option a group invents has to be entered into the codebook as a verdict and paid for in the ledger, so that creativity isn't free.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — I expect at least the third-option problem, one group quietly turning the exercise into cost-benefit arithmetic, and one group producing an asymmetric case by accident — and how to handle each without collapsing the discovery. And be frank about which part of the dispute the game cannot generate on its own and which I will simply have to say myself in the last five minutes; I'd rather know that now than find out live.
