I have no academic background — I'm a retired electrical engineer who has spent the last couple of winters reading about origin-of-life research on my own — and I'm preparing a short illustrated talk for a general audience at a monthly science café my local library runs. I want to close the talk with a single constructed case instead of the usual parade of familiar puzzles, and I'd like you to build it to the specification below.

The claim I want the case to carry: I've come to suspect that the familiar definitions of life are not three rival answers to one question but three answers to different questions — one about a bounded, self-maintaining individual, one about a self-sustaining chemical process, one about a lineage undergoing evolution — so that pointing at a single physical object and asking whether it is alive may be ill-posed rather than merely hard to answer. I want a case that brings out that structure, not one more entry in the list of hard cases.

The three definitions to test (please use these, roughly as I've put them, rather than substituting your own):
1. The metabolic or chemical definition: life is a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution — the working definition one meets everywhere in astrobiology writing.
2. The organisational definition: life is a system that produces and maintains its own bounded organisation — it makes its own boundary and continuously rebuilds the machinery that keeps it going.
3. The Darwinian or evolution-first definition: life is whatever evolves by natural selection — a population of entities with heritable variation in fitness.

The verdicts I need: (1) says yes; (2) says no; (3) cannot return a verdict about the particular object you are pointing at, but can only say something about the lineage or population it belongs to. These have to fall out of the definitions as stated, not be stipulated by the scenario.

Constraints on the scenario:
- Realistic chemistry and realistic settings: something a laboratory could plausibly produce within a few decades, or a probe might plausibly encounter — a porous rock fed by a geochemical gradient, a cold brine, a subsurface aquifer. No engineered nanomachines, no exotic physics, no science-fictional technology.
- Not one of the examples that gets trotted out every time: no viruses, prions, sterile hybrids, dormant seeds or spores, fire, crystals, software or computer viruses, or organelles. The point of the exercise is a case my listeners haven't heard a hundred times.
- One short paragraph, written so that a listener with no chemistry can follow it.

After the scenario, I need:
- A line or two per definition stating its verdict and why.
- Identification of the single feature doing the work, and an honest statement of what happens to each of the three verdicts if that feature is removed. If removing it changes more than one verdict, say so rather than pretending it is a clean isolation.
- The strongest reply available to the defender of the definition that comes off worst here. My guess is that it is the Darwinian one, since a definition that declines to say whether the thing in front of you is alive will strike my audience as evasive, and I would like to give it its best shot before I dismiss it.
- A flag on any part of the chemistry you are not confident is possible, together with the nearest variant that is.

One wrinkle I would like if it can be managed without strain: build the case so that its cycle is far slower than any human observation window, with generations or regeneration times measured in centuries rather than minutes. If you do, add a short paragraph on whether the three verdicts would change if the same system ran at ordinary biological speed, since the definitions' silence about rates strikes me as a genuine gap. If the slow version strains chemical plausibility, say plainly that you dropped it and why.

Finally, two or three sentences I can actually say at the end of the talk about whether anything could settle the question of the case in principle — I don't want to close on it's all just a matter of definition, which is the line my audience will expect and which I think is too easy.
