I write about science and ideas for a national magazine — mostly biology, medicine, and psychology — and I keep running into the same problem at the level of a single sentence. Papers and university press offices arrive announcing that some category is real: a new species carved out of a well-known one, a new cell type, a newly identified subtype of a familiar diagnosis, a distinct kind of memory. Just as often they arrive announcing the reverse: that a category everyone has been using does not correspond to anything. My copy has to choose between saying that researchers have discovered a new kind of thing and saying that they have proposed a new way of grouping things, and that choice is not cosmetic. Readers take discovery language as a claim about the world, the scientists care how they are portrayed, and when the category is a diagnosis there are advocacy groups for whom the difference is consequential. I need a procedure, not a theory.

What I have been using is a rough two-part test I made up. First, an export test: does the proposed category license predictions that were not built into its definition — about a new sample, in another lab, in a related species? Second, a re-identification test: when the same domain is examined by an independent method that does not share the assumptions of the first one, does roughly the same grouping fall out again? I have a feeling these two tests are doing different work, that they track things philosophers distinguish — inductive usefulness, stability across methods, causal structure, explanatory depth — and that some of those come apart in cases I actually cover. I also suspect I have quietly assumed that discovery language is only earned by one style of category, and that I have no way to handle categories that look real at one resolution and dissolve at another.

So here is what I would like. Give me a decision procedure my desk can run in about ten minutes, from a paper abstract, a methods paragraph, a press release, and usually one short call with an outside scientist who is not on the author list. It should tell me, in order: the questions to ask; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops with write this as a proposal rather than a discovery; where it stops with write this as a discovery; and where it has to hand the decision back to me because the remaining question is one of values rather than evidence. It should tell me what to do when I cannot get the information a step calls for — what the default is, and whether that default is conservative or merely convenient. It needs to distinguish at least three outcomes that I think I currently conflate: a new label for something the field was already tracking, a recut of an old domain into different groupings, and a claim about underlying causal structure. Assume I already know how to ask whether a finding replicates: what I need is guidance on what kind of thing has been found, conditional on its holding up.

Then run it. Case one: a genomic clustering study splits a familiar, well-studied organism into five species, and taxonomists quoted elsewhere are openly divided, some treating species as segments of lineages and others requiring something like reproductive isolation. Case two — the one I expect to like least — a team reports a biological subtype of a common psychiatric diagnosis, defined by clustering inflammatory markers in a few thousand patients, and a patient-advocacy foundation is already campaigning for it to be recognized as a disease. Case three, briefly: a review argues that a category in my own beat, long treated as a single thing, does not pick out any one natural grouping, and I have to decide whether scientists say it does not exist is ever the sentence to print. For case two in particular, tell me what the procedure says even though I will not enjoy it, and how to write the sentence so that it is honest without being dismissive.

Finally, two constraints on how you present this. Be explicit about which claims behind the procedure are settled among people who work on this and which are live disputes — whether kinds must be mind-independent, whether there can be several equally legitimate ways of cutting the same domain, whether categories in the human sciences can be kinds at all — and do not present a contested position as the consensus. And say plainly where my two tests survive, where they are confused, and where the procedure itself will misfire. I am not asking for citations or further reading; I want the reasoning and the procedure. Please answer in American English, in roughly 1,200 words.
