Hi — I work on a small modelling team at a public health agency (wont name it) and we maintain four competing transmission models for the same disease: they disagree about things like how much transmission is asymptomatic, how fast immunity wanes, and whether contact rates are age-structured or not. Twice a year we have to write a "model comparison statement" that says, in so many words, which of them is closest to the truth about how this disease actually spreads, and that statement decides which model feeds into capacity planning for the coming season and which team gets its funding renewed. I've got about half a day to write it, it gets signed off by a senior epidemiologist who has never done any philosophy and will not tolerate jargon, and it has to survive being read both by external critics and by whichever modelling team just lost. Right now we basically rank by fit to last season's surveillance data plus how much people like the model, and everyone on the team knows that isn't the same thing as being closer to the truth — but we've never had a way to say what is.

A philosopher friend mentioned over dinner that there's a literature on this under the name verisimilitude or truthlikeness, and I've been reading around the edges since. What I've pieced together (correct me in one line if I've got this wrong, then carry on) is that the obvious definition — a theory is closer to the truth when it has more true consequences and no more false ones — collapses: there's apparently a result to the effect that on that definition a false theory can never be closer to the truth than another one, which is useless to us because all four of our models are definitely false in some respects, they're all simplifications. And there seem to be three rough families of repair: ones that count content (how much truth, how much falsity), ones that measure some kind of similarity or distance between what the theory says and how the world actually is across a space of possibilities, and ones that go via expected evidence or likelihood. Plus something that worries me more: the ranking seems to depend on how you carve up the space of possible states of the world, so that if you redescribe the possibilities more finely the ordering can flip — it smells like the same kind of language-dependence trick as the grue business in induction, and I don't want to build a procedure that silently depends on a choice of vocabulary nobody wrote down.

So: please turn whatever is actually usable here into a written procedure my team can run, in British English, and please don't give me citations or a reading list — I can't get hold of the papers and I need something I can apply, not something I can follow up. Concretely I want: a step that forces us to declare up front what the target is and at what grain (which quantities, over what population and time window, at what resolution — e.g. seasonal totals versus weekly age-stratified incidence), because I suspect most of our arguments are really about that; a rule for separating "got the causal structure right" from "got the numerical value right" when a theory is partly right in each way; an actual comparison rule for two theories we already know to be false, given that the naive one collapses, including the conditions under which the correct output is "these cannot be ranked"; a sensitivity check that re-runs the comparison one step finer and one step coarser on the partition and reports whether the ordering holds; explicit flags at the points where someone has to make a judgment call rather than follow a rule (weights, which distinctions count as relevant, how much falsity in an irrelevant corner of the space is worth tolerating) with a note on who should sign off on those; and an explicit warning step about not letting "closer to the truth" quietly become "fit last season's data best" or "most useful for the planning decision". Then run the whole thing on three cases: (i) Model A has the waning-immunity rate about right but omits asymptomatic transmission entirely, while Model B includes asymptomatic transmission with a badly wrong rate; (ii) two models that agree on coarse quantities — size and timing of the seasonal peak, total burden — but disagree about the fine-grained story of who infects whom, where I need a ranking at the finer grain; (iii) our team's favourite model, the one that fit last year's data best and that two people have built careers on, against a rival — and I specifically want at least one case where the honest output is unwelcome, either the favourite loses or the procedure refuses to rank them. Finish by saying plainly what the procedure cannot do. Somewhere around 1000–1300 words would be ideal.
