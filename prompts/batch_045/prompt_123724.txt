I'm assembling the problem set for a graduate seminar on causal versus evidential decision theory, and I want to replace my usual treatment of Death in Damascus with something better. The discussion always dies in the same place: a student says the case is just Newcomb's problem in a macabre costume, I say no, because here what unsettles the agent is her own uncertainty about what she is going to do rather than a prediction already sitting in a box, and neither of us can cash that out. So I need a designed case that forces the distinction into the open. For reference, the baseline I have in mind is the familiar one: you are in Damascus, you learn that Death will come for you tomorrow, Death is an extremely reliable predictor of where you will be and will be waiting in that city, and you must choose between staying in Damascus and fleeing to Aleppo; on either supposition your chance of dying is near one, since whatever you end up doing is itself strong evidence that Death will be there. I want a variant of that case, not a commentary on it.

The constraints are these. First, realism: keep the two-city structure and the sense of doom, but the predictor's reliability must have a mundane source — a tracker, a stalker, a bounty hunter, someone who has studied your habits and is right maybe eight or nine times out of ten, and who has to commit to a city tonight. No demons, no foreknowledge, no time travel, no predictor better than a shrewd human being; state the reliability, and use round numbers. Second, one of the two options must carry a hazard of its own that is causally independent of the predictor — a dangerous road, an uninsured surgery, a costly delay, whatever you like — so that fleeing is not merely auspicious or inauspicious but also causally risky in a way that has nothing to do with the prediction. Third, the characteristic doom has to survive: conditional on each act, the agent's own estimate of her chance of dying must still be high, so that neither option is a safe harbour. Fourth, evidential and causal decision theory must come apart in the two-way choice itself — no bookies, no side bets, no extra buttons; the disagreement has to be visible in stay-versus-flee. Fifth, I want the disagreement located rather than asserted: give each theory's verdict as a function of the agent's prior credence that she will flee, and tell me over what range they disagree and where the flip occurs. A colleague of mine also objected last year that any such case just reimports the mixed-strategy fix, so I need to know whether that is true here.

After the scenario, show the machinery: the acts, the states, the utilities, and every conditional and counterfactual probability you use, with the arithmetic for each theory's verdict written out. Then give me the self-ratifying policy for the causal theorist — say whether it is a pure act or a mixed one, give the mixing probability if it is mixed, and give the threshold (in the tracker's reliability, or in the size of the independent hazard) at which it flips from one kind to the other. My own half-formed hunch, which I could not make come out when I tried it on the back of an envelope, is that the flip point in the prior credence at which the two theories stop disagreeing is the very same number as the mixing probability of the self-ratifying policy; check that and tell me if it is right. Then say which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens if you remove it — in particular, confirm or deny that deleting the independent causal hazard collapses the case back into the original one, in which both acts are equally bad news and the only self-consistent policy is a coin flip. Finally, an honest paragraph on what this case is worth: does the mundane predictor plus the independent hazard make it genuinely a different case from Newcomb's problem, or is it Newcomb's problem with noise added, and what would a defender of whichever theory comes out looking worse say to get out of it? Do not bother with citations or a reading list; I will supply those myself.
