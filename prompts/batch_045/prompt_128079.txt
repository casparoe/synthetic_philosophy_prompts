I'm retired — forty-odd years in physical chemistry, mostly spectroscopy, and for the last year or so I've been going to a philosophy reading group at the local library, which is how I ended up losing an argument last week that I'd like to understand better than I do.

The argument was the one about dead theories. Phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous ether, the circulation of the humours: smart people, wildly successful for a while, and every last one of them turned out to be wrong about what's actually there. So, said the man who beat me, by induction, our own electrons and genes and tectonic plates are probably wrong in the same way, and we just don't know it yet. I didn't have an answer. Then somebody else cut in with — and this is the line I want taken apart — "well, if it weren't roughly true, it would be a miracle." Everyone nodded, argument over.

I've heard that line, or its twin "science works, therefore it's basically true", since I was a graduate student, and I've probably used it myself on students. I've never once asked what it actually asserts. I gather it has some fancy name in the literature; I don't know it and I don't much care, and please don't give me a reading list, I already have a pile of those and I won't read them.

Here's the wrinkle I can't shake, from my own end of things. In a working lab you know perfectly well which parts of a model are load-bearing and which parts are scenery. The load-bearing bits are the ones you actually compute with, the ones that get you a number you then go and measure; the scenery is the story you tell around them, the picture that makes the thing teachable, the parameters you fit and nobody seriously believes in. I spent years with a lineshape model whose damping constant was pure fiction, and nobody minded, because it wasn't the part doing the work. What struck me after the meeting is that when I look back at those dead theories, it's mostly the scenery that died. Which means the miracle line could be saying something obviously false (every part of a successful theory is true) or something much narrower that the pessimistic induction may not touch at all (the load-bearing parts latch onto something real). I can't tell which one my friend committed himself to, and I suspect he can't either.

So: lay out, as a menu with a short label on each (R1, R2, R3, ...), the distinct precise claims that line could be making, from the platitudinous to the wild. For each one say whether it is trivial, whether it is substantive and defensible, whether it is just false, and — this is the part I actually care about — whether it would really stop the pessimistic induction as I've stated it, or only stop a straw version of it. A few things I'd like covered specifically: does the word "miracle" smuggle anything in, or is it only a way of saying "I don't have a better explanation"? Does "roughly true" mean the theory's claims, or its structure, or just that its central terms manage to refer to something? Does the line need to cover the scenery as well as the load-bearing parts, and what happens to it if it doesn't? And is there a reading on which it's true but says nothing about truth in the sense I care about — my instruments and my spectra — and is only a claim about what we should assume on Mondays?

If my load-bearing/scenery distinction already has a name, tell me what it is called in one line so I can look it up, but don't make that the answer.

Finish with a single last line, nothing after it, in exactly this form: \answer{R3} — the label of the one reading on your list that is both genuinely defensible and genuinely strong enough to stop the pessimistic induction as I've stated it, scenery point included. If your honest answer is that none of them does, put \answer{none}.
