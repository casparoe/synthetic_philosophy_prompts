i'm a PhD student working on non-human causation in historical explanation (history of science dept, but i sit with the geographers a lot) and i keep hitting this same move. A senior person in my field who works on epidemic disease in colonial settings writes things like "smallpox invaded the valley," "the louse defeated the Grande Armée," "the blight was the real author of the famine." When i pushed him on it in a seminar he said: look, an invading army is a historical actor, and germs do everything an invading army does — they arrive from somewhere else, they exploit weaknesses in the defending polity, they get stronger the longer they hold the field, they recieve reinforcements, they withdraw when the population adapts — so of course they're actors too, and denying it is just human exceptionalism.

The work the analogy is doing in my context: i've got a chapter arguing that treating a pathogen as an actor does explanatory work that a purely structural story (trade routes, settlement density, state capacity, housing) can't do, and a reviewer says the war framing is carrying the whole claim and that once you drop it what's left is either determinism (the germ made it happen) or a banality (people died of disease). So i need to know how much of my chapter is actually resting on the metaphor.

Can you audit it? I want the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the invading force, the frontier, the defending army, logistics, intelligence, strategy, surrender, occupation, scorched earth — and then where the mapping breaks. In particular: an army has objectives, a command structure, and can be deterred, bribed, or negotiated with; a pathogen lineage has none of those, and (here's the one that seems load-bearing to me) an army does not win by annihilating the territory it wants to hold, whereas a pathogen's "success" is often self-limiting — the traits that make it good at spreading can get selected against once it's burned through available hosts. Does that reversal make the analogy actively misleading about virulence over time, or is it a detail?

I've been sketching a ladder of grades of agency and I'd like you to place both relata on it and tell me if the ladder is any use: (i) mere causal relevance; (ii) difference-making under intervention — had you quarantined or vaccinated, the outcome is different; (iii) plasticity, where the thing adjusts its own traits to the situation in a way that looks like tactics, but the mechanism is selection across a lineage rather than deliberation by anybody; (iv) representational intent. Where does the germ land, where does the army land, and is the gap between (iii) and (iv) the whole ballgame, or is (iii) already enough for the kind of agency a historian needs? Also flag the tempo problem: a lineage's "adjustment" takes generations, an army's takes hours — does that disqualify it from figuring in a narrative explanation of a single campaign, or is that just a resolution mismatch?

Two more things I care about. First, whether the analogy smuggles in inevitability — armies are trying, so outcomes look planned and lockable-in, and I suspect "the germ invaded" quietly makes an outcome look more guided than it was, which is probably exactly where the reviewer's determinism charge bites. Second, whether it flips the moral pointer: we blame invaders, so calling typhus an invader in the retreat from Moscow may turn a logistics and sanitation failure into an external enemy. Test both on the 1918 flu, please, since that's the case everyone reaches for — does the flu "end a war" in the same sense an army does?

Then: which disanalogies actually matter for the use I'm making of it and which are just decoration. Whether the conclusion survives at all — can I keep "actor" in some deflated sense, and if so what's the minimal deflation that still does the work? And give me one better analogy, with its own mapping and its own liabilities: fire in a city, a species colonizing an island, a price signal in a market, whatever you think is doing less smuggling. I'm going to actually put the table in an appendix, so be blunt about where my side is cheating.
