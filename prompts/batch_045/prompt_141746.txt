im a science/ideas journalist and im finally writing the peice ive been putting off for a year about how we weigh the future — climate targets, pandemic prep, the longtermist crowd insisting the year 3000 matters more than everything before it, and the boring technocratic question of what discount rate goes into a government cost-benefit analysis. I've got a pile of intuitions and they definately dont add up, so before I start calling people I want to know what I actually think. No citations needed, I cant look anything up right now anyway. Here are my gut verdicts; I want the principle.

1. Certainty case: 300 people in serious chronic pain for a year starting now, or 300 people in exactly the same pain for a year starting in 2125. We're told for certain both will happen if we don't act. Verdict: these are the same. If it's worth paying to fix the first, it's worth paying to fix the second, and anyone who says the 2125 group counts for less purely because of the date is making a mistake, not stating a preference.

2. Next year vs 2085: an outbreak we can stop now that will kill 8,000 people next year, or an outbreak we can stop now for the same money that will kill 8,000 people in sixty years. Verdict: the next-year one feels way more urgent and I think that's legit — I'd fund it first. This flatly contradicts 1 and I have no idea how to repair it.

3. Money vs lives: I'm basically fine with a government discounting future *money* at a few percent a year when it appraises a flood barrier. I am not fine with it discounting future *lives saved* at a few percent a year; that reads as ghoulish. But the money only matters because of what it does for people, so this looks like straightforward inconsistency. Unless it isn't.

4. Growth: if the people of 2125 will be much richer than us, I'm comfortable shifting resources toward us now, even in welfare terms, because a given benefit matters less to them. If they'll be poorer because we wrecked the climate, I want us to give up a lot more now. So: discounting for growth, sure.

5. Arrival risk: I do quietly discount benefits 200 years out, because there's some chance there's nobody there, or we never actually deliver, or the institution collapses. Feels legitimate, but I can't tell whether it's doing exactly the work I rejected in 1.

6. Big numbers far away: a project that removes a mild annoyance from 10 billion people in 2900, versus one that cures a horrible disease for 500 people today. The arithmetic says the first is astronomically bigger. My gut does not care at all, and if pressed I think I'd fund the 500. I feel slightly ashamed of that.

7. Grandkids: I care vastly more about my own grandkids' welfare than about a stranger's grandkids, and I don't think I'm making an error. Not even sure this is the same topic, but it might be.

The principle I've been carrying around: no pure time preference at all — every apparent discount is really one of three things, probability that the benefit ever lands, growth and diminishing marginal value, and something about causal leverage or still being able to change your mind later. Tell me where it breaks. I think it handles 3, 4, 5 and maybe 6, but it can't handle 2, and 2 is the verdict I feel most strongly about.

Two more things I've picked up and can't evaluate. A friend who reads this stuff proposes a test he calls "date-swap invariance": shift an entire scenario a hundred years later or earlier — everyone's position, not just the date on the calendar — and your verdict shouldn't move. I like it as a slogan but I'm not sure it's even coherent (can you shift "now"?), and I don't know what it says about 7. And someone else says the real argument is a birth-date lottery: you don't know what year you're born in, so you should be neutral across times. Does that argument actually establish what people take it to establish, or does it smuggle in a claim about who counts?

So: is there one principle that gets most of these, or a small set of candidates? Which clause is motivated by which case, which of my verdicts would each one force me to drop, and what does your favorite say about the case I keep dodging — spending a real slice of current income on reducing existential risk for people who don't exist yet and may never exist? My gut says yes, some, but "some" is doing an enormous amount of work and I can't defend any particular number.
