So — bit of background before the actual ask. My youngest had an exome done about seven months ago (long story, developmental delay, nothing dramatic in the end), and the report came back with three variants of uncertain significance. The geneticist we saw was lovely but she kept saying things like 'we don't know whether this gene does anything' and 'we can't rule out an effect on function', and I went home and started reading about what 'function' even means in this context, which has become a slightly embarassing obsession. It isn't purely academic either: I'm on the variant curation team at the same hospital, so I'm one of the people who write those sentences, maybe a hundred times a year.

I've been asked to give an hour at our joint clinical-molecular meeting in a couple of weeks. The room will be maybe twenty-five people: clinical geneticists, the other curators, two molecular biologists who run the perturbation-and-rescue assays, a few registrars, and one philosopher of biology from the university who's coming along and who I happen to know thinks the whole etiological-versus-causal-role debate was settled decades ago by selected-effects theory. Almost none of them have read any philosophy of biology, and I've learnt the hard way that the clinicians switch off the second anyone says 'teleology'.

The view I'm defending — I've been calling it the bookkeeping account, tell me if that name is terrible — goes like this. A part or process of an organism has function F not because F is what it was selected for, and not because F contributes to some capacity of a container system that an analyst happens to find interesting, but because F is the thing the organism's own detection-and-response machinery is set up to track and restore: proofreading and DNA repair, the unfolded protein response, immune surveillance, homeostatic feedback, that sort of thing. Functions are marked out by the organism's own bookkeeping rather than by its history. Two pay-offs I want to sell to this room: a sequence that arose de novo and has no selection history whatsoever can already have a function if the cell's machinery is already policing it, and a broken part can still have the function it's failing at, because the monitoring still targets it — you don't need an evolutionary story to say what it's meant to be doing.

What I need is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defence of the view and definitely not a survey of the function literature — I've read all that and it'll just make me cross. Give me the seven or eight objections I'll actually get from that room, in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way a clinical geneticist or an assay person would really say them rather than how a philosopher would, each with a one- or two-minute answer and a line on where that answer is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes to take the heat off, and what the single reply is that has to land if the talk is going to be worth anything. At least two of the objections should be the philosopher's, in his voice, because he will talk.

The thing I'm dreading is someone asking what happens when the monitoring machinery itself is broken or captured — cancer, autoimmunity, that kind of case — because I think my account says something slightly mad there and I haven't worked out the answer. But tell me honestly if you think the real killer is a different one. And no reading lists, please: I can't chase references before the talk.
