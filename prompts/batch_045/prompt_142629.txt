I am writing a paper on the theory of the pramāṇas, and before I read any more of the theories themselves I want the specification. The purpose of the theory is fixed in advance: it should be usable as a tool for classifying new sources of belief — remote-sensing instruments, brain–computer interfaces, institutional testimony, machine-generated reports — and for telling an agent whether a given deliverance is to be trusted and acted on, in a way that does not simply collapse into the claim that it is produced reliably.

Here are the working characterizations I am starting from. Take them as stipulations of mine, not as exegesis: do not correct my Sanskrit, and rest nothing on any exact textual formulation. A pramāṇa is a source or mechanism which, when operative, produces a pramā — a cognition that is (i) true, (ii) of something not already cognized by that subject, and (iii) not subsequently defeated. The stock list has four members: perception, inference, identification of an unfamiliar thing by a remembered description, and verbal testimony. One influential critic holds that the list should collapse to two. Others want to add postulation from apparent conflict, and cognition of absence.

Write me the desiderata: the requirements any adequate theory of the pramāṇas must meet, with the case or consideration behind each, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are local preferences of one research programme. Anchor the requirements in the following cases at minimum:

- The remote prosthesis. A subject sits in a darkened room wearing a device that produces in her a state type-identical, by her own introspection and by every functional test we can run, to her ordinary visual experience of a busy intersection three kilometres away. The states are in fact reliably accurate about that intersection. There is no sense–object contact with it, and no inference she can reconstruct. Is this perception, a fifth pramāṇa, or no pramāṇa at all?
- Rediscovery. A subject infers from smoke that there is fire on the hill, then forgets the conclusion and the episode entirely. Years later, by a different line of reasoning, she arrives at the true belief that there was fire on the hill at that time. The second occurrence is a new event; the content is not new to her history. Does the novelty condition attach to occurrences, to contents, or to both — and what exactly is lost by dropping it?
- Purely negative competence. An organism's only reliable contact with its environment is a signal meaning that no predator is within range; it never identifies anything positive. Its deliverances are veridical, action-guiding and undefeated. Must a theory countenance a source whose content is exclusively negative, and if so is that a distinct pramāṇa or a special case of perception?
- Gettierized testimony. A speaker sincerely asserts that p, understanding what she says; p is true; the hearer understands and believes. But the speaker's own ground is one of those cases where justified true belief falls short of knowledge: her evidence is misleading and p is true for unrelated reasons. Does the hearer acquire a pramā? A theory that treats testimony as basic and a theory that requires the speaker herself to have a pramā will diverge here.
- The probabilistic instrument. A device is accurate seven times in ten and well calibrated; on a given occasion it issues a true reading, though the very same mechanism misfires on three near-identical occasions out of ten. Does it produce a pramā on that occasion? Say whether the theory needs a graded successor notion, and at what cost.
- Corporate cognition. A laboratory concludes that a sample is contaminated. No individual member's perception, reasoning or testimony suffices; the reliability is a property of the institution's procedures. Is what results a property of a single subject, as the classical account has it, and if not, what becomes of the requirement that a pramāṇa be a mechanism operating on a subject?
- Demarcation. Give identity and difference conditions for pramāṇas: what makes two deliverances instances of the same source, and what makes a source genuinely distinct? Is a satellite image interpreted by a computer model perception, inference, or testimony? If it is two of these at once, is confluence — one fact reached by several sources — permitted, or are the domains of the sources rigidly partitioned? State this as an explicit requirement and say why a theory needs it.
- The regress of extrinsic validity. If a cognition's credentials are established by a further cognition whose credentials require yet another, the account needs a stopping point; several theories locate it in practical success. State this as a requirement, say what would count as satisfying it, and say whether the appeal to practice merely relocates the problem into a theory of action.

Then separate three things.

First: which of the requirements people standardly impose are genuine desiderata, and which are artifacts of one programme's metaphysics — for instance the demand that knowledge be a quality of a substantial self, that every pramāṇa exhibit a distinct instrument-and-result structure, or that the list be closed at some particular number.

Second: which requirements cannot be jointly satisfied. Argue for one jointly unsatisfiable pair or triple; this is the part I most want. My own suspicion is that you cannot simultaneously have a source-by-source taxonomy with rigid domain partitioning, a reduction of the list to two items, and the demand that every historically recognized case be accommodated under its usual heading. If you disagree, name the pair that does break.

Third: the single requirement that excludes the most candidate theories, and why.

Finish with no more than one paragraph running the four-source list, the two-source reduction, and a contemporary reliabilist account of knowledge-sources against your list — just enough for me to see which requirement is doing the work. No bibliography, no citations, no textual exegesis, and no appeal to what the tradition holds beyond what you can state in your own terms.
