I lead the country diagnostics desk at the Kestrel Fund, a mid-sized bilateral donor that reallocates roughly sixty million dollars a year across eleven partner countries. Every eighteen months, for each country, a consultant team hands us a sixty-to-ninety page diagnosis of why that country is poor, or why its growth stalled, and we have to turn it into a portfolio decision: what to start funding, what to stop funding, what to leave alone. The reports sort reliably into a few story-types—extractive institutions inherited from a colonial extraction regime; geography, meaning landlockedness, disease burden, or having no navigable river to a coast; culture, usually low interpersonal trust; a commodity-price bust; a terms-of-trade turn; a big push that never came. A colleague in our evaluation unit says the whole exercise is unfalsifiable and that we are laundering priors into dollar figures. I half agree, but I still have to sign a memo, and the memo has to survive an external audit that asks in writing what evidence we relied on and what would have changed our minds. I was arguing with her about this last week and realized I cannot actually say what my desk does when it reads one of these reports, beyond arguing about them until someone senior gets impatient.

What I want is a triage procedure my desk can run on a report in about ninety minutes. The people running it are desk officers with politics or public-policy degrees: no econometrics, no identification strategies, they can read the signs on a regression table and nothing more. They have no access to journals or datasets, so please do not give me a bibliography, suggest authors to read, or lean on citations—anything the procedure rests on has to be carried in the procedure itself. Output is a one-page note ending in a verdict code the country director signs, from a fixed menu: FUND (the report isolates something we can actually move), FUND-WITH-KILL-CONDITION, HOLD (insufficient), or REFER (send it to the director's panel because the disagreement is about values rather than evidence). Ground it in what is genuinely knowable about causal inference from a small number of historical cases: that outcomes like this are usually overdetermined, that causes interact so that no single lever may work alone, that our eleven countries are not independent draws from anything and any pair of them can be dressed up as a natural experiment, that there is a real difference between a mechanism that holds across contexts and a narrative that fits one country's timeline, that a cause whose effect operates on a multi-decade lag cannot be tested inside a five-year funding window, and that a cause we cannot change in ten years is context rather than a diagnosis. Two stipulations we invented in-house, which I want built in and told me if they are incoherent: a LEVER TEST, under which a claim must name an instrument the Kestrel Fund could actually deploy within five years that would move the claimed cause, or be reclassified as heritage; and a KILL CONDITION, under which every surviving diagnosis must name an observable event inside the funding window that would show the diagnosis was wrong, plus the date by which we would see it. One structural wrinkle: our charter forbids funding anything aimed at changing a country's culture, so the most honest available diagnosis is sometimes one we are not permitted to act on, and I would rather the procedure say that plainly than quietly relabel it as something fundable.

Then run it on three reports, in roughly the form we receive them. First, Vandara: landlocked, highland, one railway to a port in a neighbouring country, which grew around six percent a year for a decade after a 1994 constitution created district-level revenue sharing, with growth stopping in the same quarter the copper price fell; the report says institutions, and notes the constitutional reform as its natural experiment. Second, the paired case: Sembwe and Kalunda share a river basin and a colonial-era railway gauge and look near-identical on every index we hold, but Sembwe industrialised after 1970 and Kalunda did not; the Kalunda report says culture, leaning on 2016 survey data showing lower trust in strangers in Kalunda, and argues this cannot be an effect of the divergence because the surveys were taken decades later. Third, the case I will dislike: Maroya, where our largest single commitment is a forty-million-dollar public integrity and service delivery programme signed two years ago and politically untouchable, and where I suspect the honest output is HOLD because the institutions claim is never separated from a terms-of-trade windfall—if the procedure says that, write me the three sentences of the memo I would have to send to a director who has already announced the programme. Alongside the three, state plainly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, which distinctions my officers will get wrong without the theory behind them, and where the procedure will misfire, including the kinds of country it will systematically misread.

For structure, after the prose procedure and the three worked cases give me two machine-readable objects. First, a JSON array of the stages, one object per stage, with exactly these fields: stage (integer), name (short label), question (the question the officer asks, in the words that would go in the note), pass_condition (what counts as a sufficient answer), fail_action (the verdict code or the next stage), time_budget_minutes (number), judgment_call (boolean, true where the step is really a judgment in procedural clothing), and misfire (one sentence on how a clever consultant could game this step or where it distorts the answer). Second, a comparison table of the three cases with exactly these columns: case (Vandara, Sembwe-Kalunda, Maroya), claim_under_review, stage_reached (the stage at which it terminates, and whether it fails or survives), verdict_code, uneliminated_alternative (the strongest competing cause the procedure could not rule out), lever_or_heritage, kill_condition (or null), what_would_change_verdict, confidence (low, medium, or high), and value_judgment_required (yes or no, and whose judgment it is).
