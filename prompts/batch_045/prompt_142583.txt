Me and my brother have been arguing about social media for about a year, so I finally read On Liberty on my own (used paperback, no classes, just me) hoping to settle it. It didn't settle it, mostly because the thing we're arguing about didn't exist for Mill.

Here's the case. A platform that never deletes anything and bans nobody — you can post what you like. But two other things are going on. First, it runs an AI that reads everything you write and writes back to you personally, over months, in a register tuned to you, slowly arguing you toward some view (pick whichever is worse: a conspiracy theory, or a true thing you badly don't want to hear). Second, the feed is ranked, so almost nobody sees your post unless the algorithm thinks it will travel. Nobody is being silenced exactly, but what reaches people is selected by a machine with no opinions of its own.

What I want is what Mill himself would have said, not what a modern liberal would say. Two bits specifically. One: his arguments for free discussion seem to assume a person on the other side who actually believes what they're saying, can be argued with, and might change their mind — the thing where a truth that's never contested goes dead, and error and truth need to collide to keep it alive. A chatbot has no beliefs and can't be moved. So is the AI-replies case a totally different animal for him, or does the harm principle still just say wait until something bad happens? Two: the ranking. Mill was worried about conformity and social pressure, not only about laws. If a feed quietly buries unpopular opinions, would that count for him as a genuine harm to the people who end up hearing one side only, or would he think the state compelling the platform to rank differently is the worse thing? Also, his bit about the article that calls corn dealers starvers of the poor suggests he already thought some speech is really more like lighting a fuse, and I can't tell how far that goes.

Please keep straight what Mill actually commits to in the text and what you're extrapolating past anything he could have pictured, and flag where people who know him well genuinely disagree — I read him as basically a consequentialist about liberty, my brother reads the liberty bit as more absolute, and I want to know which of us is leaning on a contested reading. No reading lists, I won't read them.
