I'm revising a paper for a graduate workshop on confirmation and I'd like substantive comments on the draft notes below before I commit to a full write-up. The paper starts from an argumentative move I keep running into: a long-run prediction fails to arrive, and its defenders reply that the theory was "right but early." I want to say something sharper than "that's unfalsifiable" — I want a taxonomy of what "early" can mean, a norm for when the excuse is earned, and an honest verdict on the Malthus case itself. I've got the literature side handled, so please don't spend effort on who said what or on what I should be reading; I'm after conceptual criticism. Draft notes follow.

Setup. Say a theory T predicts that condition C will obtain by horizon h; C has not obtained by h; and a defender replies that T was "right but early." My claim is that "early" is not one excuse but a slot in a taxonomy, and that filling the slot honestly usually changes the theory's content — sometimes past recognition.

Four rescue-shapes.
(i) Delay: the same variable, at a later date. The pressure was to be famine-driven mortality in England by 1850; now it is famine-driven mortality somewhere, by 2200.
(ii) Displacement: the mechanism binds now, but on a different variable than the one predicted — the pressure is transduced into ecological overshoot, groundwater drawdown, or fishery collapse rather than into food prices and starvation.
(iii) Localization: the mechanism binds, but in a domain smaller than the one originally claimed, and the domain shrinks a little with each miss.
(iv) Inversion: the mechanism's causal arrow was mis-specified, in a way that removes the predicted pressure rather than postponing it. Two illustrations: rising income and security lower fertility rather than raising it, and population pressure induces agricultural innovation rather than being throttled by a fixed supply of land.

The receipt requirement. My proposed norm: a deferral must come with a dated receipt. Name the shielding factor S; say what observation would show S to be exhausted or withdrawn; and say by when that observation should be available. A rescue without a receipt is a latency alibi. The argument for requiring one is that without it the deferral has no exchange rate — you cannot say how much delay you are buying with which piece of evidence, so no observation constrains the theory's probability at all, and each failed horizon is costless.

The Malthus application. The formal core of the Essay on the Principle of Population is a comparative-statics contrast — population tends to grow geometrically, subsistence arithmetically — with misery, vice, and moral restraint as the checks that close the gap. What that picture omits is a homeostat: the very variable predicted to be squeezed is itself what damps the source of the pressure, because prosperity suppresses fertility. If that is right, Malthus did not issue a famine prediction with a wrong date; he omitted a feedback whose sign runs opposite to his mechanism. That is shape (iv), not shape (i), and (iv) is not "early," it is a different theory wearing the old one's clothes. The stronger Malthusian reply is shape (ii): the pressure is real and is being felt now, just through sinks, soils, and aquifers rather than through grain prices. My worry is that (ii) is too cheap. Pressure can always be located somewhere. The discriminating question is whether the original theory picked out the receiving variable in advance, or whether we are matching pressure to variable after the fact.

The calibration worry. Long-run disaster predictions presumably have a terrible base rate, and deferral is cheap while confirmation is loud, so a rational audience should discount a deferred prediction by the rate at which deferred predictions ever come true. But the selection effect cuts both ways: the deferred predictions still in circulation are the ones that kept being repeated, so the observed base rate may be an artifact of which predictions survived. I don't yet know how to state the discount so that it isn't just a rhetorical device.

Second case. I want to test the machinery on something contemporary rather than on a nineteenth-century economist. The candidates are resource-peaking predictions, which came with genuinely dated receipts and mostly expired, and the current claim that frontier model training will exhaust the stock of high-quality human text. I lean toward the latter because the rescue-shapes are already visible in it — synthetic training data as delay, benchmark saturation as displacement — but I may be choosing it because it is fashionable.

What I'd like from you, in roughly this order of importance: (1) Is the four-fold taxonomy doing real work, or do delay and displacement collapse into each other on inspection — is any dated delay just displacement plus a story? (2) Attack the receipt requirement directly. Does it beg the question by building verificationism into the norms of rescue? Is there a version that survives that objection without collapsing into "predict or shut up"? (3) Is the homeostat point in the Malthus section carrying more weight than it can bear, and what exactly follows for the "right but early" verdict if it does? (4) How would you repair or replace the calibration argument so that it isn't hostage to the selection effect? (5) Argue me out of or into the second case study, and say which of the two would actually discriminate between rival readings of "early." (6) Flag any part of the above that is a well-worn move in philosophy of science that I could compress into a sentence rather than develop.
