The practice. Over roughly the last fifteen years a recognisable institutional style has grown up around spending money so as to do good. Independent evaluators study interventions of the sort that aid and development agencies run — unconditional cash transfers, insecticide-treated bednets, deworming programmes, micronutrient supplementation, and the like — and publish a headline figure: dollars per life saved, or health-adjusted life years averted per thousand dollars. The headline figure is a point estimate, stated to two or three significant digits. The uncertainty around it is large and heterogeneous: plausible bounds differ by factors of five or ten, and not merely because of sampling noise but because of model choice, whether effects decay, whether they generalise to a new country, and what the intervention does to people the study never measured — local markets, siblings, future births, government budgets.

The evaluators know this, and their practice contains all of the following moves at once, which is what puzzles me:

1. They say that what matters is the *sign* — we can be confident the intervention helps rather than harms, and we should not pretend to fine-grained precision about magnitude.
2. They apply what I will call a cluelessness haircut: effects that are further away in causal space, more systemic, or further in the future are discounted before they enter the ranking, on the grounds that we are likelier to be wrong about them.
3. They nevertheless rank interventions by the headline point estimate and recommend whatever is on top.
4. Some participants propose replacing (3) with what I will call the Lower-Bound Rule: rank by the pessimistic end of the interval rather than the middle, because the middle is a fiction.
5. The very same community that demands tight experimental evidence before acting on near-term effects will, in other contexts, make confident comparative claims about interventions whose effects are supposed to run centuries or millennia into the future.

Two distinctions I want you to use, though you should tell me if they are ill-drawn. First: separate *outcome-cluelessness* — we cannot predict the total downstream consequences of an action — from *weighting-cluelessness* — even granted a complete and correct list of consequences, we do not know how to aggregate them, whether because the values at stake are incommensurable, or because the aggregation rule itself is contested (how to weigh near-term against far-term welfare, how to handle effects on the number and identity of future people, whether welfare is the only thing that goes into the sum). Most discussion I have seen concerns the first; I suspect this practice carries more commitments about the second than anyone states aloud, since publishing a single number denominated in health-per-dollar already assumes a great deal about commensurability. Second: distinguish the consequentialist *criterion* of rightness from the *decision procedure* an agent or institution should actually run.
