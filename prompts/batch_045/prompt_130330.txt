Hey — need a hand prepping next week's episode. I host a small podcast out of my department (mostly me and a colleague talking to one guest for 45 minutes), and this time the guest is a moral philosopher who has spent years defending a broadly Moorean axiology: intrinsic value is a non-natural property, known by intuition; what bears it primarily are states of consciousness; the two great goods are, roughly, personal affection (especially friendship) and the appreciation of beauty in art and nature; and everything else people name — knowledge, virtue, achievement — gets its value either as a means to those two or as a part of an organic whole containing them, where the value of such a whole is not the sum of the values of its parts. He also defends Moore's method of isolation: to find out what a thing contributes, imagine it existing quite alone, and intuition tells you. And he's willing to follow Moore on the hard cases: a beautiful object no one ever perceives is worth very little, while wrong or delusive appreciation of beauty is worth a good deal less than the real thing, even though the experience from the inside may feel the same.

I'll handle the setup in the intro, so please don't spend questions on having him restate organic unities or the isolation method. Give me eight to ten questions that start from inside the view and push, and for each one write out the answer he's most likely to give plus a follow-up that that answer can't comfortably absorb. He's very good at two dodges — 'that's Moore's own example, I don't need it' and 'the intuition is self-evident once you isolate properly' — so I'd like at least two questions designed so those moves get progressively harder.

Things I want covered, in whatever order works:

- The two goods' border: if friendship is affection plus a true appreciation of a person's qualities, and appreciation of beauty is cognition plus a fitting emotion toward an object, why aren't these the same good applied to different objects? Make him say what distinguishes them, then press on whether the distinction survives.
- A case I've been turning over: a companion who is behaviourally and conversationally indistinguishable from a friend but has no inner life at all. He'll want to say the value drops hugely because the object isn't real. Ask why, given that the value is supposed to live in his state of consciousness — and then ask what he says about continuing to love someone who has died, which seems to push the other way.
- The machinery that handles the paradox of tragedy (a whole made of painful material plus fitting emotional response can be better than the response alone): does it also imply that compassion for a friend in agony has a positive value that partly redeems the agony? That's a consequence I think a Moorean should find repugnant, and I want to know where he gets off.
- Ranking. If value isn't additive and the isolation test only gives verdicts on things taken alone, how does he get from 'these are the two great goods' to any claim about what to do with a life? Maybe the honest answer is that the ranking in the closing chapters of Principia Ethica isn't supported by the method — I'd like a question that forces him to either say that or explain why not.
- What would actually count against him: what evidence, cases, or results in psychology would move a committed defender rather than just annoy one? And is the view insulated — can any putative counterexample be absorbed by postulating an organic whole — and if so is that a problem for him?

End with one closing question where he has to say where Moore himself got something wrong, and keep the tone friendly but not soft. If a question would work better with a quick bit of framing from me, say so in a line.
