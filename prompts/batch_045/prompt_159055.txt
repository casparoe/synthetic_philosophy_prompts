Background: we give out around $2M a year, mostly global-health-ish stuff, and the committee currently ranks everything by one number, expected DALYs averted per dollar, summed across everyone the program touches. I got into this a while back through some effective-altruism reading, ended up reading about Scanlon's contractualism, and I've been arguing (first to myself, now unfortunately out loud) that summing is the wrong operation. The version I've arrived at, which is what I'm putting to them: for each option, work out for each person how much worse off they'd be than they'd otherwise be, and call that the strength of their complaint; compare options by comparing complaints one person against another and never adding two different people's complaints together; do add across outcomes within a single person's life, so that a one-in-a-thousand chance of a severe harm counts as a complaint about a thousandth the size of a certain one; and let bare numbers break ties, but only when the individual complaints are otherwise equally strong. I've picked up jargon like "the aggregation problem" and "the individualist restriction" but I may well be using it wrong. The case I know will blow up: program A prevents a severe lifelong condition in about 15 children a year, program B gives a small but real nutrition benefit to roughly 200,000 children a year, and on any totaling view B wins by a mile while on my rule A wins, since no single child in B has anything close to the complaint one of the 15 has. Then there's the case I'm less sure about but think is the real stress test: option A gives one identified person a certain severe harm, option B gives each of 10,000 people a one-in-a-thousand chance of that same harm, and my rule says take B even though B predictably produces something like ten times as much harm in total — and I'd rather defend that than apologize for it.

The room is eight people and I know roughly what each of them cares about. Two are the quant types who built our cost-effectiveness spreadsheets and think almost entirely in expected value and QALYs.
