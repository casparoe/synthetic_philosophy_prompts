I have had no color vision since birth (rod monochromacy), and for the past four months I have been enrolled in a pilot study of a sensory-substitution device that maps hue onto vibrotactile patterns across my torso, so that I can now sort, name, and anticipate wavelengths I have never actually seen. The question I want help with arose at work. My group builds large vision models, and we are now describing one of them as a complete physical-functional model of human color processing. A colleague says that even a perfect model of that kind would not thereby know what red looks like — Mary's room, more or less, applied to a system rather than a person. My supervisor replies that the argument is empty, and he leans on a single analogy: Mary is in the position of a congenitally deaf acoustical engineer who knows the complete physics of sound, the score of a symphony, and the full neuroscience of her own auditory system, and who then has a cochlear implant switched on. What she gains is the ability to recognize the theme, to imagine it, to hum along — abilities, not new facts about the world. So, he concludes, what Mary acquires on leaving the room is know-how rather than knowledge-that, and nothing was ever missing from the physical story.

I would like an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on the knowledge argument in general. Please give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the score, of the physics, of the implant, of the hearing, of the humming — and then separate the disanalogies that actually bear on the conclusion he wants from the ones that do not. I am suspicious of two in particular. First, that the source case quietly imports what is contested: a sound's character is plausibly exhausted by its acoustic structure, so of course a complete physical description of it leaves nothing out, whereas whether the character of an experience is exhausted by its physical-functional structure is exactly what is in dispute. Second, the modality: in his case the implant restores the very sense whose physics she had mastered, while in mine the substitution device delivers hue through touch. That makes me want to know what the ability hypothesis is committed to about a Mary who has used a hue-to-touch device since birth: she can discriminate, categorize, name and arguably imagine the relevant wavelengths, and she knows all the physical facts besides. If the hypothesis says she already knows what red is like, that looks to me like a reductio of it. If it says she does not, then what exactly is the ability she lacks, why is the tactile surrogate not an instance of it, and does specifying it collapse back into acquaintance, that is, into a relation to the experience itself, which was the thing the hypothesis was supposed to avoid?

Two further things: please say whether it changes anything that Mary is stipulated to know the physical facts about her own post-release brain state as well, and whether the analogy has to work in reverse — someone who loses a sense seemingly loses abilities without losing facts. Then, on that basis: does his conclusion survive, or was the symphony doing the work? Is there a better analogy for what is really at stake, and is any part of this dispute merely verbal — a fight about whether to call the new state knowledge?
