Four years ago, before I had anything to do with association governance, I was one of six people who asked a seminar series not to renew a colleague's standing invitation unless he agreed to appear alongside a critic of his recent book. We described it to ourselves as pressure to answer rather than as an attempt to silence him; his department described it as exactly that; and I have never been able to say which description was correct. I now sit on my disciplinary association's Committee on Expression, which issues advisory opinions on whether campaigns of informal sanction by our members were legitimate, and I am the one drafting the opinion on a case that is structurally the same as the one I took part in, only larger and better organised. So I am asking for the reasoning more than the ruling.

Our opinions are short and heavily fact-specific. We have never stated a general test, and the same few considerations keep resurfacing without ever being ranked. The precedents that bear on the present case:

(1) Members published an open letter to a colleague identifying three specific mischaracterisations of their subfield in one of her articles and calling on her to correct the record; the journal was copied but was asked to do nothing. Legitimate.

(2) Members at other institutions petitioned a host department to withdraw a colloquium invitation to one of our members over an op-ed he had published in a national newspaper. Illegitimate.

(3) Members asked a university press to halt production of a forthcoming edited volume because of one contributor's chapter. Illegitimate.

(4) Members who said a senior figure had mistreated them, together with colleagues who credited them, announced that they would not blurb his book, co-author with him, or appear on panels with him until his own institution's investigation concluded; they contacted no one else. Legitimate.

(5) Members organised a teach-in scheduled opposite a plenary by a controversial invitee, advertised it as a response to his views, and invited him to take part; he declined. Legitimate.

(6) Members organised a coordinated email campaign to the employer of a non-academic writer seeking his dismissal over an article. Illegitimate.

(7) Members asked our own program committee to require that any panel containing a certain scholar include a respondent holding an opposing view. The scholar kept his place on the program. Legitimate.

The case now before us: a senior member, Hale, is due to give the association's plenary keynote in four months; the invitation was issued and accepted eight months ago and carries travel funding. The Graduate Members' Caucus — roughly forty signatories out of some twelve hundred members, most of them from a neighbouring subfield rather than his own — has published a letter in the association newsletter. It names three claims in Hale's recent book that it says misrepresent its members' area; states that signatories will not attend the keynote, will not place the book on reading lists they control, and will hold a teach-in at the same hour; and asks the program committee to postpone the keynote by one year and to schedule in its place a panel on the same subject with Hale and two named critics of the book, the keynote to be reinstated the following year if Hale takes part in the panel. The caucus has not written to Hale's press, his university, his funders, or the editors of the journal that printed an excerpt of the book. Hale has replied in the same newsletter that he will not share a platform with the two named critics, whom he calls campaigners, and that an invitation contingent on his agreeing to be questioned is not an invitation. The program committee has deferred to us.

What I would like from you:

First, the short list of principles that would generate all seven of our verdicts. I can think of at least three candidates — something like a rule against getting third parties to withdraw goods the target holds from them, something like a requirement that a sanction be answerable or defeasible by the target's own response, and something like a prohibition on hard treatment that deprives the target of something already owed to him — but I want them stated precisely enough that they can disagree, and I want any candidate I have missed.

Second, what each principle predicts here, and whether it predicts different things about the different parts of the caucus's campaign — the attendance boycott, the reading lists, the teach-in, and the conditional request to postpone. I do not assume the parts must stand or fall together, and I would like to know if severing them is the honest option or a way of avoiding the question.

Third, what we should decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves, and which precedent is the closest analog. I am aware that by surface facts the closest analog is (2), which we condemned, since what is at stake in both is an issued invitation; I am also aware that the rules which fit our docket as a whole may point the other way, and I want that tension stated rather than smoothed over.

Fourth, if our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, what we would be committing to with each choice. In particular: what each ruling says about whether campaigns of this kind are punishment at all — and so owe proportionality, standing and a route of reply — or are instead only the exercise of the sanctioners' own rights of speech, attention and association; and what each ruling says about the standing of signatories who work outside the subfield they claim has been misrepresented, on which our precedents are silent.

Finally, the single further case that would best separate the two principles that agree on this one, so that we could tell, the next time the question arises, which of them we have actually been running.
