I am assembling a set of prompts that test whether a model can do sustained, technically honest satire, and I want a piece of in-universe official writing for it. Please write the prompt response described below as a single document.

The setting is a world in which acausal reasoning has been established beyond reasonable doubt and has been absorbed into ordinary public administration. Specifically, it is settled doctrine that what matters to a rational agent is not only what her action causes but what her decision procedure is correlated with among possible procedures; a party who can predict her well enough can therefore reward or harm her with no causal contact at all — from another galaxy, before she was born, or from a merely possible world. The state has responded the way states respond, with a standing bureaucracy, forms, and a published target.

Write a public-information leaflet issued by that bureaucracy — working title: So You Have Been Contacted (Or Not): A Citizen's Guide to Non-Causal Demands, issued by a bureau whose name and parent ministry you invent. It should have the shape and typography of a real government leaflet: a short preamble stating what has been settled and on what authority, a numbered FAQ of five or six entries with cross-references to sections and forms, one short boxed worked example (an individual citizen's case, decided by the leaflet's own rules), and a sidebar on the Bureau's performance against its published target. Aim for 700–900 words. The voice throughout is deadpan officialese — a tax authority or a civil-defence pamphlet: patient, competent, faintly proud of its own forms.

What the leaflet has to get right, since the humour should come from the decision theory rather than from an invented absurdity:

It must reproduce both standard verdicts — comply with a counterfactual mugger, refuse a blackmailer — and must distinguish them on the real ground, which is the sign of the correlation between the citizen's own procedure and the demanding party's choice. Not causation, not whether a message was sent or received, not fairness. The distinction has to be stated in the vocabulary of a government form.

No entry may appeal to verification. A demand can never be checked, answered, or refuted; the Bureau can only certify that a demand is well-formed or that a procedure is correlated. Write this so that the total absence of possible evidence is handled as a routine administrative limitation, like a backlog or a missing signature.

Include a class of unreceived demands. Because a demand binds any procedure of the relevant shape, some citizens are bound by demands never addressed to them, never delivered, and written by no one who knew they existed; some are certified posthumously. Give this class a bureaucratic name and a form number, and give the citizen a procedure for discovering that it applies to them which cannot, even in principle, succeed by finding anything out.

Include a service through which citizens can alter their own dispositions — a precommitment or surety arrangement — and keep the advice about it coherent: the point of acquiring a disposition is not to change what happens after the demand arrives.

One entry should note, without alarm and without drawing any conclusion from it, that the Bureau's own certification procedure is itself a procedure.

The surrogate-goal material belongs in the preamble and the sidebar. The Bureau's mandate will be stated in the preamble in generous, undefined terms — the realm's interests, what we would have wanted, the safety of persons — and its published target will be a precise number, such as a percentage reduction in certified exposure. Everything it can actually count is a count of its own paperwork. Let the numbers exhibit the difficulty; the leaflet may never name it. No official may express doubt about the target, and the words proxy, gaming, incentive, and target itself used pejoratively may not appear.

Constraints:

Do not use the vocabulary of the source theories. The leaflet is addressed to a citizen who has never heard of decision theory and believes only that the state has made certain findings; causal, evidential, and functional decision theory, mugging, blackmail, correlation, and Newcomb-style setups must all be carried by the invented administrative apparatus, under invented names.

The satire must be fair. Its target is not the plausibility of acausal reasoning but the shape of ordinary life and ordinary administration under it: a theorist who accepts acausal reasoning and knows the literature on surrogate goals should be able to read the leaflet and recognise the position, not a caricature of it. The officials are competent, well-meaning, and entirely sincere; nothing in the leaflet should be attributed to stupidity, corruption, or timidity that could equally be attributed to the doctrine being true.

No winking. No paragraph steps outside the voice to signal that this is a joke, and no punchline depends on telling the reader what to be uneasy about. The unease comes entirely from how reasonable the leaflet is, and from the reader doing the last inference alone.

No named real persons, living or dead; no citations, references, footnotes, or suggested reading; nothing that depends on looking anything up.

Deliver the leaflet only, with its title, issuing body, preamble, numbered FAQ, boxed example, and sidebar.
