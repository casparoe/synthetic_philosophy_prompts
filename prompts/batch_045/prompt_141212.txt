I'm an engineer, not a philosopher, and I've been arguing with a coworker about this for like a week, so I want an outside read.

Here's roughly what we actually do at work. We've got a code-completion model, and the interpretability team trains sparse autoencoders on its middle layers: take a layer's activations, learn an overcomplete dictionary of directions, call each direction a "feature", look at which inputs light it up the most, and give it a human-readable name. Some come out looking like real concepts ("this loop has an off-by-one bug", "the variable is being shadowed", "the user is asking for something unsafe"). Then people write things like "the model represents off-by-one errors here" or "this is the model's internal notion of an unsafe request", and they use the features: clamp one, and the model's behavior shifts in the direction you'd predict if it really were tracking that property.

But the same people, sometimes in the same doc, will also say: the decomposition isn't privileged, there's no ground-truth feature list, and if you change the dictionary size or the random seed you get a different set of directions that reconstruct the activations just as well and steer the model just as reliably. And of course, under the hood its all just matrices and floats — nothing in there looks like a belief.

So what does this practice actually presuppose? Does calling these things "representations" and "the model's concept of X" commit us to some kind of realism — a fact of the matter about what the model represents, with some carvings of activation space correct and others wrong? Or can someone who thinks representation-talk is just a useful fiction (in the way we might say a thermostat "wants" the room at 70 degrees) do every bit of the same work and just be careful how the paper is worded? If it's the second option, does anything get lost — do we loose the ability to say that some interpretation is just wrong?

The case that got me stuck: say we train two autoencoders on the exact same layer and the same data. Both reconstruct activations equally well, both give features that steer the model as expected, but they carve the space along different directions — so SAE-A has a clean "off-by-one bug" feature, and SAE-B has no counterpart to it; SAE-B spreads that work over three features, each of which fires on some off-by-one bugs and also on unrelated stuff like docstrings in a particular format. Neither decomposition seems worse. Is there any sense in which one of them found the model's real representation and the other one didn't? Or is "what does this model represent" just not a well-defined question at the level of individual features?

And a follow-up my coworker raised that I can't answer: imagine a feature that fits the activations perfectly and steers the model reliably, but corresponds to something no one would ever call a concept — say a direction that fires on "Python with a type error OR code that has anything to do with dogs", because the training corpus happens to have some weird correlation in it. It's a real, useful, predictive feature. Would a realist about representations have to say the model represents that insane disjunctive thing? And if they don't want to say that, whats principled about the features they do want to call concepts — is it just that they're the ones we have words for?

Please don't give me citations or a reading list, and please don't stop at "it depends what you mean by representation" — I get that. Tell me which ways of meaning it are actually on the table, what each one costs you, and what would concretely change about how we build, report, and use these features if we stopped believing in representations altogether.
