I'm defending a job-market paper in a criminal law faculty workshop next Thursday. Nine people: mostly criminal law theory, one torts/remedies person who writes on unclean hands, one evidence person, and a visiting judge who actually sentences people for a living. Most of the room thinks of punishment as communicative or expressive, and two of them think "standing to blame" is just moralism with footnotes.

My thesis: standing to blame doesn't track the blamer's moral record at all. It tracks whether the blamer is a reliable interpreter of the norm they're invoking. Hypocrisy defeats standing because the hypocrite's own conduct is evidence that they read the norm as narrower than the way they're applying it to the target — they've quietly built in an exception for people like them — so their blame misrepresents what the target actually had reason to do. I got to it from sitting in on restorative justice circles for the past two years, where the person whos blame actually lands is usually not the one with the cleanest hands, it's the one who has obviously worked out what the norm really requires of people.

The part I know is exposed, and that I also like most: on my view a fully self-aware hypocrite — I do this too, I've done it worse, and I'm blaming you anyway — gets standing back, and arguably comes out with more standing than the clean-handed blamer, since owning it is evidence they aren't quietly excepting themselves. There's also a corollary about institutions: a state that systematically produces the conditions for an offense lacks standing to condemn it, but only where the condemnation is doing the work of not reckoning with its own role.
