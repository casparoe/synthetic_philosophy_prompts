I have no academic background; I read about this stuff on my own in the evenings and I audit a small reading group on paradoxes that meets at the public library. Two of its members, Miriam and Dev, have been deadlocked for three sessions and asked me to referee. I couldn't. I have no stake in who wins, and I've reproduced the exchange as faithfully as I can without signalling which way I lean (I don't lean either way).

They agree about the problem. Our language has one word "true", it applies to our own sentences, and it can be applied to sentences that themselves mention truth. Hence: *This sentence is false* — call it L. If L is true, what it says is the case, so it is false; if it is false, then what it says is so, so it is true. They also agree that the old fix of splitting language into levels, with a separate truth predicate for each level, is a dodge: English doesn't work that way, and the paradox was supposed to be about our one word. (I've seen these two views called the "gap" and "glut" approaches somewhere online and linked to names like Kripke and Priest, but I haven't read any of it, so please argue from the positions as I describe them, not from anything you know about those authors.)

(1) **Miriam:** L is neither true nor false. Picture it in stages. Start with sentences that say nothing about truth and get their values from the world. Now let "true" apply to those, and add the new sentences you can now evaluate, and go on like that. Sentences that get a value at some stage are grounded; L never gets a value at any stage, because any value you try lands you back where you started. So it falls outside the extension of "true" and also outside its anti-extension. The payoff: no contradiction, no true contradictions, classical reasoning untouched for every sentence that has a value at all. And the stage picture is independently plausible — it is just how we actually learn the word: a child learns "snow is white" long before she learns what "true" means.

(2) **Dev:** L is both true and false. His argument is not about L but about Miriam's theory: it cannot say what it wants to say. Her own verdict, "L is not true", is by her own lights itself not true — it is one of the gappy ones. So she cannot state her solution in the language the problem arose in; she has to climb to a metalanguage, where a new sentence arises that says of itself that it is not true *there*, and she must climb again. She has reinstated the hierarchy she rejected and simply moved the paradox upstairs. His view keeps the language closed: it can talk about the truth of its own sentences, including the paradoxical ones, without leaving the language. He adds that Miriam's "neither" is less principled than it looks: the stage procedure has many stopping points, and she picked the smallest one, which is a way of building the answer in.

(3) **Miriam:** the revenge problem is symmetrical. Take: *This sentence is only false and not true.* Dev cannot give it his favourite treatment — if it is both true and false, then what it says (that it is false and not true) holds, so it is not true, and he has to say the same thing again, and again. Which shows the real cost: on his view the word "not" has stopped doing the work of ruling things out. He can assert anything of the form "S and not-S" and has no resource left for rejecting a claim, and rejection is most of what truth-talk is *for*. She also argues that his logic is not "classical logic plus a few odd cases": once you give up the rule that a contradiction entails everything, you lose other inference patterns people rely on, and the usual defence of his logic proceeds by describing models that already contain the gluts — which assumes the very thing in dispute.

(4) **Dev:** rejection is not lost. We reject contradictions unless something forces us to accept them; a paradox is a proof, so it forces us; ordinary contradictions have no such proof behind them, so we reject them. That is not ad hoc, it is ordinary fallibilism. Miriam's alternative is worse, because hers is an unstatable silence: every time she says "these sentences are not true", her own remark joins the gappy class, so what she is committed to is an infinite tower of things she is not allowed to say. And her stage procedure does not by itself deliver "neither" in every case: consider *This sentence is true*, which says nothing at all about the world. There are stopping points of her procedure where it comes out true, others where it comes out gappy, and nothing in the procedure picks one — so the "neither" verdict must be coming from an extra intuition she hasn't defended.

(5) To break the deadlock they wrote three lines on the whiteboard in the library meeting room, on the grounds that "This sentence is false" is too simple and both theories can be shaped to fit it:

> Line 1: Line 2 is true.
> Line 2: Line 3 is true.
> Line 3: Line 1 is not true.

They agreed in advance that a theory should be judged on whether it gives a verdict at all, whether the verdict is the only one the theory allows, and whether the verdict is believable. Miriam worked it out and got: all three gappy, since no assignment of true and false to the three lines is consistent (the loop is odd, with one negation), and her stage procedure never grounds any of them. She adds that the note is a good test case because it shows paradox needs no "this very sentence" trick — just a loop. Dev worked it out in his logic and got: all three both true and false, and he claims this is the *only* stopping point his logic permits, so if anything his theory is the more decisive of the two. Miriam replies that uniqueness is cheap when your only options are true, false and both, and that his verdict asserts of Line 1 that it is true, which entails that Line 2 is true, which entails that Line 3 is true, which says that Line 1 is not true — he is presenting a chain of inference whose end contradicts its beginning and calling that a solution. Dev replies that each step of that chain is fine, and that the chain ending where it does is the phenomenon rather than an objection; calling it an objection assumes in advance that contradictions cannot be true, which is the whole question.

(6) A third member, a retired programmer, said neither of them should be looking for a final verdict at all. On her view, you guess a value for each line, get a new value for each line by applying the truth predicate to your previous guess, and repeat; a sentence is healthy if it eventually settles and stays put, and pathological if it keeps flipping forever. I tried this on the note starting from "all three false" and got a repeating cycle of six configurations, with all three lines flipping. I don't know what to make of that, and neither did they: Dev said it was his oscillation dressed up in more steps, and Miriam said she wanted to know whether Line 1 is true, not how my guesses wobble.
