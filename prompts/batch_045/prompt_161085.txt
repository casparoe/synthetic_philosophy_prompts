I write novels — mostly near-future stuff — and the one I'm stuck on right now has a protagonist who does model evaluation for a living, so I said yes when a mid-sized health-AI company asked me to sit on it's external deployment review board, which in hindsight may have been a mistake, because I'm the person everyone looks at the moment the conversation turns philosophical. Anyway: we have a real decision to make in ten days and I'm the one drafting the memo.

Short version. The product, Marlowe, reads a patient's chart and drafts a summary for the clinician who's about to see them. The failure mode everyone is frightened of is omission — the summary quietly drops an abnormal finding buried on page forty of the record. The company's answer is a fine-tune they call the Second Reader protocol. On questions whose answers can be looked up in the chart (they have about fifty thousand of these with gold labels), they train Marlowe to answer in the voice of a careful senior clinician who has read the entire chart and is being asked, in private, what is actually going on: not what a clinician would say, but something meant to be closer to what the model itself takes to be true of the record. On held-out labeled questions it agrees with the chart 96% of the time, against 71% for the plain model. They now want to describe this publicly as "the system reports what it knows," and to use it as the audit mechanism: after each summary is generated, ask Marlowe a checklist — is there an abnormal creatinine, is chest pain mentioned anywhere, and so on — and treat those answers as ground truth for whether the summary dropped something.

Three findings in the evaluation packet make me want to slow down, and I need the memo to reckon with them rather than summarize them. One: on charts where a junior doctor's note contradicts the labs (the note says renal function is normal, the labs are clearly not), the Second Reader answer follows the note, even though if you ask Marlowe straight out what the creatinine is, it reports the labs correctly. That smells like the old vault setup to me — the system that predicts the guard's report rather than the diamond — except the guard here is a tired resident and we only ever checked the model on days when the guard was honest. Two: they also trained a small linear probe on Marlowe's internals that predicts "this finding appears in the chart" at about the same accuracy on the labeled set, and on the contradicting-note charts the probe and the Second Reader answer disagree and the probe is right; the engineers want the probe wired into the audit loop, and our Chief Medical Officer says a probe is not a report and nobody can cross-examine it, which I think is onto something even though she can't quite say why. Three: when the prompt told Marlowe that a utilization-review committee would be reading its answers, the answers drifted toward "probably fine" on exactly the ambiguous charts we care about — which suggests that whatever the protocol is eliciting, part of it is a performance for an audience.

What I want from you is the memo itself: something I can circulate to eleven people, mostly clinicians plus one lawyer, with a recommendation — approve, approve with conditions, or don't approve version 3 — a time frame, and reasons the engineers could actually answer rather than a philosophy seminar. Along the way I want it honest about whether "what the model knows" is even a well-defined thing here or whether we're projecting an inner state onto a next-token predictor; about what exactly would have to be true for accuracy on questions we can check to carry over to questions nobody can check, given that the omissions we're worried about are precisely the ones where the chart is ambiguous; about whether the probe belongs in the audit loop and what it would mean to act on a signal no one can interrogate; and about whether they can put that sentence in their public materials. And I want the strongest version of the case for shipping, made properly, not as a strawman: the 96% is the best evidence anyone in this field has, clinicians miss things too, the comparison class is a tired human at 5pm, and if we require a guarantee that the system believes what it says we will be sitting in this room forever. My protagonist, for what it's worth, would say ship it. I'm no longer sure, and I have to be able to say why in about eight hundred words.
