I host a long-form philosophy podcast for listeners who have some undergraduate background but no specialist training, and next week's guest is Dr Ingrid Halvorsen, a philosopher of mind who has spent fifteen years defending an unusual, asymmetric form of eliminative materialism. Her claim is that our talk of beliefs — states with representational content and satisfaction conditions — will find no home in a mature neuroscience and should be expected to drop out of serious vocabulary, while our talk of desires (or, as she prefers, valence-laden motivational states) will survive, because conative states already have a tolerable neurobiological home in reward and aversion systems. The successor to belief-talk, she argues, will be quantitative and dispositional: agents will be described by intervention-profiles, structured sets of counterfactual response-dispositions to experimental interventions, with no appeal to inner items that can be true or false. She has a label for the fate of the word belief: referential evaporation rather than reduction, because she expects the folk platitudes to fragment across several distinct neural kinds, no single one of which inherits the role. She is closer to the Churchlands' eliminativism than to any fictionalist or expressivist alternative.

Her signature argument — call it the granularity argument — is that folk-psychological generalisations hold only at the coarse grain of whole-organism behaviour under ecologically normal conditions, and that every attempt to tighten them either collapses into vacuity or breaks down at the fine grain where the causal work is actually done. On her view the regularities we exploit are artefacts of a coarse partition of the state space, not approximations to real laws.

She also has one stock move I want you to plan around: whenever an objection is framed in belief–desire terms, she replies that the objection is couched in the idiom she is retiring, so answering it in those terms would concede what is at issue.
