I'm in a small reading group on value-driven epistemology and we've now spent three sessions on the Meno question — why, if at all, is knowledge worth more than mere true belief — and it's stalled in a way I find suspicious rather than merely hard. Everyone grants the setup: true belief already delivers whatever practical payoff the truth was good for, so if knowledge is worth extra it has to be worth more *than the very truth it contains*; and everyone more or less grants the swamping worry, that once you hold the content fixed as true, any further feature knowledge adds looks like it merely borrows its value from the truth rather than adding to it. But the responses people offer seem to slide past each other: one person says knowledge is what you can rely on across nearby possibilities, another that it's a kind of creditable achievement, another that it's about being in a position to act or to assert, another that the question should be re-run for understanding or explanation instead, and the one economist in the group says it's just the value of information and should be handed to decision theory, while the Plato person says that misses that the question is whether knowledge is worth more *to the knower*. Nobody has moved anybody.

My own pet hypothesis, which I'm attached to but not confident about, is that the stall is partly a disagreement about the comparison class and the currency. If you compare a single belief-token to a single belief-token at a time, in one currency of final value, swamping looks fatal; but if you compare whole cognitive lives, the modal stability knowledge buys shows up as a real aggregate advantage even though it's invisible case by case. I tried this out with a made-up case: an agent set loose to make a hundred decisions where at each one they can either act on their own belief or defer to someone whose belief constitutes knowledge, and my sense is the knowledge-haver's edge compounds — a bit like an insurance policy or an option value that only pays out across many plays, never within one. The economist liked this; the Plato person said I'd changed the subject from what knowledge is *worth* to what it's *worth having around*, and I honestly can't tell whether that objection is deep or just a way of restating the original question.

So I'd like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. What kind of disagreement is this actually: a verbal one about 'value' and 'better than', a substantive one about the metaphysics of achievement or of modal stability, a clash of bedrock intuitions about what a theory of knowledge is even for, or several different questions — what's worth aiming at in inquiry, what a mind-state is worth to its possessor, what norms govern action and assertion — being run under one heading? Where exactly does the swamping argument get its grip, and does my portfolio framing actually dodge it or just relabel the same problem at a higher level? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or should we expect no resolution and why? Please commit to one diagnosis and defend it rather than listing the options, tell me roughly how confident you are in each part of it, and be explicit about which single objection or consideration would most likely make you drop it.
