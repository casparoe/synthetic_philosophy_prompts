Quick context: I'm in the second year of a philosophy MA and I have to hand in a ~2000-word thesis proposal by the end of the month — it's for a one-semester project of about 10k words, supervised reading only, no empirical work, no archival stuff. My supervisor thinks my idea is too big and I half agree, but I also think she's hearing a different question than the one I actually mean, so I need the thing written down precisely enough that we can argue about the real question instead of her reconstruction of it.

The half-formed idea is this. Everyone works the teletransporter and fission cases the same way: one person goes in, two equally good candidates come out, identity can't relate one thing to two, so identity isn't what matters in survival — what matters is the psychological continuity/connectedness relation (call it R) that holds between the original and both branches. Fine. But nobody seems to work the versions where the branches aren't equal, or where the copy is imperfect, and I think that's where the actual problem is. Two variants I keep chewing on. (1) Degraded transmission: the scan isn't perfect, so the person who arrives has 96% of the memories, traits, projects and dispositions of the person who left — not a different person, just a slightly eroded one. Do a hundred trips and you get someone with almost nothing in common with the original, while no individual trip looks like the one where they stopped being me. (2) Lopsided fission: instead of 50/50, one branch comes out with most of the continuity and the other with a thin fragment of it — 90/10, say — and then you can slide it continuously down to 50/50. My suspicion is that once you let the cases come in degrees, what matters in survival has to come in degrees too, and then there's a pile of questions nobody has answers to: is what matters a scalar magnitude or does it have a cutoff; what does it even mean to say I now have 0.9 of the prudential concern for some future person that I'd have for a full continuer; in lopsided fission is my concern divided between the branches, or does the thin branch get nothing, or does it get something that just isn't enough to compete; and does the closest-continuer move — which was always the fallback for getting fission to come out as identity — survive the slide from 90/10 to 50/50, given that at 50/50 it has literally nothing to say. There's also a real-world upshot I like: ordinary ageing just is the degraded case, so if what matters is scalar then what matters between me now and me at eighty is less than what matters between me now and me tomorrow, and our actual patterns of self-concern both do and don't track that, and I want to know what that mismatch is evidence about.

What I need from you is the proposal itself, not the bibliography — I'll fill in citations, so please don't invent any. State the question so that it has a determinate-ish answer in a semester. Say why the standard answers (the no-branching requirement, closest continuer, the reductionist line that since R holds to both there's nothing to choose between them) leave it open — no literature survey, I want the gap. Give the method: what I'd actually do month by month across four months, how I'd build the family of cases, what I'd test each candidate account against, and why that approach rather than the obvious alternatives, like importing a worked-out theory of vagueness and applying it, or settling the metaphysics of degrees of identity first. Then: the two or three conclusions the project could realistically reach, which considerations would decide between them, and an honest account of what would count as failure — e.g. if the only defensible answer turns out to be that it's vague so stipulate a threshold and move on, is there a thesis left? And flag where it's liable to blow past a semester's scope, because she will ask me that.
