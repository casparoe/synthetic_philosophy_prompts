I've got myself roped into leading the january session of our little philosophy reading group — about a dozen people, mostly retired like me, plus two grad students who come for the wine — on that phrase you see everywhere, the 'strange inversion of reasoning', Darwin's and Turing's. I was an enzyme kineticist for thirty-odd years, bacteria and metabolic pathways, and I only started reading philosophy properly three years ago, so I know the biology cold and the philosophy patchily. That's exactly why I'm asking.

Don't write the session for me. Tell me how to build it. Forty-five minutes, no slides, one whiteboard, and the group has already met the phrase 'competence without comprehension' in a handout, so they've got the label but not the shape of it.

What I want:

The order. Which inversion has to come first, and what exactly does it make available to the second one? My instinct is Darwin first, because 'a process with no foresight produces something that looks foreseen' goes down easily at the bacterial scale, and then Turing's version — that comprehension itself might be assembled out of parts that don't comprehend — is the same shape turned on the mind. But I can't tell whether that order just makes the second one look obvious by analogy, which would be a mistake of its own. Tell me which dependency is real and which one I'm imagining.

The carrying example. I've got two from my own bench and I'll vouch for both. First: we knocked out the gene for the main step in a pathway, and the bug rerouted flux through a promiscuous side reaction — badly at first, then measurably better over a few hundred generations. It looks for all the world like a cell working out a solution, and of course nothing worked anything out. Second: chemotaxis, a cell climbing a sugar gradient with no representation of sugar anywhere in it. Which one should carry the session, what must the carrying example have, and — more usefully — what must it lack? And do I need a separate example for the Turing half, or can I transform the first one into the machine case on the board in front of them?

The misconceptions, in the order they'll actually arrive. I can predict three people. Bill, ex-aerospace, will say selection only ever removes things and can never build anything. Margaret, who taught English, will say a machine can't mean anything because nothing has ever happened to it. And there's a young compsci regular who thinks the question is settled and that language models understand perfectly well. I also expect the generic ones: 'so Darwin proved there's no purpose', 'survival of the fittest is just a tautology', 'so it's all just statistics really'. Give me the point in the sequence where each gets headed off rather than answered, and say plainly if any of them is an objection I should let stand.

Something I've been chewing on and want your verdict on. It looks to me as though the end state does indispensable work in the explanation — you can't even pick out the mechanism as a mechanism without saying what it's for — while doing no work at all in the process itself. The explanation leans on the goal; the process doesn't. If that asymmetry is the actual crux, where in the sequence does it belong, and how do I stop the group sliding from it straight into 'so purpose is real after all'?

One more, which I suspect is where the session defines ite self: if the Turing inversion is right it seems to boomerang, because our own comprehension would be built out of uncomprehending parts and we'd be in the same boat as the machine. Should I raise that at all, and where, so it lands as a consequence rather than a gotcha and the compsci guy can't use it to eat the last ten minutes?

And the check at the end. I don't want to finish on 'any questions'. I've been thinking of asking them to say what a non-inverted explanation would even look like, and what would count as evidence for going back to one — my reverse-Paley question — on the grounds that if they can say what it would take to find real foresight in evolution, they've got the move and not the slogan. Is that the right test, what does it show me that asking for a restatement wouldn't, and what should I keep out of the session altogether and say out loud that I'm leaving out?
