I've been reading LessWrong and the EA Forum on and off since the original sequences, and one thing that keeps resurfacing in the corners of that world that worry about wild-animal suffering, insect sentience and the replaceability argument is a short passage in Robert Nozick's *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* — the same 1974 book that gave us the experience machine and the utility monster — where he turns, almost in passing, to animals. As I remember the structure (please don't quote him verbatim; I can't check the page from where I'm writing, and I'd rather no invented sentences get attributed to him), it is two-tier: human beings are protected by side constraints of a broadly Kantian kind, so they may not be sacrificed or aggregated for the greater good, whereas animals are covered by something that gets summarised as utilitarianism for animals — their suffering genuinely counts and must be weighed, but it counts aggregatively, and no constraint is violated by painlessly killing an animal whose life has, on balance, been worth living. My possibly-contested reading is that he goes further and floats breeding livestock with a diminished capacity to suffer, so that the practice as a whole can be made to come out well by the only calculus that applies to them.

What I'd like from you is a modest proposal: 800–900 words, in the deadpan register of a UK government white paper or statutory consultation document, issued by an invented Office for Animal Settlement that has decided to take that two-tier structure completely literally as administrative policy. Format it as numbered clauses, with a short costings annex and a closing FAQ. The apparatus I'd like you to build it around: Statutory Instrument 2026 No. 414, the Livestock (Moral Settlement) Regulations; the Bodmin Demonstration Herd; the Halswell Threshold, which is where the office has had to locate the boundary between the two tiers; and a named, invented author — a senior civil servant or an academic on secondment — who is entirely sincere. The reductio should do itself: because the office may only aggregate animal welfare (never constrain how persons are treated), it is obliged to bring into existence as many net-positive animal lives as possible, to engineer out the capacity to suffer, to slaughter at the welfare-maximising moment, and therefore to expand the herd and to require citizens to eat more, since vegetarianism lowers net welfare; and because the aggregative tier covers all sentient life, it must eventually extend to the hedonic management of the countryside — predators, parasites, wild populations. Let the comedy come from the actual content: the replaceability obligation, the paperwork for the Halswell Threshold and the appeals against it (pigs, great apes, infants, the severely cognitively disabled, hypothetical uplifted animals), the modelling of insect sentience in the costings annex, and the fact that every clause is scrupulously humane. Keep it fair rather than a hatchet job — concede within the piece, in the office's own voice, what is genuinely appealing about taking animal suffering seriously while keeping persons special — and stay deadpan: no winking, no rhetorical questions, no horror the narrator seems to notice. End with a single short clause noting that a delegation from a species of greater cognitive capacity has recently submitted the same white paper with the tiers reversed, and that the office has filed it as Out of Scope (Pending Consultation).

After the piece, add a brief note of about 150–200 words: separate what you take to be settled about Nozick's actual commitments here from what is genuinely contested or that you are simply unsure of (especially the bit about breeding animals not to suffer, which may be my own reading rather than his), say plainly which premises I supplied and which you would have put differently, and flag any sentence in the piece that depends on a claim about the text you would not want to defend.
