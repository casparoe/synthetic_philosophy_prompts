I'm retired now — I used to be a sensory physiologist, mostly vision — and I've been reading philosophy for fun the last couple of years. Something keeps bugging me and I want to know whether there's a word for it.

The case I keep coming back to is Molyneux's question: someone blind from birth, given sight, shown a cube and a sphere he's only ever handled — does he know which is which? Fine. Here's what I keep noticing. People actually go and run versions of this: adults who had congenital cataracts removed later in life, the gadgets that pipe a camera feed onto your tongue or your back so blind users can pick out shapes, brain imaging of the visual cortex before and after training. And whatever comes out, the argument doesn't move. If the result is no, the side it goes against says: fair enough, but that only shows the wiring needs a bit of experience to switch on, which isn't what anybody was asking. If the result is yes, the side it goes against says: fair enough, but that's a few minutes of learning, or some low-level cue, not the real question. Both replies are individually reasonable. And nobody ever says the thing I'd have said in the lab: this question has now absorbed four or five attempts to answer it, maybe it isn't the kind of thing that has an answer.

Two instances, so you can see the edges. First: "at first sight" has to be given a time window, and the window seems to be doing all the work — a test at half a second and a test at five minutes can come out opposite ways, and where you set the window decides the philosophy. Second, a thing I thought up on a walk: forget whether he recognises the shapes, and ask about savings instead — does having handled the shape make him learn the visual discrimination faster than for a shape he's never held? If yes, something carried over even where nothing was recognised. If no, the two senses share nothing at all. It feels like a sharper instrument than yes-or-no, and I genuinely can't tell whether it dissolves the old question or just relocates it.

So: is there an established name for a question that absorbs every result aimed at it, where the re-descriptions are each defensible in isolation? It's not moving the goalposts — nobody is cheating, the target seems to genuinely not have a fixed position. In the lab I'd have muttered something about a badly conditioned measurement, or an instrument who's reading depends on a setting nobody ever agreed on.

If there is a name, I want the carving and not just the label: what counts as a case of it, what's a case of plain underdetermination or of a merely contested term instead, and where the border between those runs. If there's no name, coin one and define it so that I could apply the definition myself, and give me one clear case and one near miss. Either way, tell me whether my two wrinkles are instances of the same thing: is the time-window business the same phenomenon or a different one, and does the savings version sit inside it or escape it?

No citations please — my journal access lapsed when I retired and I'm not chasing references. Names of concepts or people are fine, just nothing I'd have to look up.

Sections, in this order, roughly these lengths:

- Short answer (two or three sentences): is there a name, and what is it.
- The nearest established notions (about 400 words): what each one is, and why it's close to what I described.
- Where each falls short (about 300 words): the specific mismatch with my case.
- Definition and boundary (about 250 words): the term you'd settle on, stated tightly enough that I could apply it, plus one clear case and one near miss.
- Back to Molyneux (about 200 words): what the diagnosis implies for that question, and whether my savings test is a way out of it or just another instance.
