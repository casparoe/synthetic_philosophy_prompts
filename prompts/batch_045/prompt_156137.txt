So I got talked into jury duty for a mid-sized literary prize — fiction, including translated fiction, around 180 submissions, five jurors, two months, and at the end we all have to put our names on a short citation for the winner. We have three freelance readers who do first-pass reports for us, about 400 words each. Which means the actual job is mostly this: deciding how far I'm allowed to believe what a stranger's report says about a book I haven't read, and then deciding whether I can sign my name to it anyway.

I gather there's a whole philosophical literature about exactly this — aesthetic testimony, whether you can come to know (or justifiably believe) that a thing is good by being told, versus the idea that you have to experience the thing yourself for the judgment to be yours at all. There's a name for that second view that I half remember, something like the acquaintance principle. I don't want a summary of the debate and I don't want reading recommendations. I want it turned into something I can actually run, repeatedly, at speed, on a stack of reports, without any of us having the theory.

Here's the raw material I bring, from doing this badly last year. Tell me which of these distinctions are load-bearing and which are noise:

- A report mixes different kinds of claim. A verdict ("this is the best thing in the pile"), a texture claim ("the sentences flatten out after chapter six"), a "what it's like" claim ("reading it feels like being cornered at a party"), and a fit claim ("it's doing something genuinely new with the immigrant-family novel"). My hunch is these should not all travel the same way, and that the last one is the one you can most reasonably take second-hand because it's about a tradition rather than about a response.
- There's a difference between deferring to a reader and deputizing one. Two of our readers I've calibrated: I've read six books they loved and scored them roughly where I did. Is a calibrated reader's verdict a different epistemic object from an uncalibrated one's, or am I kidding myself?
- Bad news travels better than good news. "It's a mess, here are three places it breaks" is checkable by sampling thirty pages, whereas "it's a masterpiece" seems to need the whole thing. If that asymmetry is real, put it in the procedure as a rule and not as a mood.
- Reports decay as the book gets further from what I would be experiencing if I read it myself: a novel in verse, a 900-page experimental thing, a translation from a language none of us read. At some point the report isn't reporting on an experience I could have had.
- And the signing bit is separate from the believing bit. A citation isn't only a belief report, it's a recommendation. I suspect I can be entitled to recommend something I'm not entitled to judge, and the procedure should say where those come apart, because that's the part that actually keeps me up at night.

Constraints, so you don't hand me something unusable: ten minutes per report, roughly 200 reports, and I can honestly read maybe 25 books whole and sample another 40. The reports are the only evidence I'll have for the rest. The jury chair has to sign off on any shortlist I move forward, and the citation needs all five of us.

What I want:

1. A decision procedure, as a table I can tape above my desk, in this order of columns: step number | the question to ask at that step | what counts as a sufficient answer (including what to do when I can't get an answer at all, which will be often — I can't interrogate the readers) | what happens if the answer is no | whether this step is fact or a judgment call, and who signs off | the way this step tends to misfire. Make it ordered — questions in the sequence I should actually ask them, with explicit stop points and explicit hand-offs to a human.
2. Run it on three cases: (a) a 700-page debut where two readers are rapturous about the prose and one says the last third collapses, (b) a translated novella from a language nobody on the jury reads, with one ecstatic report from the reader who does read it, and (c) my own favourite — I read sixty pages and adored it, and every other reader was lukewarm. Make sure at least one of these comes out in a way I won't like, and don't soften it.
3. Tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do, and flag the places where you've quietly built in a contested philosophical claim I'd have to defend if another juror pushed back.

Then, so I can paste the results into the longlist tracker my kid set up for me, a single JSON object with these fields:

- "procedure_steps": array of objects with fields step (integer), question (string), sufficient_answer (string), on_no (string), judgment_call (boolean), typical_failure (string)
- "case_verdicts": array of objects with fields manuscript (short label), claim_types (array of strings, from the kinds of claim above), step_reached (integer), verdict (string), do_i_read (one of "whole", "sample", "not"), can_i_sign (one of "yes", "qualified", "no"), confidence_type (string — say whether I'd be judging, deferring, or recommending), unwelcome (boolean)
- "residual": array of objects with fields situation (string), why_it_stalls (string), what_it_actually_turns_on (string), default_if_deadline_hits (string)
- "smuggled_assumptions": array of strings

The table is what I'll use; the JSON is what goes in the spreadsheet.
