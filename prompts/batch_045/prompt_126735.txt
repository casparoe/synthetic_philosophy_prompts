I edit the humour page of a philosophy-of-science reading group's newsletter, and for the next issue I want a piece that satirises structural realism — specifically the ambitious eliminativist version, the one that says the world's inventory contains relations but no relata, and that objects were a manner of speaking all along. I was talking with a friend who works in university administration about how much of institutional prose is just ontology with a budget code attached, and it occurred to me that a research institute which actually tried to run on this view would generate exactly the kind of deadpan document I'm after. So: write the first annual report of the Ontological Compliance Working Group of a fictional institute (the Pendlebury Institute for Theoretical Metrology, say) addressed to its Director, reporting on year one of the Institute's adoption of eliminativist structural realism as its official operating ontology. Roughly 700 to 900 words, formatted as numbered findings with an implementation schedule and a running apparatus of footnotes where the more technical jokes can hide.

I want the comedy to come from the position's real commitments and real embarrassments, so that a defender of the view laughs and concedes it was fair. Some of the material I'd like worked in: the Institute's stated rationale is the no-miracles thought plus the continuity-across-theory-change point — past theories got the furniture of the world wrong but the equations right, so the Institute will retain the formulas and decommission the furniture, and it is oddly proud that the previous regime's equations survive as a limiting case of the new ones even though nothing is left for them to describe. The relata problem should appear as an administrative one: the organigram survives but the boxes in it do not, there is a crate in the basement labelled with the decommissioned objects, and nobody can be fired because there is nobody to fire. The Institute's showcase case study is quantum statistics — permuting the labels of indistinguishable particles changes nothing — which Human Resources has implemented literally, so that payroll now runs on occupation numbers and there is an unsettled question about what happens when two members of staff are assigned to the same node. The report should then discover, without ever naming it, the Newman-style worry: the Institute's official structural description turns out to be satisfied by any sufficiently large domain whatsoever, so the compliance certificate is, in perfect good faith, awarded to a bucket of gravel (or a lost-property box, or ten thousand rubber ducks), and the Compliance Officer cannot explain what went wrong without smuggling back in the very thing the reform deleted — something to pin down which relations are which. Close with the file's last line rather than a punchline wink: the Institute's last remaining substances-and-properties realist, a maintenance engineer, gets one unimpressed remark, and a minority report from the moderate faction — the people who wanted to keep the objects and merely admit we can only know their structure — accuses the eliminativists of having thrown away precisely what the equations were about. The tone throughout should be flat, bureaucratic and completely sincere; a reader who knows nothing about the debate should still be able to tell what is being made fun of.
