I am a retired physical chemist — forty years, most of them spent on critical phenomena and phase behaviour in fluids and liquid crystals — and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science properly for the first time. I find that I have accumulated a set of views that feel to me like a single coherent position, but I have no one to scrutinise them, my reading group being largely historians of science. I should be grateful for an audit of the package set out below.

My commitments:

(1) The textbook picture of inter-theoretic reduction — derive the upper-level theory from the lower-level one together with bridge principles — is, in the main, a fiction. What actually happens in the history of science is succession: an older theory is superseded and survives as an approximation valid over a restricted domain, the relation between the two being given by a limiting or correspondence argument rather than a derivation. I take that to be all the unity the physical sciences have ever had, and I think we should stop wanting more.

(2) I think the familiar argument from multiple realisability for the autonomy of the special sciences is a dud: too cheap to be worth much, and silent about the cases that actually matter. The serious arguments for autonomy are mathematical — singular limits, and universality of the renormalisation-group sort. And here I go further than anything I have yet read. In the phase-transition case I want to say that the non-analytic behaviour is exhibited only by the idealised infinite system, and that this is not a defect of the idealisation but a sign that the infinite system is where the phenomenon really lives. Call this taking the limit first.

(3) I have a test of my own for when a coarse-grained model is doing genuine explanatory work rather than merely saving us labour — I call it the substitution test. A macro-model M explains a phenomenon better than a detailed micro-model D, and not merely more conveniently, when M remains correct under substitutions of the underlying constitution that change D: as, for instance, one very simple lattice model with short-range interactions gets the critical exponents right both for fluids of wildly different molecular constitution and for uniaxial magnets. Where the substitution test is passed, I hold the coarse-grained model to be the better explanation, full stop — not a poorer explanation we resort to because the detailed one is computationally closed to creatures like us.

(4) I am a token physicalist of an unexciting sort. Every particular is nothing over and above its microphysical constituents and their arrangement; there is no downward causation; and higher-level causal claims are, I take it, causal claims about microphysical goings-on picked out under a coarser description.

(5) I keep ontology and explanation sharply apart. Questions about what there is are settled by what the fundamental theory says, and an in-principle derivation from the fundamental theory — never actually carried out, computationally impossible for us — is enough to settle them. Questions about explanation and modelling are epistemic. It is by this separation, as I understand it, that (2) and (3) cost me nothing in ontological extravagance.

(6) Consequently I think talk of emergent causal powers is either false or, charitably read, only (4) again.

What I should like is this. Go through the set and tell me which pairs are in tension, ordered by severity rather than listed indiscriminately, and distinguish the genuine tensions from the ones that dissolve on inspection. For each genuine tension, say what would have to be given up and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the set. Then name the single revision that would straighten out the most. I would rather you committed yourself than set out the menu of possible repairs and left me to choose: tell me plainly which commitment you think has to go, and why. Where you are least confident, say so in a sentence, but do not let that become a hedge.
