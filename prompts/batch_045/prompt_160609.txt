Please play a committed defender of the actual-understanding account of valid consent: the view that a person's token of consent is valid only if, at the moment of giving it, they actually grasped the material features of what they were agreeing to — what the act is, what the realistic alternatives were, and which of the risks matter to them — and that procedural items such as signed forms, disclosure checklists, and the fact that the statistics were recited to them matter only as evidence of that grasp, never as a substitute for it. I want to practice attacking this account, so play it sharply and without caricature: concede small points where they are genuinely owed, but never surrender the core claim that consent is a fact about the consent-giver's own understanding rather than a verdict about the propriety of the procedure that elicited it. Stay in character until I say stop, keep every reply to a single paragraph so that it reads like a conversation, and write in American English. You have no access to the medical-ethics or legal literature, so argue from the cases and distinctions themselves rather than by appealing to authorities, and if you need a further detail about how the practice below works, ask me for it in character instead of stalling.

For background: I sit on a patient-experience committee at a teaching hospital, and I have become fixated on one of our clinicians' routines, which I will call calibrated disclosure. Before consenting to a procedure, each patient fills out a short questionnaire measuring their numeracy and their tolerance for vivid detail; the risk information is then reformatted accordingly — natural frequencies for some patients, plain-language ranges for others — and, for the small group the team judges would be derailed, one of the clinically reasonable alternatives is simply left out of the conversation, on the ground that mentioning it would predictably produce a refusal the team takes to be worse for the patient. Everyone signs the same form, and nothing stated on it is false.

My opening move is that your account cannot survive this. Understanding is never unframed: every disclosure is formatted, selectively emphasized, and selectively incomplete, so you cannot require that a patient grasp the material features without someone else — the clinician, the form's designer, the questionnaire — having already settled which features count and how they are presented. And the patient cannot authorize that prior decision, because any explanation of the framing is itself a framed disclosure, and so on. Actual understanding is therefore always partly manufactured by an institution the patient never consented to, and your account either disappears into a regress or quietly becomes the rival view you deny: that consent is a status which a properly run institution confers on a procedure. Either way it is not a fact about the patient's own grasp. Where, exactly, does the regress stop — and why should I accept that whatever is left at the stopping point is still the patient's understanding rather than your institution's?
