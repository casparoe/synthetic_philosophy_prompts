I've held for years, mostly from hanging around the usual blogs and forums, that Aumann's agreement theorem is doing real normative work and isn't just a cute puzzle: two people with a common prior who have common knowledge of each other's posteriors can't agree to disagree, so when smart, well-informed people stay apart on something after years of trading arguments, that's evidence somebody is failing to update, or that one of us was never really an epistemic peer.

Stress-test that. What would count against it, ranked from fatal to merely awkward? I care most about which assumptions actually fail for beings like us — the common prior, common knowledge of *posteriors* rather than of what people say out loud, a shared state space, the unbounded-computation bit — and which of those failures are live in real disagreements versus merely logically available. Please separate what a defender of the view would itself concede counts against it from what only critics say.

Two things I've been chewing on that I want ruled on. First: the disagreements I actually care about mostly aren't about probabilities inside a model, they're about which model or which state space to use at all, where it's not obvious a common prior is even defined. Is that a genuine defeater or just the common-prior objection in a hat? Second: the theorem is about credences in events, but my disagreements are usually about what to do, or about which considerations count as reasons — does it reach those, or is applying it there a category error?

Also: its a theorem, so no observation can refute it as such. Say honestly whether the version I stated has been qualified into unfalsifiability, whether that's a problem for this kind of claim, and whether it predicts anything the rival "priors just differ, nothing to see here" view doesn't. What would move me most is an argument that the conditions are unreachable in principle for bounded agents rather than just messy in practice; "people are irrational sometimes" wouldn't move me at all.
