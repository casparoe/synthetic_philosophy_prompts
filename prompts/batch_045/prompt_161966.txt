Right — I'm a novelist, about two thirds through a book set in Copenhagen in 1846, and there's one scene I keep botching. My protagonist is Marie, nineteen, a compositor at a penny weekly I've invented called Lygten (The Lantern): mostly gossip, letters, and a column where readers send in anonymous confessions and subscribers vote by post on which one sounds most sincere. The winner gets nothing except being printed again. In the scene she somehow gets an hour with Søren Kierkegaard to ask him about the press and the public, and I need questions he'd actually have to think about, not ones where he just re-issues his famous line about the crowd and untruth.

So: draft me eight to ten interview questions for him. Ground rules:

- Nothing biographical or soft (no how did you come to write this, no what do you hope readers take away). Each question has to turn on the actual machinery of his critique of the press and of the public as a phantom.
- For each question, give me the follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give, since in my experience he'll have a stock reply ready and I want Marie to be able to push past it.
- I want at least one question on each of these, and the rest can be your own:
  1. whether the damage is done by the press as a technology or by the social form it creates — Marie sets the same letterpress type for a devotional booklet in the morning and for the gossip sheet in the afternoon, so she'd want to know whether print itself is the culprit or only what print has become;
  2. the confession column: a reader's shame is printed but nobody knows whose it is, so nothing is risked and yet something is exposed. Does that count as inwardness for her, or is it the opposite — inwardness with the danger taken out? I especially want the follow-up here, because I think he'll say it's counterfeit and I don't know what Marie says back;
  3. the flat contradiction that he used the press constantly and cleverly — pseudonyms, self-publishing, and later street-sold pamphlets against the church — and evidently thought his own use was somehow exempt. What exactly is supposed to mark the difference, and could any editor claim the same exemption;
  4. whether the critique needs Christianity to stand up. Marie is not remotely pious, and I need to know whether she can make it in her own voice or whether the single individual only makes sense as the individual before God;
  5. a test he'd accept for telling a genuine community or congregation from a public — something concrete I can put in her mouth, not the abstract definition;
  6. what would count as evidence he's wrong: the press exposing an abuse that no individual could have exposed, a public that holds power to account, that sort of thing. I want him cornered on the case where the thing he hates does the work no one else will.
- One of the questions should be about the business with the satirical paper that made him a figure of fun in the street — I may have the details muddled, so write it so that he can correct Marie, and aim it at the gap between his account of what the press does to a person and how he actually took it when it happened to him.
- One question should be the one he least wants to be asked, and tell me why it's that one.
- End with a question Marie can ask that would let a reader of my novel tell whether he's changed his mind by the end of the hour — I don't want the scene to be a lecture, I want it to move.

Write the whole thing in British English please (colour, honour, and so on), and don't pad it with introductions.
