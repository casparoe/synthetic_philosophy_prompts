I convene a small graduate reading group on the philosophy of consciousness, and our last two sessions went badly in a way I want to fix in writing. Two of the strongest members — one defending the multiple-drafts picture associated with Dennett, on which there is no stage or instant at which content is finally presented to an inner observer and no fact of the matter beyond what is observable about which draft wins, and one who happily concedes that the literal spatial metaphor is a bad image but insists that there is still a determinate time at which a content becomes conscious and that a subject's experience at a time is unified across modalities and features — have spent two evenings restating their views in their own vocabularies without ever locating the point where they genuinely diverge. What I want from you is the document an adversarial collaboration between them should have produced: a text both could sign, with the remaining disagreement made exact rather than rhetorical. Both parties should be equally competent; neither may be a caricature, so the drafts theorist has to engage the strongest version of the opposing view (as a claim about the structure and timing of experience rather than about a little person inside the head), and the other side has to engage the strongest version of the anti-theater argument rather than a naive homunculus.

Please impose two stipulations of my own, because they are where I think the real work lies. First, split the dispute into three sub-questions and keep them separate throughout, rather than letting the two characters slide between them: whether there is a determinate time at which an individual content becomes conscious; whether experience at a time is unified across modalities and feature-dimensions; and whether the grain of experience is momentary or smeared across a window of tens of milliseconds. Have the document say explicitly, for each pair of sub-questions, whether someone could consistently answer them differently. Second, stipulate some measurement technique of your own devising — say something that settles, to within a few milliseconds and from neural or behavioral evidence alone, whether at a given time the subject had a determinate experience as of red — and require the drafts theorist to say whether such a technique would settle the first sub-question or whether the point is that no possible observation could, and require the other party to say what outcome of that technique would count against them.

For shared material, give them the color-phi style case: a red dot is flashed briefly at one location and a green dot at a neighboring location a fraction of a second later, and observers report a single dot that moves between the two locations and that appears to change color partway along its path, that is, before the second dot has been presented. Both parties grant the report and grant that nothing changed color in the world at that moment. Format the result as a numbered section of agreed claims — about the data, about what needs explaining, about what the no-inner-observer argument does and does not rule out, and about how much weight introspective report carries — followed by a disagreements table. Each row of the table should give one crux, the claim phrased as the drafts theorist would accept it, the same claim phrased as the consolidation theorist would accept it, one concrete case, argument, or hypothetical result both agree would bear on that crux, and each side's prediction about that case recorded in advance. Include at least one crux for which each party states what would count as evidence against its own position. Close with a short section identifying which parts of the dispute are substantive and which, if any, are merely verbal — about what conscious, at a time, or determinate fact mean, or about whether a fact must be observable to count — and say why.
