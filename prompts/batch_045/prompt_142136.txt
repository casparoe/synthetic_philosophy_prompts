My flatmate is doing a PhD in computational neuroscience and every time we end up talking about philosophy of mind she falls back on the same move: the mind is to the brain as software is to hardware. She uses it for two things. First, to argue that psychology is autonomous from neuroscience — you can't read the program off the voltages, and the same program could be compiled onto completely different machines, so hunting for the neural basis of a belief is a category error. Second, more concretely, to argue that if someone built a detailed enough functional replica of a person's brain in silicon — a whole brain emulation, whatever the substrate — that thing would have the person's beliefs, memories, and everything else, because programs don't care what they run on. I've been reading around functionalism and multiple realizability on my own, and the analogy now seems to be doing way more work than anyone ever checks.

A few things bug me specifically, and I'd rather you engage with these than give me a general essay. (1) In a computer the hardware/software boundary is engineered: signals are pushed to thresholds, everything is clocked and error-corrected, and the instruction set is written down in advance, so the fine-grained causal powers of the realizer really are screened off from the software level. Nothing like that is true of a brain — neuromodulators diffuse, temperature, glial stuff, and dendritic nonlinearities all bleed into what the "program" does, and there's no spec to compile against. So the analogy seems to smuggle in the exact thing that's at issue, namely that there's a level with a closed causal repertoire. Relatedly, the hardware side of a computer is itself described functionally (microcode, logic gates, transistors, doping), so the distinction reappears inside the hardware and you never reach a ground floor, which is awkward if what you wanted was two levels, mental and physical. (2) I can't work out what "the same program" even means when we have no formal description of the brain's organization. As far as I can tell, all we'd ever have is agreement on some finite batch of input-output pairs we happened to test, and there are always indefinitely many different programs that agree on those and diverge later. I've started calling that test-set realizability, and it makes me think the "compilation" step in the analogy is fake. (3) Even granting a functional organization, whether two systems share one seems to depend on the grain and the timescale you individuate states at: carve states finely enough and nothing is ever realized twice, carve them coarsely enough and everything is. And here's the case that got me: take the same transition table and run it on photonic hardware a million times faster, or on some slow wetware a million times slower. The analogy says, obviously, same mind, just faster or slower. But if anything mental has an in-built duration, or if what you care about is a life unfolding in world-time, that's not obvious at all. (4) There's a trade-off I keep tripping over: define mental states narrowly, by their causal relations to inputs, outputs and other states, and multiple realizability looks easy but the states stop looking much like beliefs; define them holistically, as the roles picked out by our entire folk theory of the mind, and it gets very hard for two different systems to share any state at all, since the roles are fixed by the whole network. The analogy seems to let people slide between those two pictures depending on which one helps in the moment.

So: audit the analogy for me. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the program, the machine, the compiler, the instruction set, the clock, the user, the crash, the port — and where each row breaks. Then separate the disanalogies that actually bite against each of my flatmate's two conclusions (the autonomy of psychology; the emulation having the same mental states) from the ones that are just noise. Tell me whether the conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying them the whole time, and whether the two conclusions stand or fall together. And if there's a better analogy for what functionalism and multiple realizability actually claim — song and instrument, map and medium, whatever — tell me what it buys and what it costs. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating my question back at me.
