I work in nonlinear control theory and I think in terms of dissipativity and passivity (storage functions, supply rates, cyclo-passivity, available storage and required supply), Lyapunov and storage-function arguments, value functions and the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation, Bellman's principle of optimality, and the difference between executing an open-loop plan and executing a closed-loop policy, as that difference shows up in receding-horizon implementations. I have no training in philosophy, so please do not explain my own vocabulary to me.

I keep encountering a family of arguments, the money pump among them, which are supposed to show that certain patterns of preference are not merely expensive but irrational. I want that family of arguments, and the surrounding dispute about what rationality requires of an agent across time, restated in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can, and then I want an honest account of what the translation loses.

The material to be translated is roughly the following. First, the synchronic case: an agent whose preferences run in a cycle — it prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A — can be led through a sequence of trades, each of which it accepts willingly, and can end up holding what it started with and less money than it began with; the conclusion drawn is that such preferences are irrational. There is a familiar reply, that a foresighted agent simply declines the trade that closes the cycle, and a familiar counter-reply that the pump can be built with steps small enough, or losses spread thinly enough, or with the refusal point made unsalient enough, that the foresighted agent has nothing to grab hold of. Second, the diachronic case: an agent's preferences themselves change over time, and philosophers distinguish three broad responses to this — requiring the later self to defer to the earlier one; allowing the earlier self to anticipate its own future change of heart and select the best plan consistent with that anticipation; and permitting the earlier self to adopt a plan and then carry it out even at moments when it no longer wants to. Third, the question of what the argument is meant to establish in the first place: whether being exploitable is itself the defect, or only an costly symptom of a defect located elsewhere, and whether the argument needs an exploiter who actually exists or only one who could.

My own guess, which I do not trust and which I would like you to correct rather than flatter, is that the whole business is a claim about path-dependence: preferences that are not the gradient of any scalar potential can be integrated around a closed loop with nonzero net change, so the pump is what I would call a non-passive system, the utility function plays the role of a storage function, and the argument for transitivity is close kin to the argument against perpetual motion. Give me the real translation, tell me how far this guess goes and where it breaks, and then give me the residue report. In particular:

For each of the three items above, state the claim as cleanly as you can in my vocabulary, and mark explicitly where you had to bend my vocabulary, or bend the philosophers' claim, to get a fit.

In dissipativity terms exploitability is not all-or-nothing: there is a quantity of value extractable by a worst-case environment, and it may be finite or unbounded, and whether it is unbounded depends on details of the loop rather than on the bare existence of a cycle. Does that gradation matter to the philosophical question, or is it an artefact of my framework? Give me your considered view on the agent who would lose only a bounded amount before noticing and walking away, and on whether irrationality here is correctly treated as a binary property.

Map the three diachronic responses onto open-loop execution of a fixed plan, re-planning at every step, and the self-consistent or fixed-point policy; say which of these mappings is forced and which is a stretch, and say what happens to each when the cost functional itself varies with time.

My framework always takes the cost as supplied from outside, by a designer. The philosophical dispute is partly about which quantities rationality should hold fixed across time: the agent's preferences at the initial time, its preferences at the time of action, its reasons, its earlier verdict about its later self. Tell me whether this is a point at which my vocabulary is simply silent, or a point at which the philosophers' question is less well defined than it sounds, and argue for the answer you give.

I can specify an adversarial environment that extracts energy from a non-passive system without anyone's having built it and without any intention being involved. Is that a fair model of what the money pump is doing, or does the argument need something my vocabulary cannot supply — an offer, a deliberating agent, a reason to which the agent fails to respond?

Finally, tell me what in the debate has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all, and whether that residue is a limitation of control-theoretic language or a sign that the original claim was doing less work than it appeared to. Keep the translation honest: where the target vocabulary would have to be quietly altered to produce an answer, say so instead of altering it.
