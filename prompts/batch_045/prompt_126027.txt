The question was:

Block distinguishes phenomenal consciousness from access consciousness. Global workspace theory (Baars; Dehaene and Changeux) holds that a mental content is conscious when it is broadcast to, or made globally available to, a wide range of consumer systems.
(a) State the access/phenomenal distinction, and say which of the two global workspace theory is best read as a theory of.
(b) Suppose global workspace theory is true. Does it follow that phenomenal consciousness is nothing over and above global availability — i.e. that Block's distinction collapses?
(c) Consider a hypothetical case. A subject shows everything global workspace theory countenances as global availability for a stimulus: cross-modal priming, delayed forced-choice performance well above chance, and use of the content in a deliberative decision. Yet she sincerely reports no experience of the stimulus whatsoever. Call this transparent access. Does global workspace theory have the resources to describe it, and does the possibility of transparent access help either side of the dispute?

The student's answer:

Block's distinction is between phenomenal consciousness — there being something it is like for the subject — and access consciousness, which is a matter of a state's content being available to the subject's other cognitive systems: available for verbal report, for deliberate reasoning, and for the rational control of action. The two can come apart in principle: a state might be phenomenally conscious without being accessed, or accessed without being phenomenally conscious. Global workspace theory is best read as a theory of access consciousness rather than of phenomenal consciousness. Its central posit is a bottleneck: many specialised unconscious processors compete for entry to a limited-capacity workspace, and the winning content gets broadcast to a wide range of consumer systems. On Dehaene and Changeux's version the broadcast is implemented by long-range workspace neurons in prefrontal and parietal cortex and is all-or-nothing, which is why only a few items at a time can be consciously accessed. So what the theory explains is reportability, deliberate control, and the other access functions.

If the theory is true, though, I think the distinction collapses. The functions Block uses to characterise access — report, reasoning, control — are exactly the functions that global availability subserves. So a complete mechanistic account of which contents get broadcast and why is an account of which contents are access-conscious, and phenomenal consciousness has nothing left to be. Block's own argument for keeping them apart, the overflow argument, actually helps here. In partial-report experiments the subject seems to enjoy a rich visual experience of the whole array while being able to access only a few items. But if the workspace is a limited-capacity bottleneck, the items that are never broadcast are not available to any consumer system, and a content available to no system is not conscious in any sense we could ever detect or that should bother us. Hence either the unbroadcast items are accessed after all, or they are not phenomenally conscious and the apparent richness of the array is an illusion built from a few accessed items plus expectations. Either way global workspace theory ends up with a complete story about consciousness, and phenomenal consciousness reduces to global availability.

Transparent access is not a case the theory can take seriously. In global workspace theory 'conscious' just means globally available, so if the content really is globally available — priming other modalities, guiding a decision — then by the theory's own criterion the subject is conscious of it in the only sense there is. Her denial is itself only a report, and report is one consumer system among several, so the forced-choice and decision data should be trusted over what she says. The case would also be a problem for Block rather than for global workspace theory. Block needs it to be conceivable that there is access without phenomenality, but on the workspace picture there is no room for that gap: access is defined functionally, and there is no further fact about what it is like for the subject. So the hypothesis of transparent access is either incoherent or question-begging, and it gives no support to the claim that phenomenal consciousness is a distinct property.
