You're a comparative political theorist who has also spent a couple of years doing compliance work for an anti-corruption NGO, so you know both the literature and the mess on the ground. Take your time and think carefully: start by brainstorming a short bullet list of every axis you can think of along which gifts, favours and payments to people in official roles come apart, and only after that build the tree — and tell me which of those axes you ended up throwing away, and why.

My friend Priya and I spent about three hours last night going in circles about corruption. She grew up in Kerala, I grew up in Norway, and it went like this: her family gave the electricity board engineer who restored their power after a storm a small gift, worth maybe a week's wages when he recieved it; she calls that gratitude, I call it tipping a public official. Then her cousin got a job because an uncle knew somebody — she calls that looking after your own, I call it nepotism. Then she pointed out that where I'm from a civil servant can't accept even a bottle of wine, and asked why that should be a moral truth rather than just the local habit of a high-trust country that we then export to everyone else as "integrity". Somewhere in there I realised we weren't disagreeing about any facts: she was using the word to mean harmful, I was using it to mean breaks a rule about offices, and neither of us noticed we were answering different questions in the same arguement.

So don't settle it for us — I want a sorting device instead. A one-page decision tree I can bring to my development-economics reading group next week (eight of us, mixed backgrounds). Every branch has to be a question you could answer about a concrete case without knowing which position it leads to — no "do you think culture matters?" — and it has to actually sort these: the storm gift; the cousin's job; a truck driver slipping a note into his passport at a border post to avoid a two-day delay; a family handing a surgeon an envelope the night before an operation; a mining company building a school in the village where it wants a licence; a father paying a headman for access to land his family has farmed for generations.

Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names, but the leaves should land on recognisable positions: the universalist one behind the standard NGO definition (a position of trust used for private advantage); the relativist "it's just how we do things here"; the social-norms view, where the very same act counts as black, grey or nothing depending on what the local norm actually forbids; the "second-best" view that some payments repair a system that is already broken (I half-remember that some countries' laws carve out payments to speed up routine paperwork, which feels like an admission my side doesn't want to make); and the skeptic who says the whole category is just a pejorative for exchanges that powerful people happen to dislike. One axis I insist on, because I think it's doing most of the damage in our argument: whether the transfer makes things come out the way the written rules already said they should, or buys an outcome the rules existed to prevent — I think Priya's engineer and my border post land on opposite sides of that and neither of us saw it. Finally, flag the boxes in the logical space that nobody seems to occupy and say whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unclaimed, and flag any two leaves your tree genuinely cannot separate — my bet is that the relativist and the social-norms view collapse into each other on every case I can invent.
