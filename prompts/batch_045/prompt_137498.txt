I'm a software engineer by trade and I sit as one of the lay members on the interventions panel of a small nature charity in the east of England. The panel decides what position the charity takes on things that affect animals: management interventions on our own reserves, and planning applications near them. I have no philosophy training — what I know about wild animal suffering comes from a few essays I read online about r-selected lives and the possibility that most wild lives contain more suffering than enjoyment, so please translate out of the jargon. For the last two years I've kept the panel's decision log in a git repository, and mostly as an exercise I've been trying to write our verdicts as a scoring function over outcomes, so that I could run our past decisions back through it as regression tests. I can get two quite different functions to reproduce every one of our precedents, and they diverge sharply on the application in front of us this month. I'm the one drafting the response the panel will sign off, so I need to be able to defend it in terms of what we've already done.

Our precedents, as recorded in the log:

- Immunocontraception for an over-abundant muntjac population in a 200-hectare wood, darting does to prevent conception — endorsed, with the logged reason that it avoids fawns being born into winter starvation.
- A winter supplementary feeding station for the same herd — opposed, on the grounds that it props up numbers and makes the following spring worse for the animals that are there.
- Humane euthanasia of badly injured animals after a storm on one of our reserves — endorsed.
- Rewetting 80 hectares of drained peat bog, which our ecologists expect to increase invertebrate and wading-bird numbers substantially — endorsed, on the grounds that a functioning bog is better for the animals that live in it.
- A neighbouring estate's request that we back a cull of resident greylag geese damaging their crops — opposed; the log says we don't kill healthy animals to protect a commercial interest.
- A 40-hectare solar farm on intensive arable land — no objection, with a note that one member wanted us positively to welcome it because the site would be sealed under panels and hardstanding for forty years. We never voted on that, and the log records no agreed reason.

The new case is a planning application for a data-centre campus on 58 hectares at the edge of a lowland heath: roughly 30 hectares of gorse and acid grassland mosaic, 20 hectares of birch scrub that self-seeded over old gravel workings, and 8 hectares of shallow flooded pit where a large population of common toads breeds. The scheme is two data halls, a substation, hardstanding, two attenuation ponds, mown lawns and ornamental planting, with the biodiversity net gain obligations that now come with permission here, so the developer has to show a measurable uplift in habitat units. Construction means stripping and grading the whole site over eighteen months; our ecologist's rough estimate is a few hundred small vertebrates and something in the order of millions of soil and leaf-litter invertebrates killed outright, with very wide error bars. Her guess at the steady state afterwards is that reptile and ground-nesting bird numbers collapse by 80 to 90 per cent, but that irrigated lawns, the ponds and the ornamental beds could leave soil invertebrate biomass flat or even higher than at present, since earthworms, slugs and midge larvae do very well in that sort of setting. The buildings are expected to stand for about sixty years. If we object and the application is refused, our realistic expectation is that the land scrubs over and something similar comes back in five years, and the developer has let it be known that there is an arable site two miles away — though our remit is this application only.

What I'd like from you: set out the two or three rules that fit all six precedents, and say what each of them predicts we should do here — object, stay silent, or support — and on what part of the arithmetic each prediction turns. Give me your best guess at what we should file if we want to be consistent with ourselves, name the precedent that is the closest analogue and the one that is the hardest to square with that decision, and be honest about where our precedents simply underdetermine the answer and what principle we would be committing ourselves to with each option. I'd also like your view on an uncomfortable point: one reading seems to imply that, if the sealed campus really does support fewer sentient animals, we should be asking for *less* mitigation rather than more, and no one on the panel will put their name to that. If we can't say it out loud, is a bare no objection a coherent position or just squeamishness? Finally, suggest one further application, ideally of a kind we might realistically be consulted on, that we could put in front of the panel purely to break the tie between the readings, and say what each reading predicts about it.
