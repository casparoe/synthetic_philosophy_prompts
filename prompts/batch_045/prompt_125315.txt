hey — I need a satirical piece for a small zine I put together, and I want it to be the kind of thing that actually lands for people who know the free-will literature rather than just name-dropping at them.

Write a parody of a leadership-development firm's self-assessment instrument: something like "The SPHEX Audit — a maturity model for organisational flexibility." About 700 words. Format: a short intro blurb selling the product, then 6–8 numbered items each with a five-point agreement scale, then a closing "scoring and maturity levels" section. Deadpan consultant voice throughout: no winks, no "silly, right?" asides, no hedging. It should read like something a real firm would email you at 7am.

The core joke: it is a tool for detecting rigid, unthinking, stimulus-bound routines, and it is itself one hundred percent a rigid unthinking routine. Whatever you answer, every item routes to the same recommendation — book a follow-up, escalate to a coach, re-run the audit next quarter. The scale labels should do some of the work: put something at the top end like "I always stop and re-derive my objectives from first principles before acting," which is plainly not flexibility but paralysis.

Things I want it to get right underneath the jokes:

- The wasp doesn't look stupid in its normal environment. Its routine is a perfectly good solution to a problem that doesn't normally involve a philosopher with a stick; only the perturbation reveals the rigidity. So the audit should be measuring the one thing that shows up under weird off-distribution conditions and then treating it as a character flaw in ordinary life. Have the blurb brag about "stress-testing under adversarial perturbation" or the like.
- The opposite of a fixed pattern isn't obviously freedom. Include at least one item or level where "flexibility" cashes out as random variation (a "disrupt yourself", "shake up your morning" item), and at least one where it cashes out as a much longer list of pre-loaded patterns, so a reader notices the instrument can't tell the difference between being free and just having a bigger repertoire.
- The maturity levels should climb toward something self-exempting or self-undermining. Best version: Level 5 "Adaptive" is defined as having internalised the audit so completely that you now run it automatically — i.e. the highest grade on the flexibility scale is being perfectly rigid about the flexibility scale. That's the closing beat, don't soften it.
- Somewhere, maybe a footnote or sidebar, let the wasp off the hook in a way that still damns the firm: the routine is cheap, fast and works, and a creature that re-plans from scratch every time it hits a snag never finishes anything. The consultant should present this as an objection they've already handled, without noticing it's fatal.
- Feel free to have the firm retro-fit "sphexishness" into a backronym — very on-brand for the genre, and funny to anyone who knows the word comes from a wasp.

Target is the practice, not a person: what's being sent up is the idea that rigidity is a measurable vice and that more flexibility is always more freedom. For orientation (don't put this in the piece): "sphexishness" is Dennett's word, and the case behind it is the digger wasp that keeps going back to inspect its burrow every time someone nudges the prey it parked at the entrance. Don't cite anything or name-check sources in the text, and don't build jokes that need the reader to have read a specific book — someone who's never heard of either should still find it funny, and someone who has should feel seen.
