My decision theory reading group has been going round in circles for about a year on money pump arguments and I want a diagnosis, not a verdict.

Here's the core case. Take an agent with cyclic strict preferences over three things: they prefer B to A, C to B, and A to C. A would-be exploiter who knows this and owns the objects can sell them B for A plus a dollar, then C for B plus a dollar, then A for C plus a dollar, and the agent ends up holding what they started with and three dollars poorer, having accepted every trade by their own lights. Everybody in the group agrees on the mechanics. What we can't settle is what the pump shows.

Camp one: it shows cyclic preferences are irrational, full stop. A preference structure that guarantees you end up materially worse off and back where you began, no matter what else is true, isn't unlucky, it's incoherent — there's nothing it's for you to be maximizing.

Camp two: it shows nothing about coherence at all. The agent loses only if they choose myopically at each node and can't bind themselves. Give them a commitment device, or just let them be the kind of agent that adopts plans and sticks to them, and the pump never gets off the ground. And here's the move I keep making and nobody answers properly: we don't call hyperbolic discounters incoherent. We say their preferences shift over time, that being unable to commit is costly for them, and that they'd pay for the ability to bind themselves. Cycling looks exactly like that — an expensive preference structure to have, not a logical or conceptual fault. So why does one get labelled 'irrational' and the other get labelled 'expensive'?

The comeback I get is that it's different in kind: at a single time the cyclic agent prefers A to B, B to C and C to A — one agent, one moment, one ordering that contradicts itself — whereas the hyperbolic discounter's inconsistency is spread across times and each momentary ordering is perfectly consistent. My reply is that this just relocates the question.

So, my working hypothesis: the two camps are using 'irrational' for two different objects — incoherence of a preference relation at a time versus predictable self-defeat of a policy over time — and the money pump looks decisive only because it slides between the two. Same shape in the sequential-choice case that got us started: sophisticated choosers who plan on the assumption that they'll maximize at each later node do worse than resolute ones who bind themselves to the ex ante best plan, and the fight there is identical — is the resolute chooser being rational (executing the plan they had most reason to adopt) or irrational (at the final node knowingly picking what they now judge worse)?

Am I right that this is a disagreement about the unit of evaluation, or is something else doing the work? Some candidates I can't adjudicate: a hidden formal question about what resolute choice even requires — can a resolute chooser be modeled without contradiction if the plan has to survive its own re-evaluation at every node? A hidden question about the exploiter — does the argument need an exploiter who knows your preferences, who can make arbitrarily many offers, and who acts for free, so that if each offer costs something and the total extractable amount is bounded and small the 'exploitation' reading quietly collapses? Or a disagreement about which cases count as data: is the relevant datum the single agent who gets pumped, or the class of agents who never do because they plan?

What I want from you: tell me what kind of disagreement this is — verbal, substantive, two questions under one name, or a real but buried formal or empirical question — arguing from what each side actually commits to rather than surveying views. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a formal result about commitment or sequential choice, a change of question, or an argument that nothing should be expected to move it and why. And please say how confident you are in the diagnosis overall and flag the one bit you're least sure of.
