I've just come out of a long, circular argument with a friend who does volunteer work for an animal-welfare charity, and I can't reconstruct where our disagreement actually lived. Her position was that brain size is morally irrelevant: what matters is what a being can suffer and enjoy, and if a shrimp turned out to have as rich a hedonic life as a cow, then a shrimp counts for exactly as much, with every fact about neural tissue demoted to mere evidence and never to a reason. I kept finding myself wanting to say that the sheer amount of organised neural machinery has to do some work — that a being able to hold a hundred simultaneous discriminations in experience at a time is undergoing something a being with room for one is not, or that a system processing the world at a much finer temporal grain packs more experience into a minute than one with a coarse grain — and she answered that both moves smuggle in a proxy and dress it up as a principle. What finished the evening was a case I raised about a human at the small end of the normal range of brain volume, or a child who had an entire cerebral hemisphere removed in infancy and grew up with roughly ordinary mental life: she granted that the difference does no moral work there, while still wanting neuron counts to matter across species, and neither of us could say why those two concessions were compatible.

What I want from you is a taxonomy of the positions on whether and how brain size is morally relevant, built as a branching tree in which every internal node is a concrete question or case and every leaf is a position, so that someone is sorted by what they say about cases rather than by which label they like. Please build it from the logical space first and put the names on afterwards: roughly eight branch questions and eight to ten leaves, with each leaf carrying a short descriptive name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. The distinctions I want doing the cutting are at least these: which quantity is in play (raw mass, total neuron count, ratio to body mass, neurons in association rather than sensory tissue, synapse count, or a functional capacity such as synchronic discriminative bandwidth); whether the quantity is evidential, constitutive, or irrelevant; whether relevance is scalar or a threshold matter of counting at all; whether it operates through intensity of valence or through the number of distinct experiences per unit of time; whether it is symmetric across moral patients and moral agents; and whether, even if the quantity is strictly irrelevant to the truth, it may still be the right thing to use under uncertainty because it is measurable and legible to institutions. The cases should include the ones above plus at least these, all phrased hypothetically so that nothing turns on a specific empirical finding: an animal with three times as many association neurons as a human and no language or long-term planning; two beings indistinguishable in behaviour and in every report they could give but differing a hundredfold in neural substrate, one running on redundant and wasteful hardware; a very large brain that is almost entirely a single sensory map; a chicken against a shrimp; and two artificial systems, one with a hundred times the parameters and a thousand times the training compute of the other, where my friend and I could not agree even on whether the question was well formed.

A few constraints on form. Since I'll be running your answer on a machine with no internet access, please don't cite, name, or date any published work, and don't appeal to how the literature has actually come out — coin descriptive labels for the positions instead of attributing them. No node may be phrased as a request for a view's name or as a definitional question about a quantity; each should be answerable by a thoughtful person who has never thought about this before. Where a combination of answers is unoccupied, say so and diagnose briefly whether it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored, and where two leaves survive every question you can think of, flag them as inseparable rather than inventing a distinction to pull them apart. The audience is a mixed reading group of moral philosophers and one neuroscientist, so the prose should stay non-technical outside the neuroanatomy itself, and I'd like it to work as a handout someone could fill in during the session.
