I convene a small working group on the governance of risky interventions, and two of its members have spent a year arguing past each other about one concrete case: whether a single research team may release, on its own authority, a self-spreading gene-drive construct designed to suppress a mosquito population that carries malaria. Roughly a dozen labs worldwide could each do this alone; a release would be effectively irreversible and impossible to recall; expert opinion on the ecological outcome is genuinely and persistently split.

The unilateralist's three strongest moves, as I'd reconstruct them: first, that the curse-style selection effect only bites under assumptions that fail here — the acting team holds local ecological knowledge and a track record that no distant panel can cheaply aggregate, so deference is not automatically epistemic improvement. Second, that requiring broad agreement imposes an unpriced cost: delay is itself lethal, and a consensus rule hands a veto to whoever is most cautious or least informed, so it selects for inaction in precisely the cases where acting matters most — a symmetric selection effect on omissions that the standard argument does not model. Third, that where the intervention can be re-engineered to be graded and partly reversible — split-drive designs that dilute out of a population, staged and spatially confined releases — the all-or-nothing structure that generates the selection effect is partly dissolved, making redesign rather than deference the right response.

The conformist's three strongest replies: first, that the selection effect does not require independent errors to bite, only that a single sufficient, irrevocable act makes the realised intervention disproportionately likely to be drawn from the worlds where it goes badly. Second, that error independence fails in the direction that hurts the unilateralist — shared training, funding and publication incentives produce correlated optimism — so the enthusiast's private judgment is the worst available filter even if a committee is not the best one. Third, that the symmetry claim is unearned without a baseline: the deaths from delay are bounded and measurable, the downside is not, and the comparison should be between acting now and acting after a multilateral process, not between acting now and never acting.

Please write the document they should have produced instead: an adversarial-collaboration statement. Give me a numbered section of claims both can sign — about the decision structure, about what the selection argument does and does not establish, about correlated error, about what would count as redesign — followed by a disagreements table in which each residual dispute gets a neutral formulation, each side's own preferred wording for its position, and, where the dispute is substantive rather than verbal, the crux plus a concrete test, case, or piece of evidence both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Flag explicitly any part of the disagreement you think is merely verbal, and say what separates it from the parts that are not.
