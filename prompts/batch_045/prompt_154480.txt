I lead the country-diagnostics team at a bilateral development agency. Three of us — myself and two economists with solid econometrics training but no historical expertise — write roughly forty country growth notes a year, about two weeks each, and every note feeds directly into lending decisions between fifty and three hundred million dollars. Each one has to be signed off by a regional director who is openly skeptical of causal stories and who now requires us to state, in writing, why we believe one cause rather than another. The decisions are not academic: they are whether to attach a governance condition to a loan, whether to fund a transport corridor instead of a judicial reform program, whether to tell a government that its growth problem is institutional. So what I need from you is a decision procedure my team can actually run, not an essay about the state of the debate.

The candidates we argue over are the usual ones — colonial legal origin, disease ecology and the disease environment inherited from the past, landlockedness and distance from the coast, terms of trade and resource rents, culture, religion and social trust, institutional quality — and the difficulty is that none of our decisions is about which of them is true in general. It is about one country, over ten to fifteen years, with money attached. I want the procedure to be explicit about the mismatch between the horizon of the cause and the horizon of the decision: a cause that explains levels of income over five centuries may say very little about what a loan can change in fifteen years, and a cause that explains a growth episode may not explain the level.

Over two years we have accumulated five loose tests that we apply inconsistently, and I want you to decide which of them survive, in what order they should be run, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than evidence. The tests, as we use them now: one, for any claim that cause C explains outcome Y in country X, name a country that had C without Y and a country that had Y without C, and say whether the two are matched on the other candidate causes — counting effective independent cases rather than nominal ones, so that countries sharing a colonial origin, a legal family, or a border are not counted as separate confirmations; if no matched pair can be named, cap the claim at consistent with. Two, run the story backwards: using only what was knowable about X twenty-five years earlier, would this story have ranked X's subsequent trajectory as likely, or does it depend on a fact that only became visible after the fact. Three, say whether C and the competing cause D are rivals or links in a chain, since if D operates through C then the statistical strength of C does not refute D. Four, name the range of contexts in which C's effect was actually observed and the distance from that range to X, state the discount being applied for the distance, and flag the claim as extrapolation when the distance is large. Five, check whether the measure of C was itself constructed partly from income or growth, as expert-coded perceptions of governance frequently are, and treat a contaminated measure as unusable for the purpose rather than merely imprecise.

Then run the procedure on three cases. The first is a landlocked, malaria-burdened country whose income per head has barely moved in forty years and which is now requesting a public financial management reform loan premised on the claim that institutions are the binding constraint. The second is a coastal country that opened export processing zones twenty-five years ago and has grown steadily since, and whose government now wants us to fund a replication of the policy in two more cities. The third is the one I will not like: a judicial strengthening and land titling program my own unit has already announced in a country where our previous notes attributed recent growth to institutional quality, and where the program's approval documents rest on that attribution. Give me the answer the procedure yields on all three even where it undercuts us, and say plainly in the third case which part of the decision then belongs to a person, and on what grounds a person could still choose to proceed.

Please also state what the procedure cannot do, and mark the points where judgment cannot be eliminated rather than smoothing them over. Do not cite papers, authors, or datasets: I do not want a reading list, and I would rather you not reconstruct references from memory. Where a step needs an empirical number, tell me how to get one instead of supplying one you are not sure of. Write it as continuous prose, the way one would write a long email to a colleague — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables; if you need to order the steps, number them inside the sentences rather than with a list — and aim for somewhere between twelve hundred and fifteen hundred words.
