I teach a first-year seminar on moral psychology, and next term I want to pair Sophocles' Antigone with moral foundations theory as it is usually presented: the five foundations most people learn first (care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity), the liberty foundation that was appended to them, the individualizing-versus-binding split, and the headline claim that left and right differ less in how much they moralize than in which foundations they moralize with. What I need from you is a reading of the play.

The reading I want tested is this. I want to argue that the tragedy is not, in the first instance, a disagreement about how much weight a given foundation should carry, but a case of what I am calling foundation-blindness: Creon cannot register Antigone's piety toward her dead brother as a moral reason at all and hears it only as subversion and betrayal, while Antigone cannot register his concern with authority and the ordering of the city as a moral reason and hears only impiety and the bullying of a tyrant, so that each concludes that the other is immoral rather than moral-but-differently-tuned. Take that claim through the play and tell me whether it holds: the burial and the dust thrown over the corpse, Creon's language about the city and the ship of state, the appeal to unwritten and everlasting laws, Haemon's intervention, the pollution and the birds and the altars, Tiresias, and Creon's collapse at the end. Where does the reading illuminate a scene, and where does the play refuse it? I especially want to know whether Sophocles gives either character any genuine access to the other's moral perception, or whether he deliberately withholds it, and what follows either way.

There are two complications I want handled honestly rather than smoothed over. The first is that if I bring the liberty foundation into the reading, the whole thing changes shape: Antigone becomes a figure of resistance to domination and Creon the oppressor, which is the reading my students will reach on their own in about five minutes and which turns the play into something comfortable rather than tragic. Does the theory license that reading, or is the fact that the same theory comfortably generates both a sign that its list of foundations is underdetermined by its own evidence and a little too easy to extend? The second is that the theory's rhetoric leans toward a kind of moral-epistemological breadth: a moral outlook that runs on more of the foundations is supposed to see more of the moral landscape, and the narrow outlook is supposed to be blinkered. Yet Antigone is a play in which two people, each running on a full-blooded set of foundations, destroy each other and everyone around them. So does the tragedy actually argue that breadth without a rule for which foundation yields is worse than narrowness? And if it does, is that something moral foundations theory can say, or something it structurally cannot say, given that it is a descriptive psychology of difference?

Finally, I want the normative question faced rather than deferred, because it is the question my students will actually ask. Grant whatever the empirical claim is about the asymmetry between left and right, and tell me how confident I should be about it. Does that psychology tell us anything whatsoever about who is right about burial rites, about loyalty to kin versus loyalty to the state, about authority and who owes what to whom? I want to know what the theory entitles us to conclude and what it does not. If the honest conclusion is deflationary, give me the deflationary one and show me what it costs; I would rather hand my students a smaller claim I can defend than a large one that collapses under a good question.

Please do not give me quotations from the play, a bibliography, or a reading list: work from the play as an educated reader remembers it, and from the theory as it is standardly stated. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you would write a long letter to a colleague who has read the play and knows the literature: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, nothing that would not survive being pasted into an email.
