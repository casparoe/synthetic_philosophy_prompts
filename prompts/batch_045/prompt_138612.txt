Hey — I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a ~1200 word magazine piece on the question of what emotions actually are. I've got the spine of an argument sketched out below and I'd really like comments on it before I start writting it up properly. Not looking for citations or a reading list (I'll do that legwork myself) — what I want is for someone to poke holes in the reasoning and tell me where it's weakest. Also tell me honestly if it's just boring.

Here's the draft argument.

There are basically three standard answers. Emotions are *judgments* (you're afraid of the dog because you judge it dangerous). Emotions are *perceptions* of value (you don't infer the danger, you just see it, the way you see the teeth). Or emotions are *feelings* — raw bodily states that don't really have content of their own, and the "aboutness" we attribute to them is a story we tell afterwards.

I want to float a fourth: emotions are error signals on a fast predictive model of what the current situation affords you. Your brain runs a quick model of your options — escape, freeze, approach, attack, submit — and an emotion is the flag that goes up when incoming information doesn't match what that model predicted, weighted by how much the mismatch matters for action. Its job isn't to describe the world, it's to interrupt whatever you were doing.

Three cases motivate this.

*The elevator.* The lift lurches, and there's a jolt of alarm that's there in a fraction of a second and gone within a couple of seconds once it settles, faster than any belief you could have formed and revised. But the jolt clearly has content: it's about the elevator, and about falling. The judgment theorist has to say there was a judgment you assented to and withdrew too fast to notice, which feels like a fudge. The feeling theorist has to say the content isn't really in the jolt at all. An error signal is fast and contentful by design, so it handles this one easily.

*The plastic spider.* You're creeped out, then someone tells you it's plastic, and you believe them immediately. The creepiness doesn't leave. What's worse for the judgment theorist: the residue still seems to be *about the spider*. Call this orphaned intentionality — emotional content with no judgment left to host it. On my view that's exactly what you'd expect: the slow belief-updating system has revised, but the fast model hasn't been retrained yet. (Side prediction, which I like: the lag should be systematically longer for ancestrally old triggers like spiders, snakes and heights than for modern ones like a gun or a carbon monoxide alarm, because the fast model was tuned on an ancestral error statistics.)

*Objectless dread.* The free-floating anxiety where nothing in particular seems to be the object. Judgment theorists say there's still a judgment with vague content. My view says the mismatch can sit at a high level of the model — something's off, can't say what — so no particular object is needed.

And here's the payoff, plus the bit I'm least sure about. If intensity tracks expected prediction error rather than probability-times-badness, then emotional intensity should peak at *intermediate* probability, near 50%, and a fully expected bad outcome should hit you less hard than a surprising one of the same size. I half believe this (dread before a likely-but-uncertain diagnosis vs. a certain one) but I also worry I've confused "surprising" with "uncertain."

The other thing I need is a way to stop this from collapsing into the perception theory, since perceptions are fast too, laggy too, and contentful too. My current move: perceptual systems are under pressure to track their distal causes, whereas the emotion system is *supposed* to be mis-calibrated, because the costs are asymmetric — a missed predator costs vastly more than a false alarm. Emotions are deliberately over-flagging signals; perceptions aren't. That also gives me a nice line for the ending: an anxiety disorder isn't a false belief that needs correcting, it's a gain knob turned up too high.

3. Is the ancestral-lag prediction testable in principle, or is it the kind of thing that sounds like an explanation but couldn't ever be falsified? I don't want to print pseudo-science.
4. Am I strawmanning the three views I set up, particularly the feeling theory?
5. Be blunt: is any of this interesting to a general reader, or is it a dud that only makes sense inside the debate?
