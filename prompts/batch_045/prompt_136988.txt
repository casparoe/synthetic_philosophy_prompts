I build and maintain backend services for online games, and I've never studied philosophy, so I don't have vocabulary for this beyond what the systems force on me. I keep going back and forth on what, if anything, is real about the things we store, and on when something that happens to a player counts as something being done to them. Here are my gut verdicts on cases I've actually dealt with.

A duping bug gives another player a second copy of a mount that was supposed to be unique; I keep mine. I don't think I've been harmed, and I don't think my mount stopped being the one I earned — though I also can't say which of the two copies is mine in any deep sense, and I'd be annoyed if the company deleted mine and left the duplicate.

Someone social-engineers their way into my account and transfers away items I spent about two hundred hours earning, in a way that can't be reversed. That's a real harm, and I think the company owes me something.

Same theft, except the studio rolls the database back to the previous nightly snapshot and the items reappear. Here I want to say the harm has been undone: nothing to compensate, nothing to make up for. This is the verdict I'm least sure of, because if someone took my laptop and the police returned it intact a day later, I'd still say I'd been wronged — the scare and the lost day don't vanish.

A stranger uses only mechanics the game allows to wall my avatar into a sealed box and leaves me there for six hours. Nothing of mine is deleted or altered. I still count it as a genuine harm.

Another player kills my character in a zone where the rules explicitly permit it and where I clicked through a warning saying so. That's a real loss to me, a painful one, but I don't think I've been wronged.

My single-player save file on my own disk, which no one else can read or write: losing it would upset me badly, but I resist saying the character in it is real in the way an object in a shared world is.

A stretch of procedurally generated terrain that the generator function would produce but that no client has ever rendered: I want to say it doesn't exist, and I also want to say that if a player walked there they would discover it rather than create it.

My tentative principle is in two independent parts. An object is real just in case its state lives in an authoritative shared store that more than one agent can read and write. A harm is real just in case it sets back someone's interests, full stop — and the two questions have nothing to do with each other. I can't make that principle do the work, though. It needs something extra about consenting to a rule system to say yes to the boxing case and no to the PvP kill, and if it takes the setback-of-interests clause seriously it should say the rollback case is still a harm, which is the one verdict I'd most like to keep.

What principle, or small set of principles, captures this pattern? I'd like it stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new ticket without guessing, with the case that motivates each clause. I also want to know honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, including whether the rollback one is just a feeling I should abandon. And please say what it predicts about two cases I haven't listed: the same theft carried out in a game whose currency is officially exchangeable for real money, and a studio shutting a world down permanently at end of life and deleting the database.
