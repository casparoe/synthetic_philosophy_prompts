You're a professional philosopher who works on signalling theory and social conventions — I want you to approach this as someone who's spent years on both the biology side (the handicap principle, costly signalling, honest-vs-fake signals) and the social-ontology side (how conventions and symbolic objects get their content). Before you commit to anything, spend a bit of time brainstorming the space of options out loud, then settle on the answer you actually think is right, and don't smooth over the parts where you'd have to guess.

Here's what I'm chewing on. Take the familiar engagement-ring norm — the one the diamond cartel's mid-century marketing is supposed to have cooked up: the stone is supposed to cost a serious amount, it's supposed to be a mined diamond rather than a lab-grown one even though the lab stuff is basically the same material and far cheaper, and it's resale value collapses the moment you leave the shop, so the money really is burned rather than just moved into another asset. I keep trying to read that as a costly signal and it half works and half doesn't. The price is private — nobody at the party knows what the thing cost — so who is the audience that's supposed to be reading the signal, and what are they reading off it if not the number? The most romantic ring of all is the grandmother's ring that cost the giver nothing, which looks like it breaks the requirement that the cost be borne by the signaller. And then there's the strangest bit: people react to a lab-grown stone with something close to suspicion, as though a cheap physically identical ring weren't merely less impressive but were making a false claim, like a bad photocopy of a signature rather than a modest version of one.

So what does the practice actually presuppose? I don't want "it's costly signalling" as the answer — I want the implicit theory of communication and commitment reconstructed properly. Does the practice strictly require the differential-cost story, or would a story where the ring is a hostage or bond posted with the recipient do the same work (I think in a lot of places the law treats it as different from an ordinary gift, though I might be misremembering that)? Are those two stories the same theory in different vocabulary, or do they come apart somewhere I could in principle check? Where does the practice's content outrun signalling theory entirely: is the objection to the lab stone a signal-theoretic objection at all, or is it the logic of a relic, where the causal history of the object is doing the work and its cost is beside the point? How much of the practice is underdetermined — could a plain status-goods view, or a view on which the ring partly constitutes the relationship rather than evidencing anything, license exactly the same norms? And tell me what would concretely have to change if we dropped the signalling presupposition for one of those rivals: not "people would stop buying diamonds," but which specific feature of the norm would go and which would stay.

Also, and this matters more than the rest: separate clearly what's settled among people who actually work on this from what's a live dispute, and mark where you yourself are unsure. I can't tell the difference when I read about this stuff on my own and I really don't want a contested reading handed to me as the consensus. And please write the whole thing as continuous prose, like an email to a friend — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
