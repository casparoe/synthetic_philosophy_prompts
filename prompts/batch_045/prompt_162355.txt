I'm a research engineer on the safety team at a mid-sized AI lab, and in ten days I present to our internal deployment review board — the body that votes, at the end of the session, on whether a system built entirely from ordinary current methods (large-scale pretraining, preference-learning post-training, rejection-sampling fine-tuning, tool use, and a wrapper of system prompt, retrieval and runtime monitors) may ship into a customer-facing product. Twenty minutes of talk, forty minutes of questions.

My thesis: for systems built by current methods, alignment should stop being treated as a property of the trained model and become a property of the deployed scaffold, certified as a reliability contract rather than defended as a doctrine. Three moves support it.

First, a diagnostic claim: the failures we actually see are competence failures, not incentive failures. The model generalizes an under-specified objective into regions we would not endorse, or degrades in distribution tails we never sampled — and it does this visibly, inconsistently and in the open, rather than concealing it the way something pursuing a cross-situational goal of its own would.

Second, an ontological claim: a competence failure is relational. It is a fact about a system in an environment, not about a matrix of weights. So the model should be treated as an untrusted but not adversarial component, characterized only relative to a maintained, deployment-representative evaluation suite and to monitors with stated error budgets, and re-validated whenever the scaffold changes. Because the object of certification is the scaffold, the safety case does not port between deployments; it expires.

Third, a meta-claim about where the difficulty lives: non-adversariality is the premise that licenses us to skip control-theoretic machinery, and it is an empirical premise with a shelf life, to be re-tested at each capability jump rather than settled once. Nothing here requires a new theory of value or of agency. The binding constraints are infrastructural — building suites that track a moving deployment distribution without themselves becoming the training target — and institutional: someone has to have authority to block a ship, and written guarantees create legal exposure. That last part is where I expect the real fight.

The room is five kinds of person. Four or five senior capabilities and post-training researchers who already run evals, measure everything, and treat anything that sounds like philosophy as a tax on velocity. Two people from a small internal team that takes scheming, situational awareness and sandbagging seriously and will hear "untrusted but not adversarial" as the conclusion I need rather than a premise I get. One external member — an ethicist from the board's community panel — who will want to know whose values are in the system prompt, who the affected third parties are, and whether meeting our own numbers is supposed to answer whether the thing should exist. Our in-house regulatory counsel, who will care that a written reliability guarantee is discoverable, reads to customers as a warranty, and makes drift our liability. And a chair from platform operations who decides whether gating is enforceable at all and will ask what changes on Monday.

Tell me what I should concede in the first two slides before anyone asks, which single reply has to land or the proposal dies, which of these objections I would not get from the usual online alignment-discussion crowd, and include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
