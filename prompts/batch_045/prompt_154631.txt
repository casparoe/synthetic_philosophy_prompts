I teach an upper-level seminar on philosophies of history, and I want to close the term by using Tolkien's The Lord of the Rings as a case rather than assigning yet another excerpt from Spengler or Toynbee. The obstacle is that the book seems to run three incompatible shapes of history at once. At the scale of kinds of being it is a decline narrative: the Elves are diminishing, their Rings will fail, the Third Age ends with their departure, and the Elves' talk of the long defeat suggests a process that cannot be reversed, only prolonged. At the political scale it is a restoration narrative: a lost kingship returns, the two successor realms are reunited, and the Stewards' long interim closes. At the material scale it looks like directional change of a modernizing kind: Saruman's mechanized Isengard, the felling of the forests, and then the Scouring of the Shire, which repairs damage that a restoration of kings could not have repaired. I want a reading that tells me what philosophy of history the novel actually presupposes, whether its parts are consistent, and which of the three scales Tolkien makes load-bearing.

Two distinctions from my own lectures should structure the answer. First, I separate three ways a cycle can behave: closed recurrence, in which the same sequence of phases returns with no inherited residue; ratcheted recurrence, in which each turn inherits the previous turn's ruins, techniques, languages, and population floors, so that the cycle travels even while it repeats; and damped recurrence, in which the amplitude shrinks toward a steady end-state, closer to Spengler's late phase of a culture than to a collapse. Second, I insist that cyclical claims are scale-relative: Polybius and Ibn Khaldun cycle regime types and dynastic solidarity over generations, whereas Spengler cycstals whole cultures as organisms and Toynbee works with civilizations meeting challenges through a creative minority. A narrative with a three-thousand-year backstory can be cyclical at one scale and irreversible at another without self-contradiction, and I want to know where each of the three shapes is doing real work in the novel. Note also that I distinguish directional history from ameliorative history: a story can be one-way without being progressive, since it may narrow, fade, or exhaust rather than improve.

The critical question I care about is what I call the restoration trap: any restoration that succeeds must import the capacities that ended what it restores, so it buys permanence by becoming the very thing that will next fall. Is Aragorn's Reunited Kingdom shown as buying into that trap, or as escaping it? And if it escapes, is the escape achieved by changing the unit rather than the regime: the Shire as a small, low-technology, self-regulating order that is simply not the kind of thing that rises and falls; the Elves departing the circles of the world while Men, whose gift is mortality, inherit a history they do not repeat? I would also like the reading to take seriously the possibility that the novel closes off recurrence rather than undergoing it: with the One Ring destroyed, certain kinds of power, being, and enchantment cannot come back, which makes the Fourth Age a contracted space of possibilities in a way no cyclical scheme predicts.

Please also say where the reading fails. Tolkien's history is providential rather than organismic, his own term eucatastrophe marks an irruption that no phase-scheme allows, the central causal factor is a person's will rather than a law of growth and decay, and he was notoriously hostile to reading his work as allegory. Tell me how much of the cyclical-and-linear apparatus survives those resistances, which three or four scenes you would put on a syllabus as evidence, and what a philosopher of history should think the novel gets right that the theoretical literature does not. Paraphrase rather than quoting from memory, and do not give me a bibliography or citations; I want the reading, not the sources.
