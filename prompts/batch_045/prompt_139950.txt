I'm in a philosophy of psychiatry reading group (about half clinicians, two philosophers, me somewhere in between) and we did Hacking's "looping effects of human kinds" paper a few weeks back, and I've been arguing about it with a friend who's a clinical psychologist ever since. Her line is that looping effects just make psychiatric kinds *moving targets*: annoying if you want stable prevalence estimates over time or a clean replication of an old study, but not a deep threat to validity, because you can in principle re-validate your instruments each time the kind drifts. My hunch is that something worse is going on with at least some categories — that the feedback doesn't merely move the target but is partly constitutive of what it is to be an instance of the kind, so that for those categories there's no target sitting there, independent of the classification, for the criteria to be more or less accurate about. But I can't get the two ideas apart cleanly, and I suspect I'm conflating "the extension keeps changing" with "there's no fact of the matter about the boundary."

So: explain the conceptual difference I'm groping at. Specifically, I want to understand what's at stake in the step from *instability* (the set of people who fall under the category, or how they present, shifts in response to the classification) to something like *co-constitution* (what it is to be an instance of the category is itself partly shaped by the classification, so that talk of the "real" prevalence or of the criteria carving nature at the joints stops having a clear sense). Is that second claim actually distinct from the first, or does it collapse back into it once you're careful? And does it matter for this that looping seems to work through at least three seperate channels — people's presentations changing, who shows up in the clinic and how they describe themselves, and the experts' own concepts revisions feeding back into the next edition of the manual — or is one of those doing all the real work? Hacking's own examples (child abuse, homosexuality, teenage pregnancy, multiple personality) are all ones where the kind is picked out by self-report and social description, and I can't tell how much of his argument is supposed to carry over when the criteria are defintately not self-report-based.

Here's the bit where I need the most help, and where my friend and I invented our own vocabulary, so don't expect these terms to be standard. She proposed what she calls "sealed criteria": define a category only over signals the subject can't introspect and can't steer — actigraphy-measured sleep fragmentation, inflammatory markers, speech-timing features — on the grounds that if there's no channel from the classification back to the measurement, there's no loop. I pointed out that the loop just relocates instead of vanishing: people titrate their behaviour to hit the marker (all-nighters before the actigraphy week, stimulants, deliberate sleep restriction) and clinicians' thresholds drift as the measured distribution shifts, so you've moved the feedback one level out rather than removed it. Her fallback was a "naivety clause": the diagnosis requires that the presentation not be substantially shaped by the subject's own knowledge of the category. I think that's either incoherent or much more committal than it looks — it seems to presuppose a presentation that the classification hasn't touched, i.e. exactly the pre-classificatory target whose existence was the thing in dispute — but I can't say exactly why, or whether the incoherence is fatal or just makes the clause a rough practical heuristic. There's also what I've started calling "loop-aversion": people who've read about looping and medicalization deliberately avoid the label, which changes presentations again in the opposite direction, and I don't know whether to treat that as a further order of the same effect or as something that could in principle settle the kind down. Please say which of these, if any, is a stable resting point, and what it would mean for a category to explicitly build its own looping into its criteria (a criterion that predicts and expects presentations to be shaped by diagnostic awareness) — is that self-fulfilling, self-defeating, or just honest? Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence — the actual claim about what looping effects do and don't threaten — then give me the reasoning under it; no preamble and don't restate my question back at me.
