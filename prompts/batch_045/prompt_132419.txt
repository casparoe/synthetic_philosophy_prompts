You are a philosopher of technology and science-and-technology-studies scholar who has published on values in design and has sat on both sides of the fence: you have argued that some technical arrangements carry political content in their very geometry, and you have also argued that nothing is political except within a social arrangement that produces, funds, maintains, and enforces it. You are meticulous about separating empirical claims from conceptual ones and you dislike documents that paper over disagreement with vague compromise language.

Before you draft anything, spend some working notes (not part of the deliverable) on three things: (a) the full space of sub-questions such that settling one of them would move at least one side, including the ones neither camp currently has on its agenda; (b) which of the apparent disagreements are really disagreements about the words politics, artifact, inhere, or design; and (c) which of the cases described below actually discriminate between the camps, and which would be dismissed by both as irrelevant. Discard any crux for which you cannot find a case both sides would accept as evidence. Only then write the document.

The task. Two camps have been talking past each other for about two decades on the question whether artifacts have politics. Call them the inherence camp: some artifacts, in virtue of their material or technical arrangement and independently of who wields them, require or strongly favour particular relations of authority, subordination, or exclusion, and this is a genuine political property of the thing. And the arrangement camp: an artifact has no politics on its own; what is political is the sociotechnical arrangement of which it is a part, including its production, funding, interpretation, maintenance, repair, regulation, and the possibilities of re-signification and repurposing. Assume an eighteen-month, good-faith adversarial collaboration between a committed member of each camp. Draft the joint statement they could both sign.

Use these four cases, which the collaboration designed together, as the tests. Keep them as described; do not substitute famous textbook examples.

1. The matched pair. Two irrigation pumps for a smallholders' cooperative, identical in measured output, fuel use, purchase price, and expected lifespan. Pump M is sealed, uses proprietary fittings, and can be recalibrated only by a licensed technician carrying a dongle. Pump O unbolts, ships with its calibration curve printed in the manual, and can be retuned by any member. Both are deployed across many villages. Question: is the difference between M and O itself a political difference, or does it become one only given the surrounding arrangement?
2. The orphan artifact. A company town's housing courtyard built by a paternalistic mining company: every entrance faces inward, and the single gate is lockable from the manager's office. The mine closes, the company dissolves, the buildings are sold to a self-managing housing cooperative with no manager and no gatekeeper. Question: does the courtyard still carry its politics, or has it become merely inconvenient?
3. The omission. A benefits portal that accepts only uploads in one format, offers no paper or in-person route, and writes no log of rejected submissions. Nothing was added; something was left out. Question: can an omission be political in the same way a feature can, and does the answer differ between the two camps?
4. What travels. A physical constraint that excludes (a stair-only entrance, a low clearance on a road, a minimum order size) versus a constraint implemented in code (a rate limit, a licence check that expires, a device that stops working when a server stops answering). Which kind, if either, keeps its political effect when the artifact is moved unchanged into a society with very different politics?

Build the document on this scaffolding, and use it if you think it is right: require that any comparison between two designs hold measurable function fixed, so that the debate does not collapse into a tradeoff both camps agree is ordinary politics; and distinguish at least these four questions, which the literature routinely conflates — the causal question of whether an artifact brought about a political effect, the modal question of whether any functionally equivalent alternative would have done so, the normative question of who is responsible and whether designers bear a distinctive responsibility, and the semiotic question of whether the artifact is read as political.

Deliverable, in this order.

1. A short scope note: what the document is about, what it explicitly sets aside.
2. A numbered section of agreed claims. Tag every claim with its epistemic status: settled among people who actually work on this; majority view but genuinely contested; or accepted by one side only for the sake of getting a signed document. Do not let a contested claim pass as consensus, and do not pad the list with claims so weak that no one could deny them.
3. A disagreements table, one row per crux, with columns: the crux; the inherence camp's formulation in wording its own side would accept; the arrangement camp's formulation likewise; the discriminating case from the list above (name the case); what each side pre-registers as its prediction about that case, given that neither can run the study; and what each side would count as evidence that would move it. I expect cruxes at least about the modal strength of the inherence claim, about where the artifact ends and the arrangement begins and whether that boundary can be drawn without begging the question, about who bears the burden of proof on counterfactual parity, about omissions, and about whether there is anything left for a distinct ethics of design if the arrangement camp is right. Add cruxes of your own if they survive your screening step; say explicitly if you judge one of the disagreements above to be verbal, and then state the substantive residue that remains once the words are disambiguated.
4. A short closing section on the limits of the agreement: one claim you expect a third party — an engineer who thinks the word politics is being stretched past usefulness, or a theorist who thinks the whole framing mislocates agency — would reject outright, and where each camp would concede its own case is weakest.

Constraints. No citations, bibliographies, or references to specific authors, papers, or URLs: this has to stand on argument alone. Define any term you introduce rather than relying on a shared technical vocabulary. Each table cell at most two sentences. Total under 1400 words. Do not adjudicate; this is a document both parties sign, not a verdict.
