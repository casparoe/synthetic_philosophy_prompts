The situation: one of the regulars in our department's decision-theory reading group — a game theorist by training, and an unrepentant defender of backward induction — has returned a verdict in each of the following seven cases. In every case the players are risk-neutral money-maximizers, the payoff table is common knowledge, and (except where noted) there is common knowledge of that, common knowledge of rationality, no communication, no commitment device, no pre-play correlation, and no exogenous uncertainty about types.

(a) The standard six-node centipede. Player 1 moves at odd nodes, Player 2 at even nodes; payoffs (P1, P2) if the mover takes are: node 1 (1, 0), node 2 (0, 2), node 3 (3, 1), node 4 (2, 4), node 5 (5, 3), node 6 (4, 6); if everyone passes, (7, 5). His verdict: take at the first node.

(b) The same game, except that each player is truthfully and privately told his opponent's type, and it is common knowledge that with probability 1% a player is an always-passer who passes at every node. Verdict: pass at the first and second nodes, plan to take in the last two or three.

(c) The same game, except that each player is told, and knows the other is told, that his opponent is a verified perfect copy of his own decision procedure, running on separate hardware, with no communication: whatever he would choose at his own nodes, the copy chooses at hers. Verdict: pass at every node.

(d) A hundred-round prisoners' dilemma with per-round payoffs of (2, 2) for mutual cooperation, (1, 1) for mutual defection, (3, 0) and (0, 3) for unilateral defection. Verdict: defect in round 1.

(e) The same hundred-round game, except that with 1% probability the opponent is a tit-for-tat player. Verdict: cooperate through most of the game, defect in the last few rounds.

(f) The chain-store set-up: an incumbent faces twenty potential entrants in sequence, one per period, with the number of periods known in advance. Verdict: accommodate the first entrant.

(g) The six-node centipede of case (a), except that each player first writes down a complete contingent plan — what to do at each of the nodes he might reach — and hands it to a referee, who then plays the two plans against each other; no one can revise. Verdict: commit to passing at every node.

Next week's case is a centipede with the same structure, the same number of nodes, and exactly the same stipulations as case (a); only the numbers change. Payoffs (P1, P2) if the mover takes: node 1 (1.00, 0.00), node 2 (0.99, 2.00), node 3 (3.00, 1.99), node 4 (2.99, 4.00), node 5 (5.00, 3.99), node 6 (3.99, 6.00); if everyone passes, (7.00, 5.98). So each player's cost of being preempted one node later is a single cent, while reaching the end of the game is worth six dollars more than taking at the first node.
