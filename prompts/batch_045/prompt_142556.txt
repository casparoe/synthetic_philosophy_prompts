I spent most of last night in an argument with an old friend about insect sentience, and I have not been able to let it go. He works near enough to effective altruism to be fluent in its habits of mind, and he is a very competent populariser of other people's arguments, which is part of why he is difficult to argue with: everything he said sounded as though it must already be someone's considered view. I came away both more persuaded than I expected to be and, in places, suspicious that I had been handed a conclusion that the argument does not actually support. I would like to know which is which.

The argument was the one that runs from the sheer number of insects to the claim that their suffering should dominate any expected-value calculation, even granting that the probability that any individual insect is sentient is low. As nearly as I can reconstruct it, my friend's summary went like this:

"Insects are the overwhelming majority of animals. There are something like ten quintillion of them alive at any moment, and the number born and killed each year is larger still, because their lives are short and their populations turn over fast. Now put the probability that a beetle or an aphid has any felt experience at all at one percent — a sceptical number, since they have nothing remotely like our nervous system, but not zero. One percent of ten quintillion is a hundred quadrillion sentient beings, and their combined suffering swamps every other moral problem on the planet. That is what makes the scale argument immune to scepticism: doubt about insect minds is already priced in, and the numbers are so vast that no plausible downward revision of the probability makes the problem go away. The same arithmetic settles what we should do, because expected value is expected value: a dollar spent reducing insect suffering does more good than a dollar spent almost anywhere else, and the fact that we cannot say which insects, if any, are sentient, or whether their lives are on balance good or bad, is a reason for more research rather than for doing nothing."

What I balked at, without being able to say why precisely, was the move from a multiplication to a verdict. It seems to me that the argument needs a great deal more than numbers: it needs some assumption about how much experience, if any, an insect life contains; about whether the insects we might affect have lives that are on balance worth living; and about whether the things we could actually do would reduce or merely relocate the total. But my friend waved this off as the usual search for an excuse.

I also raised something he had not encountered and that neither of us could develop. If ants, termites or bees are sentient at all, there is at least a case for treating the colony rather than the individual as the unit of moral concern, since a eusocial colony thermoregulates, forages, defends itself and reproduces as a coordinated whole and looks more like an organism than like a population. If that is right, the headline number should be divided by something like the number of individuals per colony. But it also seemed to me that the point might cut the other way: a colony may have a longer-lived and more unified experiential profile than any of its members, which would raise the stakes per unit rather than lower them. My friend replied that the arithmetic is indifferent to how you carve up the units so long as the total quantity of experience is conserved. I am not sure either of us knew what we were talking about.

So I would like an accuracy audit I can send back to him. Please go through the summary claim by claim and tell me what someone who has genuinely thought about invertebrate sentience and decision-making under uncertainty would accept as stated, what is a defendable simplification, what is an overstatement, and what a defender of the argument would say has been misrepresented — in particular whether the sceptical objections he brushes past (imprecise priors, insensitivity of the conclusion to the probability, the worry that this is a Pascal's-mugging in a lab coat, the possibility that our interventions increase rather than reduce total insect-years) really are as harmless as he makes out, or whether they are live problems with only partial replies. Please also say what you think of the colony question, and whether it is genuinely irrelevant to the arithmetic.

Finally: once the summary has been corrected, does his practical conclusion survive? I would rather you argued the matter on its merits than told me who has written about it. I do not want to win the argument; I want to know whether the strong version of the claim is one I should believe, or whether the honest version supports something narrower and less exciting, and if so what that is.
