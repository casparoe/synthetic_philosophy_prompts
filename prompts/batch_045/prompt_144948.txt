So I'm giving the eulogy on Saturday for Marguerite, who was my undergrad philosophy teacher and then, for thirty years, the friend I sent every draft to. You maybe know the kind of thing she worked on: whether a book could be conscious — the old Einstein's-brain-in-a-jar move where you imagine every state of a mind written out on pages, every sentence it could ever produce, all there in the right order, and then ask why there's still nobody home. She said no, flatly, and she used to get properly cross at funerals where someone says the person lives on in their books. She left a note saying nobody is to say that at hers, and I won't.

Which leaves me with five minutes for a room that's half her old colleagues and grad students, who'll here any weasel move a mile off, and half her family, her neighbours, and a few people who only came because they read my novels — and those people need something they can hold, not a tidy argument that she's simply gone.

The bit I actually want help with: the novel I've been failing to finish for three years runs on a trick — a book that contains, in it's own pages, a complete discription of someone reading it, every neural event specified, so the whole reading is already inside the covers. My own sense is that this is a cheat, that writing the reader down buys you nothing the plain book didn't have, but I never got to argue it with her. I'd like the speech to use that honestly, whatever you think it shows. No "in some sense", no "her voice is still with us", and nothing half the room could go home and repeat as the exact opposite of what she spent her life arguing. There's a reading from her essays after, so I don't need to quote her.
