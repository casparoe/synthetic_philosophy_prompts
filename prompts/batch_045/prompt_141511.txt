You are a decision theorist who has also spent years teaching game theory and rational choice to mixed audiences of philosophers, economists and computer scientists. Think carefully, and before you settle on a design, sketch two or three candidate skeletons for the session and tell me briefly why you rejected the ones you did not use. Here is what I need. I run a 90-minute seminar on rational choice for about eighteen students — roughly half philosophy, half economics and computer science — who have already met the prisoner's dilemma and Newcomb's problem but have never seen Hofstadter's Platonia setup (twenty people, one of whom may send a telegram for an enormous prize that is paid only if exactly one is sent) or the Luring Lottery he ran with Scientific American readers in the 1980s, where the prize was a fixed sum divided by the total number of entries and one entry was drawn at random. I have one room, index cards, envelopes, a hat, a whiteboard, and a single book voucher worth about forty pounds which I am willing to destroy in front of them — or, better, hand over to a cause I know this particular group dislikes — if the collective outcome is bad. I do not want any lecture before the debrief: the tension between the two lines of reasoning should show up in what the students actually do, and only get named afterwards. What I most want them to feel is that both of the following are compelling and that they cannot both be right: on the one hand, you might as well send the telegram, since your card cannot causally affect anyone else's card and sending costs nothing; on the other hand, whatever a rational person decides in this situation, all of them will decide, so in choosing you are effectively choosing for everyone, and the only way anyone gets anything is if you restrain yourself (or, more precisely, randomise with the right small probability).

My provisional plan, which you should improve or replace if it is mistaken: start with a silent, strictly simultaneous one-shot ballot in which each student marks send or abstain, and the voucher goes to a unique sender and is destroyed if there is more than one sender or none; then, when that fails and the group starts trying to organise itself, allow exactly three minutes of open talk and hold a second ballot, so that any temptation to defect from an agreement the group reaches appears on its own rather than being announced by me — tell me whether to permit cheap talk at all, and how to keep the second ballot honest. After that I want a Luring-Lottery-style phase scaled to eighteen people with numbers small enough that each additional entry's cost to everyone else is visible in one line of arithmetic on the board (a pot of points or of something edible, split by the total number of tickets claimed, with one ticket drawn at random), including whether to let them submit absurd numbers the way the magazine's readers did, and whether to run a second round after the arms race has become obvious. Then two wrinkles of my own that I would like you to pressure-test and, where the arithmetic matters, actually work out. First, an asymmetry variant: privately hand about a third of the room a sealed card saying that if they abstain they are guaranteed a smaller separate prize, while the rest get nothing for abstaining. This breaks the symmetry the superrational argument runs on, and I want the debrief to say what the argument then delivers — whether similarity has to be exact or merely sufficient, and what happens with agents who are, say, ninety per cent similar. Second, a device I invented: before a final ballot, announce a copy rule, under which with some probability q a student's card is discarded and replaced by a photocopy of a randomly chosen other student's card. At q = 0 this is ordinary Platonia; as q rises the correlation the superrationalists merely assert becomes literally causal, and restraint starts to look attractive. Give me the threshold q at which a student who expects a fraction r of the others to abstain should herself abstain, and tell me honestly what this device does and does not show: my worry is that it manufactures by mechanism the very dependence the real argument claims to get from logic alone, and so proves less than it appears to.

For the debrief I want the specific moments mapped onto the specific ideas: the dominant-strategy pull as causal decision theory, the symmetry move as evidential or functional decision theory with an extra premise about who counts as similar, Newcomb's problem as the anchor they already have, the one-over-N randomisation answer and why it is unstable once the agents are only partly similar or unknown to each other, and the runaway in the Luring phase as the many-person version of the same failure. My own hypothesis — argue with it if it is wrong — is that the most instructive moment will be the group's attempt to build an institution, a lottery or a public promise, and the collapse of that institution, and that superrationality will then look like a private substitute for coordination the group is not allowed to perform. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: the student who announces that everyone else should abstain because they are sending, the joker who sends for fun, the economists who defuse phase one by simply asserting dominance, the fact that with eighteen people the probability of exactly one sender is so low that failure may teach nothing at all, and sabotage entries in the Luring phase. Finally, be frank about what this activity cannot reproduce: the stakes are trivial where the original is a billion dollars, the session is one-shot with no shadow of the future, the similarity between real agents is partial and contested rather than exact, and no copy rule is ever really in force.
