ok so before I read any more proposed solutions I want the spec, because I think half the arguing in this area is people scoring theories against requirements nobody actually stated.

Setup so we don't talk past each other. The Pasadena game: fair coin, tossed until it first lands heads; if the first heads is on toss n you recieve (−1)^{n−1}·2^n/n, so +2, −2, +8/3, −4, +32/5 … with probabilities 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16 … The expectation comes out as the alternating harmonic series, which sums to about +0.69 but only conditionally, and the absolute expectation is the harmonic series, which diverges. So on the usual bookkeeping the expected value doesn't exist, and by Riemann rearrangement you can make the sum come out to whatever you like by reordering terms.

What I want is not "what's the ticket worth". It's: what does a rule for choosing have to deliver to count as adequate for gambles like this? And I want the purpose fixed up front, since I suspect a lot of the disagreement is people answering different questions. My purpose: a decision procedure for an actual agent — bounded, living through time, has to say yes or no to one ticket now, may get the chance to sell or hedge it partway through, does not get to survey infinitely many outcomes. Not a ranking of abstract gambles for an ideal agent. Tell me if that purpose is ill-posed.

Cases I think the list has to cover, with why I think each bites — add ones I've missed and drop any that are really just camp prejudice:

1. Truncation. Chop the game off at toss N; now expectation exists and is the Nth partial sum, which oscillates around 0.69 and converges to it. Does a theory have to be continuous with its own verdicts on the truncations, or is the infinite case allowed to come apart from the limit of the finite ones?
2. Sure shifts. Is Pasadena-plus-a-sure-pound strictly better than Pasadena? If a theory says "meh, indifferent", I think it's pumpable; say whether that's right and what the requirement really is.
3. Bundling and repetition. One ticket or two independent ones? And the long-run worry: since E|X| is infinite, the strong law doesn't apply, so nobody gets to justify a price by "you'll average out over many plays". Is that a desideratum (don't smuggle in frequentist support the maths doesn't give you) or is it fine to be silent on repeat play?
4. Redescription. Sell the same gamble to me as a rearranged series, or split into stages, or bundled with an unrelated gamble. Must the verdict be invariant? If yes, say what does the invariant-making — what feature of the game fixes the "real" order.
5. Mid-game revaluation, the one I care most about. Conditional on the first k tosses all being tails, the natural-order conditional expectation is roughly −0.61 after one tail, +0.77 after two, about −1.12 after three, and the swings keep doubling in size. So a natural-order agent's willingness to pay to keep or sell the ticket flip-flops with growing magnitude as the game goes on. Does an adequate theory owe us a verdict on what that agent may pay at each point, does it owe dynamic consistency / no money pump, and is "no money pump" here a genuine requirement or does it quietly presuppose the expectation-based pricing that's the thing in question? (My arithmetic is back-of-envelope; correct me if I've botched it.)
6. Bounded utility. If utility is bounded in money, the series converges absolutely and the problem evaporates. Is "handle the case where utility is genuinely unbounded" a requirement, or is dissolving it by bounding utility a legitimate answer?
7. Ordinary cases. Must the theory reproduce expected-utility verdicts wherever expectation does exist? Is that non-negotiable, and does it have to solve the St Petersburg game as well or is that allowed to be a separate problem?

Deliverable: the requirements list, each with the case or consideration behind it, marked non-negotiable vs preference-of-a-camp. Tell me which single requirement excludes the most candidate theories, and which pair you think can't be jointly satisfied — my guess is determinacy (every gamble gets a verdict) plus invariance under redescription, but make the case and name a different pair if that's not where the real conflict is. Also: people say paying any particular finite amount for the ticket feels arbitrary in a way that paying for a St Petersburg ticket doesn't. Is that a desideratum or a prejudice? A one-line-per-family scorecard at the end is fine — expectation-free dominance/structural views, nonstandard-expectation views, weak-expectation views, permissive-range views, bounded-utility views — but keep the weight on the criteria, that's the bit I can't do myself. And write it in British English please.
