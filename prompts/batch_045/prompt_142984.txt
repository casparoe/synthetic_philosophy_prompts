The cases:

1. My collaborator presented a draft of mine as partly his own. I thought it was carelessness until my friend Priya told me it was a real betrayal. 2. My friend Marcus told me that going around my colleague to the dean was disloyal. I believed him, apologized, and again couldn't reconstruct the reasoning. Verdict: fine — even modest and decent of me. 3. A colleague with a disability told me that the joke in the department meeting was demeaning. I still don't quite see it, and I accepted her verdict. Verdict: fine, and not merely fine — here deferring seems to be partly what respect consists in, and asking her to first make me understand feels like an imposition.

4. A philosopher friend I trust told me to quit a project because of how the data was collected. I quit. I can't reconstruct her argument and would be embarrassed to say in a meeting only that she told me to. Verdict: fine, but with a residue — I'd rather not have to answer for it publicly.

5. The teacher in my religious community said my relationship was sinful. I accepted it and ended the relationship. Verdict: not fine. Structurally this looks like case 4 — an authority I regard as reliable telling me an act of mine is wrong, and me deferring — but my reaction is the opposite.

6. As a line manager, I was told by HR that an engineer's remark counted as harassment and that suspending him was the right response. I deferred entirely and still couldn't define what made it harassment. Verdict: fine, perhaps even what I owed him and the team; trying to work it out for myself first would have been a way of stalling.

7. My friend told me I was furious at him and shouldn't trust my read on whether he'd wronged me. I set my judgment aside. Verdict: fine. But note this deference works by removing a verdict rather than installing one, and I don't see how any principle about transmitting understanding handles it.

8. A friend gave me the cost-per-life-saved figures for two charities, and I switched my donation. Verdict: fine, trivially — but I already believed I should do as much good as I can with the money, so arguably nothing moral was transmitted at all, and I suspect that's why some of my "fine" verdicts are cheap.

My best tentative principle is: deference to a moral verdict is fine exactly when the testifier has standing in the matter, roughly when they are among those the verdict is about or whom it will affect.
