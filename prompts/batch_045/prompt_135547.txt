I am in a graduate seminar on formal epistemology, and last week we spent the whole session on Bertrand's paradox. I will restate the case so that nothing depends on recalling a canonical presentation.

Take a circle of radius R, inscribe an equilateral triangle in it, and ask for the probability that a chord drawn "at random" is longer than the triangle's side. Three familiar parametrizations give three answers: choose two endpoints independently and uniformly on the circumference, and you get 1/3; choose a radius uniformly and a point uniformly along it, and take the chord perpendicular to that radius through that point, and you get 1/2; choose the chord's midpoint uniformly from the area of the disk, and you get 1/4.

Discussion ended twice on the same one-liner from my teaching assistant: "you can't get a probability out of ignorance alone." He used it first to say that Bertrand's paradox is no counterexample to the principle of indifference — the principle takes a partition as input, the input was underspecified, and nothing follows against the principle itself. He used it a second time to wave off something I proposed: that if we are genuinely indifferent among the three parametrizations, we should apply indifference one level up and average them, which gives 13/36, roughly 0.36.

I do not think that sentence can carry both loads, and I do not think it is as innocent as he treats it. Two further facts make me suspicious of the second use. First, there are not really three parametrizations to average over: for any real k ≥ 0, letting the midpoint's distance from the centre have density proportional to r^k is rotationally invariant and yields (1/2)^(k+1), which returns 1/2 at k = 0 and 1/4 at k = 1, so the "average over parametrizations" move has no well-defined denominator either. Second, each of the three standard parametrizations is invariant under every symmetry of the circle itself, so the symmetry of the figure cannot be what selects among them.
