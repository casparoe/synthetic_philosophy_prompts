I am writing a paper on Szasz's myth-of-mental-illness thesis, and specifically on whether its ontological half (mental illnesses are not diseases, because there is no lesion) does any work for its political half (involuntary psychiatric confinement cannot be justified by calling the confined person ill). A friend in the seminar, who is unsympathetic to Szasz, handed me the argument below. It looks valid to me and I dislike the conclusion, but I also think it may be destroying more than either of us wants destroyed, so I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict on Szasz.

The argument, as he wrote it out:

(1) Szasz's central contrast is between two kinds of ascription. A disease ascription reports a deviation in the person's body, ascertainable without judging their conduct. A mental-illness ascription, whose criteria are behaviours, reports instead a verdict on conduct: that the person is doing things the ascriber, or the ascriber's community, disapproves of. Call this the asymmetry claim — disease ascriptions are norm-independent, mental-illness ascriptions are not.

(2) The asymmetry claim is what is supposed to carry the political conclusion. If 'he is mentally ill' really means 'he behaves in ways we disapprove of,' then confining him on the ground that he is ill is confining him on the ground that we disapprove of his conduct, and that is not a medical justification at all but a moral or political one in a white coat.

(3) But ordinary disease predicates are not norm-independent in any sense strong enough to bear that weight. Their extensions are fixed partly by decisions about thresholds, measurement technologies, and the consequences of labelling: which blood pressure counts as hypertensive, which bone density counts as osteoporotic, which blood glucose counts as diabetic. Committees revise these in the light of trial evidence, cost, instrumentation, and how much over-treatment the category would licence; each revision moves very large numbers of people across the boundary at once. And the removal of homosexuality from the psychiatric manual in the 1970s followed campaigning and the failure of research to find pathology, not the discovery of a lesion.

(4) If a category's boundary is set partly by weighing the consequences of applying the label, then applying the label is not merely reporting a fact about the person's body.

(5) So disease ascriptions are not norm-independent either. The asymmetry in (1) fails, and disease and problem-in-living differ at most in degree.

(6) If they differ at most in degree, Szasz has not shown that mental illness is a myth or a metaphor or a category error. He has shown at most that psychiatric categories are vague and over-extended, which is equally true of poverty and species, and which threatens no institution.

(7) Worse: since the political conclusion ran through the asymmetry, it goes too. Treating the patient with pneumonia who asks for treatment, confining the psychiatric patient who refuses it, and diagnosing the political dissident then differ only in how much local consensus stands behind each. Szasz's protest becomes a protest against a local consensus, carrying no more authority than the consensus it protests, and his charge that Soviet political psychiatry was a *misuse* of medicine loses its footing.

(8) And there is no third option. Either the threshold politics of hypertension are relevantly unlike the politics of schizophrenia — and nothing in Szasz's argument so much as gestures at such a difference — or the asymmetry goes, and with it the critique.

I cannot accept (8). I would rather give up any of (1) through (7) than give up either of these two: that involuntary psychiatric confinement stands in need of a justification that treating a consenting patient does not; and that the psychiatric diagnosis of political dissidents was an abuse of medicine rather than merely a local convention with which we happen to disagree.

There is also a probe of my own that I cannot place, and which I suspect is doing something the argument needs to answer. Suppose that next year a serum marker B is discovered, present in most people who meet current criteria for schizophrenia and in very few who do not; suppose also that nobody can say what B has to do with the symptoms, and that lowering B leaves them unchanged. Call the resulting diagnosis B-positive. Under Szasz's lesion criterion B-positive now looks like a bodily disease, and yet nothing in the practical situation changes: most people so diagnosed are still brought in by others, still deny that anything is wrong with them, and are still confined against their will. My sense is that the probe prises the ontological question apart from the political one, but I cannot tell whether that rescues Szasz — his real argument was always about consent, and the lesion criterion was decoration — or ruins him, since the criterion he staked the myth charge on turns out to settle nothing.
