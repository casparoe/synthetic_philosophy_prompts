Hey, I need help settleing an argument at work and I've gone way down a rabbit hole for something that started as a bug ticket.

We're shipping a review panel feature: 5 reviewers each get a short checklist of yes/no questions, and the system produces one final approve/reject. The catch is that the last question isn't independent — call it C, and C is just "A AND B", where A is "does this meet condition 1" and B is "does this meet condition 2". So a ballot looks like:

R1: A=y, B=y, C=y
R2: A=y, B=y, C=y
R3: A=y, B=n, C=n
R4: A=n, B=y, C=n
R5: A=n, B=n, C=n

Majority yes on A (3 of 5), majority yes on B (3 of 5), majority no on C (3 of 5). Which is just inconsistent, and apparently this is a known thing in philosophy (doctrinal paradox? discursive dilemma? someone mentioned it in a thread and now I'm here).

Priya's take: A and B are the inputs, C is a derived field, so aggregate the inputs and compute C from those. Letting people also vote on C directly and counting that is double-counting the same opinion three times.

Marco's take: the only thing the user ever sees is the verdict. A and B are just how each reviewer personally got to their answer; the panel's job is to output a decision, so take the majority on C and ignore the rest. He also points out that if the final gate were an OR instead of an AND, his rule and Priya's rule swap which side wins, and he says that shows premise-first isn't more principled, it's just an artifact of the AND.

I made up a third thing and now I can't tell if its clever or if it just hides the choice: instead of aggregating question by question, take the three majority answers as a starting point and then pick the logically consistent set of answers closest to them — you choose which cells to flip to make the group consistent, and flipping a premise costs 1 while flipping the downstream conclusion costs more than 1, because it's derived and overruleing it ought to be expensive. The weights obviously decide the answer, which is exactly what makes me suspicious of my own idea.
