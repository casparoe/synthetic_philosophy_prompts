So a production company optioned a book about a factory scandal from the 1970s and they want to make my grandfather one of the main characters — he was the accountant, or at least the guy who signed off on some things. He's been dead eleven years and there is almost nothing left of him: a ledger, two letters, one photo. They've told us they'll be "compositing" him with two other people and inventing a marriage that was quietly falling apart, because otherwise he has no arc. My sister definately wants to cooperate and hand over the letters. I dont. My mother goes quiet whenever it comes up, which is how I know it matters.

Could you write me a dialogue between two people arguing about what we owe the real dead when we dramatize them — basically me and my sister, but both of them much better at this than we actually are. One holds that the dead are themselves the stake-holders here: that a portrayal can be a kind of theft or slander of a person who can't answer back, and that inventing scenes about someone real is a way of using them. The other holds that nobody can be harmed once they're dead, so every real duty in this situation runs to the living — the family, the people who remember the event, the public's interest in how the event is remembered — and that this can be just as demanding as the other view, just demanding about different things. Keep them equally competent and make them actually answer each other rather than deliver speeches past each other. No real films, no real lawsuits, no named productions and no legal talk about who owns a life story; work the whole thing out of the situation above.

Two things I want them to fight over specifically. First, my own test, which I keep coming back to and can't tell if it's doing any work: there's a difference between invention that fills a gap in the record and invention that reverses a verdict, and the composite plus the bad marriage both do the second thing — they make him legible as a man having a crisis of conscience, which as far as I can tell is not what he was. The other side should push back hard on that: when the archive is this thin, and when what's in it was shaped by who got to keep paperwork and who didn't, gap-filling and verdict-reversing come apart much less than I want them to. Second, whether the fact that he left almost nothing behind changes what we owe him — whether his silence is a kind of statement that deserves respect, or just an absence that we're free to write into.

By the end I want each of them to have conceded one concrete thing (not just "fair enough"), and to have said plainly whether they'd hand over the letters, and to still disagree. Nobody gets to say "there's a balance to be struck" or "its a matter of degree."

Give it as exactly five numbered exchanges, each exchange one turn from each speaker of two or three sentences, speaker names and nothing else — no intro, no sign-off.
