So I got roped onto the arts commission of the town I live in (I'm a PhD student, I work on Rousseau's political thought — mostly the Discourse on Inequality, though I've read the letter to d'Alembert a few times — and of course my neighbor nominated me the second a theatre funding fight looked like it was coming). Here's where things stand. Two applications for the same pot, about 40k total. One is from a small professional company that wants to convert a disused warehouse into a 90-seat indoor house: ticketed subscription season, four plays, proscenium, dark auditorium, paying actors who'd commute in from the city. The other is from a loose collective of local teachers and two semi-professional performers for a summer of site-specific pieces staged in the market square and along the old ramparts — mostly amateur casts drawn from the town, free admission, no fixed seating, the audience walks after the action, and every night ends with a communal meal. The town is about 7,000 people, fairly homogeneous, and extremely pleased with itself about being self-governing.

The reason this has landed on me is that one commissioner — a retired history teacher, and not a fool — has started arguing that Rousseau thought a republic shouldn't have a theatre at all: that theatre softens a people, gives them a taste for spectacle in place of participation, and sets up a class of people whose trade is pretending to be someone else. She's now saying that if we take that seriously we should also look again at the small amateur company we already fund. My own half-baked attempt to make it precise is a three-part test — whether the audience is visible to itself and to each other or hidden in the dark; whether the performers are a separate paid class or just us; whether the thing is something you consume or something you do — but I'm not sure the test tracks anything moral rather than just my own taste, and I also don't want us funding the festival purely because it flatters a 18th-century text.

Can you draft the memo I'll circulate before the meeting? Concrete bottom line please — fund one, fund both, split it with conditions, whatever, with a number and a time frame — and then the reasoning, including a real paragraph on the strongest thing on the losing side, including the strongest version of the "and therefore defund what we already fund" move and whether it survives. Two constraints: no citations, reading lists, or named scholars, since I need sentences I can say out loud in a room rather than footnotes; and don't rest the case on empirical claims about whether theatre actually corrupts mores, because neither of us has any data on that — it has to work as an argument about what the objection is really committed to. If my three-part test is worth keeping, say which way it sorts the two applications and where it's arbitrary; if it's junk, replace it with criteria the commission could actually apply to next year's applications too. Lead with the recommendation in the very first sentence, no preamble and no restating my question back at me.
