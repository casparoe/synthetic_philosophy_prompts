I'm writing a blog post arguing that in Jeffrey conditionalization the rigidity condition is doing all the work, and I need a case built to order for the section where I make that point. Setting: a ward, a lab, or a site inspection — ordinary professional life, no demons, no ideal sensors, nothing a real person couldn't run into on a Tuesday.

Specifications:

1. Three propositions: a hypothesis H; an evidence proposition E that the observation bears on; and a channel variable C (roughly: the reading came from the source you took it to come from). H and E must be positively correlated under the prior.
2. One experience — a glance, a half-heard sentence, a misread printout — that leaves the agent genuinely uncertain about E (give me q ≈ 0.85, strictly between 0 and 1), raises P(E) above its prior value, and moves her credence in C by at least 0.5.
3. Verdicts: (a) the as-if-certain answer P(H|E) is above the prior; (b) Jeffrey updating with rigidity over the coarse partition {E, ¬E}, plugging in the new q, puts H above the prior; (c) Jeffrey updating with rigidity over the four-cell partition {E, ¬E} × {C, ¬C} puts H below the prior. So the correct update reverses the sign the coarse one gives, and the gap between (b) and (c) should be at least 0.1 in absolute probability. This is not the Judy Benjamin point: both updates must be fully determinate, with nothing left pending some min-relative-entropy or proportionality supplement.
4. Give me the eight-cell joint over H × C × E — no zeros, no numbers that would embarrass a clinician — and show the arithmetic for (a), (b), (c).
5. Two sentences on what a strict conditionalizer who assigns likelihoods to her total experience gets, and why she lands with (c) rather than (b).

Then, briefly: which feature is doing the work, and specifically which rigidity assumption actually fails on this experience and why; what happens to the reversal if the off-channel reading is merely uninformative about H given ¬C rather than adverse; and the strongest reply available to a Jeffrey partisan. I expect something like "you applied the rule to a partition you already knew was evidentially heterogeneous — user error, not rule failure," and I want to know whether that reply quietly concedes my thesis.

Two things I believe going in, which you should correct if wrong: (b) is automatic given (a) plus q > P(E), so the real design work is all in (c); and sign-flipping requires the E-cell receiving the new mass to be low in H, either because the reading means something different on the wrong channel or because the channel itself is bad news — a merely uninformative off-channel reading attenuates but never reverses.

Keep the whole response under 700 words: scenario in 120 or fewer, then the numbers and the three verdicts, then at most 80 words each on the load-bearing feature, the removal test, and the partisan's reply.
