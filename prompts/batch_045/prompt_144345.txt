hey — hoping you can help me untangle something in welfare economics / social choice that I keep going in circles on. Please answer in American English, and please skip any bibliographic stuff — I'm not after references, and I'd rather you reason it out than try to recall who said what.

Here's the setup as I understand it. Each person has a von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function over outcomes, but it's only pinned down up to positive affine rescaling — u → au + b, with a > 0. So there are two separate comparision problems: the units (the a's — whose utils are "bigger") and the origins (the b's — where each person's zero sits). With no interpersonal comparability at all you're stuck with ordinal non-comparable information and you get Arrow-flavored impossibility results; with full comparability you can just add people up; and there are intermediate informational setups in between (differences comparable but levels not, and so on).

What I can't get straight is a move I half-remember from a paper I read a while ago and can't find again. I'll reconstruct it badly and call it the mirror procedure. Say we've got two people, Amara and Ben. Build an extended outcome space whose elements look like "being Amara in circumstances x" and "being Ben in circumstances x". Ask each of them to rank lotteries over that whole extended space, and assume each has vNM preferences on it. Now Amara's extended utility function assigns numbers to "being Ben in x" as well as to "being Amara in x", and Ben's does the reverse. Since they're both assigning numbers to the same extended outcomes, we can read off a ratio — how much Amara cares about Ben's circumstances relative to her own, which is invariant under any rescaling of Amara's own function — and similarly a ratio from Ben's side. Then combine them somehow (geometric mean? or iterate the comparison until it settles down?) and that's supposed to give you the interpersonal unit ratio. The appeal is that you'd get comparability out of nothing but each person's own preferences plus some sympathy, without anyone having to make a raw intuitive judgment like "Ben's gains count one-point-four times as much as Amara's." (I have a vague sense this is in the neighborhood of "extended sympathy" / impartial-spectator style arguments, but never mind the provenance.)

My questions:

1. Is the procedure even well posed? Does the move from "each person has a vNM function over outcomes" to "each person has a vNM function over identity-plus-outcome lotteries" already smuggle in something, either about the identity of the outcomes or about what it means to be someone else?

2. Suppose it is well posed. What does it actually deliver — units, origins, neither, both? My hunch is that the two ratios you get need not agree with each other in any way, and that "take the geometric mean" is doing a lot of unacknowledged work, but I can't say precisely what breaks. And does the answer change depending on whether each person is asked to imagine being the other person with the other person's tastes, or with their own tastes retained? (I suspect this is where the whole thing either becomes a nonstarter or quietly stops being about welfare at all, but again I can't pin it down.)

3. Separately: how much would any of this matter for what you can then do with the comparisons? My sense is that for a fixed population, summing is insensitive to where everyone's zero is, so the origin problem only really bites once population size can vary — but I'm definately not confident about that, and I'd like to know the reason either way.

Be concrete please: a tiny two-person, two-or-three-outcome example would help me a lot more than general hand-waving about invariance classes.
