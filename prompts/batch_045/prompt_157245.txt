I'm putting together a two-page proposal for a small "second career" fellowship thing my old university runs for emeriti — six months, part time, no teaching, and at the end you hand over a paper draft and give one talk to the department. I spent about thirty years as a field ecologist doing population monitoring for a state agency, so most of my working life was spent counting proxies: calling counts instead of actual frogs, nest counts instead of actual birds, that sort of thing. I retired a couple of years ago and have been sitting in on philosophy of science seminars and going to a reading group with some grad students, and the thing I keep circling back to is Goodhart's law and specification gaming — the business where a measure quietly stops tracking the thing you cared about once you start pushing on it.

My half-formed idea, which I'd like you to turn into an actual proposal rather than just agree with: the usual taxonomies (the ones that separate a causal or regress version from an extremal or adversarial one) sort cases by what causes the breakdown, but when I was the person who had to decide what to do about a broken monitoring protocol, cause wasn't the distinction that mattered. What mattered was which intervention would fix it and whether the fix was cheap. Two failure modes can have the same cause and still call for completely different responses, and I think the standard taxonomy hides that. So I want the central question to be something like: does sorting Goodhart-type failures by the remedies actually available cut the cases up differently than sorting them by cause, and if so, which classification is the one worth using? I also want to work in something from my own old field, because it's the reason I find this interesting at all: in calibration work you have a range over which an instrument was checked against the quantity you actually care about, readings inside that range are trustworthy and readings outside it aren't, and nobody says the instrument broke. I'd like to try out the idea that a lot of specification gaming is just optimization pressure shoving a system outside the region where the proxy was ever calibrated in the first place, rather than anyone cheating or the measure corrupting. That suggests a second question I definately want in there: how much of this literature quietly assumes the target is well defined independent of the proxy — a real population size out there, with the frog calls just a noisy window onto it — as opposed to cases where the target is partly constituted by the measure, so pushing on the proxy changes what the target even is. I suspect those two kinds of case need totally different treatment and mostly get run together.
