Hi — I run the integrity/compliance function at a mid-sized INGO (health and cash transfer programmes, around 40 country offices). We have a whistleblowing line that staff and beneficiaries can text into, and we get maybe 200 reports a year, of which something like 60 are about money or favours rather than safeguarding or HR stuff. Right now triage is basically: whoever is on duty reads it, forms a view, and the country director decides. Unsuprisingly we are wildly inconsistent — one office dismissed someone for a $15 facilitation payment, another office treated a much bigger thing as "local custom" and closed it. Reports go to donors in aggregate and occasionally individually, and some end in dismissal or a police referral, so the inconsistency is starting to be a real problem for us.

What I need from you is a written procedure my team can actually run. Constraints: triage is done by a two-person team (neither is a lawyer, both are from the region), they have maybe 30 minutes per report, the output has to be a short written record that a regional director can sign off on and that would survive being read aloud to (a) a donor and (b) the local staff whose practice it is. And it has to work in translation — most offices don't operate in English.

Conceptually I think we keep conflating four questions and I want the procedure to keep them apart:
(i) does the thing fit the definition of corruption at all;
(ii) if it does, who is made worse off, and how identifiable are those people;
(iii) is the person involved blameworthy given what their actual options were — I keep running into cases where refusing to pay isn't a real option, as opposed to cases where both sides are collaborating to get something they're not entitled to;
(iv) what we do about it: sanction the individual, report it upward, change our own process so the payment stops being necessary, or leave it alone.
I want the procedure to output both a classification and a response recommendation, and to be explicit that these can come apart — something can be corruption and still warrant no sanction.

Two design requirements I care about, and please argue with me if you think either is wrong:

- Currency neutrality. The transfer might be cash, but it might be a job for a nephew, school fees, a goat, an invitation to a wedding, a promise to turn up at a rally, or help getting a relative out of a police station. Our current practice treats cash as the serious case and the rest as "culture", which I'm fairly sure is just parochialism with a nicer name. I want the classification to come out the same if you swap the currency, and I want the procedure to say so out loud rather than leave it implicit.
- Relationship-shaped cases. A lot of what reaches us isn't a single act, it's a pattern over years: the same intermediary connecting the same people across completely different domains — a small sub-contract here, help getting a kid into school there, a hospital bed, a police matter quietly sorted. Each act is defensible on its own; the thing that worries me is the relationship. So I want a branch that takes a pattern rather than an act as the unit of analysis, and a test for when a pile of individually fine acts adds up to something we should call clientelism. My guess at the test is something about convertibility (can credit earned in one domain be cashed in another?) and something about who else could have obtained the same thing, but please do better than my guess.

Things I want flagged rather than smoothed over:

- Cases where nobody personally profited. Quite often an office does something improper and the benefit accrues to the programme or to the community, not to any individual. Our rules — which are basically "use of a position for private gain" — fit these badly, and I'd rather the procedure tell me it doesn't know what to do with them than pretend they're just the easy case.
- Where our own standard is the imported one. I'd like some kind of stress test that catches us applying a Western or northern intuition and calling it universal. But I also know that kind of test gets abused as a blanket excuse, so tell me where it misfires and what stops someone using it to wave anything through.
- Where the collective-action version bites. Even where everyone agrees a practice is bad, one office that opts out on its own loses its licence, its shipments, its land, while the practice carries on regardless — which means our own rule creates pressure to not report rather than to not pay. I want the procedure to register this where it applies and to say how the record should note it.
- Which decisions genuinely can't be made by procedure and have to go to a named person as a values call. I need to know which boxes those are and who signs.

Deliverable: a one-page version I can hand to the team — ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, where it hands off — plus a short appendix saying, for each step, which distinction is doing the work and which distinctions you deliberately dropped to make it usable, and where it will misfire. I definately don't need citations or a reading list, I can't check them and they'd be no use to me; just give me the reasoning.

Then run it on three cases and show me the records you'd actually produce:

1. Enumerators registering households for a cash programme are routinely given eggs or a chicken afterwards. The teams say refusing is an insult and head office has told them to accept. One enumerator later helped a household that had given her a chicken fill in their form "properly" so they'd qualify. Small stuff in itself, but it's dozens of times a week across a whole district, and the second bit feels different to me in a way I can't articulate — if the procedure can articulate it, that would be worth a lot.

2. A logistics assistant pays the equivalent of about $12 to a customs clerk to release a consignment of therapeutic food that is already legally cleared and has been sitting for nine days after three weeks of written requests that went nowhere. He reports it himself the same day, he has the receipt, and he's the person who kept telling the regional office the shipment was stuck. Nobody was personally enriched and the food reached a feeding programme.

3. A country office needed land for a distribution site and a chief provided it, essentially for free, because the office had a long relationship with his family. Later the office hired his daughter as a casual enumerator ahead of 40 applicants with better test scores. For two years the office has also routed small sub-contracts, staff medical referrals and a couple of school-fee advances through the same local fixer, who is also a youth organiser for the governing party. Staff say he is the only reason anything gets done in that district and that they owe him. Classify it, tell me who is harmed and how identifiable they are, and tell me what we do — because my instinct is that sanctioning anyone here would be insane and would cost us the programme, and I want to know whether the procedure agrees with me or whether it tells me I'm just protecting my own office.
