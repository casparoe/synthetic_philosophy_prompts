I want to know where I think the line between persuasion and manipulation actually falls, and I would rather derive it from my own reactions to cases than adopt a definition and apply it. My working rule has been what I call the out-loud test: an attempt to influence someone is permissible if and only if the influencer could tell the target, there and then and in plain terms, that the target is being influenced and by what mechanism, without the influence thereby losing its effect. The test handles much of what I care about and then embarrasses me in at least one place, so I am not sure it has the right shape.

Here are my verdicts.

1. A sponsorship charity shows me the photograph and first name of one particular child rather than the figures on how many children the money reaches. I give. Verdict: fine; ordinary persuasion.

2. My navigation app, paid by a doughnut chain, routes me past one of its branches on the days when my own history predicts I will not resist stopping. Verdict: not fine.

3. A grocery delivery app infers that I am in a hurry from how fast I tap, how often I reopen it, and how long I hover, and raises the quoted price accordingly — call it cadence pricing. Verdict: not fine, and noticeably worse than case 2. It also seems to pass the out-loud test, since the company could say in its terms that prices respond to urgency, so the test is in trouble from this side as well.

4. My therapist, knowing that the word confrontation makes me freeze, schedules the exposure exercise under the label check-in. Verdict: fine, and I would want her to keep doing it.

5. My coach tells the squad before a match that he overheard the other team laughing at us. He did not, and the team plays better for it. Verdict: not fine — and this one troubles me because it looks a great deal like case 4.

6. A colleague sends me an invitation titled quick sync for what is really a two-hour session in which our director will press me on a decision I made. Verdict: not fine.

7a. My partner, who knows that high bridges panic me, drives the longer route home without mentioning why. Verdict: fine.
7b. My mother, with the same knowledge, tells me the bridge is closed so that I will agree to the longer route. Verdict: not fine.

8. A restaurant adds a second-cheapest bottle to the wine list so that the mid-priced one no longer feels like the cheapest thing on offer. I order the mid-priced one and, when told later, shrug. Verdict: fine.

9. My friends lie to me for three weeks in order to stage a surprise party, which I enjoy. Verdict: fine.

Cases 2 and 8 are the pair I cannot reconcile. Both are commercial, both work on a quirk in my psychology that I would not endorse if I noticed it, and neither announces itself; I resent one and shrug at the other. If there is nothing that separates them, say so and tell me which verdict to give up.

The case I keep reversing on: my mother's surgeon, having noticed that numbers frighten her, tells her that most people her age come through this fine instead of giving her the six percent figure she would give me. I alternate between protective and robbed of a choice.

What I would like from you:

State a single principle, or at most two or three candidates, precisely enough that I could apply it myself, and tie each clause to the case that forces it. Tell me whether the out-loud test can be repaired with an additional clause or should be abandoned. Give the verdicts your principle yields on three cases I have not listed: a friend who quietly stops suggesting we meet in bars while I am trying not to drink, never saying why; my union representative, who thinks I would vote against a clause for bad reasons, leaving that clause out of the summary she sends me even though the full contract is available; and a campaign that shows an advertisement only to people whose posting history suggests they follow little news, with content tuned to a worry each of them has stated. Then tell me, honestly, which of my nine verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and rule on the surgeon. If the real choice is between a multi-clause principle that saves all nine and a simpler one that overturns a verdict, present both, name the verdict that dies, and say what is lost. If you think the binary of persuasion versus manipulation is the wrong axis and the notion is scalar, argue for that instead. Do not rest anything on empirical findings or on what some theorist has argued; I want the reasoning to stand on the cases. Please answer in American English, in a formal register.
