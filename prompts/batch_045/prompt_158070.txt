I am finishing a dissertation chapter in political theory on the normative status of collective memory, and I need the document that my two supervisors should have produced instead of two more years of arguing past each other. One of them is broadly Margalitian: there are genuine duties to remember, grounded partly in what we owe the victims of radical evil simply as fellow humans, and partly in the thick relations that shared memory partly constitutes rather than merely records. The other is broadly Rieffian: institutionalized remembrance is a volatile political technology, its effects on future violence are not what commemorative practice assumes, and in a substantial range of cases the morally better outcome is collective forgetting. I want the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between them.

Two constraints on how you write it. First, argue the positions, not the texts: no bibliography, no citations, no appeals to what either author actually wrote, and no claims about the reception history. Both parties must be able to sign on the strength of the arguments alone. Second, nothing should hang on factual material you would have to look up; I stipulate the case and the evidence below, and you should treat those stipulations as fixed.

The stipulated case. The Republic of Vardena emerged seventy years ago from a negotiated transition after two decades of authoritarian rule known as the Silence Years. The regime's internal security service kept a complete card index of roughly forty thousand dossiers; each dossier names a surveilled person, members of that person's family, and at least one recruited informer. Roughly one adult in twelve appears in the index, on one side or the other. The transition involved an amnesty and no trials, and the index was sealed. Two generations on, the youngest person with first-hand memory of the Silence Years is in her eighties; within a decade there will be no living witness, and no one alive will have known a named victim personally. A grandchildren's movement, the Opening, demands that the index be published in full, online, with names searchable. The government has proposed instead the Sealed Deposit: digitize the index, lodge it in the national archive, open it on request to any descendant of a named person and to accredited researchers, but with no public index, no memorial day, no curriculum module, and no renaming of streets or squares.

The stipulated evidence, which both parties accept for the sake of the collaboration. (a) A cross-national study of thirty post-authoritarian transitions finds no reliable correlation between the intensity of official remembrance and the recurrence of mass atrocity over the following twenty-five years; but its measure of remembrance counts memorials, trials and curricula, and says nothing about archival access. (b) In the five transitions where collaborator files were published in full, recorded incidents of retaliatory violence against named informers' descendants rose for three to five years and then fell below baseline; in none did mass violence resume. (c) Survey work in comparable populations: descendants of victims describe publication as acknowledgement, descendants of informers describe the same publication as inherited accusation, and about forty percent of the latter report learning from it something they had not known about a grandparent. (d) Individual recollection decays and is reconstructively distorted, whereas institutional records do not decay but are consulted at falling rates; under a sealed-deposit design roughly one half of one percent of eligible descendants consult the record in any given year.

What I want from you: the claims both can sign, each non-vacuous — not the platitude that memory matters and forgetting has costs, but the substantive common ground about what a duty of memory would have to be like, about who can bear such a duty, about what the Sealed Deposit does and does not do, and about what follows from the death of the last witness. Then a short section dissolving the disputes that turn out to be about words: I suspect at least one is, since the parties use remembering for both an individual's occurrent recollection and a state's program of commemoration, and I suspect the parties mean different things by forgetting as well — one an act of suppression, one merely the absence of institutional address. Say which disputes are verbal and give the reformulation both accept.

Then the residual disagreements, in a table: each crux in its own row, with the Margalitian position phrased so that the Margalitian would sign it, the Rieffian position phrased so that the Rieffian would sign it, the diagnostic case or test both agree would bear on the crux, and — written down in advance — what each side predicts about it. Candidate cruxes, which you should keep, merge, discard, or replace as the drafting requires: whether any duty of memory is owed to the dead themselves or is always a duty of the living concerning the living; whether the relevant duty-bearer is an individual, a family, or a political collective; whether a duty to remember requires memory of particulars such as names, or is satisfied by memory of a structural fact; whether the Sealed Deposit is a case of remembering, of forgetting, or of a third thing neither side's vocabulary covers; whether expected future violence can defeat a duty of memory, and whether it matters that the beneficiaries of forgetting are third parties while those to whom the duty would be owed are the victims; whether one and the same public act can have two divergent meanings for two audiences and how a duty-bearer should weigh that; and whether a society with a fixed attention budget and a growing calendar of commemorations can owe remembrance to each of many groups in diluted form, or whether dilution converts remembrance into something else. For any crux that admits no test both would accept as bearing on it, say so and say why.

Two further requirements. Each side must state what would count as evidence against its own view, not merely against the other's. And each side must concede something real: the Margalitian should not be allowed to imply that commemoration is never weaponized, and the Rieffian should not be allowed to treat every duty in the personal register as reducible to consequences for the living — in particular, say what each of them thinks the children of survivors owe to their parents' story.

Close with the recommendation each party would give the Vardenan government about the index, stated as guidance rather than as a verdict on who has won, plus a short note on whether either expects the residual disagreement to be settled and by what. Aim for about 1,200 to 1,600 words, in the register of a working paper rather than of an essay: numbered agreed-claims section first, then the verbal-disputes section, then the disagreements table, then the closing recommendations. Where a formulation would be rejected by one side as a caricature, rewrite it until it would not be.
