I'm putting together a problem set for an upper-level seminar on modal metaphysics, and I want one question that does not reduce to the examples everyone has already read twice. The students will have seen the standard dialectic: the counterpart theorist treats de re possibility as a matter of some sufficiently similar thing in some other world bearing the property, while the transworld-identity theorist insists that the very individual herself must bear it in that world, and the usual illustrations — a candidate who might have won an election he lost, a table that might have been made of different matter, a child who might have been born to different parents — all work by making the counterpart theorist the more permissive of the two. Every time I try to write a fresh question I lapse back into one of those. So I want a case that relocates the disagreement somewhere the stock examples do not reach, and that is self-contained enough that a student can extract the verdicts from the two frameworks' truth-conditions alone, without reaching for anything they have read.

Assume the counterpart-theoretic translation in which each modal operator introduces a fresh world variable together with fresh counterpart variables for the individuals in play, and assume a counterpart relation fixed by comparative overall similarity together with whatever is salient in the context, rather than a primitive relation with transitivity stipulated as a postulate. Assume that the transworld-identity theorist is working with transworld identity plus a plain S5 frame, so that nested modalities collapse. Now build me an ordinary, realistic scenario — a family, a school, a hospital, a conservatory, an emigration; one paragraph, with a named individual; no duplication, fission or branching, no science fiction, no one originating from different parents or different matter, and no more than two nested modal steps — in which that individual ends up, in some other world, in a position I will call modal stranding with respect to some ordinary attainable good F: some counterpart of the actual individual is such that none of its own counterparts bear F, even though some counterpart of the actual individual does bear F. Both frameworks should accept, without argument, that the actual individual could have borne F, so the disagreement is not about whether F was ever on the table for her. Then give me the two sentences an ordinary speaker would produce having heard the story: the one saying that she could have ended up somewhere from which F was out of reach for her, and its contradictory, the one saying that however things had gone for her, F would still have been within reach. Both modals should be metaphysical and de re rather than causal or circumstantial, and the pair should be one a non-philosopher who knows the story hears as plainly true in the first case and plainly false in the second.

Then show me that the case does what I am asking for. State the target claim, render it in each framework's own idiom, and derive each verdict rather than announcing it: say which worlds, which candidates for counterparthood, and which assignments of the variables do the work, and argue for the stranding from how the counterpart relation behaves in this case instead of stipulating that it obtains. Tell me which single feature of the scenario is load-bearing, and what happens to the verdicts when that feature is removed — in particular, if the counterpart relation is stipulated transitive and context-invariant, or if the transworld-identity theorist is allowed to weaken S5 or to hear the embedded modal as circumstantial rather than metaphysical, does the disagreement survive, or does the case collapse back into agreement? Finish with the strongest reply available to whichever framework comes out worse: can she accuse you of equivocating about whose context fixes the respects of similarity, of having built the answer into the description of the scenario, or of having shown only that two formalisms disagree rather than two metaphysics? Tell me honestly whether that reply is a cost she can pay or a cheat. If part of the specification turns out not to be satisfiable jointly with the rest, say which conjunct fails and build the nearest case that satisfies everything else.
