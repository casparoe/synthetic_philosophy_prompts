You are a professional moral philosopher who has spent a lot of time thinking about the line between what we owe people and what's just extra. Take your time with this one: do a bit of unstructured brainstorming first, then give me your considered answer, and don't be afraid to tell me one of my gut reactions is simply wrong.

I've been trying to work out from my own gut reactions when something is a duty and when it's optional-but-great (supererogatory, I think the word is). My tentative rule is: an act is supererogatory when it helps others a lot and costs the agent a lot. But my cases won't sit still for it, and I want the actual principle, if there is one.

Here's where I land on concrete cases:

- Pulling a toddler out of a shallow fountain when I'm right there and my shoes stay dry: obviously I have to. Not optional at all. But by my rule it helps a lot and costs nearly nothing, so it should be supererogatory. It isn't.
- Jumping on a grenade to save three strangers: amazing, and I don't blame the people who freeze. Feels like the clearest case of "extra".
- Donating a kidney to a complete stranger: also extra, and yet somehow I feel it's *more* optional than the grenade thing, even though it's far less dangerous. That pair bugs me.
- Giving 10% of my income to effective charities: I feel it's optional. My friend says it's a duty and that not doing it makes you a bad person. I want to say she's wrong but I cant quite say why.
- A nurse on my ward who stays two hours unpaid every shift: I want to praise her, but it makes the rest of the team look bad and management now pretty much expects it. I've started calling this the norm ratchet. Praiseworthy once, and bad if everyone does it — I don't know how to hold both thoughts.
- A guy who secretly pays off the school lunch debts of a whole class and never tells a soul: I feel he deserves credit even though no one can give it to him, and I feel slightly odd about praising him once I find out, like praise somehow needs an audience.
- My neighbour, who donated a kidney years ago and could now do it again for someone else: I feel she's off the hook, she's done her bit. I call that credit exhaustion. But I also feel queasy, because the person who needs the kidney today doesn't care what she did before.
- A mother of two small kids who donates a kidney to a stranger, when there's maybe a 5% chance one of her own kids will need one in twenty years: I don't think this is praiseworthy. I might even think it's a bit wrong. Yet it helps a lot and costs a lot, so my rule calls it supererogatory or even saintly.

Is there one principle — or two rival candidates — that gets most of these, and where exactly does cost have to enter: as a threshold, as a trade-off against the benefit, or as something about *whose* cost it is and what they already owe? Tell me which of my verdicts your principle can't save, and what it predicts for the case I keep flip-flopping on: quitting my job to care for my mum with early-stage dementia for two years, when a good care home was available and I could have afforded it. I go back and forth between "that was beautiful" and "that was more than anyone could ask, so it was optional" — and I can't tell whether "optional" is praise or an insult.
