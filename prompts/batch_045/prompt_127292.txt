I've been making generative art with code for about a decade — write the rules, run it, keep what comes out — and I've never once wondered whether those were mine, even the ones that surprised me, because I can always point at the line that made them look like that. Over the last year or so I've been using image models too, and something has come apart in a way I didn't expect, and it isn't the usual is-it-art question. When the output matches a picture I had precisely in mind — careful prompt, result comes back exactly as pictured — I feel like I ordered it from somebody. The outputs that land sideways, better than what I was aiming at, sometimes from a half-typed prompt with a typo in it, are the ones where the I made this feeling actually shows up. But those are also the ones I'd feel like a fraud signing, and the ones where I can't tell you why they look the way they do, only that I chose them. So the feeling of having made something and the feeling of being entitled to credit for it are pointing in opposite directions, and I can't tell if I've noticed something about creativity or just two unrelated quirks in how people assign credit to themselves.

My own hypothesis is that there are two different things jammed together in creativity here — one about how much of the result my choices determined, one about whether I could have said in advance what the result was going to be — and that my old code setup made them look like a single thing because there I got both at once. Is that close to anything established? People say vague things at me about creating by writing rules rather than specifying outcomes, about different kinds of creativity depending on whether you already know what you're after, and about us valuing things in proportion to the effort we put in; none of them seems to be about the split itself, and especially not about the two halves pulling in opposite directions.

I need this for something real: I'm on the commitee rewriting my department's policy on AI-assisted work in studio classes, and if I can't say what a student loses when the model does the surprising part for them, I can't say what the policy is for. So — is there a name for this, or for either half of it? If there isn't, coin one and define it. Either way don't just hand me a label: I want to know what exactly this thing is, what nearby things it isn't, and where its edges are. And be straight with me about which parts are settled and which parts people who actually work on this are still fighting about, and definately flag where you're speculating — I've sat through too many meetings where somebody's personal theory got introduced as the consensus.
