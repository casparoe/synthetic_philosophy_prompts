I'm retired, spent my career in experimental condensed matter physics, and for the last year or so Ive been going to a philosophy reading group at the library, so forgive me if I mangle the vocabulary.

We did the KK principle last week and I realised I dont understand how it connects to luminosity, or whether the margin-for-error argument that kills luminosity is also supposed to kill KK. Three things I'd like explained at the level of a working scientist rather than a journal referee:

1. What exactly is the difference between the KK principle (if you know p then you know that you know p) and the luminosity of a condition (whenever you are in condition C, you are in a position to know that you are)? Is KK just luminosity applied to the condition knows-that-p, or is there a gap between the two?

2. The margin-for-error argument. I can follow the version where the room is cooled a fraction of a degree at a time and being-cold turns out not to be luminous, but I want the gut-level reason. In my own terms it looked like this: if C is luminous then the set of cases where C holds must be open in whatever similarity structure cases have, and then a chain of ever-finer steps from a clear C case to a clear not-C case forces that set to be everything, which is absurd. Is that the right shape of the argument, or does it smuggle something in?

3. The bit I keep chewing on: the argument seems to need the case space to be finely graded, so that every case has arbitrarily close neighbours. What if it isn't? Suppose there is a smallest discriminable difference, a grain, like pixels on a detector or a digital readout, so cases come in discrete units with a minimum gap. Then the margin for error looks like it goes vacuous: if the required margin is smaller than the minimum gap, knowing C in a case demands only that C hold in that very case, and luminosity is back on the table. Concretely, take the condition the counter has clicked at least once in the last minute: there is no gradual route from no click to click, just a jump. Is that condition luminous? And if graininess does rescue luminosity for some conditions, does it rescue it for knows-that-p, so that KK survives for grainy creatures like us? Or is the graininess move self-undermining somewhere — because any safety margin can be beaten by making the series finer, or because the case space relevant to knowledge isn't grainy even if perception is?

Happy to be told I've set the whole thing up wrong. A few paragraphs each is plenty, and please no reading lists — our group has no library access and half of us are still on dialup.
