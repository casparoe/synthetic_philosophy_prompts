The standoff over higher-order evidence has now run for roughly two decades without visible convergence, and I want a diagnosis of its structure rather than a survey of positions. Here is the dispute as I see it. Conciliationists: when you learn that a trusted peer disagrees with you, or that you formed your view while tired, emotionally compromised, or under conditions known to degrade judgment, rationality requires you to move toward suspension or toward the peer's view, because your first-order reasoning is not exempt from assessment by your total evidence, and relying on that very reasoning to dismiss the higher-order evidence is question-begging. Steadfasters: you have already counted the higher-order consideration, you have assessed the peer's argument and found it wanting, and any further revision either double-counts the same evidence or makes inquiry self-undermining, since on any nontrivial question you can always find someone who disagrees. Each side has replies to the other's best argument — the conciliationist says the steadfast reply just restarts the question-begging; the steadfaster says the conciliationist's symmetry principle cannot get inquiry off the ground — and neither side has moved appreciably in years.

My hypothesis is that at least three separable theses are being run together under one name. First, whether higher-order evidence is evidence in the normatively relevant sense at all, or whether it is merely a defeater that changes what it is permissible to rely on without entering the evidential tally. Second, whether some level-connection or enkratic principle holds, such that it is a defect to believe a proposition while believing that your evidence does not support it or that you should suspend — including whether the label epistemic akrasia is doing covert work by importing the practical verdict, since in the practical case acting against your own better judgment is defective almost by definition whereas in the epistemic case the analogous coherence requirement is a substantive, contestable normative claim. Third, how to apply a total-evidence standard when the total evidence includes a report about the reliability of the very process you would use to assess that report, which gives the case a self-referential or fixed-point structure rather than a simple Bayesian-update structure.

To make the diagnosis concrete, consider a nested variant rather than the usual two-person disagreement: A reports a proposition to you; B reports that A is systematically unreliable on this topic; C reports that B manufactures such reports maliciously; and you additionally learn that some fixed share of people in your situation are fed wholly fabricated reports. Take the two positions through that case and tell me whether they diverge where the standard cases make them look convergent, and whether the divergence tracks one of the three theses above or something I have not listed.

Two further candidate diagnoses I want assessed rather than assumed. One: the dispute is verbal at the level of the word rational, with one side using a veritist or expected-accuracy reading and the other a procedural or blame-avoidance reading, so that both can be right simultaneously. Two: a hidden empirical question is doing the work — how reliable first-order reasoning actually is when higher-order evidence is present — which is unanswerable because calibrating the faculty seems to require using it. A third possibility worth considering: the sides differ about which cases count as data, treating peer disagreement, evidence of impairment, and debunking explanations of why you believe as if they were one phenomenon.

Give me the diagnosis with reasons grounded in what each side actually commits itself to, and then, given that diagnosis, an account of what would count as progress: a distinction that dissolves the issue, a formal result about coherence or fixed points, an imprecise-credence treatment in which the higher-order information widens rather than shifts your commitment, a reframing of the question, or an argument that no convergence should be expected and why.
