I'm giving a short talk to a political-theory reading group next month on whether visitors have to follow local conventions — the everyday kind: how much you tip, whether you form a line or crowd the door at a bus stop, whether you cross against the light on a visibly empty street. I've circulated a one-page statement of where I've landed so the others can poke holes in it before I present, so I'd like you to treat the list below the way they will: as a position to audit rather than a question to answer. Assume I'm reasonably happy with each item taken on its own; what I can't see is whether they hold up together.

Here is the statement, more or less as I wrote it. (1) The reason a convention gives me to conform is exhausted by the social facts: others conform, they expect me to, and my conforming helps keep the coordination going. Nothing more is needed, and the reason is conditional — it lapses once enough others stop conforming, because then there's nothing left for me to coordinate with. This is the standard coordination account of convention: an arbitrary resolution of a recurring coordination problem, held in place by common knowledge of general conformity; Lewis's name is the one always attached to it. (2) Some breaches of convention are nonetheless genuine moral wrongs and not mere impoliteness: queue-jumping wrongs the specific people standing behind you, and that is a moral matter. (3) Whether a newcomer must follow a local convention depends only on what is locally in force — not on whether she thinks the convention is a good one, not on how long she has been in the country, and not on whether she intends to stay. She must queue in a queueing city on her first afternoon; she need not tip in a city where tipping isn't done. (4) Not tipping where servers' wages are built on the assumption of tips is morally wrong, and not merely a breach of local convention; by contrast, jaywalking across an empty street is at most a conventional failing, since nobody is wronged. (5) My preferred test for whether an act is morally permissible is the generalization test: what if everyone in relevantly similar circumstances did that? (6) I don't think we owe anyone the preservation of a bad convention. Where a convention is a worse equilibrium than an available alternative, deliberately deviating in order to help move the group to the better one can be justified — I'm thinking of things like a queueing practice that wastes everyone's time, or settled norms of deference in a workplace. (7) This holds even for norms that solve no coordination problem at all: in a city where people greet strangers in the street, and in a city where they studiously don't, a visitor should in each case do what is done there, and is being offensive if she doesn't.

There are two cases I keep failing to get a verdict on that I'm comfortable with: a tourist in a country where tipping isn't done, whose café staff are plainly underpaid; and a tourist who is unmistakably a tourist — map, camera, wrong clothes for the weather — who pushes ahead at a bus stop in a city with a strong queueing norm. A friend says part of my trouble is that (5) is the wrong test for conventions, since conventions tolerate a non-zero rate of deviation and the universal version of the test destroys the very facts my reason to conform depends on; she thinks the right counterfactual is what if one more person did that rather than what if everyone did. I can't tell whether that rescues (5) or hollows it out.

What I want from you: work through the list and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it, ranked by severity rather than flagged uniformly — I'd rather have one or two real problems located precisely than seven items all called shaky. For each real tension, say exactly what would have to give, what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the view, and whether there is a cheaper repair available; then tell me which single revision cleans up the most and why. Give me verdicts on the two cases both under the view as I've stated it and under the least-costly repaired version, and note whether the reasoning that reaches them changes even if the verdicts don't. Finally, if part of my difficulty is that I'm calling two or three different things conventions, say so explicitly — that would be useful to know before I stand up in front of the group.
