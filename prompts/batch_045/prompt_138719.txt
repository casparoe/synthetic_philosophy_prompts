Hey — me and a friend from my "algorithmic fairness" reading group have been going down a rabbit hole on this and I'd love a sanity check before I sink another weekend into it.

We're helping out with a little model at a hospital that scores patients for enrollment in a post-discharge care-management program, and the standard fairness audit flagged the usual stuff: the score isn't calibrated within group, and the false positive and false negative rates differ noticeably between patients on Medicaid and patients with commercial insurance. I've read about the Chouldechova / Kleinberg-style impossibility results, and I take the headline to be that you can't have one score that's calibrated within each group and also has equal false positive and equal false negative rates across groups, unless the base rates happen to match or the predictor is basically perfect. But the more I sit with it, the more it feels like these theorems are really telling us something about the *data* and not about fairness — like, they only bite because our outcome variable is a mess and because we insist on using a hard cutoff. So I've got a three-part fix and I want to know which part to lead with when I pitch it. One: the label is contaminated, since "readmitted within 30 days" partly measures whether someone can afford follow-up care, so I want to relabel the outcome as "clinically preventable readmission" via nurse chart review, which should roughly equalize the base rates and make the conflict disappear. Two: strip out insurance type and anything correlated with it like zip code, so the model is blind to group — that seems like the safest move regardless. Three: instead of a fixed threshold, make enrollment a weighted coin flip whose probability depends on the score, and tune the weights separately per group so the error rates come out equal; as far as I can tell the impossibility theorems are stated for deterministic threshold rules, so this is a genuine loophole, and I figure whatever accuracy we lose we can just win back by adding more features to the model.

So: is it fair to say the impossibility results are mostly a data-quality point in disguise? And of the three steps, which one should I implement first — or is there one in there that will actually backfire and make the next audit worse? I'd rather not ship something that gets torn apart the first time someone reruns the numbers.
