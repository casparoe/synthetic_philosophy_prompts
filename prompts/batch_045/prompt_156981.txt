My position, stated as plainly as I can, has two parts.

First: the so-called Born rule problem for Everettian quantum mechanics is not, at bottom, a problem about probability. It is a problem about rational action and self-location in a branching world, and recast that way it is close to solved. The decision-theoretic derivations in the Deutsch–Wallace line and the self-locating-uncertainty derivations in the Sebens–Carroll line (with its epistemic separability principle) converge on amplitude-squared as the uniquely admissible weighting; symmetry plus branching structure, or rational constraints on self-locating uncertainty, do the same work in a branching theory that Gleason's theorem does in a single-outcome one.

Second: the complaints I know of are best read as tidying-up rather than as defeaters. That includes branch-counting or equal-weight rivals, the circularity charge (that amplitude-dependence is smuggled into the axioms or into the treatment of branching indifference), the vagueness of branch individuation, and the suspicion that the whole dispute is empirically vacuous because every outcome occurs somewhere.

Two wrinkles of my own, on which I want your reaction rather than the literature's. (a) Coarse-graining closure: I want it to be a requirement on any Everettian probability rule that the weight of a macro-outcome come out the same whether you sum over fine-grained branches or treat that macro-outcome as a single branch of a coarse-grained decomposition. Is that condition satisfiable at all, does amplitude-squared meet it, and if it fails does the failure cut against the derivations themselves or only against naive branch-counting? (b) Branch merging: suppose decoherence is reliably reversed at scale, so the branching structure is a lattice with reconvergences rather than a tree, and suppose the standard arguments then deliver different weights depending on whether a merge is read as a fission run backwards or as a fresh branching. Fatal or merely awkward?
