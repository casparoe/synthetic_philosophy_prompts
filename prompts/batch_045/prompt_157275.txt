I'm trying to settle on a method for ontology identification — working out what a theory is commited to existing — for the annoying case where some of the relevant information is inaccessible in principle, not just practically: stuff outside our light cone, events that left no trace anywhere, or two formulations of a theory that no possible observation could tell apart. The method's job, and this is the part I actually care about, is to tell me whether a dispute over entities like that is a real disagreement or merely verbal — whether there's anything there to argue about.

Before I pick one, I want the spec: what does a method like this have to deliver, with the case or consideration behind each requirement? Here are my rough candidates, and I want you to tell me which are genuine and which are just my realist reflexes: it has to seperate electrons from phlogiston (both unobservables posited to save the appearances, only one of which we now take seriously); it has to give the same kind of verdict whether the inaccessible part is tiny, like a coin under a cup, or huge, like everything past the horizon; it can't quietly collapse "we can't tell" into "there's nothing"; it has to distinguish what a theory is committed to from what's actually real; and it has to say what its input even is — a formalized theory, a research program, a community's practice — since reading quantifiers off a formalism only works for the first.

Then: which of these are non-negotiable and which are just what one camp wants, which pair can't both be satisfied (my hunch is that "the accessible evidence fixes the verdict" and "the verdict is unique and determinate" come apart, given two theories that agree on everything accessible and differ only in the hidden region, but make the case), and which single requirement rules out the most candidate methods.
