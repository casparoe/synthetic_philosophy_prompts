I have recently been offered a place in a clinical trial for a staged neural prosthesis: over roughly eighteen months, about two percent of a targeted region of my nervous system would be replaced at a time by synthetic tissue that replicates the input–output behaviour of what it replaces, until none of the original region remains. I want to work out, from the inside, which position about this I actually hold, and I would like a decision tree rather than a survey of views.

Start the tree from the one commitment I think every participant would share — that the replacement tissue could in principle do whatever the tissue it replaces does — and take it to a named position. Build the branching from the logical space first: six to eight questions, each a concrete case rather than a label, phrased so that someone can answer without knowing where it leads. Only then locate the familiar positions as leaves: biological or organism views, psychological-continuity views with and without a no-branching clause, functionalist or substrate-independent views, stage or four-dimensionalist views, degree-and-vagueness views, and the deflationary claim that there is no determinate fact here. Note which combinations of answers nobody occupies, with a sentence on whether that is because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply untried.

The cases I want doing the work are the ones that actually bear on my decision: the halfway point, at which half the region is synthetic; a variant in which the removed tissue is kept viable rather than discarded, so that at the end there exists both a wholly synthetic system and the matter of the original one, available for reimplantation; a variant in which the synthetic parts operate on a finer temporal grain than the biological ones, so that cognition continuously speeds up by a factor of about three over the course of the replacement; and a variant in which each stage is performed under general anaesthesia, with no experienced continuity between stages.

Flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot separate, and give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment. The audience is the small group of trial participants and their families, so the questions should be answerable in ordinary language even if the leaves carry philosophical names.
