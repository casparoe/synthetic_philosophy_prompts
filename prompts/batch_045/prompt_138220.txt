Setup: I run a monthly philosophy discussion group in a church basement. Eight to twelve people, mostly engineers and lab scientists and a couple of doctors, plus two or three who've done years of meditation retreats and are quietly convinced they already know what emptiness is. Ninety minutes, a whiteboard, nobody has read anything and nobody will.

I got into a three-hour argument about this last night with my friend Priya, a condensed-matter physicist, who is going to show up to the session and is going to fight me. Her line: emptiness just means everything is interdependent, which any systems biologist already accepts, so the whole thing is a banality dressed up in incense. My other friend Dave says the opposite — that the tetralemma is just refusing to answer, skepticism with extra steps, and that if nothing has a nature of its own then nothing matters. I told them both they were wrong, and now I have to actually run the thing.

So don't explain Madhyamaka to me. Plan how I explain it, in ninety minutes, in that room.

Specifically:

1. The order the ideas have to go in, and what depends on what. My guesses, correct me: first say what an intrinsic nature would have to be — what job it would have to do — before saying anything is empty of one; then the four-cornered denial as applied to origination (a thing doesn't arise from itself, or from something else, or from both, or from no cause at all); then emptiness as the upshot; then the two truths, late, because the moment I say conventional and ultimate everyone will hear two levels of reality, the visible one is fake and the real one is hidden, and I'll never get them out of that. Is that the right spine? Where am I wrong about a dependency — which step genuinely can't be understood before which, and why?

2. One example that carries the whole evening. Tell me what it has to have and what it has to not have. My instinct is a person or a self, but the retreat crew will here it as no self, I already knew that, and check out. Priya will want something from physics, and I think that's a trap though I can't say exactly why. The example also has to survive the move where someone says fine, it's just a convention, so nothing is at stake. What should it be, and what wrong example would wreck the session?

3. The misconceptions, in the order they'll actually come up out of that particular group, and the exact point in the sequence where I head each one off. The ones I can see: emptiness as nothingness or the void; emptiness as a special state of consciousness you can have on retreat; emptiness as it's all just labels so who cares; the four corners read as a truth table with funny values — Priya will definately ask whether this is just paraconsistent logic, and I don't know if I should give that two minutes or shut it down; the two truths as appearance versus reality; and the one that scares me most, that he denies everything so he holds no position and can't be argued with — Dave will say it, it'll sound like a cheap trick, and I won't have an answer. Also: can I get to the move where emptiness itself gets emptied in ninety minutes, or is that the thing to leave out?

4. What I should openly refuse to explain at this level, and say out loud that I'm refusing it, including the bit where I can see the retreat people wanting to go and the bit where Priya will want me to go.

5. How I know it landed rather than everyone just feeling vaguely nice. One question, or one thing I have them write on an index card. And Priya is the hard case: what would she have to say back to me that would actually convince me she got it, rather than just having mapped it onto something she already believed?
