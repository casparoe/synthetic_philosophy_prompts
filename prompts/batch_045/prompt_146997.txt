I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and I want a handout that takes a student from a concrete system to a verdict about two things at once: which stance — physical, design, or intentional — is the right one to adopt toward it, and whether the regularity we have found in it deserves to be called a real pattern rather than a gerrymandered or merely useful one. What I need is a decision tree, not a glossary. Every internal node should be a question about a case or a test that a student can answer without already knowing which stance it leads to — no node may simply ask whether the system really has beliefs, or whether it is "really" an agent — and every leaf should carry a name (or be marked as unnamed) plus one sentence about its most awkward commitment.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from the list of three names. Start from the diagnostic questions: whether the system's behavior admits any compression at all relative to a fixed coding scheme, measurement setup and timescale; whether whatever compression there is is available from the physical stance alone; whether it comes from attributing a function or goal together with the assumption of good design, without attributing beliefs; whether it requires attributing beliefs and desires and presupposing rationality; and so on. Then locate the familiar stances in the space those questions open up, and tell me which combinations of answers nobody occupies.

Some nodes I want included, though you should reorder, merge or add as the logic requires:

- A counterfactual or intervention test: does the pattern still hold if we push the system off its actual trajectory, or is it only a compact description of the sequence that in fact occurred? Curve-fitting and genuine structure should come apart here.
- An adversarial-redescription test: does the compression survive a change of coarse-graining, of loss function, or of who is looking — that is, is the pattern cheap to find, or does it take more work to locate than it saves once found? This is where gerrymandered pseudo-patterns should fall out.
- A horizon node, so that the tree can return "real but unforecastable": a system fully determined by its microdynamics whose pattern is nevertheless unavailable on any timescale on which we could act on it.
- A reflexivity node: cases where adopting a stance toward the system changes the system's behavior, so that the prediction is self-fulfilling or self-frustrating, and the cost accounting that selects a stance becomes unstable.
- A node for what I call borrowed patterns: the compression holds only because the system was optimized to reproduce the output of systems for which it is not borrowed — a model trained on text written by agents, or a controller trained by imitation learning. Is this a leaf of its own, or does it collapse back into the design stance once we describe the training setup?
- A node for what I call the mirroring condition: the cheapest description of the system is one that attributes to it the very stance we are taking toward it, so that system and observer are each modelling the other. Say where this branches and what it does to the idea that stance adoption is a one-way instrument.

Leaves that must appear somewhere: physical stance only; design stance; intentional stance; no exploitable pattern (noise); gerrymandered pseudo-pattern; real but unforecastable; self-fulfilling or self-frustrating target; borrowed pattern; the mirroring case; and a degenerate leaf for cases where two stances tie in predictive cost and the choice between them is pragmatic only. Name the leaves that have names in the literature and mark the rest unnamed.

Two further things matter more to me than completeness. First, flag the pairs of leaves your tree cannot separate and say why: I expect trouble with genuine intentional patterns versus borrowed imitations of them, and with the design stance versus the intentional stance for systems that are optimizers trained on human-produced data. Relatedly, mark the point at which the question "which stance is cheapest to adopt" peels apart from "is the winning pattern real," and either add the question that would separate realism about stance-relativity from instrumentalism about it, or argue that no question of the predictive-cost kind can. Second, give me a table of the unoccupied combinations your nodes permit, each with a one-line diagnosis: incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored.

Size it at roughly eight to ten internal nodes and ten to fourteen leaves. Assume a mixed audience of philosophy and cognitive science graduate students who know the three stances as common currency but have never seen them turned into a routing procedure, and write each node as a question they could answer about a system sitting in front of them. Finally, trace two or three cases all the way through the tree — I would suggest a thermostat or other simple artifact, an ant colony or slime mold, and a neural network fine-tuned to imitate human text — showing the branch taken at each node, so I can check whether the tree actually routes cases the way we would want it to.
