I am putting together a one-page handout for a graduate reading group on whether the brain is a computer, and I want a decision tree rather than a survey. Please build it from the logical space first — the questions and the combinations of answers they permit — and only then locate the actual views inside it.

Every branch node should be a question about a concrete case or claim that someone with no stake in the debate could answer without knowing where it leads. I want the following distinctions to do the splitting, though you may add or rephrase:

1. whether "the brain computes" is meant to require that it computes the specific function that explains what it does well, or merely that it computes something or other, which any physical system arguably does;
2. whether the posited computation must be recoverable by intervention — disturbing the system and checking that the intermediate states track the ones the model posits — or whether a model that simply predicts neural activity from stimuli well enough will do, even when its internal units correspond to nothing in the tissue;
3. whether the computation has to be indifferent to what realises it, so that the same programme could run in silicon or in a room full of clerks with pencils, or whether the wet, analogue, energy-hungry details of the medium are permitted to carry explanatory weight;
4. whether the right level of description is the spiking cell, the population, or something coarser, and what becomes of the view when the level shifts;
5. whether running the computation is claimed to suffice for having a mind, to be necessary for it, or merely to be the most useful vocabulary we currently have for describing the organ;
6. whether that vocabulary is doing genuine work or is a façon de parler eliminable in favour of laws stated directly over neural quantities.

Aim for roughly seven branch questions and ten to twelve leaves. Name each leaf by its doctrine — the classical or symbolic view, connectionism, mechanistic neurocomputation, the dynamical-systems alternative, embodied and enactivist rejections, the deflationary "everything computes" reading, instrumentalism about computational description, and whatever else belongs — rather than by author or paper. Mark the combinations of answers that nobody occupies and say briefly whether that is because the view is unstable, merely unfashionable, or simply unexplored. Finally, flag any two leaves your tree cannot separate, and supply a further case of your own devising — not one from the literature — that would prise them apart.
