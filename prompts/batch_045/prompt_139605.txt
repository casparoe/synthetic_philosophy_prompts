Hey — I help run a half-day workshop for newly qualified psychiatrists and two clinical ethicists on advance directives, and I want to replace the handout we currently use (which is basically just a table of the three or four named views) with an actual sorting quiz people can take before the discussion, so they find out what they believe from their own answers instead of from the label they'd pick if asked directly. The case that got me thinking about it: a man in his early sixties with early-onset Alzheimer's who had written a directive at forty saying no aggressive treatment if he ever needed full-time care, and who is now, by everyone's account, the happiest person on the ward and keeps saying he wants to stick around to see his granddaughter graduate. Our team split almost exactly down the middle and I realised I don't have a stable view of my own — I keep going back and fourth between "of course the document governs, he meant it when he was the one who had to decide" and "why on earth are we privileging the guy who wrote it over the guy who's actually living it".

So: build me the quiz, twelve questions, three options each, something a trainee can finish in about fifteen minutes, and then a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the actual positions rather than onto personalities. I want the thing built from the logical space first — the handful of questions that really separate views (does it matter whether the earlier self had full information, whether the later self is cognitively impaired at all, whether the directive expressed something enduring or something fleeting, whether anyone else would ever find out about a breach, whether a well-chosen surrogate who now disagrees changes anything, whether the earlier self knew themselves well) — and then the named positions located inside that space, with any combination nobody occupies flagged and a one-line guess as to why (incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored). The one I keep staring at is "the earlier self has full authority over a later self who is completely competent, fully informed and has no impairment whatsoever" — nobody seems to be there, and I want to know whether that's a deep incoherence or just a gap. Please make sure these leaves all show up: the strong precedent-autonomy position where the document wins even against current welfare; the narrower version where only directives expressing enduring evaluative commitments bind and ones expressing mere experiential preferences don't (this is the Dworkin-ish critical-versus-experiential-interests move, I think it's Dworkin, correct me if not); the epistemic-but-defeasible view that the document is presumptively binding because the earlier self was a better judge of my values and less compromised, but can be overturned by new information; the view that it's evidence and nothing more; something like a commitment-device or promising-based view where the bindingness comes from the practice of holding people to their word and from what breaches do to the institution; a relational or joint-authorship view where the patient, the surrogate and the clinicians are co-authors and the document is mostly a tool for that relationship; and the gradualist one where authority fades with psychological continuity instead of switching off at competence. Each leaf gets one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Two more things. Every question has to be a concrete case someone with no philosophy training can answer, phrased so that you can't tell which position it's a probe for — no asking "do you think precedent autonomy is absolute", and please don't let the whole thing collapse into eleven variants of the dementia case. I want cases with no cognitive impairment at all in the mix: someone who changes their mind while fully competent but acutely anxious or a few drinks in, a directive written at nineteen by someone who badly misjudged what they'd care about, a directive based on a factual belief the author would certainly have corrected, a religious-conversion case where the later self wants the earlier secular document torn up, a directive from someone who was severely depressed when they wrote it and now says it wasn't them, one where no one would ever know if it were quietly ignored, and one where the named proxy is still alive and well and says the document has been overtaken. Also flag any two leaves your quiz genuinely can't pull apart, and tell me whether that's fixable with a thirteenth question or whether they're just the same position wearing two hats. And give me a rule for what to do with mixed or tied scores, because in my experience half the room will answer case-by-case and land nowhere clean.
