I'm giving a talk next week at the internal research seminar of a longtermist grantmaking org, and I want a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense of the view.

The thesis I'm defending: the simulation argument should *dampen* fanaticism about the far future rather than amplify it. I know the popular move goes the other way — if there are vastly many simulations then there are vastly many more minds, so the stakes look even more astronomical. My claim is that this gets the relevant quantity wrong, because expected value depends on what I call *accessible* stakes: roughly, the number of minds whose fates actually covary with what I do, times the size of my per-copy causal share in each. Simulation hypotheses mostly inflate the first factor while shrinking the second, and they do something worse to a third thing. Two named mechanisms do the work:

1. The compute-splitting horizon effect. If a civilization has a fixed compute budget for simulations, it can run many short sims or few long ones, and there are more ways to be a short sim; and if you think you should expect to find yourself in a typical sim, then conditional on being simulated, the *remaining* runtime of your world is probably short compared to the billions-of-years future longtermists plug into their estimates. So the far future isn't mostly unreachable for the usual reasons (we might die, we might screw up), it's unreachable because there may not be one.
2. Copy-dilution. If sims are run in large batches with near-duplicate agents, then whatever I do is shared across many near-copies, and my causal share of any downstream outcome is diluted roughly like one over the number of relevant copies — so multiplying the number of minds doesn't multiply the thing my action is levered on.

Net effect on my telling: the product that enters an EV calculation for, say, an x-risk grant goes *down* under simulation hypotheses, enough that the possibility of being simulated is a reason for less fanaticism, not more.

The room, so you can guess who says what: two senior research staff who have spent years building astronomical-stakes estimates and are invested in the "even a tiny chance of affecting 10^50 lives dominates everything" framing; one decision-theory-inclined person who already dislikes fanaticism and Pascal's-mugging-ish reasoning and will want to know whether I've really said anything simulation-specific; two grant-facing people who think simulation talk is unfalsifiable metaphysics and only care what changes in the portfolio on Monday; one person who is happy with simulation reasoning but thinks simulators interested in long-run outcomes would run few long sims rather than many short ones; and a visiting philosopher who is a general skeptic about anthropic and observer-selection arguments.
