I'm a junior at a public high school, and I've been sitting in on a Hobbes reading group that a nearby college's philosophy club runs on Sunday afternoons — I'm the only person there who isn't a university student, so I mostly listen. Last week two of the regulars, Devon and Ana, got into an argument I can't stop thinking about, and at the end they turned to me and asked me to settle it, which I obviously couldn't. The question they were arguing about was whether Hobbes's obsession with geometry is load-bearing for his political theory or just an eccentric hobby that the argument of Leviathan could easily do without. It went roughly like this. (1) Devon: geometry is load-bearing. Hobbes says somewhere that geometry is the only science God has seen fit to give us, and he treats the laws of nature as theorems rather than as mere advice, so he plainly thinks his political conclusions have to be *demonstrated*, not asserted. That's what the whole design is for: if the sovereign's title were just one more opinion, each subject's private judgment would re-enter and we'd be back in the war of all against all. Only a demonstrative science yields conclusions nobody in their right mind can refuse, so the politics borrows its authority from the geometry. (2) Ana: that conflates the method with the subject matter. What Hobbes actually uses in Leviathan is the resolutive-compositive method — take the commonwealth apart into individuals, then rebuild it out of the state of nature plus the laws of nature. None of that needs circles or Euclid. His actual geometry, on the other hand, is a private hobby with a public humiliation attached: the failed attempts to square the circle, the decades-long fight with Wallis over whether algebra counts as mathematics, his treating a point as a body whose magnitude is simply being ignored. Method is detachable; the hobby is not load-bearing. (3) Devon: the method isn't neutral, though. Hobbes requires a good definition to say how the thing is *generated* — a circle by the motion of a line — and he thinks reasoning is a kind of reckoning, adding and subtracting, which only makes sense if everything is body and motion. When he defines the commonwealth as generated by covenant, he is applying the same constructive requirement, and when he calls the laws of nature theorems he is claiming the same demonstrative status for them. And what's supposed to make a demonstration *compel* assent is its publicity — anyone can walk the steps — which is a straight geometric ideal. So the metaphysics he was defending against Wallis is the same metaphysics the politics runs on. (4) Ana: even granting all that, you've given me a story about how he *arrived* at the theory, not about what supports it. More importantly, Hobbes's politics has a feature geometry can't have: in geometry, once definitions are fixed the demonstration stands forever, whereas Hobbes's sovereign gets to decide which doctrines may be taught and what counts as law. If the political conclusions were demonstrable the way a Euclidean proposition is, you wouldn't need an enforcer. The fact that you do is evidence the demonstration isn't doing the work. (5) Devon: that's backwards, and it's the interesting part — call it the definition-fixing regress. Demonstration only compels if the starting definitions are settled, and Hobbes's own life shows they aren't: he and Wallis disputed the definitions of a point and of indivisibles for decades and no proof ended it. So the sovereign isn't a fallback for failed demonstration; the sovereign is what makes demonstration possible in the first place, by settling definitions. Geometry and absolutism are entangled after all, just not in the way I said in (1). (6) Ana: if that's the argument it's self-undermining. Hobbes's behaviour in that very dispute shows demonstration failed to compel *him*, in the one science he thought was certain, with his reputation on the line. So either geometric demonstration compels only the already-obedient, in which case it adds nothing to the sovereign's authority, or it fails to compel even at home, in which case there's no model of certainty to export. Either way, invoking it in politics is rhetoric. (7) Devon: that's an ad hominem about Hobbes's psychology; a stubborn practitioner doesn't refute a method, or else Wallis's sarcasm refutes algebra. And there's a difference between what a demonstration can do in principle and what it does to someone who won't follow the steps. (8) Ana: one last substantive point, and then I'll drop it — the equality of persons in the state of nature. Hobbes's claim that the weakest can kill the strongest reads like an empirical generalisation, but I think it also functions like his account of a point: an abstraction in which you treat the individual as a unit and decline to consider magnitude. If equality is really that kind of stipulation then the *content* of his politics is geometric after all and my (2) was too quick; if it's an empirical claim then the demonstration rests on a contingent fact that a different world would falsify and isn't a demonstration. I suspect he needs it to be both and can't have both.

Then tell me who is more right overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing about what "geometry" could have meant to Hobbes.
