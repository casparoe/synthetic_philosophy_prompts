Bit of an odd request, but here goes. I'm on a tiny internal review panel at my university, and we've got enough money for something like three postdocs for two years, nominally in philosophy of machine learning. One of the proposals that's actually good is on mechanistic anomaly detection, which I'd been half-joking about with a friend in the CS department for ages before I saw it in writing. The rough idea: you have a system that behaves acceptably on some reference distribution, and you want to flag the cases where what you're seeing is being produced by a *different* mechanism than the one that produced it before — after a fine-tune, out of distribution, under some weird prompt, whatever. What struck me reading it is that the whole enterprise seems to presuppose an answer to a question nobody ever states out loud: when are two mechanisms the same mechanism? And if the answer is "it depends on the grain you pick" or "it depends on what you care about" all the way down, then it's not clear an anomaly is anything more than a difference someone happens to care about, which would make the detection problem circular in a fairly boring way. I'm in philosophy rather than ML, by the way — I teach the occasional philosophy of science course — so I can referee the conceptual part and not much else.

I won't paste the proposal itself (I probably shouldn't), but here's the taxonomy I scribbled in the margin, which may or may not be right. Call (A) the intervention-profile view: two mechanisms are the same just in case intervening on the corresponding components yields the same pattern of counterfactual differences in the output. Call (B) the role-in-a-model view: same just in case the component occupies the same node in a causal model of the system's computation that has been independently validated for the task you care about. Call (C) the transport view: sameness only gets defined relative to a deployment envelope — mechanisms count as the same when intervening on one lets you predict what intervening on the other will do across the situations you actually plan to act in, and outside that envelope there's no fact of the matter. Then there's a wrinkle the proposal skips entirely: what if there is no single reference mechanism to begin with, because two training runs of the same architecture at different random seeds implement the same behavior through different internal structures? Then anomaly detection can't be "divergence from the mechanism you had at training time," since there was never one thing to diverge from — maybe it has to be divergence from a *family*, and then you need to say what binds the family.

What I want from you is an agenda, not a survey and definitely not a reading list. If you had those three postdocs and those two years, and they were philosophers — decent formal tools, essentially no compute budget, and only limited ability to run interpretability experiments themselves or to lean on the literature — which of the questions in this neighbourhood should they actually spend the time on, and in what order? I care much more about the reasoning than the list. For each item: what makes it upstream of the others, how tractable it is for people who can't run the experiments, whether it's genuinely neglected or already being chewed on by interpretability researchers who don't realise they're doing philosophy, and what kind of result — somebody else's empirical finding, a clean negative result, a sharpened distinction — would move it up or down the list over the next couple of years. Please also say plainly if you think my three-way taxonomy is a bad way to carve things up and the real crux is something I haven't listed; I'd rather find that out now than wether or not the panel likes it.
