I am finishing the final section of a survey chapter on aggregation under infinity, and for a workshop session I am staging an adversarial collaboration between two positions that I believe have largely been talking past each other. I need the document such a collaboration would produce. Please do not adjudicate between the positions and do not supply a bibliography or citations: reconstruct the arguments from the cases below and from first principles, defining any formalism you use, since what I want to test is the argumentative structure rather than the sources.

The two positions, glossed neutrally (rename them if either side would reject my label, but keep my characterisation of what the view commits its holder to):

Position A, the extended-total or infinitarian view. Betterness among worlds is in the first instance a relation among total amounts of value. When a world contains infinitely much value, the total must be represented by a quantity in an extended ordered structure — a non-Archimedean field, a lexicographic pairing of an infinite magnitude with a finite residue, or some equivalent — so that differences at infinite scale and differences at finite scale are both representable, with infinite-scale differences taking lexical priority. Consequences the holder accepts: a density change across an infinite world is an infinite-scale improvement or deterioration; a finite blemish in an infinite world registers, but only as a finite difference, negligible next to any infinite-scale one; and under uncertainty, a nonzero probability of an infinite-scale difference dominates finite considerations, which generates both fanaticism and, when infinitely many infinite-stakes hypotheses with opposed signs are in play, paralysis.

Position B, the finite-region comparativist view. There is no such thing as the total value of an infinite world, or at any rate betterness does not track it. Betterness is settled by the pattern of value inside finite regions: either by eventual dominance (world X beats Y iff, for every sufficiently large region of a canonical exhaustion, the value in X's region exceeds the value in Y's) or by limiting density (X beats Y iff the average welfare per cell converges to a higher limit). Note that this camp has an internal fork it should surface rather than paper over: dominance and density come apart, and the collaboration should say which variant is speaking and whether the fork is itself a crux.

To keep every claim checkable, stage the dispute on toy cosmoses: a cosmos is an infinite lattice of cells (index by Z^3, one being per cell, each assigned a real welfare level). An exhaustion is a nested sequence of finite regions whose union is the whole lattice. Both camps should state their verdicts on these cases, and — this is the part I most want — each side should write down, in advance, what it predicts the other side will say about each case, so that genuine surprise is possible.

The Cone and the Cylinder. In cosmos W, welfare is +2 inside the double cone x^2 + y^2 ≤ z^2 and +1 elsewhere. Under an exhaustion by balls of radius n centred at the origin, the share of +2 cells tends to the cone's solid-angle fraction, 1 − cos 45° ≈ 0.29, so average welfare tends to ≈ 1.29. Under an exhaustion by cylinders of radius sqrt(n) and half-height n, the share of +2 cells tends to 1, so average welfare tends to 2. Both exhaustions are nested, eventually cover every cell, and are geometrically natural. So W is worse than a uniform cosmos at welfare 1.5 under the first exhaustion and better than it under the second; the same flip appears for the dominance variant, since the value difference over a region diverges with the sign of the density difference. Each side must say whether the choice of exhaustion is a moral question or a physical one; whether the actual cosmos supplies a canonical exhaustion (contrast a Big-Bang cosmology with a privileged cosmic time against an eternal steady-state cosmos or a multiverse with no global time slice); and what the view says if no canonical exhaustion exists. I also want each side to address the symmetric worry: whether A's extended totals are invariant under re-coordinatisation of the lattice, given that a hyperreal or ultrapower construction is typically built over a chosen index set and filter.

The Deposit and the Head Start. Two perturbations of the uniform +1 cosmos. In the Deposit, a single cell is replaced by a cell of welfare −10^6. In the Head Start, one extra unit of value sits in the first epoch, so the cumulative value inside any sufficiently large region is exactly one unit higher than in the uniform case. Ask: does either perturbation make the world worse (or better), by how much — finite, infinitesimal, zero — or are the worlds incomparable? I expect the crux here to be whether a finite difference registers at all, and whether registering finitely is enough to make it negligible against any infinite-scale difference.

The Flicker. A cosmos whose average welfare over the first n cells oscillates forever between 1.2 and 1.8 (say, blocks whose lengths double each cycle), so no density limit exists. Compared against a uniform cosmos at welfare 1.5, what does each view say? If the answer is incomparability, is that a cost of the view or a correct verdict, and does the view have a principled tie-break (lim-inf, lim-sup, a lexical rule) or does it fall silent?

The Grant. A real-scale choice under uncertainty about cosmic infinities: fund an intervention worth about 10^5 units of value for sure, or fund a research programme with roughly a one-in-a-million chance of permanently doubling the value density of a far future whose spatial extent is unknown and which may be infinite. Give each side's recommendation and say whether the two can co-sign any practical rule — for instance, a rule that finite aggregation governs whenever no infinite-scale difference is at stake, plus a stated fallback for when the expected infinite-scale difference is undefined.
