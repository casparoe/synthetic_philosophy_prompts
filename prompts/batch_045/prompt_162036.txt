i research cases for a podcast where the host, a self-declared Humean who has read the section on miracles too many times, rules on one claim per episode. His track record so far:

- a healing at a pagan temple, written up two centuries later by an author who wasn't there: laughed off, ignorant age, nobody attested it while it happened
- a weeping statue in a poor village, thousands of pilgrims, shaky phone footage: rejected, mutual excitement and the thrill of wonder, and not one hostile witness in the crowd
- a terminal cancer vanishing between scans at a secular hospital, agnostic doctors, patient had been praying to a saint: rejected as not even a candidate, since no law of nature was violated, only our medicine; he did leave that file open though
- two rival religious communities each with well-attested healings backing incompatible doctrines: both rejected, said they cancel each other
- an eighteenth-century cure at a famous tomb, dozens of educated contemporary witnesses, looked into by magistrates at the time: still rejected, but only because every witness turned out to belong to the sect and the skeptics were writing second-hand
- a private apparition reported by a single elderly nun of good character with nothing to gain: rejected, one witness is not corroboration

He keeps repeating, roughly, that no testimony can establish a miracle unless the falsehood of the testimony would itself be a bigger marvel than the event.

The file I've just dug up: in 2003 at a district general hospital with no religious connection, a man in his fifties is certified dead after 38 minutes of asystole on a monitored bed, ECG trace saved, two doctors sign, body tagged and put in the mortuary fridge. The fridge has an electronic access log and it shows a single entry four hours later, by an attendant who is in a rationalist society. The man is found breathing, recovers, remembers nothing, joins no church, and nobody makes a penny off it. So there's telemetry, a fridge log, two signatures, and a local-paper report a week later — but the two doctors later said only that they couldn't explain it, and one had written "cause unclear, presumed death" on the form. No religion is claiming him and no rival miracle story competes with it.

What will he rule, and what is he actually running: "uniform experience is a proof and nothing outweighs a proof", the falsehood-of-the-testimony comparison, a witness-quality checklist (educated, honest, known, public, would be caught if lying), or the rival-claims-cancel rule? More than one of those fits his past verdicts equally well, and they come apart here — so tell me what each predicts about this case, which one you'd bet on and why, and since I'm the one picking the cases, what file I should hand him next to actually flush out which rule he's using.
