I'm closing a philosophy of biology seminar next week, and I'd like to end the last session with a poem rather than a summary. The final two meetings were on whether DNA is literally a code — a text, a program, a set of instructions — or whether that is a metaphor we have mistaken for a discovery, and the discussion kept sliding between three claims I want the verse to hold apart rather than blend: the mapping claim, that there is a decodable correspondence of roughly sixty-four triplets onto about twenty amino acids plus stop, arbitrary in the sense that it could have been otherwise and is said to be a frozen accident, degenerate in the sense that different triplets yield the same product so the sequence cannot be read back off what it produced; the causal-correlational claim, that one stretch carries information about another merely by covarying with it, the way tree rings carry information about rainfall; and the semantic claim, that the sequence is about something, or prescribes it, or says it, in something like the way a sentence does. My half-formed version of the worry — improve on it if you can — is that the string has punctuation it does not contain: where to begin, where one word ends and the next begins, which of the three possible frames is the reading frame, which stretches are cut out before anything is made, and even which of the two strands is being read, are all settled by machinery that the string itself also codes for, so that message and reader are built out of the same material and neither sits outside the system in a position to say what it means. There are stretches, too, where the same sequence is a message in one frame and nonsense in another, or is read in opposite directions on opposite strands, so that talk of the message is indeterminate until someone specifies a frame.

For the form: about thirty-six to forty-eight lines in tercets, three strong beats to a line, so that each stanza reads as a triplet. Pick a fixed set of six end-words and let each of them close two different tercets, so that quite different stanzas land on the same end-word and the reader cannot reconstruct the stanza from its product. Open with a short line that returns once, near the middle, as a start signal, and close with a single short line as a stop. In the middle third I want the frame to slip: let the line endings fall one word late for several tercets, so the triple rhythm the reader has learned is still audible but displaced and the sense comes apart, and then restore it by dropping a single word, the way a compensating insertion or deletion restores a frame. The first third should build the naive picture, the genome as a written set of instructions that the cell reads; the slip is where that picture breaks; the last third should commit to whichever reply you think is genuinely strongest — that the cell is not reading but making, or that aboutness here is fixed by selection history and proper function, or something better I have not thought of — but not a both-sides shrug, and no new vocabulary after the slip that the first two thirds have not earned. No names, dates, titles or reading lists: my students have not read the literature, so the poem has to carry whatever it knows, legible to someone who has had only the one session and right enough that someone who works on this would not wince.

After the poem, add a paragraph saying which reply you committed to and why, and where the poem fudges either the biology or the philosophy. And if the brief itself is off, say so before writing anything: if you think the question is merely verbal, or that my line about punctuation the string does not contain conflates the mapping question with the reading-frame question, or that the frameshift device cannot be made to work in English triple meter, tell me plainly and tell me what you would do instead. I would rather have an argument about the framing than a compliant poem built on it.
