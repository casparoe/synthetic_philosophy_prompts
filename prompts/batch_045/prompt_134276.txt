i teach a second-year epistemology seminar — 14 students, one 80-minute slot — and they've done Gettier, reliabilism vs internalism, and the basic "knowledge isn't just true belief" material, but nothing yet on the self-verification / easy-knowledge problem. I want an in-class game that makes them feel *in their gut* why bootstrapping is supposed to be illegitimate, without me ever saying the words "circular" or "bootstrapping" up front. They should build the argument themselves, step by step, and only notice at the end what they've assembled.

Rough mechanic I have in mind (swap it for something better if you like, but keep the spine): small teams each get an instrument — a "gauge" — that answers yes/no questions about hidden cards. Key wrinkles:
1. The gauge only answers questions it has already been asked once, so its track record is always one question behind the one you actually need it for. Students have to decide whether to bet on the current question using a record built entirely out of past answers from the same gauge.
2. There's a self-test round where the question is literally about the gauge's own reliability, and the gauge answers it.
3. Teams must write a one-line justification for each bet on a slip, and a rival team can challenge it: if the justification mentions the gauge, the challenging team steals the points.
4. Payoffs in points, no money, abstaining costs more than being wrong.

Then, partway through, reveal that the gauges really were reliable all along, and still ask them whether anything they did gave them a reason to think so. Close with a short auction: how many past successes would you need before you'd assert "this gauge is reliable," and why is that number not obviously zero?

Please give me: materials list (cheap, no tech beyond cards/envelopes/dice), the rules, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a scoring scheme. Then a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the specific steps of the argument (gauge says p; gauge was right before; so gauge is reliable; so p), and where each step could be resisted and what it costs to resist it there.

Things I care about: the point should be that no single step looks bad on its own — the objection is about the shape of the whole thing, not a faulty premise — and that the problem isn't unreliability (my gauge is reliable! that's meant to feel unhelpful). Ideally they also end up seeing that the same shape shows up when you check your senses using your senses, so blocking it costs you either something skeptical or the thought that justification has to be citable.

Don't build the activity on the standard cases (the clairvoyant who trusts her own clairvoyance, the driver checking the fuel gauge by looking at it) — I want those to land as illustrations in the debrief, not be the setup. Invent the game instead.

Also: tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time i run it (my bet is someone says "this is just ordinary induction, what's the fuss" and someone else notices the two gauges agree and starts reasoning about a common maker), how to handle each, and be honest about which feature of the problem the game can't stage and I'll have to just say out loud.
