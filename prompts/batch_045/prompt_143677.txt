I'm the course assistant (basically a glorified TA) for our third-year philosophy of language seminar, and the lecturer has asked me to draft one essay question for the practice final plus a model answer and a marking scheme — and I'm stuck on how to make it hard in the right kind of way, because every version I write turns into "state deflationism and contrast it with the correspondence theory," which everyone can do from memory in their sleep.

Background on what the students actually have, so you can build inside it: we did five weeks on truth. We covered the minimalist/deflationary thought that the whole nature of truth is given by the instances of the schema ⟨p⟩ is true iff p (or the sentential version); the point that truth-talk earns its keep when we do blind ascription and unbounded generalisation (everything the pope says is true, when you have no idea what the pope has said) and in mixed inferences that hop between using a sentence and mentioning it; the norm of assertion, i.e. assert only what is true, and whether a deflationist can say anything non-trivial about it; and one week on whether truth is really a property at all. Zero formal semantics, no Tarski hierarchy, no liar paradox, no possible worlds — so please don't lean on anything outside that vocabulary, and flag it if the question you write secretly does. Also, half the class are joint-honours students who did the readings in a different order, so the lecturer has a hard rule that a question can't turn on remembering who said what or which paper a move comes from. I know this literature has names for everything, but don't write it so that naming them earns marks, and no reading lists or citations anywhere in the model answer.

The thing I want the question built around is a dilemma my flatmate came up with at about 1am last night (she does maths and absolutely would not let it go). Suppose the deflationist is right that the instances of the schema fix everything there is to say about truth. Now define a new predicate, "is schmue", by the mini-schema ⟨p⟩ is schmue iff (p, and p is not about money). Every instance of that is true, and it gives you perfectly good disquotation for the overwhelming majority of sentences you'd ever want to use it on. So: either schmuth is just as good as truth, in which case deflationism hasn't told us anything about *truth* in particular, or there's some job truth does that schmuth can't do — unbounded generalisation, mixed inference, whatever — in which case the nature of truth isn't exhausted by the schema and the deflationist has gone substantive after all. I think that's a genuine dilemma but I'm not sure where the burden of proof sits, and I really don't want the question to just be "explain the dilemma": I want students to have to take a side and defend it. I'd also like the question to force a connection to something else we did — I was thinking the norm of assertion, or the blind-ascription role — so that a top answer has to say whether one single resource (the unrestricted generality of the schema? the inferential role? something normative?) deals with both worries at once, or whether they come apart and the deflationist has to pay twice for them.

What I need from you: (1) the question itself — a sentence or two of setup plus the actual prompt, phrased the way our lecturer does it, i.e. terse and slightly intimidating; it should be answerable in 45 minutes, since our exams are two hours for three questions. (2) A model answer of roughly 700–900 words that argues for one side rather than sitting on the fence. (3) A marking scheme out of 20 with four or five must-hit moves and the marks for each, explicit bands for a first / a 2:1 / a 2:2 / a fail, the single move that separates the top band from the one under it, and for each must-hit point a one-line note on the way people usually get it wrong. (The mistake I can already see coming is someone just writing "schmuth is defective because it's restricted to non-money sentences" and stopping — I want that to earn something but definitely not a first, and I want the scheme to say exactly why not.)
