I have no formal background in philosophy and only high-school probability, and I've been puzzling over something on my own for a few weeks. I came across it in a popular-science book on probability, and I can't tell how much of what it said I should trust.

The book's chapter on self-locating belief summarizes the Sleeping Beauty problem roughly like this:

"On Sunday researchers put Beauty to sleep and toss a fair coin. If it lands heads they wake her once, on Monday. If it lands tails they wake her on Monday, give her a drug that erases her memory of that waking, and wake her again on Tuesday. Beauty knows the protocol, the amnesia is perfect, and when she wakes she cannot tell which day it is. She is asked: how confident are you that the coin landed heads?

Halfers answer one-half. She knew all along that the coin was fair and that she would certainly be woken at least once, so waking up tells her nothing she did not already know: the only thing she gains is the thought 'I am awake now,' which says where she is in the world, not how the coin fell, and there is nothing to update on. Thirders answer one-third. Run the experiment a hundred times and two-thirds of all the wakings occur in tails runs, so on any given waking the coin is twice as likely to have landed tails as heads. The entire dispute comes down to that single question — whether waking up is new evidence — and it makes no practical difference: if Beauty is offered a bet on heads every time she wakes, a halfer and a thirder compute the same fair price, which suggests the argument is really only about what the word 'probability' ought to mean."

Could you go through that passage and tell me what someone who has spent real time on this problem would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply mistaken? I can't locate the mistakes myself, though I have hunches, and I would rather know which of my hunches are worth keeping.

My instinct is with the halfers — a fair coin is a fair coin, and counting the same tails run twice feels like cheating — but the frequency argument does seem to show something, and I cannot see where it goes wrong.

Three further things I would like checked:

1. My friend says thirding must be wrong, because it implies that if the setup were changed so that a tails result produced a million wakings, Beauty should be all but certain on waking that a fair coin had landed tails, and nobody should believe that. Is this a genuine reductio, or does it only restate the original disagreement in a louder form?

2. I tried to neutralize my fair-coin intuition by changing the coin to one that lands heads two-thirds of the time, keeping heads at one waking and tails at two. If I have done the arithmetic right, the frequency-style reasoning then gives one-half while the halfer has to say two-thirds. Have I got that right, and does this variant decide anything, or is it the same dispute in a new costume?

3. Someone told me the two camps also disagree about a case the book never mentions: what Beauty should think if, after waking, she is told that today is Monday. I make it that a halfer should then be two-thirds confident in heads while a thirder should be one-half. If that is right, the disagreement has consequences after all and cannot be merely verbal. But I would like to know whether I have the numbers right, and whether this really is a fair test or whether I have smuggled in an assumption.

Finally: if the summary is wrong in the ways I suspect, does its conclusion — that nothing is at stake — survive the corrections? And if the disagreement that remains is genuine and unsettled, tell me plainly what the crux is and what an honest non-expert should end up believing: one of the two answers, or neither.
