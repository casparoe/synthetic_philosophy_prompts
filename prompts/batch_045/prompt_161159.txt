My two housemates got into it the other night and I've been turning it over ever since. It started when Grant said that putting a probability on a mathematical statement is confused: either the axioms entail it or they don't, nothing chancy is going on, and "probably true" is just a confession that you haven't finished the derivation. Mira said that's like saying you can't be uncertain about tomorrow's weather because the microphysics settles it. Grant said no, the weather case has a genuine unknown in it, the exact microstate, whereas with a math statement there's no unknown at all, everything is already fixed by the axioms, so any number other than 0 or 1 is just noise dressed up as precision.

Then it got more interesting. Mira said: take a randomly generated 3-SAT instance with 400 variables. Is it satisfiable? You have no idea. But if someone hands you an assignment you can check it in milliseconds. So there's a real asymmetry between finding and checking, and your confidence should creep along while you search and then jump to certain the moment a certificate shows up. Whatever governs that creeping can't be "be at 0 or 1," because you can't get to 1 without doing work and you can't know in advance how much work. (1)

Grant said that concedes his point, sort of: the creeping isn't tracking evidence about the world, it's just tracking how much of a search you've completed, and the honest description of that state is "not yet known," not "0.43." (2)

Grant then went on the offensive with a coherence argument: if you post odds of 0.43 on a statement and it's false, a bookie who can do the derivation you couldn't has a guaranteed way to take your money, so the only unexploitable odds are 0 and 1, rationality requires unexploitable odds, and being too slow to do the derivation isn't a defence against a normative requirement. (3)

Mira's reply was that this only bites if the bookie can actually settle the question, and in the cases that matter the bookie is in the same boat and has to compute too. So the real requirement should be something like: no trader that is itself computationally bounded, and that has to find your mistakes within the time you both have, can make unbounded money trading against your sequence of prices. She said that's satisfiable but far from trivial, and that it forces your prices to converge to the truth on questions you eventually settle without ever telling you to sit at 0 or 1 meanwhile. She added that there's an external check on good prices here: across all the logical questions where you say 0.7, roughly 70% should come out true. That's calibration, and it's checkable, not just etiquette. (4)

Grant's last move, which I thought was his strongest, was that this changes the subject. Unexploitability-by-a-bounded-trader is a property of an entire price sequence over time, and lots of sequences have it, including "say 0.5 about everything and jump at the last second." If that counts as rational, the norm is too weak to be a norm, and there is no fact of the matter about what your confidence in an unproved conjecture should be, only facts about what's useful to say. (5)

Mira said that particular sequence is exploitable after all: a trader with the same amount of compute as you can just run a solver, notice which of the questions it can settle quickly, and buy those from you at 0.5. A blanket policy like that leaves money on the table precisely where you had the ability to know better. (6)

I didn't referee it and honestly couldn't. Can you go through the six moves and rate each one from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification each, and then say who's more right overall and what both of them are missing? The thing I care about is whether there's a genuinely seperate question they're both talking past — whether "how confident should I be" is even the same question as "what odds should I post," given that the person posting odds is the one whose future computation is part of the picture.
