I edit a small satirical supplement that runs alongside a rationalist-adjacent newsletter. The readership is people who have been reading the blogs and forums for a decade or more, who know what is meant by safety-via-debate, and who have each, at some point, lost an afternoon to the obfuscated-arguments problem. I'd like a piece for them.

The piece: the abridged Annual Report of the Federal Bureau of Adversarial Legibility, an agency in a world where the debate proposal has been fully institutionalized as the basis for regulatory and judicial fact-finding. Two AI advocates argue over a claim in an argument tree, cross-examining each other's sub-claims, and a bounded human judge — paid by the word, working inside a statutory adjudication window — decides which advocate won. A conclusion the Bureau has certified this way is a Certified Legible Conclusion, and the Bureau's founding doctrine, the Ashgrove Postulate, holds that honest claims are cheaper to defend under sustained cross-examination than dishonest ones, because the liar must maintain a coherent alternative world.

I'd like the report to be built around four pieces of invented bureaucratic machinery, all of which should be treated as obviously sensible by the narrator:

the statutory adjudication window (the ninety seconds a judge may spend on any leaf of the tree);
Adversarial Parity Certification, which requires equal compute for both advocates, and so grants the honest side a matching obfuscation budget it is expected not to use;
Recursive Referral, under which a sub-claim the judge cannot resolve is not decided but referred to a fresh debate, indefinitely, with the growing registry of permanently undecided leaves reported as maturation rather than backlog;
and the Compression Warranty, the statutory axiom that any genuine answer can be compressed into a form a median judge can verify, so that legibility can serve as the operational surrogate for truth (the Bureau tracks L-scores).

The jokes have to come from getting the mechanism right, not from inventing new silliness. Someone who has actually thought about the proposal should recognize each absurdity as an exaggeration of a real commitment: cross-examination as the only enforcement mechanism; the honesty equilibrium holding only where truth is cheaper to defend; a judge too weak to re-run the work himself; and the standing worry that the scheme may select for arguments that are easy to judge rather than arguments that are true. I want one deadpan passage where the Bureau meets the genuinely hard case — a question whose answer requires a long computation nobody can compress, say which of two candidate molecules binds a target more tightly — and handles it procedurally rather than substantively, without ever noticing what it has done.

Also: one passage in which the Bureau's Chief Methodologist makes the strongest sincere case for the scheme, so the satire stays fair; one outside critic quoted only to be dismissed in the Bureau's own jargon; and no moment where the narrator winks or breaks character. A reader who disagrees with the report's premises should still be able to tell what is being ironized. Invented officials only, and nothing that turns on a named living person, a real lab, or a real paper.

Format: headed sections with a sidebar and a few footnotes, 900 to 1,100 words, in the bloodless register of an agency proud of its own metrics. Everything you need is specified above, so please don't reach for citations, reading lists, or anything requiring web access — the humor should rest on the mechanism as I've described it.
