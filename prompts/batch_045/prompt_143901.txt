I write a weekly column on science and ideas, and the word I fight about most with my editors is "know." I want a house rule I can actually run in the ten minutes before I file, for when a sentence is allowed to say that someone knows something, versus that they believe it strongly, versus that the evidence points to it. Sorry if this is rambly, Im on deadline.

Here's the machinery I want it built on, since generic hedging advice is useless to me. A Nozick-style tracking picture says roughly two things: sensitivity — if the claim were false, the person wouldn't be believing it — and safety, or adherence — across the nearby ways things could have gone, they'd believe it only if it were true. And tracking doesn't survive entailment: a source can know the sample was contaminated without knowing that the study's headline result is junk, because in the closest world where the result is junk they'd believe it anyway. For a writer that means knowledge claims have a travel distance: every inferential step away from what the source actually tracked has to be paid for again. And it means two claims I'm each allowed to assert separately can't necessarily be asserted together, since a conjunction can fail sensitivity even when both conjuncts pass on their own.

So: give me the checklist in order, the test at each step, what counts as a yes, where it just stops with a no, and where it hands the call back to a human because it's a values or legal question rather than a tracking one. Then run it on three sentences of the kind I actually write: "the team knows the effect is real" (from one preregistered study with a p-value near the threshold); "the agency knows the water is unsafe, and therefore that the 2019 tests were wrong"; and "we now know X" where the newsroom's belief is assembled out of a reporter's interviews, one anonymous source, and a preprint nobody has replicated. Include at least one case where the honest output is that I can't use the word, even though the sentence would read much better. And flag where the procedure breaks down or quietly assumes what it's meant to test — I'd rather have the seams showing than a tidy rule.
