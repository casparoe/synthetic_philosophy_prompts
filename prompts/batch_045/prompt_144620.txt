I'm in an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of religion this term, and there is one line that keeps doing an unreasonable amount of work in it: "out of nothing, nothing comes" — my professor often gives it in the Latin, ex nihilo nihil fit. It has turned up in three separate places so far, and I suspect it is not expressing the same thesis in all three:

1. As the intuitive support for the kalam argument's first premise — that anything that begins to exist has a cause of its existence — where it is offered as something we can know independently of having surveyed actual causal histories, since the case it is meant to cover is one nobody has observed.
2. As the reply to "but then what caused God?": we are told the principle concerns only things that begin to exist, and God does not begin to exist, so the question never gets off the ground.
3. As a way of dismissing cosmological models on which the universe arises from a quantum vacuum or from "nothing at all": the vacuum is not nothing, we are told — it is a structured, law-governed state — so such models do not describe anything coming from nothing in the relevant sense.

What I want is a precisification rather than an explanation of the arguments. Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses the tag could be expressing, ordered from the near-trivial to the very strong, with a verdict on each: platitude, substantive but defensible, or actually false. I'd like three axes of variation kept explicitly separate rather than run together: (i) what "nothing" ranges over — the absence of matter and energy inside an already existing spatiotemporal and nomological structure, versus the absence of any concrete state of affairs at all, including laws, causal powers, and facts about what would bring about what; (ii) what "comes from" means — efficient or material causation, as opposed to some non-causal relation of grounding or explanatory dependence of the sort the contingency argument trades in; (iii) what the principle's quantifier covers — things that begin to exist, contingent things as such, events, states of affairs. For each of the three uses above, tell me which reading is required for the slogan to do the work being asked of it there, whether those readings can all be held at once, and which single reading my professor is probably invoking.

The part I most want your judgment on is an objection I've been trying to develop myself. It seems to me the slogan has to earn its keep in one of only two ways, and neither is comfortable. Either it is an a priori or metaphysically necessary truth — in which case it is hard to see why it should be restricted to things that begin to exist, and the exemption in use (2) looks manufactured. Or it is a generalization from observed causal histories — in which case every member of the sample is a case of something coming from something, from pre-existing stuff or at least from a law-governed prior state, and the principle then gives no purchase on a case with no pre-existing material or nomological context at all. Worse, on the strong reading of "nothing" in (i), the principle seems to collapse into the claim that no cause could operate on nothing — which is perfectly compatible with things coming to be uncaused, and so cannot by itself establish that a beginning requires a cause. Is this dilemma sound? If not, which horn is avoidable, and how?

Two further wrinkles, if you can take them. First: even granting the restriction in use (2), what does "begins to exist" mean — having a first instant of existence, having a finite past, or coming to be out of some prior state of affairs? If the kalam conclusion is that time itself has a first instant, and the slogan is read as requiring the cause to be temporally prior to its effect, then the principle cannot be satisfied by the very case it is invoked to explain, unless we allow simultaneous causation or read "cause" so weakly that the reading collapses into the grounding reading in (ii). Second: my professor mentioned in passing that in its oldest philosophical uses the tag expressed a claim about generation requiring a substrate, so that strictly speaking it sits badly with creation out of nothing. Is that right, and if it is, does it make the tag an odd thing for a theist to lean on, or does it only show that the tag has changed its meaning?

Please write this as a conceptual map I can bring to office hours: I don't need citations or a bibliography, I can find those myself. At the end, say how confident you are in your verdicts overall, and single out the one claim you are least sure of.
