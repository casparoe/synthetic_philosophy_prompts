I have just come away from a long and unresolved argument with a friend about the situationist critique of virtue ethics, and I would like your help with the precise place where it stalled.

My friend's claim was that the familiar experimental results — seminarians stepping past a slumped stranger when they were in a hurry, and people who had just found a coin in a phone booth being markedly more likely to help someone who dropped their papers — show that there are no global character traits of the sort virtue ethics requires, and that once virtue ethicists retreat to traits indexed to narrow classes of situations they have abandoned the substantive thesis rather than defended it. I replied that the retreat may be no retreat at all: if a virtue like practical wisdom partly consists in a fine-grained sensitivity to the morally salient features of situations, then variability across situations is what a virtue ethics should predict, and the experiments look less like refutations than like unflattering portraits of people who are not very perceptive.

My friend then made two moves I could not answer. First, a gerrymandering objection that struck me as structurally the same as Goodman's new riddle of induction: any finite behavioral record can be captured by indefinitely many situation-indexed trait predicates that fit the record and diverge thereafter, so that if local traits are left unconstrained the theory explains everything and predicts nothing. Second, even granting that some local traits are genuine, nothing in the observed pattern tells us whether an agent's variability is responsiveness to reasons or mere stimulus control; from the outside, a virtue and a habit keyed to an arbitrary trigger look exactly alike.

I floated a reply in the argument, and I want to know whether it can bear the weight I put on it. Call it the projectability condition: an ascription such as "helpful in unhurried, uncrowded circumstances" is admissible only if its eliciting class can be specified in terms that let a third party classify a situation the agent has never been in, so that the ascription yields predictions about novel cases, including cases designed after the ascription was made. This, I suggested, is what separates a trait from a redescription of a record — and it is what would let the dispute be settled by evidence rather than by intuition.

Please explain, at some length, what is really doing the work in my friend's gerrymandering objection, whether the projectability condition blocks it, and whether a view built on projectable local traits still earns the name of virtue ethics, or whether it has quietly become a theory of stable behavioral signatures and lost the specifically ethical claim that character unifies a life and grounds an ideal of practical wisdom. If my friend and I have each mislocated the disagreement — if, for instance, the dispute is not really empirical at all but a disagreement about what character talk is for — say so, and say why.

Two requests about form. Please write in continuous prose, the way you would in a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. And open by telling me, in a sentence or two, which of us you think is closer to right, so that I know where the rest of your answer is headed. Somewhere between 700 and 900 words.
