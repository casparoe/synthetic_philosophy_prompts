I have spent the last several months trying to decide whether to have a first child, and I tried to do it the way I decide everything else: list the options, attach to each outcome some measure of how good living through it would be for me, and compare. The attempt fell apart in a way I did not expect, and the argument I built afterward to explain why it fell apart now looks to me as though it establishes far more than I am willing to accept. I want to know where to cut it and what the cut costs.

(1) A choice between options is rationally assessable only if the agent can, from her current perspective, assign each outcome a utility representing how good living through that outcome would be for her. Where she cannot, the choice is rationally indeterminate: no option is more rational than any other, and there is nothing left for deliberation to adjudicate.

(2) How good an outcome would be for me is fixed by what it is like to live through it. So if I cannot now know what it would be like to live through an outcome, I cannot now assign it a utility.

(3) What it is like to live through an experience is unknowable in advance whenever the experience is phenomenally novel, in the sense that it involves some qualitative feature I have never encountered. Descriptions, testimony, and imagination assembled out of my existing stock of experience cannot supply it; the only way to acquire it is to have it. This is the Mary's-room point generalized from colors to everything.

(4) Phenomenal novelty is not rare. The first month in a job in an unfamiliar field, the first winter in a city I have never lived in, the first mouthful of a fruit I have never tasted: each contains qualitative features I have never encountered, and each is capable of shifting what I want.

(5) Therefore ordering an unfamiliar dish and deciding whether to become a parent stand or fall together. Both are rationally indeterminate. In both, tossing a coin is as rational as weighing reasons, and anyone who deliberates carefully about either is engaged in a procedure that cannot be performed better or worse.

(6) That is not credible. We deliberate about unfamiliar jobs, cities, and meals constantly; the deliberations are not all pointless; and some of them reach conclusions that are plainly correct and plainly better than the alternatives available.

(7) So at least one of (1) through (4) is false.

Two further things I want accounted for, because they are where I suspect the real work is. First, the distinction I invented to save the phenomenon: I want to separate phenomenal novelty from what I will call evaluative novelty. An outcome is evaluatively novel for me just in case living through it would likely restructure my preferences in a way I cannot now rank, as opposed to merely teaching me a new qualitative fact while leaving my ranking of the features it realizes more or less intact. My hope is that only evaluative novelty matters decision-theoretically, that evaluative novelty is rare and concentrated in the big cases, and that this is why (5) is absurd. My worry is that the distinction collapses back into (2): whether a novelty restructures my preferences seems to depend on how I will feel about the new quality, which is exactly what I cannot know in advance.

Second, the escape route that dominates the recent literature, associated with L. A. Paul's Transformative Experience (Oxford, 2014), according to which I should stop trying to maximize expected utility in these cases and instead choose for the sake of discovery: rank the options by how much I want the revelation each offers about who I would become. I find this attractive, and I also suspect it borrows from the bank it robbed, since wanting the revelation is itself a preference about an outcome I cannot value, and the self I would become might not endorse the discovery. The same worry applies to staged probes such as a weekend of babysitting or a trial month abroad: as (3) is stated, the only way to acquire the knowledge is to have the experience, so the probe should be useless; but if (3) is weakened enough for the probe to inform me, has it not also been weakened enough for testimony to inform me, at which point the argument collapses?

If I must choose, I would rather keep (3) and (6) than give them up; (1) has always struck me as an idealization and (2) as a premise that could be weakened without being abandoned. But I want the cut made honestly, not where it is cheapest for me.

Please answer in five titled sections, in this order.

Diagnosis, roughly 250 words: name the single step at which the argument fails, or, if you think it is valid, the premise you would drop, and explain why that step looked sound enough to pass unnoticed.

The price list, roughly 450 words: take (1), (2), (3) and (4) taken together, and (6) in turn, and say what giving each up costs. For (1), what becomes of rational choice if it goes: can decision rules designed for severe uncertainty, such as interval-valued or imprecise utilities, multiple priors, or ambiguity-sensitive rules, restore determinacy, or do they only relocate the indeterminacy one level up? For (2), by what route, if any, can I attach a value to an experience whose character I cannot grasp? For (3) and (4), where the line between the transformative and the ordinary actually falls and whether my phenomenal/evaluative distinction is the right instrument for drawing it. For (6), what biting the bullet would really involve, with coin flips for dinner and for children alike.

The discovery proposal, roughly 250 words: whether it escapes the argument above or depends on the premises that generate it, what it assumes about my future endorsement, and what it implies about staged probes.

Applied to my case, roughly 200 words: what your diagnosis implies about how I ought to go about deciding whether to have a child. I want the procedure, not the verdict.

What stays open, roughly 100 words: the part of the decision that remains genuinely indeterminate once the diagnosis is in place.

I am not asking for readings, citations, or a survey of who has said what; I want the structure of the argument and the prices paid at each step.
