I am rebuilding the opening session of a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of dispositions, and I want to replace my opening lecture with a diagnostic. What I am asking you for is a classification instrument: a decision tree that takes a student from their verdicts about concrete cases to a named position on what disposition ascriptions come to, together with the battery of cases that drives the tree.

Purpose and audience. The students will have met only the simple conditional analysis — roughly, that for a thing to be disposed to give response M under stimulus condition C is for it to be the case that it would M if it were in C — and nothing beyond it. The instrument has to be usable cold, with no access to the literature, so define every term of art you rely on and stipulate the cases in full rather than alluding to them. Attach proper names to leaves only where you are confident of the attribution; where you are not, label the leaf by its characteristic move and say that you have done so. Assume the whole thing has to fit on a two-sided handout: at most seven branch questions, between eight and ten leaves.

The battery. Construct the cases before the tree. Each must be a concrete object-and-situation that a student can pronounce on without recognising it as a case of anything, and each must offer verdicts rather than labels as options — for example: the disposition is present; it is absent; it is present only if the stimulus condition is read as including the absence of interfering factors; the case is underdescribed and I need to know the causal basis; I cannot answer without a view about what the stimulus conditions are. Where it matters, add a second verdict: the manifestation would occur, versus the manifestation would occur but not in virtue of the disposition. The battery should include, at minimum:

- a plain fink, in which the stimulus removes or destroys the disposition before it could manifest;
- a plain mask, in which some condition prevents the manifestation while the disposition persists, plus a variant in which the preventing condition is intrinsic to the object and one in which it is merely nearby, so that the case bears on whether dispositions are intrinsic;
- a mimic, in which the disposition is absent but the manifestation would occur anyway, whether because the stimulus creates the disposition or because an independent mechanism produces the manifestation;
- a coincidence case: a masked disposition whose manifestation would nonetheless occur, but by an independent route, so that the counterfactual comes out true for the wrong reason;
- a delayed fink, in which the stimulus initiates the manifestation and only then destroys what the disposition depended on;
- a self-dissolving mask, in which the masking condition is itself removed by the stimulus and the manifestation follows after an interval;
- a disposition whose stimulus condition is never physically realisable;
- a disposition that is present but permanently and inescapably protected.

The tree. Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then the actual positions located in that space, then the unoccupied combinations, each with a one-line diagnosis of whether nobody is there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. The distinctions I want the tree organised around are: whether dispositions are intrinsic; whether a genuine manifestation must proceed from the disposition's basis; whether the stimulus condition may itself mention the absence of interferers; whether dispositions admit of degree; and whether the account is reductive or takes dispositional modality as primitive. Among the leaves I expect: the simple conditional analysis combined with a denial that the fink and mask cases are counterexamples; a revised conditional analysis on which the basis must be retained and must, with the stimulus, account for the manifestation; a view on which dispositions are had in virtue of a basis together with a conditional; a view on which masks and finks are built into the stimulus condition and dispositions are accordingly extrinsic and multi-track; a gradable view on which the hard cases are handled by thresholds; and at least one view on which dispositions are not analysable into conditionals at all. Any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate should be flagged as inseparable rather than split by an invented question.

Format each branch node as a question a student could answer before knowing where it leads, and give each leaf a label, one sentence stating the position, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Close with a brief note on which single question does the most classificatory work and which case in the battery, if any, no position handles cleanly.
