I am a novelist, and I would like your help with a difficulty that began as a problem of craft and has turned into something closer to a problem of philosophy.

My current manuscript is set in a fictionalized Italian city-state of the 1490s. Its protagonist, the daughter of a minor notary, talks her way into a diplomatic commission for which she has no training, survives a plague winter, a botched assassination, and the collapse of her patron's regime, and ends the book as the effective ruler of the city. A rival character makes a comparable gamble in the same season and ends on the scaffold. My trouble is not the plotting. It is that I cannot settle what the book is asserting about why one of them is alive and the other is not.

At a writers' retreat last spring, every attempt I made to raise that trouble was closed with the proverb "Fortune favors the bold." It was made to do two quite different jobs. My historical consultant used it to establish that my protagonist's survival is earned rather than lucky — that I have written a book about boldness being rewarded, and should stop hedging and let the book say so. Another writer used it in a colder direction: since the rival also gambled and died, his death must show that his boldness was counterfeit or insufficient, and so I owe the reader an early scene in which his nerve visibly fails. I am convinced that both uses are a form of cheating, but I cannot say precisely where the cheat is, and until I can, I do not know what my own ending claims.

Two distinctions I have invented while drafting may help you see what I am after. I have begun sorting the chances in my plot into gate chances and mirror chances. Gate chances open or close a course of action irrespective of the character's qualities: the flood that takes the bridge, the dispatch whose rider is thrown, the assassin's horse that shies. Mirror chances are those in which the world's response hands an identical disposition back to its owner under two different names — my protagonist's refusal to wait for instructions is called judgment if the city holds and recklessness if it falls, and nothing in the refusal itself marks the difference. My suspicion is that "Fortune favors the bold" is a sentence that can only be uttered after the mirror has already spoken, and that it therefore cannot be doing the work my retreat colleagues wanted from it. But I can also imagine readings on which it says something perfectly respectable and quite independent of that problem: something about sample sizes, since the bold take more shots and so supply more of the successes anyone bothers to count; something about the shape of the payoffs, since a strategy of frequent small losses and rare enormous gains can be rationally preferred in some environments and ruinous in others; or something about other people, since visible confidence recruits allies and defections, and the mechanism by which fortune favors the bold may simply be the expectations of the bystanders.

What I would like is a menu rather than a verdict. Please set out, as a numbered list running from the most deflationary to the most committal, the distinct precise theses that "Fortune favors the bold" could be expressing. For each reading, say what accepting it would commit a person to, and give your verdict on it: trivial, platitudinous but true, substantive and defensible, false, or incoherent. Then, for each, say whether it would actually support either of the two uses the proverb was put to at my retreat — the claim that my protagonist's survival is earned, and the claim that my rival character's death proves a failure of nerve — or whether it leaves both of them unsupported.

Close with a short paragraph on a separate question. Machiavelli's own contrast between virtù and fortuna, as I understand it, is roughly that fortune governs something like half of what befalls us while leaving the remainder to be met by preparation and force, and that fortune behaves like a destructive river which floods ruinously where no embankments have been built in advance. Is there any reading of the proverb that sits comfortably inside that contrast, or is the proverb a degeneration of the contrast into something the author of that river image would have rejected as superstition?

Please write in American English, and please do not supply sources, reading lists, or citations: I am not equipped to check them, and I want the analysis itself. I intend to carry your list into a craft essay, so I would rather have the full menu with verdicts attached than a single reading argued for at length.
