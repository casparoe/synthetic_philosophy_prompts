My friend Miriam and I spent most of last evening arguing about whether anything can be recovered about Jesus of Nazareth from the Gospels, and we ended up circling rather than disagreeing cleanly, so I want a sharper description of what she is actually doing. She thinks a real figure can be recovered and that a short list of tests does the recovering. I think the tests get applied one at a time and always come out wherever the person applying them already wanted to be.

These are the rulings I pinned her to. Jesus' baptism by John: authentic, because a church that proclaimed Jesus as Lord would hardly have invented a story in which he submits to someone else's rite. The crucifixion under Pilate: as certain as anything gets. The cry of dereliction from the cross: not authentic, because it is too neatly composed out of the opening of Psalm 22 — embarrassment value notwithstanding. The prediction that no stone of the temple will be left on another: authentic, on the grounds that it turns up in Mark, in John, in Acts and in the charges at the trial, and that it explains why the movement collided with the temple authorities. The prohibition of divorce and the instruction that a person's word should simply be yes or no: authentic, because the later church softened both, and traditions do not get stricter as they go. The Lukan saying that the kingdom of God is already present within or among you: rejected as the evangelist's editing; she holds instead that Jesus announced a kingdom about to break in from above, and that the spiritualised, deferred version is a later adaptation. The long Johannine discourse in which Jesus calls himself the bread of life: rejected, and here her reasoning inverts — it is too exactly the theology of the later church to be earlier than it. The virginal conception and the infancy stories: rejected, although she concedes the Greek is Semitic in flavour, on the grounds that style can always be imitated. Parables that appear only in Luke, such as the good Samaritan: she declines to rule at all, calling them possible but unprovable.

So here is the case I want your prediction on. Suppose a papyrus fragment is published next month, provenance unknown, dated by its handwriting to the early third century, containing a saying found in no canonical gospel. In translation it runs: do not pray that the kingdom may come; pray that you may not be put to shame when it comes, for it comes like a snare on everyone who watches for it, and those who keep watch by night will be the last to see it. The Greek is translation-Greek built out of matched parallel lines, and a very similar saying appears in Syriac in a Christian work of roughly the same period, which may or may not depend on the Greek.

What will she say — authentic, inauthentic, or unprovable — and what is she running? Please name the two or three rules that fit her whole track record equally well, say what each of them predicts about this fragment and exactly where they come apart on it (note that the saying is at once embarrassing relative to the church's own prayer practice, stocked with imagery the church used everywhere, flattering to an apocalyptic Jesus, and attested only late and perhaps only once), give me your single best guess with the reason for preferring it, and close by specifying the one further case you would put to her to tell the surviving rules apart. Under 700 words, and please don't point me at anything to read — I want the reasoning, not sources.
