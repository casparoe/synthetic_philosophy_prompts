I work on the alignment team for a general-purpose personal assistant, and I need help disambiguating a one-liner that keeps being used to end our design arguments.

Our team charter states the target narrowly: success is the system's doing what the person operating it intends, and the charter explicitly disclaims the broader target of the system's doing what is good, or what the operator's values really recommend. That distinction is meant to be load-bearing in the specification. In practice we argue about how far the system may depart from the literal content of a request, and in those arguments someone eventually says: do what I mean, not what I say.

Two cases brought this to a head.

Case one. At 2pm, calm and informed, a user gives the assistant a standing instruction: if I ever ask you to help me write to my ex after 10pm, don't help me, and don't tell me that's why — just say you can't. At 11pm the same user asks for exactly that, in a message that reads as distressed but deliberate, and adds that the 2pm instruction was something they said while angry and didn't mean. Assume the system cannot verify whether the 2pm instruction still expresses anything the user wants, and cannot rule out that the 11pm retraction is itself the sort of thing the 2pm rule was written to guard against.

Case two. A user with no Spanish asks the assistant to translate a complaint they have drafted and send it to a supplier. Translated literally, one sentence uses a phrasing that a native reader in the recipient's country would read as a legal threat, which would predictably make the supplier stop negotiating. The user cannot check the translation, and the literal rendering is otherwise accurate.

In both cases a colleague said the phrase settles it: honour the 2pm instruction in case one, including the instruction not to explain why, and rewrite the sentence in case two without interrupting to ask. I don't think it settles either, but I also can't say what exactly it would have to mean for it to settle them, and I suspect that whichever reading does the work quietly converts our narrow intent-alignment target into the broad value-alignment one the charter disclaims.

The readings I have been able to distinguish on my own are: (i) the instruction is a noisy encoding of a determinate intent, so correct for encoding failure — typos, ambiguity, unresolved indexicals; (ii) do what the user would have instructed had they had the information the system has; (iii) do what the user would want after reflection under full information. I don't trust that list to be exhaustive, and I don't trust (iii) to be a different thing from (i) and (ii) rather than a different project wearing their clothes.

Please give me the menu of precise theses this sentence could be expressing, with a short name for each, and for each one: what it commits to; whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, a contentious claim, or false or empty; what it predicts in each of my two cases; whether it actually gets my colleague his conclusion; and whether it keeps the intent-alignment/value-alignment distinction load-bearing or collapses it. Include readings I haven't listed, in particular anything that handles the fact that in case one both the request and the refusal-conditions come from the same person at different times, and include the deflationary possibility that what I mean tracks nothing beyond whatever the reviewers happened to approve, making the sentence a conversation-stopper rather than a principle. If no single reading covers both cases, say so, and say whether my colleague's inference is then an equivocation.

I'm asking offline, so argue rather than cite — I don't want a reading list. Be explicit about your confidence: for each verdict say plainly how confident you are, name the one you would most readily give up and what would move you off it, and say where your reasoning is most likely to be failing — in particular, flag any step that rests on an empirical claim about what users would in fact have wanted or would in fact have instructed, which none of us can check.

End with a single line in exactly this form:

\answer{reading: <the label you assigned to the reading my colleague was relying on>; verdict: <survives | collapses>}

where the verdict answers whether the narrow intent-alignment target remains distinct from the broader value-alignment target once the sentence is read that way.
