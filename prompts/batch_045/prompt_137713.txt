So I was at lunch with two colleagues after a talk, and they got completely stuck on whether an LLM is an agent, a simulator, or something else entirely. I'm not going to tell you which one I agreed with because I genuinely couldn't tell — please referee.

Mara opened: (1) Look at what these systems actually do. Give one an objective and it decomposes it, picks tools, notices when a step fails, and routes around the failure. That's flexible means–end organisation, which is the functionalist core of agency. If you want to deny it, you owe an account of what's missing, and "its made of transformers instead of neurons" isn't one.

Teodor: (2) What you're looking at is text continuation. The system emits the most plausible next stretch of a text in which some competent character pursues that objective. The flexibility belongs to the simulated character, not to the system. Here's the tell: perturb the context slightly and the "goal" just evaporates, because there's no persisting conative state — only a context-conditioned prediction. And it will simulate confusion, refusal, giving up, or a completely different character with equal ease, which is not what something with a goal does.

Mara: (3) Two things. First, your evaporation test is one that nothing passes: people drop goals under reframing, fatigue, bad moods, hypnosis. Persistence is a matter of degree, and how much of it you get depends on memory, which is an engineering question, not a metaphysical one. Second, the "it'll play any role" point proves too much — a human actor can play Macbeth or play at being indecisive, and that doesn't make her un-agentive. Also, the distribution it samples from was itself shaped by reinforcement fine-tuning for helpfulness, so the "simulator" is downstream of someone optimising hard for goal-achievement.

Teodor: (4) The actor case doesn't transfer. With the actor there's a second-level agent who chooses to take the role; here there's just a prompt and a forward pass, no one home choosing. And your RLHF point is a fact about causal history, not about current structure — a flight simulator is built by engineers who optimise it to fly well, and it doesn't fly. What's really missing is that nothing in the system loses anything when the goal fails, and nothing in it carries over to the next episode. The behavioural coherence is carried by the transcript, i.e. by scaffolding outside the system. Agency is supposed to explain persisting, self-maintaining behaviour; here the scaffolding does all of that work.

Mara: (5) "Loses something" and "carries over" are doing a lot of work. The weights were literally modified because trajectories in which the assistant's plan failed were downweighted during fine-tuning — that's about as close as a learning system gets to being changed by failure. And "nothing carries over" is just amnesia. Someone with dense anterograde amnesia who can only act using a notebook is still an agent; we just say the agency is spread over person-plus-notebook. Same here: model plus context window plus memory store is an agent in exactly that distributed sense.

Teodor: (6) Distributed agency is fine by me — but then the agent is the whole loop: the weights, the harness, the memory store, the deployment, the user who keeps prompting. The bare model isn't, and the bare model is what we were arguing about. In the amnesia case there's still one persisting organism with stakes who writes the notes and reads them. Here there's nothing that has stakes, and I think being the kind of thing that can be worse off is part of what agency means.
