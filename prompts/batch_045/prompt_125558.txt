I am a senior at a large public high school in Ohio, and I run the philosophy club, which is holding its last meeting of the term next week on whether any present-day AI system could properly be said to have beliefs. I have been asked to open the discussion with about five minutes of framing remarks, for an audience of classmates most of whom have heard of the Turing test and not much else, and I would rather get the shape of the argument right than string together slogans. The trouble is that the two people whose judgement I trust most seem to flatly contradict each other, and I suspect that means someone has misdescribed the options.

My government teacher, who did some philosophy at university before she went into teaching, told me that the intentional stance gives us a clean criterion: if the most efficient way to predict a system's behaviour is to treat it as a rational agent with beliefs and desires, then the system really does have those beliefs and desires, and since we predict large language models best by asking what they take to be true and what they are trying to do, we are committed to saying that they have beliefs. A friend of mine in the club says the opposite: on his reading the whole point of the stance is that it is merely instrumental, a useful projection onto something with no inner life whatsoever, so the question whether a model really believes anything does not even arise; and he adds that we should have abandoned the stance for these systems the moment we noticed that a model will happily assert that the meeting is on Tuesday in one exchange and that it is not on Tuesday in the next, which no one who genuinely believed either thing and cared about getting it right could do. What I cannot see is how to decide between them, and I would like three things explained: first, whether the stance is best understood as a test that discovers beliefs, or as a description of a strategy that can be useful whether or not there is anything to discover, and if it is the latter whether that makes my friend right that our debate is empty; second, whether a breakdown in prediction is really the right way to kill an intentional ascription at all, given that people are inconsistent about what they claim to believe all the time and we do not on that account stop calling them believers; and third, whether believing something requires the believer to have anything at stake in being right, since these systems have no projects of their own and cannot be surprised.

There is also one thing I have noticed that neither of them addresses, and I do not know whether it is a curiosity or the crux. When I use one of these systems, the ascriptions that predict best are not ascriptions to the model as such but to a kind of character: I do far better asking what the assistant I am talking to thinks than asking what the network thinks, and that character's commitments drift from conversation to conversation in a way no person's do. This makes me wonder whether the stance is landing on the model at all, or rather on a simulation of indefinitely many different agents, so that asking whether the system has beliefs is like asking whether a novel has beliefs. Could you say plainly where my teacher and my friend have each gone wrong, what distinction I should be drawing instead of the one they are fighting over, and how I might put the real question to my classmates in five minutes without either pretending the answer is obvious or leaving them with the impression that nothing is at stake? I am not asking for readings or sources, just for the argument.
