Now the context and the idea. I am a retired chemical physicist. Here is the half-formed idea I want turned into a proposal. The standard disagreement, as I have encountered it, is between those who reduce the direction of time to a thermodynamic gradient plus a boundary condition, and those who treat temporal directedness as primitive and independent of thermodynamics. Both sides, it seems to me, talk as if there were a single thing, the arrow, which either reduces or does not. But in my own field one never gets an entropy without first choosing a partition of the state space into macrostates: the gradient is a gradient of some coarse-grained entropy, and different choices of macrovariables give different gradients, and in principle gradients that do not agree. In practice, for the coarse-grainings available in a laboratory, they overwhelmingly agree, and we simply help ourselves to that agreement and speak of the arrow. I want to make the agreement itself the object of study. Call this 'coarse-graining relativity': the claim that there is a family of gradient-like asymmetries, one per admissible macrostate partition, and that the appearance of a single direction of time is the appearance of their coincidence.

Two further thoughts hang off this. The first I call the 'accessibility restriction': the partitions that matter are not arbitrary mathematical partitions but those whose variables a creature embedded in the universe could in fact measure and manipulate with the resources available to it at that epoch, and my suspicion is that a structural fact about which partitions are thus accessible does more explanatory work than the boundary condition alone, and perhaps explains why the boundary condition looks less ad hoc than it is often said to look. The second is what I call the 'plateau case'. In the familiar symmetric picture, entropy increases away from a low-entropy temporal minimum in both directions, and creatures on either side call the low-entropy side their past. But suppose the minimum is not sharp: suppose there is a temporally extended region across which the relevant macro-entropy is very nearly constant, with only small fluctuations. Then locally there is no gradient to speak of. Does the direction of time there merely become weak, or does it go indeterminate, or do local fluctuations produce small, transient, mutually disagreeing arrows that flicker? What would such a region be like from the inside, given that we are supposed to explain memory, records, and causal asymmetry by appeal to the gradient? If the plateau case is coherent, then the direction of time is at best a regional matter, and I think that is a real result rather than a curiosity.
