I'm a senior at a large public high school, and I run our philosophy club — about a dozen of us, mostly AP students, with a faculty advisor from the English department who did some graduate work on Dennett and keeps nudging us toward his vocabulary. Our last meeting of the year is on a pairing he suggested: the two inversions you get when you set Darwin and Turing side by side. Darwin's version: the appearance of design gets explained by a slow, blind, algorithmic process, so the old inference from a designed-looking thing to a designing mind gets stood on its head rather than confirmed. Turing's version: a mechanical procedure turns out to do something only an intelligent agent was supposed to be able to do, so the task never required understanding in the first place — competence without comprehension. We've read the obvious background, so please don't spend your words re-explaining the two inversions to me; assume I have them and go further.

What I actually want is a poem to close the session with, and I want it built around a disagreement we got stuck on that I don't think is in the readings at all. Half of us read the inversions as *subtractive*: nothing spooky is left over, comprehension was never part of the machinery, and the felt sense that a mind must be behind the performance is our own projection, an extra we keep adding because we can't see the gears. The other half reads them as *coronating*: comprehension is real, but it arrives last — something the process eventually earns and then gets retroactively credited with, an effect that looks like a cause because it showed up at the end and is the only part we can see from the inside. We also stumbled onto a worry that cuts the other way and never got it sorted: if the inversions show that comprehension is optional for competence, do they also hint that competence is optional for comprehension (the commentator who fully understands chess and loses every game)? I'd like that reverse worry to appear once, as a single image, and then be left alone — not answered.

Formally: a sestina, six stanzas of six lines plus a three-line envoi, roughly ten syllables a line, with these end-words — hand, mind, blind, reason, design, child (swap one if you can justify it). The point of the form should be the point of the poem: I want the first half to read as if it were composed by something that understands what it's saying, and the turn to land in the envoi, where the reader realizes the rotation was doing the work the whole time — that the machinery generated the meaning, not the other way around. The envoi should carry all six words and should not settle which of our two readings is right; it should leave them side by side and let the compression of the last three lines be the thing that makes the choice feel undecidable. Two constraints on tone: one club member is religious and the Darwin half makes her suspicious, so no sneering and no altar call either way; and someone who has never heard of either inversion should still be able to read it as a poem about something. Please take this on as both a philosopher who works on this material and a poet who genuinely writes in fixed forms. Think it through before you draft: tell me in a few sentences which end-word you're giving which idea, where exactly you're putting the turn, and which image you're using for each inversion — then give me the poem, and after it a short note marking the one line where the inversion actually happens and which of our two readings each half leans on.
