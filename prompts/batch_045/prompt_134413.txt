I'm in a decision-science reading group and we've spent most of a term on the Ellsberg stuff, and I've come away feeling that the argument hasn't actually moved since the 60s, just gotten more notation. So I want a diagnosis, not a verdict.

Here's where we are. Standard setup: urn A is 50 red / 50 black, urn B is 100 balls in unknown red/black proportion. Most people prefer betting on red from A to red from B, and also prefer betting on black from A to black from B. No single probability for red-from-B can make both of those true, so under Savage-style subjective expected utility the pair is inconsistent. Three camps:

1. The coherence camp: the pattern is a mistake. Any agent whose preferences can't be represented by a probability plus expected-utility maximization is exposed to some combination of sure-loss-over-time, preference reversal under re-framing, and planning inconsistency — and the fact that you can't name the prior doesn't mean you don't have one.
2. The axiomatizers: it isn't a mistake, it's an attitude toward uncertainty that SEU simply can't represent, and it can be axiomatized just as respectably — maxmin expected utility over a set of priors, Choquet expected utility with a non-additive capacity, smooth models where you have second-order probabilities over the possible biases and a separate ambiguity-aversion parameter. On this view the real question is empirical: estimate the capacity or the aversion parameter, don't legislate it away.
3. The debunkers: there is no stable ambiguity attitude. The effect shows up when you evaluate the ambiguous option next to an unambiguous one and mostly evaporates when you evaluate it on its own; it tracks how competent the agent feels about the domain, and a lot of it is really a belief that the guy running the experiment might have stacked the urn, or a discomfort with having your ignorance made salient by comparison. Uncertainty aversion, on this view, is a name for a family of artifact.

Everyone agrees on the choice data. Nobody moves. I've tried two probes on my group and they didn't settle anything either, but they might help you see what I'm after:

Probe 1 (homemade ambiguity): you fill urn B yourself, in the lab, using a randomizer whose output you cannot track — you watch yourself put the balls in, you know nothing is rigged, and you still don't know the proportion. There's no adversary in the causal story at all. If the aversion survives, the "it's a belief about deception" diagnosis is dead and camp 3 has to retreat to the comparative-ignorance version; if it dies, camp 3 is basically right and camps 1 and 2 have been theorizing about a lab artifact.

Probe 2 (resolve-then-switch): you choose an urn and a colour, then the composition of B is revealed before the draw and you may pay a small fee to switch. This is supposed to test whether the ambiguity-averse preference is dynamically stable or whether it's a one-shot framing effect that a sophisticated planner would pre-commit against. My sense is that camp 1's best argument is really about this kind of case, not about the one-shot urn, and that camps 1 and 2 talk past each other because they're scoring different objects: single isolated choice vs. choice as part of a temporally extended policy.

There are also well-known paired examples in the literature — the sort where you add one ball of each colour to both urns simultaneously, or where the ambiguous urn is generated by a process whose bias is itself drawn from a known distribution — that seem to embarrass every model on the market, and I honestly can't tell whether that means all the models are wrong or that the examples are just intuition-mongering with no normative weight.

My own hypothesis, such as it is: this is not one disagreement. Part of it is verbal — a fight about whether rationality means internal coherence or responsiveness to evidence, i.e. about how permissive the correct decision theory is allowed to be, where the two sides aren't using the same standard. Part of it is a genuinely hidden empirical question about what subjects think the experimenter is up to, which probe 1 could in principle answer. And part of it may be two questions under one heading: what should an individual do, versus what uncertainty-attitude should be hard-coded into the decision rule of an institution that has to justify itself to people who disagree.

So: is that roughly right, and where is it wrong? Tell me which of these threads is actually load-bearing, using what each camp literally argues rather than a survey of the models. Is there a clean distinction I'm missing — e.g. between aversion to ambiguity and mere insensitivity to likelihood differences, or between not knowing the second-order distribution and disliking spread in it — that would dissolve one of the three fights? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: an experiment like probe 1, an impossibility or dynamic-consistency result, a formal equivalence between two apparently rival models, or changing the question? Or is this one where no resolution should be expected, and if so, why exactly — is the question ill-posed, or just genuinely hard?
