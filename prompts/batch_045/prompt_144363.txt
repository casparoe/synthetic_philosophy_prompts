I'm in eleventh grade and I run the peer-tutoring scheme for Year 10 mathematics at my school: eleven volunteer tutors, all sixth-formers, half-hour sessions, and at the end of each session the tutor either signs a card saying the student has the skill for that unit or doesn't. A sign-off gates whether the student moves on to the next unit, a teacher spot-checks maybe one card in ten, and both errors cost something real: sign off too readily and a student fails the unit test three weeks later, refuse too readily and a student who already has the skill sits through it again and stops coming. Our current rubric says only that the student must be able to do the skill independently, and in practice the tutors read that four different ways — some sign off anyone who got the right answers while they were sitting there, some demand a spoken explanation of every step, and some are plainly influenced by whether the student is pleasant or trying hard. I've been reading a little about the philosophical argument over knowing how versus knowing that (I've seen the old version of the argument attributed to a philosopher named Ryle, though I may have that wrong, and a more recent view that says knowing how is really just a species of knowing that), and it seems to me that our rubric is broken exactly where that argument bites, so I want to build our procedure out of it rather than out of a new rubric.

Here is my half-formed idea, which I'd like you to take up, correct, or reject. I don't think the two-way distinction is fine enough for what we're deciding, so I want three: being able to state the rule or the steps (knows that, on any reading); being able to execute reliably in the exact format that was practised — same layout, same kind of numbers, worksheet cues present (call it practised performance); and being able to execute when the surface features change — different numbers, unfamiliar wording, the same move buried inside a longer problem, no section heading telling you which method to use (call it transfer). I also want the procedure to notice a fourth thing, which is whether the success is really the student's: several of our tutors prompt so heavily that the student's correct answers are being carried by the prompting, and I'd rather not sign off on that. My own suggestion for a cheap test, which you should feel free to throw out, is that instead of setting one more problem in the last ten minutes we hand the student a worked example with a deliberate mistake in it and ask them to find it, on the theory that spotting the error is a better ten-minute proxy for knowing how than producing another right answer. What I need from you is a procedure, runnable in the last ten minutes of a session by a seventeen-year-old with no training and no interest in philosophy: the checks in the order they should be run, what counts as passing each one, the point at which it stops and returns a no, and the points at which it has to stop and hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is about how much inconsistency we're willing to tolerate or how much a sign-off matters to this particular student, and not about facts. Two of the hard cases it must handle are the student who performs well on Tuesday and goes blank on Thursday, and the student who only performs at all because of a formal accommodation such as extra time or a quiet room. Please argue for one answer and not both: tell me whether transfer or the ability to explain is the real test of knowing how, defend that choice against the strongest thing a tutor who disagrees would say, and mark clearly the places where you are going past what any of this actually settles.

Then I want you to run the finished procedure on three students and show your work rather than just the verdicts. Aisha can recite the steps of solving a linear equation perfectly and explain why each one is legitimate, and gets most of them wrong on paper. Marcus gets every question right on the practice sheet and cannot do the same move when it turns up inside a word problem or with fractions in it. Dev, who plays competitive chess, cannot explain any step at all and gets the right answer reliably across formats and across sessions, but only with small whole numbers — give him large ones or letters and he is lost. I suspect at least one of those three answers is not the one my school wants to hear, so please tell me which verdict you think the department will resist and defend it anyway, and don't soften it to spare me the argument. I'd also like an honest note on what the procedure cannot do: where it will produce false refusals, how often you'd expect it to be wrong in one direction or the other, and the kinds of skill it should not be applied to at all. Since I can't get hold of academic articles from school, please don't spend any of your words on citations, author names beyond the ones I've already mentioned, or suggestions for further reading — I'll add those later if I need them — and write the whole thing as continuous prose with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text and no tables, because I'm going to paste it into a long email to the other tutors and I'd rather do the layout myself than unpick yours.
