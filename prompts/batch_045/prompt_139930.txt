You are a professional philosopher who works on collective action, expected difference-making, and the ethics of individual contributions to structural harms, and who has also designed and run simulation-based teaching for years. Before drafting, please brainstorm at least three genuinely different candidate activities for my group and then pick one, telling me in a short paragraph why you rejected the other two — I want to see the design space rather than only the winner, and I would rather have one activity that has been stress-tested in your head than three sketched ones.

The setting: I co-convene a student-run practical ethics reading group at a Dutch university — advanced undergraduates and master's students, around 18 people, mixed backgrounds (philosophy, economics, computer science, one biologist), one 90-minute evening session, one seminar room with movable tables, index cards, pens, a projector, and a phone-based random number generator or a die. I can spend up to about forty euros of the society's budget as a real stake (a charity donation or something to share at the end), but no real money should change hands among participants. The topic became personally urgent last month: two members almost fell out in front of everyone, one arguing that giving up beef is pointless because supermarket and abattoir orders move in bulk thresholds and her single cart changes nothing, the other replying that this style of reasoning would excuse literally everything and invoking Parfit-style cases in which each of very many tiny acts is on its own imperceptible while the aggregate is a serious harm. They are both friends of mine, both partly right, and I do not want either of them to win the evening — I want the structure of the inefficacy objection to be something the group walks into and trips over, before I ever name it.

So here is the design brief. No lecture up front: the words threshold, pivotal, expected utility, and inefficacy objection should not be spoken by me until the final debrief. Arrange the rules, roles, incentives, and information conditions so that participants hit the problem themselves. I'd like at least three short rounds that differ only in causal structure: one in which a good outcome requires crossing a threshold that everyone can see, with enough participants that each person can work out that her own act changes nothing unless the count of others lands exactly at the boundary; one in which the threshold is hidden or drawn randomly from a range, so that each individual act has a small but real chance of making the difference and nobody can know whether tonight was such an occasion; and one with no threshold at all, where contributions simply add a small increment to a continuous outcome. Please also tell me whether to give each participant a private card with a private cost of contributing (a small real sacrifice such as a chore, a seat, or five extra minutes) and possibly a private stake in the outcome, or whether that heterogeneity will muddy the effect I am after. Give me the materials list, the exact rules, the payoff scheme, the wording I can read aloud, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes. Two further constraints: first, keep the thing from collapsing into an ordinary assurance or prisoner's-dilemma game — the felt pull should be *my contribution makes no difference*, not *I would be a sucker if others free-ride*, so tell me how the rules block that reading; second, have everyone write down a one-sentence private reason before any outcome is revealed, so the debrief can work from what they actually said. Then give me the debrief: a script that maps specific moments in the activity onto the distinction between making no actual difference and making no expected difference, onto which real cases are threshold-like and which are continuous (emissions and possible tipping points, bulk purchasing in supply chains, being the decisive vote in a large electorate), onto the step from *I make no difference* to *nothing is therefore required of me*, including both the move that generalizes the excuse to everyone and the move that locates the wrongdoing at the level of the group rather than the member, and onto what I should say to someone who cheerfully bites the bullet and says a one-in-many chance of mattering is simply to be ignored. Finally, be blunt about the first-run failure modes you would predict — participants assuming I am bluffing about the rule, the economics students computing pivotality and dissolving the experience, someone abstaining for cost reasons so I cannot tell which reason was operating, the group coordinating out loud when I wanted simultaneous private choices — with a fix for each, and be equally blunt about which parts of the philosophical debate this format simply cannot stage and which I will have to supply in discussion myself, such as our empirical ignorance about whether the real-world cases have thresholds at all, group-level, complicity-based or expressive reasons for acting, repeat-play and precedent effects, and the deeper disagreement about whether very small probabilities of very large stakes should simply be aggregated.
