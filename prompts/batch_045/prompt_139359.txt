At Thanksgiving I got into an argument about a proposed levy to pay for long-term monitoring of a deep geological nuclear waste repository, where the benefits — risks avoided — mostly land on people a century or more from now. I argued that even small probabilities matter once the time horizon is that long; my uncle replied, with the air of someone closing a case, that "a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush." I'm sure the proverb is doing more work in his argument than it can actually bear, but I couldn't say what, and part of my problem is that I can't tell which precise claim he was even making. So I'd like you to treat that sentence as a piece of unfinished philosophy and take it apart for me.

To force the issue, suppose we stipulate away every ordinary, boring reason for preferring the bird you're already holding: a bonded third party guarantees delivery of the two bush-birds on a fixed date, the hand-bird neither spoils nor needs feeding, you will certainly be alive and hungry on the delivery date, and there is nothing more productive you could do with a bird in the interval. Is there anything left for the proverb to be saying — something like a preference for a good purely because it arrives sooner — and if so, is that a defensible norm or just a description of how impatient people happen to be? Then consider some variants, because I suspect the answer changes under each: the two birds are guaranteed but split among a hundred grandchildren, so that each person's share is small; the bush is slowly being cleared, so that waiting any longer destroys the option entirely; you hold one bird but could put it into the bush now and plausibly get four back later; there are thousands of bushes and you can only ever pick one, so grabbing now costs you the chance to look; and finally the version where the birds aren't yours at all but your great-grandchildren's, and you're the one holding the hand-bird. I'd especially like to know how much of the proverb's apparent wisdom is really about time at all, as opposed to risk, forgone compound returns, illiquidity, perishability, or simply uncertainty about whether a benefit that distant will arrive or whether anyone will be there to receive it.

What I want from you is a menu, not a verdict on my uncle. Lay out the distinct precise claims the proverb could be expressing — each one paraphrased sharply enough that I could deny it in one sentence — and for each say whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, genuinely contested, or flatly false; whether it is a descriptive claim about actual human impatience or a normative claim about what to count; and, most importantly, whether it would actually get my uncle his conclusion, which is that spending real money now to protect strangers in the distant future is not required of us even when the expected benefit is large. If several readings support that conclusion, tell me which one he is probably reaching for and how much of it survives once you separate pure time preference from everything else the proverb is quietly smuggling in; and if none of them support it at the scale he needs — a few percent a year compounded over centuries, which makes any cost that far out look like nothing — say so plainly, and tell me what the strongest remaining version of his position would look like.
