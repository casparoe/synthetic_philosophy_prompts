I'm in a tiny metaethics reading group this term — four of us plus one visiting PhD student who is very quick and slightly annoying — and we got stuck on something I still can't shake a week later. It started as a question about whether the normative supervenes on the descriptive and turned into an argument that Hume's law is false. I don't believe that, but I also can't find the mistake, and its not for lack of going over it on the walk home. Here's the argument as he put it on the whiteboard (cleaned up a bit, numbers mine):

(1) Supervenience: necessarily, no two possible situations are alike in every descriptive respect and different in their normative status — there is no difference in what someone ought to do without some difference in the facts somewhere.

(2) The Bridge Requirement: if the normative supervenes on the descriptive, then that covariance is either brute — a necessary connection with nothing explaining it — or it is explained by necessary truths that state, for each descriptive condition, which normative status goes with it.

(3) Brute necessary connections between the descriptive and the normative are not on offer: a necessary link that nothing explains is exactly the sort of coincidence a serious metaphysic shouldn't help itself to. (He flagged this as the one place he's making a real philosophical bet.)

(4) So there are necessary bridge truths, roughly of the form: necessarily, anything descriptively thus-and-so is pro tanto to be done, or is good.

(5) Hume's law: no valid argument has only descriptive premises and a normative conclusion — you can't derive an 'ought' from an 'is'.

(6) But if (4) is true, then those bridge truths just are the derivations that (5) says don't exist. So (5) is false, and the is-ought gap is a methodological habit rather than a logical or metaphysical barrier.

(7) And then, given enough descriptive knowledge, moral inquiry just is empirical inquiry plus the bridge truths: in principle you could read off what anyone ought to do from the natural facts alone.

Two things he added make me more suspicious rather than less. First, he insists he hasn't committed the naturalistic fallacy, because he isn't defining 'good' as any descriptive property — he's only claiming necessary coextension, which he says is weaker and still enough to kill the gap. Second, he cheerfully admits the argument generalizes: swap in phenomenal properties supervening on physical ones, or the aesthetic on the non-aesthetic, and the same three premises force either brute necessary connections — which non-reductive physicalists seem perfectly comfortable with — or entailments from physics to experience. So either the argument is broken or non-reductivists everywhere are in deeper trouble than they admit. He says he's fine with either; I think a proof that settles all of that at once is a proof that to much.

What I want from you: name the step where it actually goes wrong, or, if nothing goes wrong, tell me which premise is cheapest to give up and what giving it up commits me to. I'm attached to (1) — I can't see how there could be a moral difference with no factual difference anywhere — and I'm attached to something like (5), at least in the sense that you can't get from 'this policy will cause a lot of suffering' to 'don't do it' by logic alone without adding something. My friend says those two attachments plus (2) can't all be true. I have a hunch the real action is in how 'descriptive' and 'normative' are being drawn, and in whether (5) is a claim about arguments or a claim about the world, but whenever I try to say that precisely it goes fuzzy on me. So: is there a usable distinction between a gap in the argument and a gap in the world that this argument slides between? Is the disjunction in (2) really exhaustive — is there room for covariance explained by identity or by grounding rather than by statable bridge laws, and would that option survive the open-question style worries Moore raised? And if the honest answer is that brute necessary covariance is fine after all, what exactly do I give up by saying so, and does it cost me more in ethics than it would in the philosophy of mind?
