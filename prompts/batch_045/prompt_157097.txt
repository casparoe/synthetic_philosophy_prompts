I'm writing a dissertation chapter in philosophy on the logic of comparative value judgments — specifically on whether "better than" and "at least as good as" can fail to be transitive, and on how much work the standard spectrum arguments really do against someone who wants to keep transitivity. I have been looking for an actual institutional practice in which the operative comparative relation is given an explicit tolerance structure rather than treated as an exact ordering, and I think I have found one in drug regulation. I would like your help reconstructing what it presupposes. Please answer in American English, in about 1,000 to 1,200 words, at the level of a graduate seminar in value theory or philosophy of medicine.

The practice. In the United States, when a prescription is written for a brand-name drug and the prescriber has not forbidden substitution, the pharmacy will normally dispense a cheaper generic. The generic is not required to pass clinical trials showing that it works as well as the brand; it is approved on a showing of bioequivalence, on which measures of the rate and extent of absorption must fall within an accepted tolerance band around the brand-name product's (conventionally 80 to 125 per cent on a ratio scale). If they do, the product is rated therapeutically equivalent to the reference product and, as a matter of ordinary practice, may be substituted for it. Three features of this matter for my purposes.

First, equivalence is always established against the reference product, never pairwise among the generics themselves. Two generics, each within the band of the brand, can be outside it with respect to one another, and nothing in the approval process ever tests that pair.

Second, substitution is repeated and chained. Because pharmacies switch suppliers as contracts change, a patient can move over a series of refills from the brand to generic A, from A to B, from B to C, with every individual exchange licensed by a relation that holds between the exchanged product and a third item the patient is no longer taking, and never verified for the pair actually exchanged.

Third, the practice is not uniform, and it seems to me to contain an internal tension. For follow-on biologics (biosimilars), similarity to the reference product is not enough for substitution: under the framework created by the Biologics Price Competition and Innovation Act, only a product that has additionally been designated interchangeable — a more demanding showing, whose point is that alternating between the two products carries no greater risk than remaining on one — may be substituted at the pharmacy without the intervention of the prescriber. And for drugs described as having a narrow therapeutic index, where the distance between a dose that works and a dose that harms is small, clinicians and some jurisdictions resist routine substitution even though the regulatory equivalence finding is in place. The same broad framework therefore treats equivalence to a reference as sufficient in one place and insufficient in another.

What I want from you:

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory of "better than" and "at least as good as" that the practice as a whole needs. Separate carefully what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is underdetermined — that is, where more than one theory of the comparative would license exactly the rules we observe. In particular, say whether the practice actually requires the relation "no worse than, to within the accepted margin" to be transitive, or whether it can get by with something weaker: an incomparability reading, on which adjacent products are merely not comparable and so no chain is ever formed, or an epistemic reading, on which the products are perfectly well ordered and we simply cannot tell how.

2. Give me your judgment on the central crux. Is the failure of transitivity here a genuine logical defect, or is the real phenomenon something else — accumulated risk, or the accumulation of merely statistical evidence across links — that a defender of transitivity can concede without giving anything up? And compare this case with the familiar spectrum arguments built on imperceptible or negligible differences: is a spectrum whose links are licensed by an explicit, finite tolerance margin more or less threatening to transitivity than one built on imperceptibility, and why?

3. Say what the biosimilar and narrow-therapeutic-index departures presuppose. Do they amount to an admission that equivalence to a reference is not transitive across switches, or can they be explained otherwise — for instance, as indexing the width of the margin to the stakes, or to the shape of the dose-response curve, rather than as conceding anything about the logic of the relation? Does the interchangeability showing treat acceptability as a property of a path — of ordered pairs, or of sequences of switches — rather than of a product, and if so what does that imply about the adicity of "at least as good as"?

4. Assess two reforms I have been considering, tell me which is more defensible, and say what each costs. (a) The worst-case-reference rule: a new generic must be shown equivalent not to the brand-name product but to the already-approved product lying farthest from it within the band, so that the substitutable set is closed under the relation. (b) The current-product rule: substitution is permitted only against the product the patient is currently taking, with equivalence established pairwise, which in practice would largely end routine substitution. On your own diagnosis in (2), does either reform repair a logical defect, or does it merely reduce a risk — and is the cost worth paying on either reading?

5. Finally, the practice is explicitly respect-relative: products are equivalent with respect to the approved indication, the tested measure, and the average patient, and no claim is made about excipients, about individual patients, or about repeated switching. Does that respect-relativity help or hurt? Does locating the tolerance inside a respect give the defender of transitivity a way out, or does it merely relocate the problem?

Please argue for your conclusions rather than surveying the options, and do not spend words on the literature or on sources: I can find those myself, and what I need is the structure of the argument.
