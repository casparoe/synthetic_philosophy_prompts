I've been reading the rationalist blogs and forums for something like fifteen years, and the thing I keep tripping over is the gap between the model of belief almost everyone reaches for — the one on which believing a set of claims puts you on the hook for everything those claims entail — and what belief actually does. I've written down the verdicts I'm most confident about, hoping some principle yields them. The principle I'd have offered doesn't, and I can't see a clean repair.

A preliminary: by 'S believes that P' I mean something like — S would assent to P if asked in the right way, S reasons and acts as if P, and S would offer P as a premise without special hesitation. I don't want a metaphysics of belief. I want a principle that tells me, for an arbitrary consequence Q of the things S believes, whether S thereby believes Q. Flat yes-or-no verdicts are acceptable, but if the right answer is that the question only makes sense by degrees, say that instead.

My cases:

1. The unread theorem. A research mathematician endorses the standard axioms of her field and has never worked through the proof of a hard theorem T that follows from them — say a proof of a couple of hundred pages that she has only ever seen cited. Verdict: she does not believe T. Not 'implicitly', not 'dispositionally': she would decline to assert it, she would not build on it, and she would be genuinely unsurprised to learn it is false, though she would expect it to be true.

2. The unassembled step. A colleague is certain that the meeting is at four and certain that if the meeting is at four she will miss her train, and has simply never put the two together. Verdict: I want to say she already believes she will miss the train — or, failing that, that something is defective about her in a way that isn't defective about the mathematician in (1).

3. The sealed-off consequence. Someone has overwhelming evidence that a project she has sunk years into is failing: she has quietly stopped recruiting, moved money, and stopped mentioning the launch date. Asked directly, she says the project is on track and seems sincerely agitated rather than evasive. Verdict: she believes it is failing.

4. The checked proof. The same mathematician as in (1), except that she has now run a machine-checked proof of T through a verifier she has good reason to trust, where the proof is far too long for her to survey or reproduce. Verdict: she believes T. I'm firm about this one, and it's the case that breaks my own principle.

5. The inconsistency. An agent holds both that P and that not-P — two calendar entries, a belief about a departure time and a belief about how long the drive takes. She does not thereby believe every sentence whatsoever. Verdict: she is criticizable, but not by way of believing everything.

6. The preface. I believe each of the hundred substantive claims in my manuscript, and I also believe that at least one of them is false. Verdict: nothing incoherent here; I'd defend both attitudes under pressure.

7. Two probabilistic variants. Credence 0.99 in each of two hundred claims and 0.9 that at least one of them is false: fine. Credence 1 in P, 1 in 'if P then Q', and 0.3 in Q: not fine. I hold both verdicts, and I can see that they shouldn't come apart as cleanly as they do if full belief is just credence 1.

8. The system that asserts. A system that will assert P when asked, will assert 'if P then Q' when asked, and will deny Q when asked, and whose internal machinery nobody is in a position to inspect. Verdict: I lean toward 'it believes neither' rather than 'it believes everything', but I don't trust this verdict, and it's the one I most want tested.

The principle I tried: cost-bounded closure. S believes a consequence Q of her beliefs just in case there is a derivation of Q from them whose verification cost to S falls under a budget fixed by what is at stake, where cost is measured in S's own resources (attention, memory, search heuristics, time) together with the resources of tools she is entitled to rely on (a calculator, a proof verifier, a trusted colleague), and where the budget is what a careful person in her situation would spend.

Where it fails, as far as I can tell. In (4) it isn't her resources doing the work, so the tools clause carries all the weight and I don't know how to bound it. In (3) the derivation is cheap and she still will not run it, so the counterfactual 'she would get there if she spent the effort' comes out false in the wrong direction; that pushes me toward reading the principle normatively (believes = is committed, for the purposes of prediction and criticism), and a normative reading then sits badly with (6) and (7), where I want to say the agent is not at fault. And (1) against (2) leaves me with a Sorites problem: any cutoff on length or cost of derivation looks arbitrary, which makes me suspect the yes-or-no framing is the mistake and that I should be asking about degrees of commitment instead. I also genuinely can't tell how much of the disagreement among candidate principles here is substantive and how much is terminological.

What I'd like from you:

- Two or three candidate principles that do better than mine, each stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without coming back to you, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it.
- Their verdicts on cases I haven't listed. Mine, which I go back and forth on: a mathematician who is told by a colleague she trusts that T follows from her axioms, and who repeats it as established without ever checking — my gut flips depending on whether I imagine her being asked to assert it or asked to bet on it. Please also add at least three further cases of your own that discriminate between the candidates, especially ones that split verdicts my eight cases don't split.
- Which of my verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate, which you would overturn if you had to, and what overturning it would cost me elsewhere in the list.
- Where the fix belongs: in the model of belief (the possible-worlds apparatus that generates the omniscience problem in the first place), in the notion of consequence being applied (restricted, relevance-sensitive, or resource-bounded consequence), or in what we should mean by belief. Say which of these each of your candidates is, and why that location rather than the others.
- How confident you are in each substantive thing you claim. And, the part I most want: a flag on the single point you are least sure of, with your own rough estimate of the chance you are wrong there. If two of your candidates differ only on a verbal question, say which, and say why you think it is verbal.
