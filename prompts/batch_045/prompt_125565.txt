Some friends and I volunteer as judges for a weekend exercise in a conflict-studies summer program — think model UN but with more moral philosophy and fewer points of order. The students get the published decisions of a fictional truth commission (country: Meridia; a decade-long insurgency, the old Directorate collapses, the Congress movement takes over, and the Commission can grant amnesty for acts its statute describes as committed in the course of the conflict, applying the usual three tests: a political objective, full disclosure, and proportionality between the act and the objective) plus one undecided petition, and they have to rule on the undecided one. Every year they rule different ways and every year I realize I can't say which ruling would actually be consistent with the record, so I want to get this sorted before September. I posted a stripped-down version of the question to LessWrong last spring and the comments split roughly three ways, which I read as evidence that the record is genuinely underdetermined rather than that two thirds of the commenters were being dumb.

The six decided petitions (our Commission always gives one or two sentences of reasons, which is half the problem):

1. K., a nineteen-year-old conscript who, on his sergeant's order, shot two men his unit had detained as suspected informers and helped bury them; he later led investigators to the grave, joined a victims' support group, and has said publicly that what he did was wrong. GRANTED. Reason given: full disclosure, no personal motive, act within the conflict.
2. L., a Directorate records officer who never attended an interrogation but signed more than four hundred detention orders; she turned over the entire filing system and named the officers who carried out the beatings, says she would have been shot if she had refused, and has testified that the policy was wrong — though she has never apologized to any individual detainee. GRANTED. Reason: disclosure substantial, no personal gain or malice.
3. Colonel D., of the winning side, who during the war ordered the execution of twelve deserters from his own unit (he gave the order; he did not fire); he disclosed the location of two unmarked graves and, over his own party's objection, named the politicians who had authorized the executions, and he has publicly accepted the new government's authority, saying he would not give such an order under the current constitution. GRANTED. Reason: the mandate covers all sides, deserters in wartime were a genuine security concern, disclosure complete. Two dissenters: victors should not amnesty victors.
4. T., a Congress fighter who bombed a Directorate barracks canteen, killing nine soldiers and three civilian kitchen workers; he knew civilians were sometimes in the building, disclosed fully, and named his cell — but the cell was already known from captured files, and he has never acknowledged that the civilian deaths were wrong, calling them an accident of war. DENIED. Reason: disproportionate to any political objective; no acknowledgment.
5. V., who killed a shopkeeper he had been feuding with over a boundary dispute; the shopkeeper had in fact been passing information to the Directorate, and V. says he knew that. DENIED. Reason: predominant motive personal.
6. W., an elderly man who confessed to taking part in a village massacre and wants amnesty but cannot recall dates, names, or what was done with the bodies, and whose account is corroborated by nothing. DENIED. Reason: contrition is not disclosure. Dissent: a man shouldn't lose amnesty for having a bad memory.

The undecided petition is M. She was in the Congress movement's own internal-security unit. Three weeks after the ceasefire was signed but before the transition government was sworn in, she shot and killed the commandant of a Congress-run detention camp. The Commission's investigators have largely confirmed her account: the commandant was torturing and killing suspected collaborators, and the movement's leadership had ignored three written complaints, one of them hers. She says she killed him to make the torture stop and that there was no other way. Her motive isn't clean — the commandant personally oversaw the torture and death of her brother — and she will not say the killing was wrong; pressed by a commissioner, she said something like, if the Commission needs to call it a crime in order to feel clean, fine, call it a crime, and yes, in the same position she'd do it again. Her disclosure is the largest the Commission has ever received: copied camp records, the location of a burial site with fourteen bodies, and the names of four men who are now senior officials in the new government and who she says authorized the camp or covered it up. None of it was obtainable any other way. The dead man's widow, and his son, who is now a prosecutor for the transition government, have formally asked the Commission to refuse amnesty.

What I want from you: the rules that are actually consistent with all six verdicts, steelmanned one at a time, and what each of them predicts about M. — my read is that there's a checklist rule (political objective, disclosure, proportionality, with sub-questions about whether a post-ceasefire killing counts as committed in the course of the conflict and whether an unauthorized execution can ever be proportionate), a rule on which amnesty is basically payment for truth the society couldn't otherwise get, and a rule on which amnesty is readmission into the political community and so requires the applicant to accept the community's judgment about what she did — but I may be missing one, and I don't trust how I've individuated them. Please taboo reconciliation, or at least say which of the three or four different things you mean by it, because I've noticed it doing all the work in these arguments. Then: your best guess at the ruling, with the reason you'd bet that way rather than on the runner-up; an honest statement of whether the six actually decide it or whether we'd just be adopting a principle by fiat; and what further petition we should write into next year's packet — a variant on M., or on one of the six — because it would separate the surviving rules rather than just re-run them. Don't go hunting for real TRC case law or hand me a reading list; Meridia is invented, and I'd rather the reasoning come out of these six decisions, though if there's a structural point about what a disclosure-for-amnesty deal can and can't buy, make it.
