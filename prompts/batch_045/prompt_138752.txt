Posting this after a seminar blow-up I've been chewing on for a week and can't untangle. I'm a PhD student working mostly on James with some Peirce on the side, and a friend in the department — a James person, but of the aggressively deflationary stripe — handed me what he calls 'the self-certification argument.' His claim is that once you say anything like truth is what works, you've signed up for the view that some propositions are true because we believe them, which he calls idealism with extra steps and which I think is straightforwardly false. But I can't find the exact joint, and I have to present on this in two weeks. Here it is, cleaned up:

(1) A belief's being true consists in its working — cashing out in experience: leading well, predicting successfully, hanging together with the rest of what we take in, not getting contradicted down the line. Not in a correspondence relation to a reality we can't step outside our beliefs to check.
(2) Whether a belief works is relative to a set of aims, practices and background beliefs: the belief that the bridge will hold works for a creature that wants to get across and has a certain tolerance for risk, and a medical community's belief that ulcers are caused by stress works relative to the tests and therapies on the table before H. pylori was in play but not relative to the ones after (I know that history is messier than that, but you take the point).
(3) Those aims and practices aren't fixed independently of what we believe, because believing things changes what we do and what we care about — and for genuine options, the forced, living and momentous ones where the evidence can't settle it, James thinks our passional nature gets to decide.
(4) So there's a class of propositions whose truth depends on choices of ours: whether p works can depend on whether enough of us take up believing p, since taking it up reshapes the very practices p is being assessed against.
(5) And this isn't hypothetical — he calls them Gideon cases. If enough depositors believe the bank is insolvent, it becomes insolvent. A department that believes a junior hire will flourish mentors her accordingly, and she does. A community that believes its ritual secures the harvest plants more energetically and with more confidence, and the harvest comes in more often. In each case the belief's being held is part of why what it says becomes so.
(6) So if truth just is working, then in Gideon cases the belief is true because it was believed. At least sometimes, whether p is true is up to us.
(7) But that's absurd: we don't get to make propositions true by fiat. If the whole village sincerely and stably believes the rain dance brings rain, and it rains more because everyone plants harder while feeling hopeful, the dance still doesn't bring rain, and the belief is useful and flatly false. And there's a problem running the other way too, the Cassandra thing: a true belief can be catastrophic to hold and act on, which is already odd if truth is a species of expediency.

I'd rather keep (1) in some form and I definitely want to keep (7); I'm happy to drop the use of (3) if that's the cheapest move, but my friend says (3) isn't where the work is being done and that the real slide is somewhere between (4) and (6). So: which step do you actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If it's a scope or modal slide — working read locally, as works-for-us-now-given-our-aims, versus working read at the counterfactual limit of inquiry for an indefinite community — say so, explain why the slide looked fine, and tell me whether the Peircean fix (truth is the opinion inquiry would converge on in the long run, with the limit fixed by reality rather than by us) isn't just smuggling correspondence back in through the back door. If you think a Jamesian can simply bite the bullet on Gideon cases and say the belief really does become true, tell me what that commits them to and whether it's stable. And please be explicit about your confidence: separate what's genuinely settled about the historical positions from what's live disagreement among people who know this literature — I can usually tell when I'm being handed one reading of James and Peirce dressed up as the consensus, and I dont need to be told I'm right, I'd rather be told the truth then comforted.
