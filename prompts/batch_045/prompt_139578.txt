I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy or polisci training, just read this stuff for fun, and somehow I got invited to give a 20-minute talk next week at a social norms reading group, so I need Q&A prep more than I need encouragement.

My thesis: the tight/loose cultures literature is measuring the wrong thing. The famous cross-country survey index (the one with questions about how much freedom you have day to day and how acceptable it is to do things like swear or talk in a cinema or eat in a restaurant) is usually read as telling us how strongly a society cares about rules. I want to argue it's mostly picking up how observable rule-breaking is. I split the thing into three axes: how much people disapprove of a violation if they actually see it ("strength"), how likely a violation gets seen at all ("observability"), and how bad the sanction is once it is seen ("cost"). My claims are that societies probably don't differ all that much on the first one, that most of the cross-country spread is observability, and that observability tracks boring infrastructure — population density, how much of daily life happens in shared physical space in front of strangers, record-keeping, whether your neighbors are people you'll never see again. I also want to sneak in an analogy: tight societies validate norms "eagerly," at write time, loose societies "lazily," at read time, and neither is stricter about what they ultimately accept.

The room: about twelve people. Two cultural-evolution modeling types who'll like that I have axes but will want a formal statement of how three things collapse to one measured score. One political theorist who thinks the whole framing of ranking countries on a single scale is neocolonial and will side-eye any generalization. An anthropologist who'll say six survey items can't capture anything about a culture. And a psychology grad student who has actually run studies with this index and will defend it as a working instrument that predicts real behavior, which I think is my real problem.
