I am finishing a chapter on the suspension of payments by a regional savings bank in 1892, and I want to settle how to describe its causation before the draft goes to my supervisor. She has ruled on enough of my causal claims over the past three years that I can usually anticipate her, but this case does not match any of her previous rulings cleanly, and I would rather find out now what I would be committing myself to. She has no patience for philosophical vocabulary, so whatever rule I adopt has to be statable and defensible in plain prose.

Her verdicts so far, as I have recorded them after each chapter:

On a famine in the 1870s, where a harvest failure and a blockade each ran to completion and either alone would have produced mortality on roughly the observed scale and over roughly the same months, she allowed me to call both of them causes and to say the mortality was overdetermined, on the condition that I stated explicitly that either would have sufficed and never wrote as though both were required.

On a coup, where a colonel had a complete plan that was never activated because the general moved first: the colonel's plan is not a cause, and the coup was not overdetermined. Her comment was that a thing which would have been sufficient had it operated is not for that reason a cause.

On the fall of a government, where a lost confidence vote and a simultaneous withdrawal of union backing were each insufficient alone and jointly sufficient: both are causes, and emphatically not overdetermined. She struck the word from that draft, insisting that redundancy and conjunction are different things and that the chapter had conflated them.

On a sovereign default that a particular event brought forward by three weeks but which would have happened anyway on the same terms: a cause of the timing, not of the default. She struck "caused the default" and made me write "caused the default to come when it did."

On the fall of a regime, where without a mutiny the regime would still have fallen but by a negotiated transfer rather than by insurrection: she permitted "a cause of the form the fall took" and "a cause of the fall as it actually happened," while insisting that the fall itself was overdetermined and that I could not credit the mutiny with producing it.

On a rebellion, where I had cited a large landless peasantry that had existed for fifty years: a condition, not a cause, and not to be listed alongside the events.

My new case, as I currently understand it. A correspondent bank in the capital failed on 3 March. By 6 March a run by small depositors at the provincial branches had drained the till and the bank suspended payments; depositors eventually recovered about forty per cent. Independently of the run, the bank had been technically insolvent since late February, its only remaining source of liquidity was an emergency advance from the treasury, and on 5 March the treasury refused the advance; that refusal on its own would have forced suspension within days, and would have produced an orderly wind-down in which depositors recovered close to ninety per cent. So far this looks like her first verdict: two routes, both of which actually ran, either sufficient on its own.

The complication is that the two routes are not independent of each other. The ministry refused the advance because the run had already begun; its letter says it will not commit public money to an institution the public has already abandoned. Had the run not occurred, the same correspondence and two earlier interventions make it very likely that the advance would have been made and that the bank would have survived at least to the autumn audit. So the run looks necessary for the suspension as well as sufficient for it, the refusal looks sufficient but not necessary, and the refusal is itself an effect of the run. There is a further wrinkle I should mention: both routes were made available by an 1886 reform that permitted the bank to keep its reserves with a single correspondent and converted treasury support from an entitlement into a discretionary advance.

What I want from you is a prediction and a recommendation. Do you think she will say the suspension was overdetermined, or that there is no overdetermination here at all because the second route was downstream of the first and the run is simply the cause? What, on her own record, should I be permitted to call the treasury's refusal: a cause, a redundant accompaniment, a mechanism through which the first route worked, or an occasion? Which rule is she implicitly running — something like "an actually operating sufficient route makes its outcome overdetermined," something like "the outcome must counterfactually depend on the factor," or something like a priority rule that refuses to count a route as a cause when it is itself an effect of another route to the same outcome — and do her six verdicts really force one of these rather than the others? I care less about the bottom line than about which principle I would be adopting with each phrasing.

Finally, tell me which single further case would best discriminate between the rules that fit her record. My own candidate is a variant in which the ministry had already decided to refuse before the run began, unknown to the bank, so that the refusal was both downstream of nothing and independently guaranteed; my other candidate is a case in which the first route operates only through the second, so that there is a chain rather than a pair. Say which of these actually separates the surviving rules, and if neither does, construct the case that would. And as a secondary matter: given that she calls a fifty-year-old social structure a condition rather than a cause, does the fact that the 1886 reform was a discrete change rather than a standing condition make any difference to whether I must cite it, given that it is what made both routes possible?
