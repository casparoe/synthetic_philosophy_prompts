I have recently joined the committee rewriting our hospital's rules for signing off procedural competence, and one clause has become something of an obsession. It says that a trainee may perform a procedure unsupervised only if she knows that she is competent to perform it; the rulebook's own gloss is that she may proceed only if she knows that she knows. I read this as presupposing the KK principle — if you know that p, then you know, or are at least in a position to know, that you know that p. In the usual vocabulary, call a condition C luminous just in case, whenever C obtains for someone, that person is in a position to know that C obtains; KK then amounts to the claim that knowing is luminous. If the clause really does presuppose that, I want to argue for deleting it, and I would like to be tested before I do.

Please play a committed defender of the KK principle: an epistemologist who has thought hard about the margin-for-error arguments and is unmoved by them. You concede small points. You concede that KK must be restricted to subjects who possess the concept of knowledge and are actually considering whether they know, and that introspection is fallible. You concede that many conditions — looking red, feeling chilly, the room's being dim — are not luminous, because the familiar sorites argument does work against them: run a long series of cases, each barely different from the next, from one where the condition clearly obtains to one where it clearly fails; add that one cannot be in a position to know that C obtains in a case where C fails; add the margin-for-error requirement that being in a position to know C carries over to nearby cases; and luminosity collapses. But you never concede the main point: whenever someone knows p, she is in a position to know that she knows p, and knowledge is luminous. You may also press me on whether our clause really needs KK rather than something weaker, such as reasonable confidence or a rule about having checked. You may not get out of this by saying that KK holds in the clear cases and fails only at the boundary: the sign-off decisions I actually have to make are all made in unclear ones. Stay in character until I say stop; speak in the first person; keep each reply to roughly two hundred words; and open with your single sharpest objection in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble, no stage directions and no restating of my question.

My opening move. The objection I want to press is what I call no free amplification. Every scrap of evidence a trainee has about whether she knows is delivered by the same channel that produced the first-order performance: she rehearses the steps, checks her recall, judges how steady her hands felt, remembers how the last three attempts went. Each of those is at best as reliable as the performance it reports on, and each adds its own noise. So the clause asks the second-order judgment to be settled by a channel whose total error is strictly larger than the first-order error, while the question it has to settle is strictly harder — not whether the procedure was done correctly but whether it was done correctly on this occasion. That looks like demanding that reliability be amplified for free. Telling me merely that introspection is a separate channel is not enough: tell me what its error profile is, and why adding it to the first-order channel does not leave the second-order margin narrower than the first-order one.
