I spent four decades in atmospheric modelling, which is to say forty years of replacing problems I could not solve with approximations I could defend, and I have come to philosophy late: three years of fairly disciplined reading since I retired, almost all of it alone or with a small reading group of former colleagues. I have now decided to give the next three or four years, at something like fifteen hours a week, to a single area, and the area I keep returning to is bounded rationality and satisficing. I have already worked through the elementary material — the ant on the beach, the two blades of the scissors, the by now familiar objection that satisficing is only ordinary optimization with the costs of search written into the objective function — so please do not spend your answer restating it. I am not asking for a map of the literature or for suggestions about whom to read. What I want is an agenda.

Let me fix what the agenda is for. The effort is mine: an outsider's fifteen hours a week for three or four years, no department, no referees, no grant renewal, no students, and no laboratory. I can still do mathematics comfortably, I can build and analyse small formal models, and I can read empirical papers critically; I cannot collect data or run subjects. The end I have in mind is not a contribution to a journal debate for its own sake but something that would change how people who actually design decision procedures — triage protocols, grant panels, regulatory screening of chemicals or of planning applications — think about what they are doing when they stop looking. If you think that end is ill-chosen for someone in my position, say so before you answer the rest.

Here are the lines of work I have provisionally sketched, in no order. First, what I call the which-bound-binds question: bounded rationality tends to treat limits generically, as if it did not matter whether the constraint is time, attention, working memory, computation, information access, or the sheer cost of stating the problem well enough to optimise over it; my suspicion is that for any real decision one of these dominates, and that identifying which is prior to choosing any decision rule at all. Second, delegated satisficing: when an agent hands the search itself to another system with quite different bounds — a literature search engine, a junior analyst, an automated screen, a large language model — the binding constraint appears to move from search to specification and verification, and I cannot find in the classical picture any account of how the aspiration threshold is then set, or of what it even means to satisfice over the output of a delegate whose stopping rule you neither chose nor can inspect. My guess is that this is where the theory is thinnest relative to how decisions are actually made now, but I may be mistaking novelty for importance. Third, the level problem: the aspiration threshold must itself be chosen under the same bounds, so satisficing seems to relocate the difficulty rather than dissolve it, and there is a further empirical question about whether real thresholds track anything normatively interesting or merely drift with recent experience. Fourth, a worry I import from my old field and may be importing illegitimately: there we judged a model by the predictions it risked, and satisficing as usually stated seems able to accommodate almost any stopping point, so it is unclear what would refute it; I want to know whether the theory can be stated so that it makes a risky, checkable prediction, and whether demanding that of it is a virtue or a category mistake.

Please rank these, add the lines you think matter more, and cut the ones you think are sterile. I care less about the list than about the reasoning: what criteria you are using — importance, upstream-ness, tractability for someone who can model but not experiment, how neglected each is, how much downstream work depends on it — how you weigh those criteria against each other where they conflict, and what evidence or result over the next few years would move an item up or down. Tell me which questions you would leave alone because they need a laboratory I do not have, which are genuinely upstream of the others, and which of the implications for institutional practice follow and which are oversold. Commit to one ordering and defend it; where your confidence is lowest, say so and say what would change your mind. I would rather have a defensible ranking I can argue with than an even-handed survey of the options.
