You are a professional philosopher who works on punishment, moral responsibility, and transitional justice. Think carefully and work through the problem step by step. Before you write your answer, do a short unstructured pass — five to eight rough bullet points, unpolished, no self-editing — in which you list every candidate theory of punishment, forgiveness, responsibility, or the value of truth that the design below might be committed to, together with any oddity you notice in it. I advise the constitutional drafting committee of a country — call it Veredia — that has just come out of a twenty-year internal conflict through a negotiated settlement in which neither side won outright. We have been handed a draft enabling statute for a truth commission, and everyone on the committee keeps saying that the design "expresses a particular philosophy of punishment and reconciliation." I want to know exactly what we would be committing ourselves to if we enacted it. Here is the design, in its own terms.

Amnesty is available only on application, and only for acts committed with a political objective; an act whose dominant motive was personal gain, or a private grudge, is ineligible even if it also served a political objective. So two men who did the same thing, in the same unit, on the same night, are treated differently depending on why they did it. As a condition, the applicant must make full disclosure of everything within their knowledge about the act — including the identity of those who ordered it and the disposal of remains. Applicants whose disclosure is incomplete are refused, and a refused applicant can then be prosecuted; but the statute bars the prosecution from using the applicant's own amnesty testimony against them, while permitting the use of leads derived from it. The draft then adds a clause that I have not seen in the well-known South African model, where amnesty required that the act be politically motivated and fully disclosed: where the disclosure contains nothing the commission did not already know — the facts sit in newspaper archives, in police files, or in testimony the victims gave years ago and were not believed about — the committee may still grant amnesty, provided the applicant publicly acknowledges the acts, commits to not contesting the victims' accounts, and answers questions truthfully. The committee's stated reason is that official acknowledgement by the perpetrator gives the victims something the archive does not. Nowhere in the statute is remorse, apology, or regret required. Victims may testify at public hearings, must be informed of the outcome, and receive reparations from a state fund that are scaled to the gravity of what was done to them; but they have no vote on the grant of amnesty and no standing to block it, and amnesty extinguishes the perpetrator's civil liability to them as well. Finally, amnesty is all-or-nothing: there is no partial amnesty and no discount for the gravity of the act, even though the reparations for the same act are graded.

Several things about this puzzle me and I want them addressed head on. Why does motive affect eligibility if the commission insists it is not excusing anyone? How is what we are doing different from impunity if a person who feels and expresses no remorse, and who tells us only what we already know, walks free? And what are we doing when we grade the money to the harm but not the amnesty? The committee also says that amnesty is not forgiveness and that the victims' forgiveness is not required for it — yet the entire exercise is called national reconciliation, so I would like to know who is supposed to be reconciled with whom, and what work the victims are doing in it beyond providing testimony.

Please give me the implicit theory: reconstruct the view — or views — of punishment, desert, forgiveness, responsibility, and the value of truth that this package only makes sense under, and then separate the clauses that strictly presuppose a commitment from the ones that merely sit well with it. Tell me where the design is philosophically underdetermined, in the sense that two or three quite different theories would all license the same clause, and tell me where the clauses pull against each other — in particular whether the disclosure condition and the acknowledgement clause can really be rationalized by a single theory, or whether the draft has quietly welded two incompatible justifications together, and whether the acknowledgement clause's stated rationale is even coherent. Assess what the statute presupposes about who holds the claim that punishment answers to, given that the state trades it away over the victims' objection and then compensates them out of its own funds. Finally, take three rival views of what a transition is for — a desert-based retributive view, a victim-centered view on which the claim against the wrongdoer belongs to the victim and cannot be traded by the state, and a purely forward-looking view concerned only with future stability and deterrence — and tell me, for each, which clauses of the draft would have to be struck or rewritten and what would replace them. If one clause does more philosophical work than the rest, or is harder to justify than the rest, say which and why.

This is conceptual work, not research: I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or citations, and please do not tell me what any named author or jurisdiction has said. Reason from the design itself. It is fine — indeed useful — to tell me where you think the honest answer is that the statute is simply silent or that the question is a genuine dilemma, and to flag explicitly any place where you are guessing about what the committee intends rather than reading it off the clauses.
