I'm a software engineer — ML infra, zero philosophy training — who got nerd-sniped into quantitative genetics via a polygenic-scores side project, and next month I'm giving a 30-minute brown-bag at a computational genomics institute. My thesis: a high heritability estimate is basically never evidence that a trait is fixed or that a gap between groups is genetic, and most of the misuse comes from reading a within-population variance decomposition as a causal claim about a mean difference.

The room: maybe twenty people. Several PGS developers, two statistical geneticists who actually run GWAS, a handful of ML folks who care about transportability and out-of-distribution generalization, and two or three who are quietly sympathetic to hereditarian conclusions about group differences and will defend them. Nobody there is a philosopher of science, and nobody wants to be lectured at about the history of eugenics.

My argument has three moves, so aim objections at these: (1) h² is defined for a population *in an environment*, so it's a property of the pair, not of the trait — I'm comparing it to a profiler telling you how much of the latency variance in the *current* deployment is attributable to your code versus the datacenter, where redeploying somewhere else moves the split, sometimes to zero. (2) A thing I'm inventing and want stress-tested: "counterfactual heritability" — instead of reporting one h², report the spread of h² across the environments you could actually bring about by intervention, and treat a wide spread as meaning today's number carries no information about what's achievable. (3) The within/between gap: within-group h² leaves the between-group decomposition completely unconstrained, and I'll use the standard illustration where a trait is strongly heritable inside a well-nourished population while the mean difference from a malnourished one is entirely environmental.

What I want is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of my position: the seven or eight objections I'll really get from *this* audience in the order they'd do me damage, each phrased the way astatistical geneticist or an ML person would say it (not the way a philosopher or a twitter hereditarian would), a roughly two-minute reply each, and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front so I don't look like I'm denying that heritability numbers mean anything. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. And please separate what's genuinely settled among people who work on this from what's actually contested — especially on the group-difference question, where I don't want to walk in claiming a consensus that isn't there.
