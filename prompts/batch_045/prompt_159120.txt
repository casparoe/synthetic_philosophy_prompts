I'm putting together one of those film-and-philosophy nights for our first-year philosophy society, and we're showing Peter Weir's 1998 film The Truman Show. My idea was to pair it with a very short intro to disjunctivism and the argument from illusion, and I've been going back and forth on whether that's actually a good match or whether I'm just forcing it. The bit of the debate I want to use: the classical argument from illusion moves from the claim that a hallucination could be subjectively indistinguishable from a real perception to the claim that perceiving and hallucinating must therefore be the same kind of state with a common object, and disjunctivists refuse that step. As I understand it there are roughly two versions people defend — a metaphysical one, where seeing and hallucinating share no common mental kind at all and genuine perception is a relation to the mind-independent objects themselves, and an epistemological one, where in the good case your perceptual standing is factive and itself gives you knowledge or warrant, with no reason of the very same kind available in the bad case.

The reason I thought of the film is that Truman seems to me to be a case that isn't in the standard textbooks. He is not hallucinating, and he isn't having illusions in the philosophers' sense — no bent sticks, no Muller-Lyer figures, no veil of ideas. The sea, the houses, the neighbours, the sun are all really there and he really does see them; nothing is edited, nothing is inserted between him and the world, and his visual system is working fine. But nearly everything he takes those things to be is false, because the environment was deliberately staged by other agents: his wife is an actor reading lines, the weather is cued, the town loops. Call it the staged-world case: perception that is fully relational and factive, paired with massive, systematically manufactured error about what you are perceiving, where the error comes from the setup of the world rather than from any defect or oddness in your own sensory state. It seems distinct from fake-barn style cases, which turn on luck and safety rather than on deliberate contrivance, and I'm not sure the disjunctivist literature has a slot for it.

So I'd like a reading rather than a verdict on the debate. Does the film end up vindicating disjunctivism or exposing a gap in it? Does the metaphysical version have to classify Truman as simply in the good case, fully in touch with reality — and is that the intuitively wrong answer, or is it actually the right one that disjunctivists should bite the bullet on? Does the epistemological version come under pressure, given that Truman's experience is factive and yet he plainly lacks knowledge about his world — or does the fact that he still knows perfectly well that there is a car in front of him show that the view was only ever making a modest claim? Which scenes should we pause on: the lighting rig that drops out of the fake sky, the rain that follows only him, the backstage reveal behind the elevator doors, his wife breaking character and talking about the washing machine, the beach scene where he reaches the painted wall of the set and finds the stairs and the door? And what does the ending do — does walking through the door mean the film thinks the only answer to the sceptic is relocation to a better world, or does it think perception was never the problem in the first place? I also want the places where the film resists the reading: Christof's voice from the control room as a stand-in for a deceiving demon, the audience framing, and the fact that this is a comedy about a TV producer and not a metaphysics seminar.

Please be faithful in both directions — don't flatten the movie into an illustration of a theory, and don't bend disjunctivism until it fits; if it's a bad pairing, that's useful to know too. Tell me what, if anything, the film adds that the arguments alone don't. On commitment: mark clearly which claims you'd actually defend, which are contested within disjunctivism, and where you're working from a hazy memory of the plot, since I'll be the one standing up in front of thirty people. And if my framing is off — if disjunctivism was never in the business of handling staged or contrived environments, or if the distinction I'm drawing between the staged-world case and fake-barn cases doesn't hold up, or if the film simply doesn't take a position on anything this subtle — say so plainly instead of politely playing along. No need for citations or reading lists, and please don't try to quote lines from memory, paraphrase is fine.
