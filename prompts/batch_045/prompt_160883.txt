I've been arguing with myself about quotas for months and I keep noticing that my gut says different things about cases that look basically the same. I'm not an academic — I work in freight logistics — so please don't point me at books or a reading list, I've read enough op-eds. I want your own principle, stated plainly, and I want to know where it bites.

Here's where I've landed.

1. Norway making big listed companies fill something like 40% of board seats with each sex: yes, I'm for it. Even though I know the women who got those seats were mostly already wealthy and well-connected, and the people squeezed out weren't the men who'd been doing the squeezing.

2. Caste reservations in Indian university admissions: for it, and especially for the part where the better-off families inside the reserved categories get excluded (I think there's a name for that). Also fine with the overall cap, which I gather sits somewhere near half the seats.

3. US universities weighing race in admissions: for it, but shakier than 2, and I definately don't like it when the person who benefits is a rich kid from a private school.

4. Legacy admissions: I hate them. But here's what bugs me — they're also picking people partly because of who their parents were, passing advantage down a family line. I can't say why that's the wrong kind of inheritance and 3 is the right kind, given that the first thing I liked about 2 was that the well-off get cut out.

5. My own made-up case, and the one I'm most sure of out of everything here: a hospital that messed up and injured a bunch of its own patients reserves two of the twelve seats on its patient-safety committee for people it harmed, and pays them for it. Obviously fine. More fine than 1.

6. Same shape as 5, one step removed: a factory recruits nationally and reserves a third of its apprenticeships for a group that the local schools in its region shortchanged for forty years. The factory never discriminated against anybody. I go lukewarm — part of me says yes, part of me says the kids who lose those apprenticeships didn't do anything.

7. Quotas for a group that's a clear majority of the population but underrepresented in the thing — say men in primary school teaching. Against. And I don't think it's just that I expect it to be pointless; I'd still be against it if it worked.

8. Where the gap is nobody's fault — the group isn't excluded or shortchanged, they just don't apply, or their families steer them elsewhere — I go cold on quotas, even when I'm sympathetic to the group. Which is odd next to 1, where I don't actually know whether anyone was excluded or whether women just weren't in the pipeline.

9. My other made-up case, which I keep flipping on: a university sitting on land taken from a tribe 150 years ago reserves places for that tribe's descendants, and those descendants are now on average better off than the applicants they displace. I think yes. Then I think about 2's exclusion of the well-off and think no. Then I think about 5 and think yes again.

The principle I keep trying out: a quota is fine when the people who pay for it are the same people who caused the problem or who've been quietly collecting an unearned advantage, and not fine when the cost lands on people who did nothing. It handles 4 and 5 beautifully. But it seems to kill 1, 6 and 8, which I'm at least half for, and it says nothing about why 2 feels firmer to me than 3.

So: can it be repaired, is there a better one, or do I need two principles — one about who pays and one about who benefits? Tell me which of my verdicts no decent principle can save. And run whatever you land on against two cases I haven't listed: (a) reparations paid out of general taxation, so the bill lands partly on people who arrived in the country last year, and (b) a professional body that sets aside a fixed number of slots for people who blew the whistle on their old employer and got blacklisted for it — is that even the same kind of thing, or is it something else wearing the same clothes?
