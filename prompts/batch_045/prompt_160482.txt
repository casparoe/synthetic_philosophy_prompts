I'm writing a novel and one of my characters — an old abbot, dying, and very much not preachy — composes his death poem in front of his granddaughter, who badly wants it to be about him. He wants it to be about not-him. I want it as a pantoum, and I want the machinery of the form to be the argument: in the version I'm using, the second and fourth lines of each quatrain turn up again as the first and third of the next, and the last line of the poem is the first line come back. So a line returns word for word and means something else, because it's now standing next to different lines — which is roughly what he thinks a person is: the aggregates going on (body, feeling, perception, formation, consciousness) with no one behind them owning the lot, a person being just a designation over arranged parts, like the chariot in the old dialogue. So pick the recurring lines so they actually shift — put a possessive in one, a name in another, an I in a third — and let the closing line land as the same sentence, changed. 16 to 20 lines, plain english, no incense and moonlit-orient filler: he's a real man talking to a kid he loves who is going to miss him badly.
