Long time lurker, first time poster etc. Anyway — I was reading one of those "AI alignment explained in 800 words" blog posts about the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents, and before I drag it into a comment thread I want to know how much of it actually holds up. The summary, more or less as written:

'Löb's theorem is a generalisation of Gödel's second incompleteness theorem. It says that for any statement P, a sufficiently strong formal system can prove "if I can prove P, then P" only in the cases where it can already prove P outright. So no system can ever trust its own reasoning: to accept your own soundness is already to be inconsistent, and that is the Löbian obstacle. For AI the consequence is stark — an agent can never trust a successor it builds, because trusting the successor means accepting that whatever the successor proves is true, and Löb's theorem makes that collapse: the parent would have to already be able to prove everything the successor can prove, which defeats the whole point of building something smarter. So recursive self-improvement is impossible in principle for any agent that reasons by proof, and any safe design has to steer clear of self-reference altogether. And since a Bayesian agent's beliefs obey the same logic, the same obstacle applies to probabilistic systems in general. It also explains why agents that reason about each other's source code can never cooperate with each other — mutual trust between two copies is exactly the kind of self-trust Löb rules out.'

My instinct is that the first couple of sentences are fine and that the rest is increasingly overselling, and the last claim in particular smells wrong to me — I have a vague memory of people using Löb's theorem to *construct* agents that cooperate, rather than to show they can't — but I cant reconstruct the argument myself and I dont really trust my memory here. Could you go through it more or less sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who actually works on this would accept, what they'd wince at as a simplification, and what is just wrong? And do the big conclusions — that self-improving agents are impossible in principle, and that safe designs have to avoid self-reference — actually survive once the summary is corrected?
