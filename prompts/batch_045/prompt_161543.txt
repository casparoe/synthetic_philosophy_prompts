You are a poet who also happens to have graduate-level competence in dynamical systems theory and the philosophy of science: someone who can write a sestina without cheating the form and who knows the difference between determinism and predictability, what a Lyapunov exponent measures, and why a chaotic trajectory's failure to be forecast is not evidence of indeterminism. Work carefully, and before you draft anything, spend a short paragraph or two of prose (kept out of the final answer or compressed into the note at the end, your choice) deciding three things: which six end-words you will rotate, which of the conceptual points below you will let the envoi carry, and where in the poem you will plant the single most precise technical claim. Getting those decisions right is most of the task; the verse should follow from them.

The commission: a sestina, thirty-nine lines (six stanzas of six, plus the three-line tornada), to close the last session of an undergraduate philosophy of science seminar on determinism. It should be a poem an undergraduate who has met Laplace's demon and played with the logistic map can follow on one reading, and one that the instructor, who works on this material, would not have to apologize for.

Constraints on the content, in rough order of importance:

- The poem's central distinction is between determinism (unique evolution from a state, no dice anywhere in the laws) and predictability (our ability to say what the state will be). Chaos is a failure of the second while the first is intact. Do not let a line imply that chaotic systems are random, stochastic, lawless, or indeterministic; a single such line ruins the poem for the instructor.
- Put real weight on the arithmetic of the failure rather than on rhetoric: because error grows roughly exponentially, each additional digit of precision in the initial data buys a fixed, small increment of foresight rather than a proportionate one. The demon is not defeated by a limit on its intelligence but by the grain of its ruler, and no amount of computation refunds that.
- Give the reader something to hold once point-prediction is gone: the individual trajectory is lost, but the shape it lives on is not. The orbit you compute is not the orbit that occurs, yet on hyperbolic sets it is shadowed by a genuine one, and what survives the horizon is statistical — the measure on the attractor, the difference between saying what the weather will do and saying what the climate does. Let the tornada be where this is cashed out.
- If you want a concrete image, use the two lobes of the Lorenz attractor: the system circles one wing for a while and then crosses to the other, and which turn the crossing comes on is the thing that cannot be had in advance. Do not use a butterfly flapping its wings, and do not end by moralizing that chaos leaves room for free will or for the soul. Sensitivity amplifies noise; it does not manufacture agency.
- Let the form do the work. The sestina's retrograde rotation is a strict rule whose output reads as wandering, which is exactly the philosophical point; and the whole poem is exquisitely sensitive to its initial conditions, since swapping one end-word redistributes every stanza. Don't announce either fact inside the poem.

After the poem, add a note of at most 120 words, addressed to the seminar instructor, naming which lines carry the determinism/predictability distinction, the cost of precision, and what survives the horizon, and flagging any line you think an undergraduate will misread.
