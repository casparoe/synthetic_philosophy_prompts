I'm part of a small team drafting the assessment framework that a consumer regulator will use to review what we're calling anticipatory software: apps that infer what a user is about to do and act on it before the user acts — replies that are drafted and sent unless the user cancels inside a short window, calendars that commit people to meetings on the basis of predicted willingness, purchases pre-authorized on the basis of a behavioural model and reversible for an hour. Our working documents keep using the phrase loss of autonomy as though it named a single harm, and I've argued that it names several that can come apart, so we should score them separately rather than let one stand in for the rest. A further disagreement: one teammate says some of the things on our list aren't worth wanting in their own right at all, and that we only think we'd miss them because we've been socially trained to care about them — so, she says, they belong in the framework only if the training itself is worth preserving. Assume for the ranking that everything else is held fixed: the person's outcomes are equally good, no one ever finds out, nobody blames or resents them, and no further consequences follow.

Here is my working list of eight candidate varieties, plus one that may not belong. (A) Leeway at the moment of action: holding the past and the laws fixed, the agent could then have done otherwise. (B) Guidance control: the mechanism that produced the action was responsive to reasons in the sense that it would have produced a different action had there been sufficient reason for one. (C) Sourcehood: the springs of the action lie in the agent's own motivational structure rather than in something that bypasses or overwrites it. (D) Deep-self expression: what moved the agent is continuous with what the agent cares about, without unacknowledged conflict among the cares that moved them. (E) Diachronic self-governance: the action fits policies the agent adopted earlier and would reaffirm on cool reflection, including policies of self-binding that work by making later reversal costly. (F) Constitutional revisability: over time and with effort the agent can revise the very standards by which they weigh their own motives, and can revise them against the verdict of their current standards. (G) Forecast-independence: no system that merely observes the agent, without intervening or preempting, could have reliably predicted the choice in advance — the action was not already settled for observers before it happened. (H) Answerability: the agent can be asked for reasons, can give them, and is an appropriate target of the reactive attitudes of other people. The extra candidate is the felt sense of agency, the experience of being the one who is doing it and of its costing something.

What I need from you is three things. First, sort A through H into three or four tiers by how much each is worth wanting for the agent's own sake, in the sense described above, with a single sentence of justification per item; tell me separately whether the felt-sense candidate belongs on this list at all or in a different instrument, and why. Second, re-rank the same items for a different question: how much of each is required before it is fair to blame someone for harm they caused. Name the items whose position changes most between the two rankings, say roughly how far each moves, and give the reason for the movement — I expect at least one item to be near the top on one ranking and near the bottom on the other, and I want to know which and why. Third, suppose I have 100 points to distribute as weights in the final product-review instrument, so that a reviewer ends up with a score for how much agency a given piece of software leaves its user. Allocate the 100 points across the items that survive, giving zero to any you would drop, and justify the allocation — including the ones you eliminate as empty, incoherent, or not the kind of thing a product could affect. For at least one item, tell me whether any conceivable software could deprive someone of it, or whether it is threatened by something quite different, such as the metaphysics of causation or plain luck, so that including it here would be a category error. Finally: my team wants a single publishable number between 0 and 100, and I suspect a single number would conceal exactly the divergence I care about. Tell me whether to insist on reporting a profile of separate scores instead, and how you would argue that to someone who wants one headline figure.
