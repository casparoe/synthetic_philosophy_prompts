I got pulled into an argument at our local rationalist meetup after the city charter commission floated replacing plurality with ranked ballots, and I have not been able to find the exit. One member says the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem settles the whole question, and not in favour of the reform. Here is his argument, cleaned up into steps I can't fault individually:

(1) Call a decision rule honest if no voter ever does better by reporting a ranking other than her true one, whatever the other voters report. (2) Gibbard–Satterthwaite: with three or more options, every honest and onto rule is dictatorial — some fixed voter's favourite always wins. (3) So no non-dictatorial voting rule is honest, and every candidate reform — ranked ballots, approval, score, whatever — is manipulable somewhere. (4) And manipulability cannot really be a defect in a decision rule, because the same reasoning applies to a single agent. Suppose I have committed myself to a fixed procedure for choosing among three routes home — a budgeting rule, or an app I have promised to obey — which takes my ranking as input and returns a choice, is onto, and does not simply hand me my favourite, since overruling me is the whole point of having a rule. Then by (2) there is some ranking at which I do better by feeding the procedure a ranking that is not mine, i.e. by lying to myself. (5) But a single agent cannot gain by lying to herself: I know the procedure, I know what I want, and nothing stands between my report and the truth for me to exploit. So the manipulation in (4) is an artifact of the formal setup, not something that could count against a procedure. (6) Hence honesty was never a genuine virtue in a decision rule in the first place, the theorem is just a fact about a badly chosen formal property, and strategic voting — the familiar business of deserting your favourite minor candidate — is not a cost that any institutional design can reduce or that anyone has reason to worry about.

The conclusion is one I don't want, and I can't locate the move that generates it. If I have to choose, I would rather keep the thought that the theorem is a genuine constraint on institutional design than the thought that strategic voting is costless, but I would also rather not end up telling the commission that voting reform is pointless. A second person at the meetup added that random dictatorship — draw a voter uniformly and implement her top — is honest by the definition in (1) while not being dictatorial, which she says shows the theorem's bite comes entirely from requiring a single deterministic winner rather than from anything about voting; I can't tell whether that helps or just moves the bump under the carpet.

Reply as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion: name the step you reject, say why it looked fine, price the two most tempting alternatives to rejecting it, and finish with what I should actually say to the charter commission.
