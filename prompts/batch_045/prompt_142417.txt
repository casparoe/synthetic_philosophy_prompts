I'm writing a paper on the inefficacy objection and I've built an argument that I now can't get out of. It concludes something I don't believe, but every premise looks true and every repair I know of fails. I'd like you to tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs. The argument, in my own notation:

**(1) Difference-making.** I am morally required to refrain from doing X only if refraining makes an expected difference to something that matters — some outcome is better in expectation given that I refrain than given that I don't. If the expected difference is zero, refraining is at best optional; and if it is zero while refraining costs me something, then I have no reason at all to do it, and telling me that I do is manipulating me with a causal claim I know to be false.

**(2) Threshold structure.** Individual consumption doesn't propagate to individual production. Take the numbers generously in the activist's favour: a household buys one chicken a week; the retailer orders from the processor in cases of forty birds, rounded up; the processor's breeding contract with the hatchery is set quarterly in lots of ten thousand and revised on a forecast. My one bird is three or four orders of magnitude below the smallest decision threshold in the chain, and the rounding is a matter of accounting policy, not of chance. So it isn't that I have a small probability of being the pivotal purchase; the probability is zero. The counterfactual *had I not bought, one fewer bird would have been bred* is false, and the same goes for each of my emissions, my gallon of water, my share in an index fund.

**(3)** So by (1) and (2) I am not required to refrain, and I have no reason to. And what holds for me holds for every consumer: no individual consumer anywhere is required to change what they buy, and the person who says otherwise is making a false causal claim.

**(4) The standard repairs fail.** The pivotality reply — a tiny chance of tipping the rounding is still a chance, and multiplied by the harm to ten thousand birds it dominates the cost to me — is worthless here, because in the deterministic case the chance is zero and zero times anything is zero. The group-membership reply — I am part of the group that is doing this — looks like a redescription of the fact that many people each make no difference, not an answer to it. And the universalization reply is irrelevant: *what if everyone did that?* is not a counterfactual about my act. If everyone else refrained, my purchase would still fall below the threshold; if no one else refrained, my refraining would still change nothing. Nothing about my situation shifts, so nothing about it can generate a duty for me.

**(5) It generalizes past the point of credibility.** Nothing in the argument is specific to chickens or to consumers. It applies wherever an agent's contribution sits below a decision threshold in a system that aggregates contributions. That covers my emissions; but it equally covers the emissions of a single plant, a single firm, a single city, a single country — each is far below the threshold at which any global climate outcome changes. So no agent at any scale has an obligation to do anything about climate change, and the aggregate outcome, which is straightforwardly *caused* by the sum of all these inert contributions, is nobody's responsibility. An argument that relieves every agent of every obligation while leaving the harm fully caused seems to prove too much to be sound.

**(6) The escape routes are morally the wrong shape.** Suppose I grant that the threshold's location is never known exactly: then over my uncertainty each bird has roughly a one-in-ten-thousand chance of tipping a lot, so my expected difference is about one bird and the duty comes back. But now my obligation is hostage to facts about poultry logistics — it exists if the hatchery's forecast is noisy and vanishes if it rounds deterministically, and it exists or not depending on whether I happen to know the rounding rule. Those two cases are not morally different in any way I can locate, so a view that separates them has mislocated something. And the same worry repeats at the level of policies rather than acts: sixty years of one bird a week is about three thousand birds, still under one quarterly lot, so even a lifetime of abstention is inert if the response function is a step function — whereas if the contract is set on a moving average, the lifetime habit eventually tips it. So whether my life's conduct matters at all turns on which accounting convention a firm uses. I don't accept that my obligations have that dependency, but I can't say what's wrong with the inference.

**(7) It is self-limiting in a way no moral argument should be.** Suppose someone says: fine, but *believing* (3) makes you and everyone you tell consume more, so accepting the conclusion is itself difference-making and you have reason not to accept or spread it. Then the argument is true only for as long as it is not widely believed, and the more persuasive it is the more likely it is to become false. Arguments in ethics are not normally contingent on their own uptake in that way, which makes me think the error is upstream — but not where.

My own preference, if I must choose: I'd rather keep (2) than (1). I think the empirical claim about lumpy supply chains is right, and I don't want a moral theory that commits me to saying my Thursday night purchase raises the number of birds bred. But every replacement for (1) I can think of proves too much in the other direction: if mere membership in a causally inefficacious group can obligate me, or if I must do my share of what the group ought to do, then I owe it to everyone to join every boycott, sign every petition, and offset every emission, and the size of what I owe is fixed by facts about aggregates I cannot observe.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a survey. Name the step you reject — premise or inference — and say which kind of mistake it is: a false premise, or a step that is valid only on a reading the premises don't support. Then price the alternatives: what do I have to accept if I keep (2) and drop (1), and what if I keep (1) and attack (2) or (4)? Tell me whether the deterministic case and the noisy case can differ in what I owe, and why or why not; tell me whether the policy/act distinction in (6) is doing real work or is a composition confusion in the other direction; and tell me what principle should replace (1), and what that principle implies in overdetermination cases — ten people each dumping a unit into a lake where any five units would kill the fish, so that five of the ten are causally inert — and in the demandingness worry at the end. Take a definite position, argue for it against the alternatives rather than listing them, and say how confident you are in the diagnosis and what finding would change your mind.
