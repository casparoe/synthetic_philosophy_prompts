I run a fortnightly reading group on limits of knowledge — about 18 people, mostly philosophy grad students and advanced undergrads, plus four or five CS people who've taken algorithms but basically no philosophy. I've got one spare 75-minute session before the end of term and I want to spend it on the philosophical bite of computational complexity. The room has a whiteboard and a projector I'd rather not switch on; I can print handouts and I've got index cards, dice, and about forty envelopes. No laptops if I can help it — last time somebody opened one, the whole thing turned into a puzzle hunt, everybody had a good time, and nobody noticed there was supposed to be a philosophical point. The thing I want them to feel rather than be told is the difference between three claims that are incredibly easy to run together: call them (1) opacity — for this deterministic system, fully specified, no hidden variables, the only way we can currently find out what it does at step N is to grind it out; (2) irreducibility — there is no algorithm that gets there substantially faster than grinding it out, which is a claim about the space of possible algorithms and not a claim about us being slow, lazy, or short of paper; and (3) the awkward meta-point that we basically can't establish (2) for any system anyone actually cares about, that the felt experience of having run into irreducible hardness is indistinguishable from the experience of not having found the trick yet, and that the person running the session doesn't know either. Ideally the room spends most of the hour living inside (1), gets powerfully tempted by (2), and only at the end finds out that nothing they did was evidence for (2). If they walk out saying something like complexity theory has shown that some things are impossible to predict in practice, I've definately failed.

So: design me the activity. Concrete rules, materials, a timeline with minute marks, how to split 18 people up, and a payoff scheme that doesn't need money (I can do chocolate or participation credit, and I'd quite like a betting element that punishes overconfidence). The idea has to arrive through what happens to them rather than through me talking — I'd like the words irreducible, intractable, and P vs NP to not be said out loud until at least the forty-minute mark, and there should be no lecture up front beyond how to play. My rough thought, which you should improve on or throw out: give different groups different deterministic rules where you can jump straight to step N if you spot the structure (something like x → (ax + b) mod m, where there's a closed form you can evaluate fast), and others where nobody knows a shortcut and nobody can prove there isn't one (something Collatz-ish), don't tell them which kind they've got, and let them discover in the debrief that some groups had an easy time for a structural reason and others didn't. There should also be a moment where they extrapolate — they grind out ten steps by hand in a couple of minutes, then get handed an N in the billions and have to say out loud what that would actually cost them. I'm slightly worried the shortcut systems will read as there's obviously a trick and the hard ones as this is obviously a famous open problem, so if you can think of systems that dodge both readings, use those instead.

Then the debrief: map specific moments of the activity onto (1), (2) and (3) — which minute each claim becomes available to them and what the facilitator says or asks at that moment. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, including the smart-aleck who finds a route into the hard system I didn't anticipate (isn't that the lesson happening live? how do I use it rather than shut it down?), the CS people who already have the vocabulary and will blurt it out early, the group that gives up and starts chatting, and whoever decides the whole exercise is really about human patience. And be honest about the part the activity can't reproduce: my sense is that a room full of people with paper and an hour can generate the feeling of irreducibility but not one scrap of evidence for it, since hardness is a claim about an infinite family of instances and about how difficulty scales, and a classroom only ever touches individual instances at one or two sizes. Say how the facilitator has to supply that, and what the honest takeaway is for the determinism-and-predictability question (the Laplace's demon sort of thing) that made me want to run this session in the first place.
