I'm writing a short critical piece on geometric rationality, and I need one concrete example built to order rather than borrowed from the standard expositions.

The thesis I want to put pressure on is that geometric rationality quietly runs together two criteria that are usually advertised as the same thing: maximize the expected logarithm of wealth (the Kelly rule), and maximize the median terminal outcome. Popular treatments slide between them freely, and in the long run they do coincide. I want a small case where they don't.

Specifically: I need a decision problem in which the stake that maximizes the median terminal wealth is strictly greater than the Kelly stake, where that greater stake is genuinely the median-maximizing one and not merely better than Kelly at a single comparison point.

Specifications for the scenario:

1. Realistic and small. A repeatable, independent, even-money opportunity: a stake equal to some fraction f of current wealth is either doubled or lost, over a fixed and modest number n of rounds, with a modest but real edge. I'd like n no larger than 12 and the win probability no higher than 0.65, so the edge is one a person could actually have. No borrowing or leverage, so f ranges over [0,1]; no outside capital injected or withdrawn mid-play. Give it a concrete skin in one short paragraph — a small firm deciding what share of working capital to commit to a repeatable venture, a trader with a fixed number of opportunities, a tournament bankroll — rather than an abstract betting machine.

2. Three verdicts, derived rather than stipulated. State the stake selected by maximizing expected log growth, the stake that maximizes median terminal wealth over all f in [0,1], and the stake that maximizes expected terminal wealth, with a note on where the last one sits relative to the other two. The second must exceed the first, and the third must not be confused with either.

3. The separation has to be substantive, not cosmetic. The median terminal wealth at the median-maximizing stake should exceed that at the Kelly stake by at least a couple of percent, and it must not rest on a tie, on rounding, or on a contestable convention about which value counts as the median of a discrete distribution. If your chosen parameters leave the relevant median ambiguous, choose different ones.

4. Everything must be checkable by exact enumeration. Give enough of the binomial arithmetic that a reader with a calculator can verify all three verdicts by hand. No simulations, no asymptotics, and no continuous-time limit anywhere in the construction itself.

What I want after the scenario:

(a) Walk through each of the three criteria in turn: name the quantity being optimized, the stake it selects, and the resulting figure for terminal wealth — expected log growth rate, median, and expected value respectively — so I can see the rankings come out of the numbers.

(b) Identify the feature of the case that is doing the work. My suspicion is that it has something to do with the median of the distribution of the number of wins failing to coincide with its mean when the horizon is short, but I have not been able to get the arithmetic to come out, so confirm or correct that hunch. If there is a general expression for the median-optimal stake in terms of the number of rounds and the median number of wins, give it.

(c) Remove the feature and show the gap close. Recompute for one nearby variant — a larger n, or a win probability that makes the expected number of wins a whole number, or the Gaussian limiting case — and say precisely which of these restores the coincidence between the two criteria and why.

(d) Be honest about what the example does and does not establish. Is the divergence a real disagreement between two live readings of geometric rationality, or an artifact of a tiny horizon and of summarizing a whole distribution by one order statistic? Which criterion should someone who calls themselves a geometric rationalist actually adopt here, and what is the strongest case available against the other? Address the obvious line of defense on the expected-log side — that median maximization is not myopic, depends on an arbitrarily chosen horizon, and fails to compose when an extra round is added — and tell me whether that defense actually lands.

(e) Close with a single sentence on why any of this matters to someone who has to size a real bet, rather than to someone taxonomizing decision rules. I suspect the honest version of this involves the median-maximizing stake carrying a lower, perhaps even slightly negative, expected growth rate, but report what the numbers say.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in each load-bearing claim, and single out the one you are least sure of. If some combination of my specifications turns out to be unsatisfiable, tell me which constraint has to give and why, rather than quietly relaxing it: a case that stipulates the verdicts instead of generating them from the criteria is the one thing I don't want.
