ok so I need help settling an argument I had with my friend Mara last night that went on way too long. It started because she repeated the "you only use 10% of your brain" thing for the hundredth time and I said something snarky about how the most successful ideas aren't the true ones, and three drinks later we were still going.

Here's where we got stuck. Mara is into meme theory in the old, pre-internet sense: ideas as replicators. A cultural item is a thing that gets copied, the copies vary, some variants are better at getting themselves copied than others, and the ones that spread are the ones that are good at spreading — not the ones that are true, or useful, or good for the people carrying them. I pushed back with a different picture: transmission isn't copying at all, it's rebuilding. When you pass an idea on you don't hand over a file, you reconstruct it from what you remember plus what makes sense to you, and that reconstruction is systematically biased toward what's easy to picture, what's emotionally punchy, what slots into what you already believe. So on my picture the reason some ideas keep showing up isn't that a faithful lineage won a competition, it's that lots of different starting points drain into the same resting place. I tried to sharpen this with a distinction she hated: call an item copy-proof if it survives transmission by resisting distortion (a rhyme, a number, a slogan with a rigid form), and self-correcting if it survives because distortions get pulled back — you garble it, the person you tell it to re-garbles it into the same shape, over and over. My claim was that Darwinian selection needs something like copy-proofness, and that once most of the stability is self-correction you're not looking at a Darwinian process anymore, you're looking at convergence: the same shape arriving over and over from unrelated starting points, the way similar body plans show up again and again in lineages that never shared an ancestor. Mara's reply, which I couldn't answer, was that biology is exactly as messy — genes get mutated, broken, recombined, repaired — and nobody concludes that evolution isn't Darwinian because of it.

So: is the difference between our two pictures an actual empirical disagreement, or are we just describing one process at two zoom levels and fighting about words? Please run the three usual conditions for something to count as a Darwinian process — variation, heritable-ish transmission, differential fitness — and tell me for each whether my "self-correcting" case really fails it or only meets it loosely, and whether meeting it loosely is enough. Then tell me what observation would actually decide the thing between us, something checkable in principle with a telephone-chain experiment or with historical records of variants, no library required; and whether there's a real distinction hiding in my "stability that needs a lineage vs. stability that doesn't" line, or whether that's just a bad way of restating the disagreement. Also: does any of this license the snarky thing I said at the start, or does neither picture support "widespread therefore probably false"?

Give me your bottom line in the very first sentence — which picture is doing more work, or whether the fight is verbal — then a compact table putting the two accounts side by side on those dimensions, then the reasoning. No preamble, no restating my question back at me, and please don't open with anything like "great question."
