Draft the joint statement that a welfarist defender of choice architecture and an autonomy-based critic of nudging could both sign after a year-long adversarial collaboration. Take the defender's position to be the asymmetric-paternalist one: architecture is justified when it cheaply helps agents who are error-prone while leaving agents who are not error-prone able to opt out at trivial cost. Take the critic's position to be that a nudge can wrong its target even when it raises that target's own expected welfare and preserves cheap opt-out, because it shapes behaviour in a way that bypasses the agent's own reasoning.

I want four things, in this format.

1. A numbered section of claims both sides can sign. My candidates: that no environment of choice is architecturally neutral, so the question is never architecture versus no architecture; that the right counterfactual against which to measure a nudge's benefit is itself part of what is in dispute; and that the critic's central complaint is not a claim about welfare loss and should not be evaluated as one. Add others you think genuinely survive, and cut any that are vacuous.

2. A table of residual disagreements, one per row, each row giving the defender's formulation in wording the defender would accept and then the critic's formulation in wording the critic would accept — no question-begging terms in either column without a stipulative gloss. The rows I have in mind: the unit of moral evaluation, whether it is the single nudged choice or the lifetime sequence of defaults a single agent accumulates under one architect (call this default sedimentation); whether disclosure or transparency repairs whatever is lost; and whether habitual reliance on architecture degrades an agent's capacity to decide well in unarchitected settings (call this the de-skilling worry). For each row, say whether the disagreement is substantive or merely verbal, and why.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one side.

4. For each crux, one case or piece of evidence both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Possible material: replacing auto-enrollment in retirement savings with mandatory active choice and then measuring the same agents' later, un-nudged financial decisions; or longitudinal work on whether the gains from a default persist once the default is withdrawn. Say plainly if you think a crux has no evidence either side would accept as bearing on it.
