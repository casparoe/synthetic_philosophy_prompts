I'm giving the toast at my mother's eightieth birthday dinner: roughly forty people, about half of them her present and former colleagues in a philosophy department and half family who have spent a career nodding politely through shop talk. She has spent that career defending the Kripkean package together — names as rigid designators, the necessity of origin, the necessary a posteriori — and she lives inside it more literally than most people do: she was adopted, met her birth family in her fifties, and has always insisted both that she could not have had different parents and that finding out who they were changed nothing about who she is. One of her oldest friends, who will be at the table, is a Lewis counterpart theorist who is perfectly happy to say things about her origins that she regards as strictly impossible.

The difficulty is that most of the warmth available in a birthday toast runs on one of two engines she has disabled: counterfactuals about where or from whom someone might have come, or discoveries that quietly re-make a person. Her own view also makes the discovery of her origins the discovery of necessary truths about her, which she refuses to treat either as trivial or as re-identifying. Please write the five-minute toast so that every line is one both she and her counterpart-theoretic friend could accept, without the disagreement ever being named, and so that it lands as affection rather than as a lecture. Then list the two or three warm lines you reached for and had to discard, and say exactly which commitment blocked each. Finally, the department is announcing a scholarship in her name and the plaque will bear one of the two surnames she has published under; whatever the toast does with that, it should not imply that a name is a description of the person it names.
