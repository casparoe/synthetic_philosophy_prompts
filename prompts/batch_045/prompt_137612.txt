I hold, and am about to publish, the following account of credit for discovery. Define the counterfactual delay of a result as the interval in years between the year the result was actually published and the year in which the first person other than the actual author would have published it, holding fixed the publicly available resources of the field at the time but not the subsequent behaviour of the field's members. Where that interval is under about a decade, call the result interchangeable. My thesis is that the credit owed to a discoverer varies inversely with the counterfactual delay of her result, with the corollary that for an interchangeable result the discoverer deserves no distinctive credit at all, since what she did is what the next competent worker in the field would have done within the decade. On this account the calculus owes little to Newton or to Leibniz, Darwin's priority over Wallace secures him considerably less than the tradition grants him, and Mendel, whose result sat unread for some three decades, gets nearly everything.

What I want from you is the case against it. Reply as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, ordered from the consideration that should move me most to the one that should move me least, with no introduction and no conclusion. For each item say whether it is fatal or merely awkward, whether a defender in my position would concede it or could absorb it by qualifying the thesis, and whether it is live in the historical record as I would recognise it or only a logical possibility that no actual case instantiates.

At least one item should concern whether the counterfactual delay is well defined at all, given that removing Newton removes everything Newton contributed to the resources available to Leibniz and to everyone else, and whether my thesis therefore risks being insulated from evidence rather than supported by it. At least one item should concern a distinction I have been sliding over, between arrival delay, where a field could not yet have produced the result, and uptake delay, where the result was produced and then ignored, and should say whether that distinction breaks the metric or merely refines it. At least one should consider whether credit might properly attach to the form in which a result is delivered, its notation, its argumentative architecture, its integration into a research programme, rather than to its propositional content, and whether the metric as stated can see that difference.

I am unmoved by anything that amounts to insisting that credit tracks genius, effort, or moral desert independently of counterfactuals, since that is precisely the view I am arguing against; what moves me is a consideration that shows the metric counts the wrong thing while granting my framing. Please suggest no readings, and let nothing in your answer depend on a citation.
