I want one joke for the last slide of my lecture on Berkeley's master argument, and I would like it to do genuine pedagogical work rather than gesture at the subject.

Here is the machinery I have in mind. The argument's premise is an invitation: the objector is asked to conceive of a sensible thing existing unconceived, as in the passage in the Principles where he protests that nothing is easier than imagining trees standing in a park with nobody by to perceive them. The trap is supposed to be that the attempt itself supplies a conceiving, so that what you end up conceiving is not what you were asked to conceive. The critic's reply is that this shows at most something about the activity of conceiving, not about trees: there is a slide from a fact about the attempt (my framing of an unconceived tree is itself a framing of it) to a conclusion about the object of the attempt (that unconceived existence is impossible, or inconceivable). Call that the reflexivity shift.

So: write me a joke whose punchline turns on the reflexivity shift, in a setting with no trees, no forests, no falling timber, and no appeal to God perceiving things when we are not looking, and without punning on the slogan that to be is to be perceived. The laugh should come from someone drawing a conclusion about the thing attempted from a feature of the attempt, and it should be recognizably that mistake rather than a generic absurdity. I would also like the joke to work for an audience that thinks the master argument is a bad argument: it should dramatize why the shift is illegitimate, not assume the argument succeeds.

Then give me, in at most two sentences, the mapping: which element of the joke plays the role of the conceiving, which plays the sensible object, and what the attempted-but-self-defeating task is. Joke plus mapping, under 130 words total, no preamble.
