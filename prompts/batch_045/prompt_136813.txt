I run a fortnightly reading group that does a sort of shared wiki, and last session we spent ninety minutes going in circles on quantum suicide and quantum immortality and got nowhere, because everyone kept saying "yes but what do you mean, expect?" So I want to put a decision tree on the wiki: something that starts from a commitment everyone in the group already shares and ends at a named position, so next time someone can be told which leaf they're on instead of re-arguing the setup. Nobody needs convincing of Everett or decoherence or that macroscopic outcomes really branch, so don't spend nodes on that; start the tree after it.

What I want, concretely:

Start node: "I accept no-collapse QM, branching, and the Born rule as the right third-personal probability rule." From there, seven or eight branch nodes, at most twelve leaves. Every node has to be a question someone could answer before they know which leaf it's pushing them toward — a concrete case or a claim, never the name of a view, and ideally not even a word like "measure" or "fission" where you can avoid it. Each leaf gets a name (or "unnamed, as far as I know") plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

The angle I want is this: build the tree around the word *expect*. The question the tree is really sorting people by is what "what should I expect to experience after the trigger fires?" even means, and whether it has a well-defined answer. So your top split should be about whether that's a factual question about which future observer-moment is yours, or a question about which anticipation it's sensible to adopt, or a question with no answer at all. Don't bury that distinction three nodes down.

Distinctions I want to see doing work somewhere in the tree: whether first-personal expectation tracks squared amplitude or whether every branch containing a suitable successor counts the same; what happens to personal identity at a branch point (full continuity into both, divided or diminished, two new people, no fact of the matter); whether a successor needs any psychological continuity or whether bare continued existence is enough; whether there's a threshold below which a branch stops containing you at all; whether any of this generalises past a clean kill / no-kill setup to the messy way people actually die.

And please build the diagnostic questions out of these cases rather than out of the textbook one:

1. The graded gun. Same apparatus, but instead of kill (amplitude-squared .99) or unharmed (.01), it's kill (.99) or knocked out and left with dense anterograde amnesia and no memory of ever volunteering for the experiment (.01). Only one branch has any experience in it. Do you expect to be the amnesiac in the hospital? Somebody who says yes is in a very different place from somebody who says "I expect nothing, with probability .99", and I want the tree to force that.

2. The inverted case. Wire it so the *low*-amplitude branch is the one where you die: you're fine in .99, dead in .01. And alongside it, an ordinary quantum lottery where the .01 branch is you winning a huge sum. The uniform-over-existing-branches reading seems to say you should bank on the jackpot, and nobody believes that, but I want the node phrased so people commit before they notice.

3. The thousandth round. You've run it a thousand times; there's a live, coherent successor of you in a branch of measure around 2^-1000. Is that still survival in the relevant sense, is the experiment still supposed to work, and should you expect to find yourself extremely old? I know Tegmark's own reply is that real death is gradual rather than binary, and the tree should have a place for the person who says quantum immortality is fine as a claim about idealised boxes and nonsense as a claim about dying.

4. The friend outside the box. Most of the measure of your friends sees you die. So: does running the experiment give the subject evidence for many worlds, evidence only in principle and unshareable in practice, or no evidence at all — and is that a separate node from the ones about survival, or does it collapse into one? Decide and say why.

Leaves I want present somewhere: strong quantum immortality as genuine evidence for MWI; the deflationary reading where of course you survive but the thought experiment shows nothing; the measure-weighted reading where you should expect death and the whole thing is a fallacy; no-fact-of-the-matter about which branch is you; first-personal probability simply undefined under branching; the pragmatic anticipation-policy reading; and the degradation reading where it works until your branch stops being able to support you. If you think two of those can't be pulled apart by any question you can honestly ask, say so explicitly rather than inventing a node that pretends to separate them — I'd rather have a flagged collision than a fake fork.

Finally: the tree will have answer-combinations that are allowed by the logical space but that nobody seems to be standing in. Pick three or four of the emptier ones and give me a line each on whether they're empty because they're incoherent, because they're just unattractive, or because nobody has bothered. No references or reading list needed, I know who wrote what — I only want the structure. Two pages-ish, definately no more than four.
