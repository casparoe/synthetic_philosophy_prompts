I sit on my country's church–state commission — we're the body that has to sign off on any new arrangement between the state and religious communities before it goes to parliament. We've never written down an actual rule, we just decide case by case, and I'm starting to think we're all over the place. Here's everything we've decided so far:

- Yes to the state collecting a church tax through the payroll system for any registered religious community that clears 0.5% of taxpayers, as long as leaving is a one-line form at the town hall.
- No to crucifixes in every public school classroom.
- Yes to church-run hospitals and kindergartens getting public money on exactly the same terms as secular ones.
- Yes to denominational religion classes in state schools, taught by teachers the religious community itself accredits, with a real ethics alternative, no pressure about which one you pick, and any registered community allowed to run one if enough parents ask.
- No to a short opening reflection led by the mayor at the start of municipal council meetings, which councillors were expected to stand for.
- Yes to a state-funded faculty of theology and religious studies at the public university, hiring on academic grounds only, nobody signing a confession.
- Yes to individual teachers and pupils wearing visible religious symbols of their own, no to the school itself putting any up.

Next month's case: the education ministry wants to put state-paid chaplains into every secondary school. The chaplains would be recruited and salaried by the state, any pupil could walk in and talk to them, and kids who don't want one get a secular counsellor instead. The catch is that only the four communities with "recognised" status from the old concordat — Catholic, Lutheran, Reformed, Jewish — can supply chaplains; the Muslim, Buddhist and humanist associations asked in and were told not yet. There's also talk of funding the whole thing through a per-pupil pastoral-care voucher that follows the kid instead of a block grant to the communities.

So: what should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves, and which of our old decisions is the closest analogue — and how good is that analogy really? I can see at least three rules that fit all seven precedents fine: something like "OK as long as nobody is made to take part and there's a real way out", "the state may pay for the secular and social things religion does but must never speak religiously itself", and "OK whenever any worldview, religious or not, can get the same deal on equal terms". They definately don't all give the same answer here. Which one do you think we've actually been running, what does each predict about the chaplaincy proposal, does the voucher bit change anything, and what single tweak to the proposal — or what follow-up case — would flush out which rule we're on? And if our precedents genuinely don't decide it, I'd rather hear what principle we'd each be signing up to with the yes and with the no than just get a verdict.
