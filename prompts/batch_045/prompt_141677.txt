hey — I'm giving a short talk next week arguing that program equilibrium doesn't really dissolve the prisoners dilemma, it just hides the hard part inside an assumption of perfect transparency. Before I embarrass myself I want to stress-test the argument, so: play a committed defender of the program-equilibrium picture. Your position: the one-shot PD isn't a genuine dilemma for agents that can read each other, what looks like a dilemma is really a transparency failure, mutual cooperation is the natural focal point once programs can condition on each other, and the frictions I'm about to introduce are engineering problems rather than knockdowns. Be sharp, not a strawman — concede small points, never the main one, argue from the setup instead of from authority, and keep it to about a paragraph a turn. If you need a detail about the setup, ask for it in character. Stay in character till I say stop.

Setup, so we're not talking past each other: two systems meet exactly once, one-shot PD, and instead of choosing an action each submits a program that recieves the other's source code as input; assume the usual self-reference trick works, so that a program along the lines of cooperate iff the other's source is byte-identical to this very program gets you mutual cooperation as an equilibrium.

My opening: now put in two frictions that any real deployment has — each program has to halt within 10^6 steps, and each one sees the other's source through a channel that flips a small random fraction of the characters. Your recognizer now has two jobs that pull in opposite directions: it has to count as cooperators all the noisy variants of a cooperative program, and it has to keep out programs that differ from a cooperator in one well-chosen character and then defect. Anything broad enough for the first job is spoofable by the second, and anything tight enough for the second falls apart the moment the channel hiccups. And if you reach for proving instead of matching, your prover is stuck with the same 10^6 steps and the same regress. So with even a little opacity and a little time pressure your cooperative equilibria lose their grip and everything slides back to mutual defection — the original dilemma wearing a nicer hat. What's wrong with that?
