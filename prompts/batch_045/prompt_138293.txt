I'm a third-year undergraduate and I sit, as the student representative, on the working group that is writing the rules for the permanent version of our campus "Exchange" pilot. This term I'm also taking a seminar on Aristotle's practical philosophy, and because the reading seemed directly on point I volunteered to draft the group's recommendation memo. I'd like you to draft it for me to revise and circulate.

The background: eighteen months ago the university launched a peer-service exchange. Students log hours of work for other students — tutoring, lab assistance, moving, bike repair, note-taking — and receive one Exchange Unit per hour, a flat rate that ignores what the service was. Units are then spent on anyone else's services. The pilot has been popular but thinly traded, and it sunsets at the end of spring term. The working group must send the Dean of Students a recommendation on four contested design questions:

1. Resale. Students have built an app that lets holders sell unused units to other students at negotiated rates, typically well below the unit's notional value. Do we prohibit resale, permit it, or permit it only at par — that is, only through the platform and only by transferring units rather than converting them to cash?
2. Accumulation and expiry. There is currently no cap and no expiry. Some members want balances to decay a fixed percentage each term to keep units circulating; others want a hard cap on what one student may hold.
3. Bulk purchase. Several student organizations want to buy units from the university at a discount in bulk and distribute them as they see fit, including as payment for event setup. This would inject outside money into the exchange.
4. Yield. One member proposes that students holding large positive balances for a full term receive a bonus credited to their account, as an incentive to participate early.

Our staff analyst has framed the choice for us in a way I find helpful but incomplete. She distinguishes what she calls the "tally" role of the unit — a unit as a public record that one student owes the community an hour of service — from its "holding" role, a durable claim on future services. She argues that a measure and a store of value have opposite design requirements: a measure has to stay stable to do its job, while a store has to stay durable, so a decaying unit is a bad store but a perfectly good measure, and a cashable unit is a good store but stops tracking the thing it was supposed to measure. She concludes that we can answer the four questions separately for each role. I think that's too clean, and I don't think the two roles can be pulled apart as easily as she assumes, but I'm not sure why.

What I want the memo to do is take this up in Aristotelian terms rather than in the analyst's terms, and to work the arguments out instead of announcing that Aristotle would disapprove of resale. The two texts I have are the critique of wealth-getting in Politics Book I — including the thought that the same object admits of a proper use and an exchange use, and that what is naturally bounded is bounded because it is a collection of tools for a substantive end, no craft having unlimited tools — and the account in Nicomachean Ethics Book V of money as a common measure that arises by agreement rather than by nature, in order to make unlike services comparable, and that also functions as a guarantee of exchange in the future. There is a tension I can't resolve and I want the memo to address it directly: the argument that acquisition has a natural limit seems to get its grip only if what is being accumulated is, in the relevant sense, an instrument serving an end, whereas in the Ethics money is characterized primarily as a measure, and it is not obvious that the instrument argument constrains how much of a measure one may hold. Our unit is arguably both at once, and I suspect the answer to at least one of the four questions turns on which characterization has priority.

Please write the memo as follows. It should be addressed to the working group and adoptable by the Dean: roughly a thousand to thirteen hundred words, committee-readable, with every term of art defined in a single clause. For each of the four questions it should give a concrete rule, in language we could actually put into the program's terms of use, rather than a principle in the abstract — and it should say what happens to units already held on the day the new rules take effect. It should treat the strongest case against its own recommendation at least as sympathetically as the members who hold it would; I expect that case to be, first, that banning resale pushes trading onto an unregulated app anyway and that a flat hourly rate misprices scarce skills, so the gray market is doing price discovery the exchange needs, and second, that on a common reading the target of the Aristotelian critique is not exchange for money as such but the treatment of getting as an end with no limit, which our own participation incentives arguably instantiate more than resale does. Where the memo's bottom line depends on a value judgment rather than on anything in the texts, mark it in a bracketed note for the group to vote on instead of settling it inside the prose.

Two further constraints. I cannot reach the library database from where I live and the Dean will not read references anyway, so please do not supply citations, notes, or a bibliography: name the passages in plain words and work from the arguments themselves. And please close with a short section on confidence. Separate clearly what Aristotle's texts evidently say and what most people who work on them would accept, from what is genuinely disputed among specialists — for instance how much of the critique is aimed at trade itself rather than at a boundless appetite, whether the natural/unnatural contrast there is a teleological claim or a moral one, whether the measure account commits him to any view about what fixes value, and whether the instrument and measure characterizations of money are even consistent with each other. State which reading the recommendation depends on, what would follow if a different reading is right, and which of the four questions the texts simply do not settle for us. I would rather send a memo that says where we are guessing than one that presents a contested reading as what everyone thinks.
