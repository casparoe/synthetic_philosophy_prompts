Hey — I'm the "life enrichment coordinator" (fancy title for the activites person) at a small memory care house, 14 residents, most of them mid-to-late stage, and I've been here about five months. Here's why I'm asking: I had a pretty bad stretch of anxiety last year and ran into this view — tranquilism, I think it's called, the Epicurean-sounding one — that the only thing that matters for someone's well-being is the absence of craving and suffering, and that actually getting what you want adds nothing over and above simply not having wanted it. That framing helped me personally, a lot, like I stopped treating my own wants as little emergencies. And now I keep wanting to use it at work, and I can't, because I have no idea how to actually run it on a decision.

The setup: I build the weekly calendar and I have to say yes or no to a constant stream of proposals — from families, from our aides, from the volunteer coordinator, from corporate. Therapy dog visits, a baked goods cart, Friday happy hour with wine, competitive bingo with prize money, a VR "virtual travel" headset thing, a resident choir, big family birthday parties in the common room, reminiscence sessions with 1940s music, a raised garden bed. Some are obviously fine, some obviously bad, and a bunch I genuinely can't tell. What I need is a decision procedure I can run by myself in about ten minutes per proposal, that the director of nursing has to sign off on, that I can explain to a family member in one sentence without sounding like a monster, and that gives corporate a written rationale for anything I cut.

Where I want you to do the real work is in what has to survive the translation, because I've already noticed it isn't just "pick the calmest option":

- Some of the best sessions I run are made of wanting. Bingo is nothing but wanting — you want the number, you almost have it, you get it. That wanting doesn't feel like suffering while it's going on, it feels like the point. But it also didn't exist before I put the cards on the table, so by the theory I manufactured a want and then fed it, which should be worth zero at best.
- Some activities seem to install a want that outlasts them. After a few weeks of dog visits one of our residents started asking for the dog every morning and is visibly upset the other six days. Did I create a craving out of nothing there, and does that make the whole dog program net negative even though it's the happiest twenty minutes of her week?
- Happy hour has a next morning. More agitation, worse sleep, at least for a couple of people. I don't know whether to count that as the craving leaving a residue or as a separate thing entirely.
- Telling people in advance. We used to post the week's menu and the visit schedule, and now I wonder if the anticipation is itself a cost — my mom (early-stage, not here yet, but I think about it constantly) will fixate on a planned thing for days. But the aides swear that knowing what's coming cuts distress.
- Whether "nothing happening" is really neutral. If the absence of craving is the only good, then a resident parked in front of the window isn't being harmed. I'm pretty sure she is. Boredom and restlessness feel like they should count, and I don't know how to count them without smuggling regular old pleasure back in through the side door.

So: give me the procedure. The questions in order, what a yes / no / stop-and-hand-to-a-human looks like at each step, where it hard-stops with a no, and where it stops because the question is really a values call and someone just has to decide. Be explicit about which distinctions from the theory you kept because a non-philosopher can actually apply them, which you threw out to fit in ten minutes, and what breaks because you threw them out. And tell me where this kind of procedure misfires on people who can't report their own mental states, which by next year will be most of my residents.

Then run it on three things I'm deciding this week:

1. The weekly baked goods cart — the smell alone gets half the wing worked up before it arrives, and two of our diabetics beg and then get told no.
2. Cutting Friday happy hour, which is the most popular thing we do and the only thing some families show up for.
3. Whether to tell Mrs. A that her son is coming Saturday, when he's flaked four times and she asks me about it maybe twenty times a day.

I definately expect at least one of those to come back with an answer I don't want. Say it anyway — that's kind of the point of asking for a procedure instead of a gut check.
