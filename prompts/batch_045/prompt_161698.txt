Question: (a) Explain Austin's distinction between constative and performative utterances, and explain why he ends up giving it up. (b) Jenna posts "I hereby pledge that I will run the blood drive" in the Pledge Portal, but she has already decided she is going to blow it off. Using Austin's categories of infelicity, say what has gone wrong here. Does the new handbook rule that a pledge is "void unless genuinely meant" change your answer? (c) Now suppose someone posts a pledge while logged in as their friend. What kind of failure is that, and how is it different from the failure in (b)?

Student answer: (a) Austin's starting point is that not all utterances describe or report things. Constatives describe a state of affairs and are assessed as true or false. Performatives don't describe anything, they actually do something: saying "I name this ship" or "I bet you five dollars" or "I apologise" is performing an action, not reporting one, so the question isn't whether it's true but whether it's happy or unhappy, felicitous or infelicitous. Performatives often have an explicit performative verb in the first person present ("I promise"), but they don't have to — "I'll be there at six" can be a promise with no explicit verb, and "guilty" said in court does the same job. Austin later gives up the constative/performative distinction because it doesn't really hold up: constatives can also go wrong in the "unhappy" way (saying "the cat is on the mat" when you have no idea, or don't believe it, is infelicitous), and performatives can be assessed in something like a true/false way (it's true that he promised). So he replaces it with three things in every utterance: the locutionary act of saying something with a certain sense and reference, the illocutionary act which is what you're doing in saying it (promising, warning, ordering), and the perlocutionary act which is what you bring about by saying it (persuading, alarming, getting someone to turn the car around). Austin also sorts illocutionary acts into five kinds — representatives, directives, commissives, expressives and declarations — and a pledge is obviously a commissive, committing you to a future course of action.

(b) Austin divides infelicities into misfires, where the act doesn't come off at all because the procedure doesn't exist or wasn't properly executed (wrong person, wrong circumstances, no accepted convention), and abuses, where the act does come off but is hollow because you didn't have the thoughts or feelings the procedure calls for. Normally Jenna's case would be an abuse: she really did perform the act of pledging, she just did it insincerely, and a broken pledge is a further abuse on top of that. But the handbook rule changes the classification. Since the school has now written "genuinely means it" into the rules of the procedure itself, sincerity has become a condition for the procedure to be correctly executed, so Jenna's post is no longer an abuse at all — it's a misfire, like saying the words with the wrong person or in the wrong order. She therefore hasn't promised anything: there is no pledge, the speech act simply didn't happen. This is the same kind of case as an actor saying "I promise" on stage, which Austin says is hollow and void and so not really a promise. It also shows why the Portal is interesting: by giving the pledge official backing, the school has basically upgraded a promise into a declaration, the category where the words themselves change reality and where the speaker has to occupy the right position in an institution (the umpire can call a strike, the batter can't).

(c) Posting while logged in as your friend is a different thing entirely. Here the procedure is accepted and available, but it's not executed by the right person, so it's a misfire rather than an abuse — the entrant isn't the pledger. And unlike (b), the failure doesn't depend on anybody's private mental state: it fails on the paperwork, so to speak, regardless of whether the friend genuinely meant it.
