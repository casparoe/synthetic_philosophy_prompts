I've been trying to work out what I actually think about when you're allowed to just take someone's word for something. Every time I read the reductionism vs anti-reductionism literature I come away feeling like both sides nail some cases and neither one describes my actual pattern of gut reactions, so I want to try it from the other end: here are my verdicts, you tell me what principle is underneath them, or tell me one of them has to go.

1. Train station in a city where I speak none of the language. I hold up my ticket, a guy in a station uniform says "quatre" and points at the board. I believe him. I have zero evidence about this guy — no track record, he might be a random person who found the jacket. Still: justified.

2. Ten minutes later, same guy, unprompted: station's shutting, come with him, he'll get me a cab. I don't believe him. Note this is the same speaker and the same kind of boring, practical, checkable claim; the difference is that it's the sort of thing that pays to lie about and I'd end up alone with him in a car. But I also think, if I'm honest, that I'd have believed a *different* stranger who said "trains are cancelled, it's on the board." So I don't think "stranger" is what's doing the work here.

3. My neighbor has sent me to four restaurants I thought were awful. She tells me a new Thai place opened on 5th — I believe her instantly. She tells me it's the best Thai food in the city — I believe her not at all; my credence that it's good is basically what it was for a random new restaurant. I take myself to be justified both times. And notice the contrast with case 1: there I gave a total stranger *full* trust on something I couldn't check at all, and here I'm discounting someone with a track record on something I could check in ten minutes. If you'd asked me to state a principle in advance I'd have predicted the opposite pattern.

4. My brother, who has never once lied to me and is completely hopeless at reading a room, tells me everyone at my party liked me. I don't believe him. My mother, who is extremely good at reading rooms and cheerfully admits she lies to me whenever the truth would upset me, tells me the same thing. I half believe her — my credence genuinely goes up. I can't tell you why these two go in opposite directions and I suspect one of them has to be given up.

5. My mechanic, nine years, never once wrong about my car. He says: "honestly I haven't looked at it, I've got no idea, this is a pure guess — but if I had to bet, it's the alternator." My credence that it's the alternator goes up. I go back and forth on this one constantly. One part of me says that's straightforwardly irrational: he told me he has no evidence, so a guess from him is noise. Another part says a nine-year track record is exactly the sort of thing that makes someone's hunches informative, so of course it moves me. A third part says the first two are really fighting about whether testimonial warrant has to be *transmitted* from the speaker's grounds or whether the speaker's say-so is enough on its own.

6. Two hundred reviews of a hotel, each from an account that has reviewed exactly one thing in its life, no photos, kind of generic wording, average 4.6. I trust the aggregate somewhat — enough to book. I don't trust any single one of them at all, and I'd trust one long specific review from someone with a history of eighty reviews more than the whole two hundred, even though that one person might be a shill and the two hundred obviously aren't all shills. Also: if you told me 30% of them were definately fakes, I think I'd still book, and I can't tell wether that's coherent or just laziness.

7. A note in my own handwriting from about ten years ago: "the yellow tin under the sink is the one with the BAD stuff — don't use it." I believe this more firmly than I'd believe my own memory right now, and more firmly than I'd believe a friend telling me the same thing today. Also, I now know that ten-years-ago me used "BAD" loosely — it might just mean "gives you a rash." I still believe it's the bad tin.

8. And the case that got me started on all this. I fine-tuned a language model on my own old journals and emails and asked it what I was doing in March 2017. It produced a fluent, specific narrative; the eight or nine details I can independently check, it gets right; there are two confident details I'm fairly sure are fabrications. I have some mild but real inclination to believe its unchecked claims — more than nothing, noticeably less than I'd believe a human stranger who performed the same way on the checkable bits, and much less than I believe my note in case 7, even though the model has a *better* recent accuracy record than my ten-year-old self did. I don't know what I'm trusting when I trust it.

The principle I keep half-formulating is something like: you're entitled to take someone's word for it, with no evidence needed, provided (a) they present it as true rather than as a guess or as something they're merely passing on from elsewhere, (b) you have no specific reason to doubt their honesty or their competence on the kind of question at hand, where "kind of question" is carved as finely as your evidence about them lets you carve it, and (c) evidence plays no other role — the entitlement isn't built out of any induction about how reliable testimony generally is; the doubt-clause is the only place evidence gets in.

Test it against 1 through 8 and tell me where it breaks. Two things I especially want: whether clause (c) can survive cases 6 and 8, where the thing that seems to be doing the work is a track record rather than a person presenting something as true; and what the account predicts about two cases I have no verdict on — (i) A tells me p, A's only source is B, B's only source is A, and neither has anything else to go on: is that better or worse than a chain of thirty people where each one got it from the previous one? (ii) suppose a language whose grammar forces you to tag every report as "passed on, I don't vouch for it" — would its speakers be epistemically worse off, or would clause (a) simply never get satisfied for them, and is that a problem for the principle or just a fact about them?

If nothing single and clean covers all eight, say which verdict I should drop and why. And please be straight with me about which of my cases is just confused rather than actually pressing. Start with the principle in one sentence — or with "no single principle; drop verdict #__" — then give me the reasoning; no preamble, and don't restate my cases back at me before you've said something.
