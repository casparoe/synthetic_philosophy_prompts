I work in process safety and I am on a small committee revising the hazard-ranking appendix of an industry standard, which has made me read our own rules the way a metaphysician would. Standard practice ranks hazard controls in a rough hierarchy: eliminating or substituting the hazard (designing the vessel to withstand the pressure it could actually see, replacing a flammable solvent with a water-based one) is treated as removing the hazard; engineered safeguards (a relief valve, a sprinkler system, an interlocked guard, gas detection wired to an automatic shutdown) are treated as reducing risk while the hazard itself is retained on the register; and administrative controls and personal protective equipment are treated as weaker still, on the grounds that they leave the hazard entirely intact and merely interpose a procedure or a layer between the hazard and a person. There is a familiar maxim in the field to the effect that the hazard you do not have is the hazard that cannot leak, and correspondingly we say of a safeguarded hazard that it is controlled rather than removed. What puzzles me is how clean that distinction can really be. A relief device opens precisely because of the overpressure that would otherwise rupture the vessel; a residual-current device interrupts a circuit precisely when fault current begins to flow. If a disposition were nothing more than a matter of what would happen were the stimulus present, then in both cases the disposition is absent: the vessel would not rupture, the circuit would not deliver a lethal shock. Yet the practice refuses to say the hazard is gone. It keeps the overpressure scenario on the register, and instead of deleting it, it assigns the safeguard itself a probability of failure on demand, as if the thing being graded were the reliability of an intervening mechanism rather than the presence of the hazard.

I remember enough undergraduate metaphysics to recognize the shape of the problem: there is a literature on finks — interfering conditions that are triggered by the stimulus itself and that destroy the disposition just when it would have manifested — and on masks — standing conditions that leave the disposition in place while preventing its manifestation — and on antidotes, and on what this does to the simple conditional analysis of dispositional predicates. What I cannot tell is which of these the engineering practice is committed to, and whether it is committed to anything at all. Some of our distinctions look like they track whether the intervention removes the causal basis of the hazard or merely blocks its manifestation; others look like they track nothing more than how much we trust the intervention, which would make the hierarchy an epistemic or hedging device wearing metaphysical clothing. The case I keep coming back to is what I have started calling a triggered preventer: unlike a mask, it does nothing until the stimulus arrives; unlike a fink, it does not destroy the disposition even temporarily, it merely preempts the manifestation on that occasion and leaves the hazard exactly as it was — a relief valve that re-seats, a sprinkler head that discharges and is replaced, a software interlock that aborts the sequence. Standard presentations seem to have only two boxes, fink and mask, and I cannot tell whether a triggered preventer belongs in one of them, whether it shows the taxonomy needs a third category, or whether the distinction between standing and triggered interference is superficial and the real work is being done by something else entirely, such as whether the intervening condition is intrinsic to the thing credited with the disposition.

So here is what I want. Reconstruct the implicit theory of disposition that this ranking practice presupposes: what a hazard has to be for the hierarchy to make the distinctions it makes, and which rival theories of disposition the practice quietly rules out. I want to know what the practice strictly presupposes as opposed to what merely fits comfortably with it, since I suspect more than one theory would license the same engineering advice, and I want to know where the practice is underdetermined or downright inconsistent — for instance, we will treat an electrical installation with a compliant residual-current device as though the shock hazard has been dealt with, while refusing to treat a sprinklered building as though the fire hazard has been dealt with, and I cannot locate a principle that explains both. Then tell me what would actually have to change in the practice if we adopted, successively, the simple conditional analysis; a view on which dispositions are to be identified with, or grounded in, the intrinsic causal basis of the disposed thing; a broadly Humean view on which dispositional facts are nothing over and above occurrent and nomic facts; and a propensitist view on which hazards come in degrees. Which of these would force a rewrite of the standard, and which could we adopt tomorrow with the appendix unchanged? And if you think the fink/mask taxonomy needs the third category I gestured at, say what it is and what would count as evidence that it is genuinely distinct rather than a special case.
