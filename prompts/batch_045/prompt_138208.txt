I write about AI for a general audience, and I keep having to decide whether some scenario I'm handed actually counts as an intelligence explosion, a fast takeoff, both, or neither — usually in a sentence, with no room to explain myself. So let me set out my verdicts on concrete cases and ask what principle, if any, they add up to.

Two quantities I'll use. Call R the share of the effective cognitive labor that goes into building better AI systems which is performed by AI systems themselves. Call L the lag between a research result existing and its showing up as capability in the world.

- R goes from near zero to near one in about three months, and does so because the systems are doing the work; capabilities go from roughly human-level to unrecognizably better within a year. Verdict: an intelligence explosion and a fast takeoff.
- The same capability curve, the same GDP curve, the same elapsed time — but R stays at zero, because the gains come from a human crash program of fabs, power plants and data centers, with the systems as passive products. Verdict: fast, but not an intelligence explosion. Even though nothing observable differs between this and the previous case.
- A single algorithmic insight jumps capabilities by what would otherwise have been five years of scaling, and then progress returns to its old rate and stays there. Verdict: a discontinuity, but I resist calling it a takeoff.
- AI systems fully automate AI research, so R is near one, but L is long and roughly fixed: every gain takes years to reach deployment because of chip fabs, clinical trials, licensing and physical buildout. Verdict: an explosion is underway but is being damped — and I'm not sure that's a coherent thing to say.
- R rises steadily but the loop gain is tiny: each generation is only a couple of percent better at AI research than the last, so doubling times are years. Verdict: recursive self-improvement, but not a takeoff — although over a few decades this gets you much further than case three does.
- Capabilities explode inside one narrow domain, formal mathematics say, a decade before anything comparable happens anywhere else in the economy. Verdict: I alternate between saying the takeoff has already started and saying nothing has happened yet.

Is there a single principle here, or a short list of candidates, and is it a principle about mechanism (who or what is doing the improving), about shape (how much time passes between human-level and vastly superhuman), about consequences (irreversibility, whether anyone could still intervene), or about the domain over which capability is being measured? Please state it precisely enough that it returns verdicts on cases I haven't listed — in particular, a world in which R crosses 0.9 quickly but human institutions remain the binding constraint on everything downstream of research, and a world in which the capability of any individual system is continuous and smooth but the number of systems in operation doubles every month — and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.
