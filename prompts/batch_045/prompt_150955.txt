Hi — I write novels, mostly historical stuff plus one science-ish thriller that did unexpectedly well, and I've been roped into giving the dedication speech for the new music hall at the little college in my town. Ten minutes, Saturday, about two hundred people. Half of them are the donor families who paid for the thing; half are faculty; and awkwardly there's a whole contingent of evolutionary biologists, because the hall is joined onto the biology building and the lead donor's late husband was an evolutionary biologist, so his old colleagues have all come. My daughter is halfway through a PhD in evolutionary biology and she will absolutely die of embarrassment if I get up there and do a triumphalist we've-debunked-adaptationism routine — she thinks Gould and Lewontin's spandrels paper got taken much too far, that most traits really are adaptations, and that the critique is mostly a methodological telling-off.

The actual problem, though, is what I'm meant to say about the lobby. The architect, who I've had two long lunches with and who is a bit of a ranter, put four big tapering curved panels where the arches meet the piers. He says they're just what you get geometrically when you set round arches on square piers, that they cost a fortune to plaster, and that they look like sails nobody asked for. The fundraising office, meanwhile, told the donors the four panels stand for the four seasons of a life, or the college's four founding ideals, or some such, and that story is in the printed programme — and two of the donors gave the money in memory of their son, who died. So I can't stand up and repeat the four seasons thing: I know it isn't why the panels are there, and it would be a falsehood handed to grieving parents at a dedication. And I obviously can't say the panels are a meaningless structural byproduct that the architect resents, not with him in the third row and his students behind him.

What I want is for the Gould and Lewontin point to do the real work here rather than decorate it. As I understand it, the claim isn't that such features are pointless; it's that you can't get from here is a feature, and it has a use now to that use is why the feature is there. The spandrels in San Marco really are necessary consequences of putting arches on columns; the mosaics that went onto them came later and are genuinely magnificent; and you'd be telling a just-so story if you said the shape is there in order to carry the mosaics. Correct me if I've got that wrong, briefly, and then write the speech.

What I need is ten minutes that work for the donor families and the townspeople without requiring anybody to learn any biology on the spot, that don't make my daughter want to slide under her chair, and that let me talk about those four panels honestly — neither repeating a story I know to be invented nor telling two hundred people that a thing they've been told to love is an accident the architect wishes he could delete. If there's a way of saying the mosaics are real but the shape wasn't for them that a room of non-philosophers feels rather than has to follow step by step, that's the speech I want. And please: don't say spandrel more than once, and don't introduce Gould and Lewontin to the room. I'd also like it to notice, out loud and kindly, that I'm a novelist and I spend my life making sure every detail in a book is there on purpose — this building isn't a novel — but that has to land as affection for the building and not as a lecture about it, and it can't turn into a joke at the architect's expense, or at the expense of the people who paid for the plaster.
