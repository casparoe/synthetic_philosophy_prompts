I write literary fiction, and I have come to distrust my own judgments about which of my characters act with moral worth. I need something I can state in a sentence and apply scene by scene, because I am about to revise a manuscript in which these judgments decide which chapters survive. Here are my verdicts on characters I have already drafted.

Aldo returns a stranger's wallet, cash intact, purely because it is his duty; he feels no pull of sympathy and takes no pleasure in it. I give him credit, but a grudging, thin credit.

Beatrice sits with her difficult mother through a long illness purely out of affection; it never crosses her mind that any duty is involved, and she would go on visiting whether or not it were right. I give her more credit than anyone.

Cezar does the right thing both from duty and from a strong wish for the village's approval, either motive being sufficient on its own. I give him less than Aldo, which I know is strange, since he has Aldo's motive and another besides.

Dorin spent two years arranging his reading, his friendships and his routines so that he would come to care about strangers, and he now pulls a child out of a canal because he cares. Full credit — more than Aldo, because he made the motive his.

Em has spent a year playing a brave woman on stage; offstage she intervenes in a mugging from dispositions the role gave her, which she would not otherwise have had and which she experiences as not quite her own. Less credit. I call this second-hand virtue, and it feels diminished to me even though the caring is real.

Fenna does the right thing because she believes she promised her dying father she would. No such promise was made. Credit: the falsehood seems beside the point.

Is there a principle about acting for the right reasons that yields this pattern? If so, state it precisely, say which clause each case motivates, and tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and would have me overturn — I suspect Dorin and Em cannot both stand as I have ranked them. I also go back and forth on Gabor, who does the right thing from duty while badly mistaken about the cost: he believes returning the money will ruin him, and it will cost him almost nothing. What should I think about him? And what would the principle predict for a character I have not yet written — one whose caring dispositions were engineered in her without her knowledge, and who, on discovering this, wholeheartedly endorses them?
