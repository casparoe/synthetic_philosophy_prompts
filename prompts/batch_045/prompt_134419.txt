I was reading a popular philosophy blog post about Kavka's toxin puzzle — you probably know the setup: an eccentric billionaire puts a vial in front of you and offers you a million dollars if, at midnight tonight, you intend to drink it tomorrow afternoon; the money is already in your account hours before you'd have to drink, and drinking just makes you miserably sick for a day with no lasting effects. The post runs roughly like this. The only real question is whether you can intend to do something you're certain you won't do, and this is basically Newcomb's problem in a lab coat, a payoff mechanism that reads your mind before you act. Everyone agrees these days that you can't do it — a rational agent knows she won't drink, so she can't intend to drink — and the lesson is that intending isn't up to us the way we naively assume. Then the post offers its own variant to show the puzzle isnt really about reasons: same deal, except the billionaire also promises that if you genuinely form the intention at midnight, at dawn he'll slip you a pill that makes the toxin pleasant and harmless, so you know that when drinking time arrives you'll have a reason to drink. The post says this variant is exactly as hard as the original, which proves the difficulty was never that your reasons for acting run out, only that you can't intend at will. It closes with the moral that you therefore can't sincerely promise to do something you'll have no reason to do later, which makes vows, and most of the weight we put on intentions, a kind of self-deception.

The Newcomb comparison felt like a stretch to me, and the variant struck me as if anything easier than the original, but I cant say why and I dont know the literature. Is the post fair? Where is it right, where is it subtly off, what would someone who thinks you can intend to drink the toxin say in reply, and does that final claim about promises survive once the mistakes are fixed?
