There is an argument I have taught for fifteen years and have never been able to get into focus, and I have decided that the reason may be that I am trying to see it in the wrong idiom.

I am a professor of moral philosophy. Before I took my doctorate I spent six years as a risk analyst on a commodities desk — I priced, hedged, and argued with traders about sizing, and I never fully lost the vocabulary. When an argument in ethics will not resolve for me I still try to restate it in those terms: expected value taken under a model you do not trust, tail index, value-at-risk and expected shortfall, Knightian uncertainty versus risk, ambiguity aversion, model risk, robust optimization and minimax regret, position sizing and the Kelly fraction, ergodicity and the gap between time averages and ensemble averages, discount rates and the numéraire in which a stream of payoffs is denominated. I can use these terms loosely and follow a formal argument stated in them; I have not run a book in twenty years, and I do not want a tutorial in them. I want them used on something.

The argument is the cluelessness objection to act-consequentialism, as I would state it to a graduate seminar. Act-consequentialism bids you perform, of the acts available to you, the one whose total downstream consequences are best. But for almost any act you might perform now: (i) the bulk of the consequences — in expectation, if the expectation is defined at all — falls beyond any horizon over which you have evidence about what your act will do, because causal chains do not stop and the far future is very long; (ii) the sign of the net value of those distal consequences is not recoverable from your evidence, and the distal terms are large enough that they swamp the proximal terms you can estimate; so (iii) the criterion, taken at face value, yields no usable guidance — it does not tell you to do what commonsense morality says, it does not tell you to do the opposite, and it gives you no reason to think that the acts it would recommend, if you could compute them, resemble the acts anyone would recognize as decent.

Against this there are three replies I also teach, and I want all four positions — the argument and the three replies — carried across. First, the second-order reply: even if you cannot compute expected value, you should still do what, on your evidence, you have most reason to expect to be best; maximize subjective expected value by your own lights, however noisy the estimate, and accept that you will often be badly wrong. Second, the indirect reply: stop applying the criterion to acts and apply it to rules, dispositions or institutional mandates; a rule like keep your promises, prevent the famine, do not burn the library may be vindicated by its long-run profile even though no individual application of it can be. Third, what I call proximalism: act on the consequences you can estimate, on the ground that the unknowable distal terms are either roughly symmetric across your options or, if not symmetric, are to be excluded from the decision rule even while they remain part of the truth about what would in fact be best.

Two things of my own that I want put under the same pressure. The first is a distinction I have started making in lectures and cannot tell is genuine: cluelessness of *sign* (you cannot tell whether the act is net good or net bad), cluelessness of *scale* (you can sign it but not size it, and the size is what dominates), and cluelessness of *numéraire* (you know the outcomes well enough but not what they are to be counted in — whose welfare, at what discount, under which rule for aggregating across persons and across generations). My suspicion is that the risk vocabulary can express the first two and has no organ for the third, and that much of the heat in the literature comes from sliding between them. The second is a test I have been toying with for the proximalist rule: instead of picking a truncation horizon, ask whether an option's ranking is stable as the horizon is extended — treat the choice of horizon the way one treats the choice of sample size, and see whether the estimate settles. I want to know whether that test has a principled version in your vocabulary or whether the horizon has to be chosen by hand, in which case the choice of horizon seems to me to be doing all the work and the rule is circular.

Here is what I want. Give me the cluelessness argument and the three replies restated in the risk vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, term by term — a mapping I could put on a seminar handout. Then give me the ledger of what did not come through: which claims translate cleanly; which translate only after you have quietly helped yourself to something the original does not grant — a well-specified loss function, a stationary generating process, a repeatable bet, an option to hold cash and decline the trade, a market price for the payoff in question — in which case I want the borrowed assumption named explicitly; and which claims cannot be stated in that vocabulary at all, and are not merely inconvenient but unsayable. For each item in the ledger, give me your view on which way the loss tells: is it a defect of the risk framework, which was built to price one agent's book over repeated draws and was never meant to say what anyone owes to strangers not yet born, or is it a sign that the philosophers' claim was less clear than it sounds — an anxiety that a working risk desk would have handled with fractional Kelly and gone home? Be spare and technical rather than readable. If the honest answer to one of my questions is that the vocabulary has no word for the thing, say that and stop.
