I write about physics and about the way science is done, and next month I'm giving the closing talk at a two-day, invitation-only workshop on how to assess theories that can't currently be tested — string theory, the multiverse, and the general question of whether a framework can earn support without data. I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of my thesis, and I'd rather be told where I'm weak than be set up to look good.

The thesis I'll defend: non-empirical support for a framework is legitimate only if it passes what I've been calling the provenance test. For each of the three arguments usually offered — that no one has managed to construct a workable rival; that the framework keeps turning out to explain things it wasn't built to explain; that its research tradition has a track record of converting guesses into genuine successes — you should have to name the structural feature of the theory that generates the argument, say what would have been true if things had gone the other way, and state which of the other two arguments would survive if that feature were removed. The spine of the talk is a suspicion rather than a proof: that in the case everyone will be thinking of, all three arguments may trace back to a single feature — the framework's extreme internal rigidity combined with a long-standing community practice of absorbing anomalies rather than discarding the framework — in which case they are one argument counted three times, and the arithmetic of the published case is badly inflated.

The room is about forty people. Six or seven working string and quantum-gravity theorists, most of them broadly sympathetic to the non-empirical case and impatient with outsiders who relitigate falsifiability. Three philosophers of science: one sympathetic to Bayesian treatments of non-empirical evidence, one old-school falsificationist who thinks the whole program is an oxymoron, and one who works on the problem of unconceived alternatives and treats every argument from absence of alternatives as a bad-lot argument in disguise. Three observational cosmologists and a gravitational-wave experimentalist, all of whom have data, think the session is decadent, and are acutely aware of what the money spent on it could have bought. Two historians of science. A program officer from the foundation paying for the workshop. And five or six other science writers plus two editors who cover physics and who will be listening for whether any of this is publishable.

What I want from you:

The objections I will actually get — eight or nine of them — ordered by how much damage they'd do rather than by how likely they are, and phrased the way the relevant person would actually phrase them. Where the string theorist's objection and the falsificationist's objection share a target but not a vocabulary, give me both versions; I need to hear the difference. For each: a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds, and one honest sentence on where that reply is softest. Skip the objections I'd get from a comment section; this room knows the terrain.

Also: what I should concede in the first two minutes, before anyone asks anything, so that the concessions don't get extracted from me later; the single claim that has to survive if the talk is going to have been worth giving; the two objections that are really one objection wearing different clothes; and the one that is sound but that I can't answer without losing the room. Assume twenty minutes for questions and that anything I say may end up quoted.

Finally: I think the question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks is the one about my standing — a non-physicist proposing assessment standards for a field he can't do — but if you think the genuinely dangerous question is a different one, say so, say which, and tell me why.
