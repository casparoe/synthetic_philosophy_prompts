I am trying to work out what I actually think about privacy, and I want a principle rather than a survey of positions. Below are my verdicts on a dozen cases, given as honestly as I can, plus a tentative principle of my own that I suspect is close but not right. I would like you to test the principle against the cases, repair it or replace it, and tell me which of my verdicts a better principle would force me to give up.

Please do not supply a literature review or citations; I will add those myself. You may draw on the familiar families of account — access-based, control-based, the norms-of-information-flow or contextual-integrity family, and the reductionist thought that privacy talk is dispensable in favour of property and bodily rights — but as materials, not as something to summarise.

My cases and verdicts:

1. A stranger on a commuter train reads my messages over my shoulder. She forgets them within a minute and tells no one. Violation: yes, a small one.
2. The transit authority retains carriage CCTV for thirty days. My segment of footage is never viewed by anyone. Violation: yes, though smaller than I expected to feel; and thirty days' retention feels worse to me than twenty-four hours'.
3. My old laptop, with a full-disk-encrypted drive, ends up in the hands of a retired cryptographer who never attempts to open it. Violation: no.
4. The same cryptographer does open it, finds nothing but grocery lists and drafts, tells no one, and dies a month later. Violation: yes.
5. A hospital's model, trained on records including mine, outputs a risk score used to allocate nursing staff. No human ever looks at my score or knows it exists. Violation: yes, and I cannot say why.
6. A supermarket infers from my purchases that I am pregnant and mails me coupons. Again no human sees the inference. Violation: yes, and worse than 5.
7. A tourist photographs me in a plaza to test whether a new lens focuses correctly, checks the histogram, deletes the file, and never looks at my face. Violation: no.
8. After my death my mother reads the diary I kept at fifteen. Violation of my privacy: I want to say no — but I also want to say something bad happened.
9. The camera in a shop fitting room is a convincing prop, and I believe I am being filmed. Violation of privacy: no, I think — yet something bad happened.
10. The camera in the fitting room is real and recording, but the footage is auto-deleted unviewed, and no one ever knows what it captured. Violation: yes, and a serious one.
11. Out of idle curiosity I look up an old classmate's court records, each of them properly public, and assemble them into a picture of how his life went. Violation: yes, mildly, even though every item was public.
12. A data broker holds ten thousand trivial facts about me. Each on its own I would not mind anyone knowing. The profile they compose, I suspect, matters a great deal. My verdict here is unstable: sometimes it feels like nothing, sometimes like a large violation.

The pair I keep returning to is 7 and 10. In neither case does any agent end up with a belief about me; in one I feel nothing, in the other I feel a great deal. I take it the difference must be sought in the setting, or in what the arrangement was for, rather than in anything that happened to the information — but I cannot see how to build that into a principle without smuggling in the conclusion.

Here is my tentative principle, which I offer to be corrected rather than defended: a person loses privacy with respect to a fact only if some agent is in a position to learn that fact, where being in a position is fixed by the norms of the relevant practice rather than by physical possibility, those norms do not authorise that agent to be in such a position, and it does not matter whether the agent ever exercises it.

Three things I want from you.

First, state the principle you think is actually right — or, if you think there is no one principle, the smallest set, with an account of why privacy talk bundles together things that come apart. State it precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without asking you, and give me, clause by clause, the case that motivates the clause.

Second, tell me what notion of being in a position to learn makes 3 come out clean and 2 come out a violation, and whether that notion can be stated without appealing to what is easy, what is likely, or what the institution is for. If it cannot, say so and tell me what the principle should appeal to instead. Say also whether the principle needs a subject-level knower, and therefore what it should say about the model in case 5 and the inference in case 6; my sense that 6 is worse than 5 may show that I am protecting two different goods and calling both privacy.

Third, give me the principle's verdicts on two or three cases you construct yourself, of a kind I have not listed, chosen because they are where the principle's answer is least predictable — ideally cases where it disagrees with what you expect my gut to say, so that I can check it. Then tell me plainly which of my twelve verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, which of those you would overturn, and what I would lose by overturning it. I would rather have a principle that costs me a verdict than a disjunctive one that saves all twelve, but I want to know the price, and I want to know what it says about case 12.
