So my uncle Ray died three weeks ago at eighty-four, and I've been asked to give the short speech at the dedication of the bench they're putting in for him on the causeway of the reservoir outside town. Bit of background, because it actually constrains what I can say. Ray was a civil engineer — he helped design the water system, that reservoir included — but as an eighteen-year-old he read Moore's Principia Ethica and two bits of it stuck to him permanently and he used them as working rules ever after. First, the isolation thing: whether a thing is worth having for its own sake is settled by imagining it alone in an otherwise empty world, and Ray held that Moore's short list came down to friendship and the appreciation of beauty, so any praise that works by listing what a person *produced* is, in his vocabulary, a reciept — his word for it — and not praise at all. He walked out of his own sister's memorial service muttering that the eulogy had been a balance sheet with a pulse. Second, organic unities: some wholes are worth far more than the value of their parts added up, and Ray's own extension of this, which he was tedious about, was that two people paying attention to the same beautiful thing *together* is one of those wholes, so you can't decompose it into a good friendship plus a nice view. He also thought, I think correctly but he was the one who insisted on it, that goods like that can't be got by aiming at them, only as a by-product, which is why he hated being thanked and hated speeches that ended in a resolution: go forth and appreciate things in Ray's memory would have made him physically angry.

Here's the problem. He and his friend Hal walked out to that causeway nearly every Saturday for something like forty years, in all weather, to watch the sun come up over the water — Hal is eighty-seven and says he'll cry the whole way through, so I'm doing it. The room, or the lakeside, will be: his daughter Priya, who's an effective altruist and has emailed me twice asking please to say something about the good he actually did, the treatment works and the flood defences and the couple of hundred thousand people with clean water, and who considers the Moore stuff precious nonsense; his two grandkids, eleven and fourteen; a lot of family who just want to be comforted and will want to hear that he's somewhere lovely now; and two of his old Cambridge philosophy friends who are flying in and will hear every single reference and will wince if I get the organic-unities point backwards or overstate it — Moore himself, as I understand it, still grants that a beautiful landscape nobody ever sees has *some* value, just nowhere near as much as the same landscape appreciated, and I'd like not to say anything cruder than that.

So: write me roughly five hundred words, about four minutes read aloud, that does the job of a eulogy for the people who need one and is recognisably about Ray for the ones who knew what he believed. It has to be praise he could have accepted as true — no consciousness surviving death, no beauty being enjoyed by him from anywhere, no appeal to what his life caused — but it can't be cold or read like a lecture, and it can't quietly tell Priya that the water system was merely a means. I'd also like the engineering to appear somewhere, but as the thing looked at rather than as an achievement, if you can make that work. One place in it where people laugh. And please don't name Moore on stage or use the word intrinsic: if the philosophy is doing its job it should be invisible, shaping what gets said instead of being said.
