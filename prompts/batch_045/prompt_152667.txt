I'm assembling the handout for a graduate workshop session I'm convening on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness — the view that introspection systematically represents our sensory and affective states as possessing intrinsic phenomenal properties that those states do not in fact have, so that phenomenal properties are posits of a misleading self-representation rather than features of the world. The room will be split fairly evenly between people who find the view liberating and people who think it changes the subject, and I don't want the closing item to work as a polemic for either camp. I'd like to close on a poem rather than a summary.

What I'm after is a sestina: six stanzas of six lines, unrhymed, in the standard rotation, followed by a three-line envoy. The six end-words should be: seem, red, pain, nothing, word, know. You may redeploy each of them as a different part of speech and in a different sense from stanza to stanza — that shuttling is the point — but not substitute synonyms for them. Keep the line lengths steady enough that the returning words register as returns rather than as padding.

Here is why that form and those words. The thesis the poem has to carry is not that consciousness does not exist, and not that experience is thinner than we think. It is that the vocabulary survives while the property goes: we go on saying that the wall seems red, that the tooth hurts, that we know how things are with us, and every one of those words keeps doing its ordinary work even if nothing in the world answers to the posit they smuggle in. The rotation should enact exactly that. The same six words come back in new company and new grammatical roles, and the reader hears them differently each time, while nothing whatever has been added to the world. A reader who has never heard of the debate should feel the words being emptied and refilled as the wheel turns; a reader who has should be able to say which turn corresponds to which move in the argument.

Three constraints on content, because the standard failure mode of poems about this view is that they quietly slide into a neighbouring position:

1. The poem must not say that there is no seeming, or that nothing is conscious, or that experience is a delusion in the sense of a hallucination one undergoes. Illusionism grants the datum — it undeniably, visibly seems to subjects as if there were phenomenal properties — and then explains it. What it denies is that the seeming itself consists in there being something it is like. Keep the denial on the correct side of that line.
2. The envoy must not concede that the seeming is itself a phenomenal state. That concession is realism wearing the theory's vocabulary, and it is the slide I want the final three lines specifically to resist. Requirement: the envoy uses all six words, with seem and nothing meeting in the last line.
3. Be fair to the objector, the way I have to be fair to half my audience. The realist's conviction is not inattention or stupidity, and the poem should not depict the theory as overrunning it by force. She has read the argument, she understands it, and it still seems to her exactly as it did before.

One last thing. There is a knot I've started calling the self-consuming diagnosis, and I want the poem to get at it rather than gesture toward it. The illusionist's account of why we judge that there are qualia is itself an output of the very mechanism she is describing, so the theory has to account for its own uptake — and it cannot do that by predicting that whoever accepts it stops seeming to have experiences, because they don't: the seeming is famously insensitive to being explained away. Which means the poem is being written, with the drained words, by a mechanism those words belong to, and it has to know that about itself without treating the knowledge as a reason to stop. If the envoy can land there — the poem still going, still using the words, having said what it said — that is the ending I want.
