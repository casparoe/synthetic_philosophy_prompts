Analyze the 2011 OPERA faster-than-light neutrino episode as a case study in higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia. Treat the following as the facts. In September 2011 the OPERA collaboration reported that muon neutrinos sent from CERN to Gran Sasso arrived roughly sixty nanoseconds earlier than light would have, at a stated precision well beyond the five-sigma convention for a discovery announcement — though that figure measured precision rather than accuracy, and so was silent about systematic faults. The collaboration published while refraining from claiming a discovery and explicitly invited the wider physics community to scrutinize and reproduce the result. The result sat badly with special relativity and with the neutrinos from supernova 1987A, which after a journey of well over a hundred thousand years arrived within hours of the light, where OPERA's effect would have implied an arrival years early. Other detectors at Gran Sasso using the same beam re-measured the time of flight within months and obtained values compatible with light speed. In 2012 the collaboration traced the effect to its own timing chain: a fibre-optic link from a GPS receiver to the master clock that was not properly seated, which shortened the apparent flight time by about seventy nanoseconds, together with a second timing component operating outside specification that pushed in the opposite direction.

Organize the analysis around a distinction I have found useful and that I do not think the higher-order evidence literature draws cleanly. Call higher-order evidence *local* when it targets an identified step in the chain — this clock, this cable, this calibration, this analysis choice — and *global* when it targets the enterprise wholesale: the base rate at which striking anomalies in large experiments fail to survive scrutiny, the theoretical implausibility, the track record of complex apparatus. My sense is that most of the philosophical discussion runs on global higher-order evidence while working scientists act on local higher-order evidence, and that the two have different rational upshots: local higher-order evidence tells you where to look and licenses a specific, directed doubt, whereas global higher-order evidence merely lowers confidence without saying where the error lies.

First direction: reconstruct the epistemic situation of the collaboration in September 2011 in these terms. What exactly was their first-order evidence, what local and what global higher-order evidence did they have, and what does the distinction say they should have believed and done — publish, withhold, announce and disavow, or something else? By the lights of the no-akrasia principle (roughly: believe what you take your total evidence to support), was the posture of publishing a result its own members did not believe rational, irrational, or something the principle has no vocabulary for?

Second direction: what does the episode show about the theory? Three things in particular. Is global higher-order evidence genuinely a reason to disbelieve a result, or only a reason to allocate investigative resources — evidence, or policy? Does the subsequent discovery of the loose connection vindicate the collaboration's attitude, or is that retrospective luck of a kind the epistemology should discount? And is the local/global distinction defensible, given both that the global suspicion was borne out here and that the same global reasoning would have counselled doubt about anomalies that turned out to be real? Finally, be explicit about where the theory falls silent: if the honest verdict is suspension rather than belief or disbelief, say so, and say what a theory would have to add in order to return a verdict at all.
