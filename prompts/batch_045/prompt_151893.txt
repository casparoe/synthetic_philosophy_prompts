I teach an upper-level seminar in political philosophy and I am writing the final exam. I want one essay question on Burkean conservatism and Chesterton's fence that cannot be answered by reciting Burke's suspicion of individual reason or the standard statement of Chesterton's fence. The exam is a 90-minute in-class essay; the question should be answerable at that length. The crux I want tested is this: suppose a long-standing practice originally had a clear justifying reason R, but R has since lapsed, and the practice now produces a valuable unintended consequence C that nobody designed it for. A reformer understands both R and C and proposes to abolish the practice and replace it with a new institution explicitly designed to secure C. Does Chesterton's fence, understood as the demand that one understand the reason for a practice before removing it, forbid this replacement? Would Burke's appeal to prejudice and inherited institutions as repositories of collective, partly inarticulate rationality give a different answer, because the institution's rationality may be a sedimented adaptation that includes C even though C was unintended? The question must separate students who merely equate the two views from those who can articulate their different epistemic bases: Chesterton's fence looks for a reason an individual reformer can grasp, while Burkean conservatism treats the stock of inherited practice as embodying distributed, tacit rationality that outruns any single person's understanding. Please write the essay question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, the marks each is worth (total 20), and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each wrong.
