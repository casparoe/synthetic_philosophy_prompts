I've fallen into an argument with myself about moral luck and I'd like help getting out of it. It started in a reading group that did free will last term and has now drifted onto the topic my partner says I always get stuck on — whether it can be true both that a person is only responsible for what is up to them and that the drunk driver who hits a child has done something worse than the one who gets home safely. I have a set of verdicts that I keep re-deriving from scratch each time, and I'd like to know whether any single principle yields all of them, and if not, which verdicts I should abandon. To keep things manageable I'll label the cases. (i) Two drivers leave the same party at the same blood alcohol level, drive the same speed, on comparable streets, with no difference in skill or attention. One arrives home; one kills a child who runs out between parked cars. I hold the second driver to have done something worse, to deserve more punishment, and to owe more in the way of guilt and apology. (ii) Same as (i), except the person killed turns out to have had days to live. I think this genuinely makes it less bad, and I'm uneasy with myself for thinking it. (iii) Two shooters fire at a target on different nights with the same intent, the same competence, the same ex ante chance of hitting; one bullet passes through the target's sleeve, the other misses by twenty feet because of a gust. I feel the near miss is the worse act, even though I've stipulated the risk was identical. (iv) Anna and Beth have the same character, the same marriage, the same dispositions; Anna is never offered a temptation to be unfaithful and Beth is offered one and takes it. I decline to conclude that Beth is the worse person, and I want to say the comparison itself is unfair. (v) Colm is short-tempered because of his genes and his upbringing, and he is cutting to his colleagues; Dana is naturally patient and is never cutting. I blame Colm without any discount at all, even though I'll concede his temperament is as much a matter of luck as the appearance of the child in (i). (vi) A case I don't think is on the standard fourfold list at all: Erin and Frank both text while driving every night for a year; Erin's route is through a dense neighborhood full of children, Frank's is on an empty rural road. Neither has hurt anyone. I think Erin is doing something worse and should feel worse, though the difference here is in expected rather than actual harm. (vii) A jurisdiction socializes the material consequences: all drivers pay into a no-fault fund, victims are compensated regardless, and the unlucky driver pays no more than the lucky one. My extra punishment verdict from (i) dissolves here, but my extra-guilt verdict doesn't — and I can't tell whether that means the punishment verdict was never really about desert. (viii) I think the unlucky driver in (i) should feel worse about himself, but that the rest of us should not think worse of him; said out loud, that sounds like sheer inconsistency.

The only principle I've been able to sketch is that desert tracks the quality of the will plus the expected harm one chooses to impose, while what looks like outcome-sensitivity is really a separate, non-desert-based entitlement of victims and communities to repair and acknowledgment. That handles (i), (ii) and (vi) neatly, which is why I like it — but it gives me nothing to say about (iii), where I've stipulated the risk equal, and it seems to predict verdicts in (iv) and (v) that I resist: if desert is about what one chooses to risk, then Beth has chosen to risk something Anna hasn't, and I still don't want to call her worse. So: is there a better principle, or a small set of candidates, that gets closer to the full pattern, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing which case? Where it can't accommodate a verdict, please say plainly which verdict you'd overturn and why the cost is worth paying. And I'd like to know what the principle says about cases I haven't put on the list — two lifeguards equally ready to risk themselves, only one of whom is ever in a position to make a rescue, and ten people who each carelessly drop a lit cigarette in a dry wood, one of whose cigarettes happens to start a fire.
