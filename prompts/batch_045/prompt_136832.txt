I teach a second-year PPE course, and in two weeks I have a fifty-five-minute tutorial on Sen's liberal paradox: the 1970 result that no social decision function satisfying an unrestricted domain condition, the Pareto principle, and a minimal liberalism condition — at least two people each get to settle at least one pair of alternatives that is intuitively their own business — can generate an acyclic social ranking. The group has met Condorcet's paradox and Arrow's theorem in the two previous weeks, so they have the machinery of preference aggregation and the shape of an impossibility argument, but they have never seen rights modelled inside social choice, about half of them will hear the word liberalism and think of a political position rather than a decisiveness condition, and I have whiteboard plus a one-page handout and no time for anything else. I have run this session twice and both times it went the same way: I put Sen's original case on the board — three alternatives (the prude reads the book, the lewd reads it, nobody reads it), the prude ranking nobody reads above prude reads above lewd reads on the grounds that he would sooner read it in disgust than let the lewd enjoy it, the lewd ranking prude reads above lewd reads above nobody reads — I derive the three-way cycle, and the room decides that the preferences have been rigged, that real people are not like this, and that therefore nothing follows. I do not want a third performance of that.

What I want from you is a plan for the explanation rather than the explanation itself. First, the dependency structure: what has to be established before the cycle can be derived, in what order, and what breaks if I reorder it — I am especially unsure how much of the Arrow material I need to rebuild at the start (the framing of a social decision function as producing a ranking rather than a choice, the difference between transitivity and mere acyclicity, and why Sen's condition is not a repackaging of non-dictatorship) and whether the payoff of the result lands better if the conditions are stated before the example or extracted from it. Second, the carrying example: should I keep Sen's prude-and-lewd case or build a new one, and if I build a new one, what precisely must it have and what must it lack? My sense is that it needs each person's protected pair to be something the room will concede is genuinely that person's business, and it needs the Pareto comparison to cut across the two personal spheres rather than sit inside one of them, since otherwise the obvious fix of partitioning the alternatives and letting each person decide locally looks like it works. It also seems to need at least one person whose preference reaches into what happens in the other person's sphere, and here I am stuck: a student last year asked whether the impossibility survives if neither person is meddlesome in that way, I did not know the answer, and I said something evasive. Tell me what the honest answer is and where in the sequence to give it, because I would rather raise the objection myself than be ambushed by it.

Third, the misconceptions, in the order this audience will actually form them, and the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off: that decisiveness over a pair in a social ordering is the same thing as having a right, or as getting to choose; that this is just Arrow's theorem with better branding, or just Condorcet again; that the moral is that we should abandon the Pareto principle; that the cure is to stop ranking outcomes at all and simply let each person settle their own sphere (I want them to see both what is right and what is wrong in that response, and I do not know how to stage it); and that the result shows something about markets, or about whether there can be a minimal state. Fourth, a check I can run in the last five minutes that would distinguish a student who has grasped the structure from one who has merely followed the board — my instinct is to hand them a modified profile and ask them either to produce a ranking satisfying all three conditions or to show why none exists, so tell me which modification does that work best. Finally, tell me what to refuse to explain at this level and to say out loud that I am refusing: I am thinking of the choice-functional versions of the result, strategic behaviour, and the treatment of rights as protected choices within a game form rather than as decisiveness in a social ordering — although I would like one five-minute segment in which the students see the difference between there being no coherent social ranking of these outcomes and there being no acceptable procedure for deciding them, since if that distinction is the real lesson of the session I would rather they left with it than with the cycle.
