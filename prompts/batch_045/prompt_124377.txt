I've just come out of a long argument with a friend about the special composition question, and we have agreed to settle it with a bet that I need you to adjudicate.

His position: every moderate answer either collapses into universalism or turns out to be interest-relative. His favourite move is that given any collection of things you can always find some structure worth naming among them, so a criterion that admits composites "when the parts hang together in the right way" ends up admitting everything; and if you tighten it enough to keep the junk out, what is left doing the work is us — our practices, our names, our explanatory interests. Either way there is no substantive fact about composition, only the two trivial answers.

I think that is wrong, and I want a case that shows it. The two criteria to be tested:

M (maintenance): some things compose something if and only if (i) causal processes internal to the collection preserve the arrangement of the parts within a range — the parts repair, replace, or shore each other up, so the collection recovers from perturbations — and (ii) the boundary of the collection is not ours to draw: causal traffic among the parts is recurrent and denser than traffic between the parts and their surroundings. Read "maintenance" broadly: self-repair, redundancy, regrowth and replacement all count, whether or not anyone intends them.

L (life): the parts compose something only if either there is just one of them, or their joint activity constitutes a single life.

Build me a case — real, not hypothetical; no science fiction, no invented devices; a kind of thing that actually exists, described concretely enough to have a definite beginning, a definite end, and a long stretch in the middle when nothing much is happening — meeting all of the following at once:

1. M says yes: there is exactly one composite there, and M says so for reasons that do not depend on anyone's intentions, practices, or interests.
2. L says no: it is not alive, not an organism, and not plausibly a colony-organism, so L must deny that there is any such thing there at all.
3. Untutored judgement sides with M: someone told the story and asked how many things are there would answer "one", knowing full well that it is not alive.
4. It is not a mere process or event: it persists through gradual replacement of every one of its parts and survives a long dormant period, so the reply "that is something happening, not something existing" is unavailable.
5. It survives the universalist's shrug: even granting that on the universalist's account countless other objects occupy the same place at the same time, something in the case makes that answer costly — the composite does explanatory or practical work the surplus objects cannot do.

Then give me, in this order:

- **The case** (one paragraph, 150–180 words).
- **What each view says** (~200 words): universalism, nihilism, M and L, with a verdict and one sentence of reasoning each; and confirm whether M and L agree on a cat and on a wooden chair, as they should.
- **Constraint check** (~120 words): take constraints 1 to 5 in order and show how the case meets each.
- **What is doing the work** (~120 words): the single feature the divergence turns on; what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed; and whether the case would still work if the thing stayed dormant forever.
- **My friend's best reply** (~100 words): the strongest thing he could say against the case, and where it actually bites.

If the constraints cannot all be satisfied together, say so plainly and tell me which one has to give and why — that loses me the bet, but I would rather know. Do not cite anything; we both know the literature and neither of us will be moved by a name.
