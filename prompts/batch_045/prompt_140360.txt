Two friends of mine got into a massive argument in my kitchen last night after dinner — like two hours, we never got to dessert — about a betting puzzle, and they asked me to ref it and I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning, so I'm bringing it here.

The setup: every morning for 1000 days you're offered the same bet. A coin that lands heads 60% of the time, even money, stake whatever you want. Two catches: there's a table minimum of $10, and if you ever can't meet it you're out for good — you keep whatever's left and never bet again. You start with $1000, no money from outside. Jules is the Kelly / geometric rationality guy; Meera thinks the growth-rate framing falls apart the moment you add a floor. Roughly how it went:

(1) Jules: bet 20% of your bankroll every morning. That's the Kelly fraction for an even-money bet with a 60% edge, it's the unique rule that maximizes the almost-sure long-run growth rate, anything less grows slower and anything past about 39% has negative growth, and the fact that expected-value maximization says bet the whole thousand is a reductio of expected-value maximization, not a problem for Kelly.

(2) Meera: your rule is myopic and it cannot see the floor. Full Kelly never hits zero, sure, but it has brutal drawdowns, and the floor turns a drawdown into permanent exclusion. And you can actually price that: at f = 0.2 the reciprocal of your wealth is a martingale, so the chance you ever touch $10 starting from $1000 is almost exactly 1%. One time in a hundred you don't lose 99% of your money, you lose the other 900 days of compounding at 2% a day. The loss isn't a factor of 100, it's the whole rest of the game.

(3) Jules: fine, 1%, but look at what fixing it costs. Half Kelly — 10% a day — brings the ever-hitting probability down to about one in a million (I solved for the exponent, it's a bit over 3), and it costs you half a percent a day of growth, which over 1000 days is a factor of roughly 150 in median terminal wealth. And even the log criterion itself says don't do it: weighting the $10 branch at 1% costs you about a quarter of a nat of expected log, whereas half Kelly gives up about five nats. So it isn't that Kelly ignores the floor — it's that the floor is cheap. If you want more caution than that you've stopped maximizing growth, and you're double counting anyway: log's curvature *is* the risk aversion that compounding generates.

(4) Meera: that's the circularity I'm pointing at. Log is derived from the assumption that you get to keep compounding; the floor removes the compounding, and then log dutifully reports that losing the compounding is cheap, which is just the assumption talking. And a criterion that can't tell the difference between "my wealth fell by 99%" and "the game ended" isn't reporting a fact, it's measuring the wrong thing. Second point: whenever anyone bets less than full Kelly you say it's just full Kelly on a correctly redefined bankroll — which makes your theory unfalsifiable, since literally any fraction is Kelly on some effective bankroll and no amount of observed caution could ever count against you.

(5) Jules: fractional Kelly isn't a dodge, it's what falls out when part of your wealth is earmarked elsewhere, and if you take that seriously it also says a 25-year-old with big future earnings should be betting enormous amounts on margin — which nobody believes, but that just shows the bankroll question is the real question, not the criterion. And yes, every recommendation needs something from outside; expected utility needs a whole utility function. If you want floor-aversion, state it as a constraint: maximize growth subject to P(lockout) ≤ some epsilon. The answer is a fractional Kelly, and the fraction shrinks as the horizon gets longer. The framework handles the floor fine once you tell it what you want.

(6) Meera: here's where it really breaks, and it's not about risk tolerance at all. Look near the floor. At $30 you cannot bet 20%, because 20% is $6 and the minimum is $10 — so you're forced to bet a third of your wealth, which is over-Kelly. Below somewhere in the mid-twenties, where $10 is around 39% of your wealth, *every legal stake* has negative expected log growth. So the minimum stake creates a dead zone just above the floor where the game is unplayable in growth terms, and myopic growth maximization says sit on your hands there and stop betting forever.

(7) Jules: agreed, and that's the theory working: it tells you exactly where to quit, around $26. Policy is 20% while you can, $10 in the band, nothing below. The floor's whole cost is still the 1%.

(8) Meera: but "sit still" is wrong if there are 900 days left. At $15, staking $10 either locks you out at $5 or puts you back at $25 where you can resume compounding for 900 days. The up branch isn't worth log(25/15), it's worth that plus re-entry into the live region, which is enormous with 900 rounds to go. So the right move depends on how many days remain, and it can tell you to take a bet whose instantaneous growth rate is negative. Which means "maximize the growth rate" was never the criterion — the criterion is expected log over the actual horizon, it's horizon-dependent and non-myopic, and Kelly's famous myopia is just an artifact of the unconstrained case where the value function happens to be log-linear.

(9) Jules: then we're arguing inside the same church, because expected log is my criterion too. I'll grant the floor makes the optimum state- and horizon-dependent. But "bet 20% when you're far from the floor" survives untouched, and every real-world caution you like is a fractional Kelly, i.e. a choice of effective bankroll, i.e. a risk preference you were always going to have to supply.

(10) Meera: it survives as a limiting case, which is all I ever claimed. Every actual market has a minimum ticket, a margin call, a fixed cost, something that stops you scaling down — and once it's there the famous result is a special case and the live question is a value-function question, not a growth-rate question.
