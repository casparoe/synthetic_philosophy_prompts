I co-host a tiny, very informal podcast — me and a friend, both software engineers, zero philosophy background — and the format is roughly "read one paper, then talk to someone who actually understands it." Next week's guest is a philosopher who works on the wrong kind of reasons problem. Here's my probably-garbled read of her view, from the one papre of hers I could get through plus a couple of email exchanges, and it's definately the kind of thing she'll correct me on in the first five minutes: she thinks a reason for an attitude is never just a reason, it's always a reason relative to whatever standard of correctness that attitude has. So the famous demon case — someone offers you a pile of money to admire them, and there's clearly a reason to admire them in some sense, but not the kind of reason that would make them admirable — isn't a puzzle you solve by drawing a line between two kinds of reason. It's a symptom: the fitting-attitude / buck-passing style analysis of value was trying to define value in terms of a single univocal relation, something like reason-to-favour, that doesn't exist. Her positive move is to rebuild the account on fittingness instead, with reasons fragmenting by attitude type.

She's fast, she's very good, and she has one habit I want to plan around: whenever a case gets hard, she re-describes it so that the problematic reason turns out to be a reason to get yourself into the attitude, or a reason for some action, or a reason to bring it about that you have it, and then concludes that it was never a reason for the attitude at all.

Draft me eight to ten questions that press on the view rather than having her restate it. Here's what I think is load-bearing, but argue me out of it if I'm wrong:

- One on what actually gets lost if reason-for fragments by attitude type. I expect her to say nothing is lost, that we never needed the unified thing. The follow-up I want is the one that makes her deal with cross-attitude comparisons — you have more reason to believe p than to want q, that sort of thing — and with the thought that every reason is a reason for something. If that goes, what's left of the unity of normativity, and is she happy paying that price?
- One on whether the fragmentation has a principled stopping point, or whether it just spawns a new kind for every attitude with a slightly different correctness standard. I want her to say what makes two standards the same standard.
- One built out of my actual day job. I work on ML tooling, and there's a structural wrinkle I keep hitting: for some attitudes, having the attitude causally changes the object. Trusting a model and giving it feedback on the strength of that trust literally makes it more trustworthy. Being loyal to a friend partly constitutes the friendship you're loyal to. So a reason that looks purely state-given — you'll get better outcomes if you trust — feeds back into the object and starts looking object-given. Ask her whether her account has anything to say here, and specifically whether the right/wrong kind distinction was ever really about causal direction at all, or whether the looping cases are a corner she can cordon off. Follow-up: what if the feedback loop is the normal case rather than the exception?
- One on whether the same problem has an analogue for action rather than attitude. If being paid to do something is a reason to do it but not the right kind, then any buck-passing story about rightness inherits its own wrong-kind problem, and I want to know whether her view predicts that or has to treat action as special.
- One on the messy middle: considerations that are partly one kind and partly the other, and attitudes whose object is thin or basically missing — generalized anxiety, hope that isn't hope that anything in particular, finding something meaningful. Does the machinery still run, or does it only work for attitudes with a clean propositional object?
- At least two questions constructed so that her favourite re-describing move is closed off in advance, and one that hands her the move, lets her use it, and then turns her own commitment against her.
- One where the answer might actually change what I build: if you were specifying a system that forms attitudes in response to considerations, and you wanted it to track only the right kind, what would you even be trying to specify, on her view? Is that a well-posed design problem in her terms, or does she think the whole framing is confused?
- A closer on what would count against her: what would she have to become convinced of — say, that pragmatic considerations really are reasons for belief in the full-blooded sense, not just reasons to get yourself to believe — to give the view up?

For each question give me the question itself, a line or two on why it's there and what the strongest version of her answer probably looks like, and the follow-up I should have queued up. Assume she'll answer the steelmanned version, and that it's fair game to push on any distinction she hasn't actually cashed out.
