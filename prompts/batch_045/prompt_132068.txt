I run a fortnightly formal-epistemology reading group — advanced undergraduates and first-year graduates who are comfortable with Bayes' theorem and with odds talk but who have never met probability kinematics. In two weeks we are doing uncertain evidence, and I want the centrepiece of the session to be a decision tree rather than a lecture: a text flowchart, usable as a handout, that starts from a situation anyone in the room can recognise and ends, at each leaf, with a named updating rule.

Take the root to be something like this: on Monday your credence that the specimen belongs to species A is 0.3; by Tuesday it is 0.7; nothing you have become certain of explains the change; and you still have to say what your credence in some further hypothesis H now is.

Constraints on the tree:

- Every branch node is a single question a student could answer before knowing what any of the positions are called. No node may use the words "rigid", "conditionalization", "kinematics", "cross-entropy", or any theorist's name.
- Roughly eight branch nodes, six to nine leaves.

The distinctions I want the tree built out of, not merely decorated with:

1. Whether anything happened to the agent at all — a perception, a message, a twinge — or whether she merely thought again, redid the arithmetic, and came out somewhere different.
2. Whether there is some proposition (about her own experience, or about what the message said) that she could have become certain of, in a larger algebra, so that the entire change is ordinary conditioning after all.
3. Whether the change is confined to her odds on A against not-A, leaving untouched everything she believes conditional on that partition — and, as a separate node, whether that has to hold for every hypothesis she has, or only for the ones the partition screens off from the rest of what she knows.
4. Whether what arrived was a new number for one cell or only a constraint on the whole distribution: a garbled radio message to a lost hiker that fixes no cell of the map but pins down something weaker, a weighted average over regions.
5. Whether the new state is to be picked by minimising some distance from the old one, by explicitly modelling the experience and conditioning on it, or by giving up single numbers at this step.
6. Whether she expects more shifts of the same kind, and demands that two successive ones land where one big one would have.
7. Whether the shift is one she could have endorsed in advance: she knows her own credences drift with the time of day, and a bookie who knows her schedule can exploit any plan she adopts.
8. Whether what arrived tells her only about A, or also about things entangled with A — a two-second glimpse through thickening fog tells her about the bird, but also about the fog, and the fog is correlated with the hour, and the hour with which species is active.

Build the tree from the logical space: lay out the questions and the answer-combinations they permit, then place the actual positions in that space — Jeffrey's probability kinematics, ordinary conditioning as its limiting case, distance-minimising updating, the "enlarge the algebra and condition on the total experience" escape, holistic re-evaluation with no local rule, revision by rankings or other non-probabilistic surrogates, and updating into a set of distributions rather than one — marking a leaf "unnamed" if nobody holds it. Each leaf should carry a name and one sentence on the case it handles worst. For every unoccupied combination, one line on whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate rather than pretending the final node distinguishes them.

Commit: put each question where you think it belongs and say in a sentence why it comes before or after its neighbours. If one of my eight is the wrong question, replace it, tell me which you replaced, and justify the substitution. If a distinction is missing, add it and cut one of mine to make room. Give me exactly one node whose placement you are genuinely unsure about. I want the structure argued for rather than surveyed, and I do not need citations.
