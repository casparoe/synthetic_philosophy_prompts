So: the flying man. Guy created adult, suspended in air, limbs not touching, no sight, no sound, no sensation from his own body — and the claim is that he'd still affirm that he exists, and that this shows whatever he's picking out with that affirmation isn't his body, since he isn't aware of any bodily part at all. I'm asking specifically about the step from "I'm aware of myself and not of any body part" to "the thing I'm aware of isn't a body."

My credence that this argument, granting that the flying man would indeed affirm his own existence, gives a non-question-begging reason to think the self isn't identical to a body: 0.55. Sub-questions I think belong in it, roughly:

- How much conclusion Avicenna actually wants. Is the argument meant to establish the immateriality and incorruptibility of the soul, or only that self-awareness is primitive and indubitable — closer to a cogito than to an argument for dualism? It's interpretive, but it changes what the conclusion has to be.
- The transparency premise. The inference seems to need something like: if x = y, then being aware of x requires being aware of y, or at least awareness of x transmits across the identity. Does that hold in general? I can be aware of a thing without being aware of its identity with some other thing, so does the premise survive when it's applied to awareness of myself?
- Whether the argument is really just packaging a dualist intuition — i.e. whether all the real work is done by an unstated claim that no bodily state could be a state of bare reflexive self-awareness.
- The variants, which I've been playing with. (a) The padded flying man: keep full interoception and proprioception, strip only exteroception. He still affirms, presumably — but now he is aware of something bodily, so does the inference quietly collapse? (b) The bare flying man: strip everything, including memory and every acquired concept, so there's a subject with no concepts at all. Can it affirm anything, or is the affirmation only available to something that already has the concept of existence, in which case the setup is doing work it shouldn't? (c) My favourite, the inverse case: a being with rich sensory experience and no self-awareness whatsoever. Some contemplative traditions report states like that, and so does some psychopathology — if such reports are to be trusted, self-awareness isn't automatic from having experience, so where would the flying man's self-awareness come from, and does that help his argument or hurt it?
- How much you charge the argument for the gap between "not aware of any body part" and "not a body" — maybe awareness simply doesn't catalogue the constitution of what it's aware of.
