Hi — I'm a novelist, and I'm one of five people on the committee for a regional fiction prize (small cheque, ugly medal, but the shortlist gets genuine press across the state). We vote Friday and I'm the one who has to circulate the memo everyone else will argue with, so I need it in decent shape by Thursday night.

Here's the case. One of our six shortlisted books is a first-person narrative about a woman whose business partnership collapses. The narrator has the author's first name and surname, her hometown, her dead brother, the name of the actual cafe they ran. Her publisher's hardback said "A Novel" on the copyright page. The edition entered in our prize — a paperback reissue — drops those words, and the cover carries a blurb calling it "an unflinching true story." In interviews the author has said on one podcast that everything in the book happened and on another that she invented all of it.

The problem chapter is written as a sworn statement: the narrator itemises about ninety thousand dollars missing from the company account and says her former partner forged her signature on a lease. The partner is named in full, is alive, still lives in the same county, drives a school bus. There's a public court record showing nobody was ever charged and the collapse was a mundane lease dispute. His lawyer has written to us (no suit, just a letter) saying the book is being read locally as an accusation and asking us to withdraw it.

Inside the book the framing is unstable too: it opens with an "editor's note" presenting the text as a manuscript found in a storage unit, and then about two-thirds in, the "editor" turns out to be a character in the story, so the outer frame eats itself.

I keep circling a distinction I half-remember from Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach (1979): he seperates a "frame message," an "outer message" and an "inner message." As I understood it, the frame message is the outermost one — the signal that there's a message here at all and what kind of object it is, so for a novel, roughly, "nothing in here is being asserted about the actual world." The outer message is what the sentences literally say. The inner message is what survives when you strip away the medium: the residue a reader reconstructs and carries out, the part that's the same whether you meet it as a book, a film, or a paragraph in a local paper. Fix that if I've got it wrong.

Two of my committee are literature professors with no patience for anything that smells like moralism about art; a third thinks the publisher's label on the edition entered should simply settle it. I don't want "it depends." I want a bottom line I can put to a vote, and I want the memo to be usable by people who disagree with me. No citations, no reading lists — we have a lawyer for the legal side, I need the reasoning. Roughly 1,400–1,600 words, in these sections, in this order:

1. "Bottom line" — about 100 words. Name one course of action: keep it on the shortlist, withdraw it, keep it with a published note, or something else concrete.
2. "The facts that matter" — about 250 words. Which of the details above actually carry weight, and why.
3. "Frame, outer, inner" — about 450 words. Get the three-level distinction right, and say where the assertoric force of a fiction lives — in the sentence, in the frame, in the reception, or somewhere else.
4. "Applied to this book" — about 350 words. Include whether our own decision is itself part of the frame: if we award a fiction prize to a book whose cover says "true story," are we supplying the frame message the publisher removed?
5. "The strongest case the other way" — about 250 words. The best version of the argument you're rejecting, not a version you can knock over.
6. "What we publish" — about 150 words. Either draft the sentence or two we release, or tell us to say nothing at all.
