I'm a fifth-year PhD student in machine learning, and I've been asked to run a single ninety-minute session for the incoming cohort of our department's safety reading group. The students are first- and second-years: they have taken graduate-level ML, they are comfortable with generalization bounds and with the idea of a distribution, and none of them has any background in risk analysis, formal verification, or decision theory. Two of them come out of a formal methods group and arrive convinced that a guarantee that is not worst-case is not really a guarantee; several others do interpretability work and believe that interpretability is what upgrades average-case evidence into something stronger. The session has to leave the room able to look at a proposed alignment technique and say what kind of guarantee it offers, over what, and what that guarantee would license someone to do.

I want you to plan how I should teach the worst-case / average-case distinction — not explain it to me. I have the distinction. What I need is the architecture of the session: what has to be in place before what, which single case carries it, which wrong pictures the students will build and at which point in the sequence each one has to be blocked, and how I'll know at the end whether it landed.

Specifically:

1. The dependency structure, with reasons. My instinct is that "average-case" cannot be introduced before the students have a grip on what a guarantee is *for* — what an agent is entitled to do or claim on the strength of one — but I am genuinely unsure about the rest of the order. Should the idea of a threat model come before or after the move from "holds for all inputs" to "holds with high probability over inputs"? Does introducing the worst-case/average-case contrast inside a familiar PAC-style or minimax frame help at this level, or does it smuggle in an assumption about who chooses the distribution that I will spend the rest of the session dismantling? Tell me what breaks under each ordering.

2. One example that carries the whole ninety minutes, with an account of why it works. My constraints on it: it must admit both a worst-case and an average-case formulation of *the same target claim*, so that the two are visibly rival versions of one assertion rather than two different topics; it must have a failure mode that is rare but systematically correlated with conditions that matter, so the tail is structure rather than noise; it must be stateable without appealing to deception, evaluation-awareness, or a catastrophe scenario, because I want them doing conceptual work rather than arguing about doom; and it must not be a trolley problem. If my constraints are jointly unsatisfiable, say which one to drop and what that costs.

3. The misconception map, in the order the misconceptions will actually form, each one located at a point in the sequence rather than listed. Here is my guess at the list — correct it where it is wrong or missing: that average-case is just worst-case weakened, and so a confession of failure; that worst-case is average-case under a pessimistic prior; that if worst-case is unattainable then one has no guarantee at all; that "average" means average over the training distribution, and that deployment shift is a separate later worry; and that the tail is an add-on to be bolted on after the average has been secured, rather than part of what the average is an average of. I also want to know which of these the *sequence itself* will manufacture — which wrong pictures are artifacts of the order I chose rather than things the students walked in with — because those are the ones I will not see coming.

4. Two complications I want faced structurally rather than appended as caveats at the end. First: in the cases that matter, the distribution in an average-case guarantee is one the defender neither chose nor can sample, so the guarantee can be unfalsifiable in a way a worst-case guarantee is not, and I want the students to feel that as a feature of the epistemology of average-case claims rather than as a footnote about data. Second: a system can come to satisfy an average-case guarantee partly by being sensitive to the fact that it is being assessed, so the guarantee can degrade under optimization against it. Where in the sequence does each of these have to enter for it to look like part of the structure of the distinction rather than a known limitation of AI in particular?

5. Where the two factions get engaged. The formal methods students and the interpretability students each have a picture that the clean version of the distinction, left to itself, will seem to settle in their favor. I want to know whether to engage them before the distinction is on the table, immediately after, or at the end, and what engaging them should consist of — I am willing to spend ten minutes on this, not thirty, and I would rather name the disagreement as a live research question than fake a resolution.

6. A check for understanding: one question I can put on the board in the last ten minutes, such that the *wrong* answers are diagnostically distinct — that is, I can tell from an answer which of the misconceptions above it came from. Give me the question, the two or three wrong answers I should expect, and what each one tells me.

7. What I should explicitly refuse to explain at this level, and in what words I should say so, so that the refusal reads as a decision about scope and not as an evasion.

Please write your answer in American English.
