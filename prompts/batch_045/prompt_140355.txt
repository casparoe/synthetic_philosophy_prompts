Two members of a product-ethics review board I sit on deadlocked over the following case, and I genuinely couldn't referee it.

The case: a team must pick between two rollouts. Option A adds a 0.4-second delay to a messaging app used by 400 million people, triggered roughly 30 times a day for a year — about 4.4 trillion delays. No user will ever notice a single one; each is, per instance, about as bad as a dust speck in the eye. Option B avoids the delay but requires one named contractor to review unfiltered graphic content for a year, which will leave her with lasting and severe psychological damage. Both disputants stipulate that the comparison is otherwise clean: same benefits, same costs to the company, no downstream effects, no one else affected.

Nadia: Each delay is a real if tiny harm, harms to different people are additive, and the arithmetic isn't in dispute — 4.4 trillion dust-speck-sized harms vastly outweigh one severe injury. So Option B is the wrong choice and A is right.

Rafael: That treats the difference as one of size when it is one of grade. A dust speck falls below the threshold at which something becomes a significant setback to a life, and no quantity of sub-threshold items composes a single above-threshold one. Comparisons within a grade are quantitative; across grades they are lexical, and the severe harm always wins.

Nadia: Then locate the grade boundary. Build a ladder — a speck, a marginally worse speck, and so on up to a year of torture — with each rung only imperceptibly worse than the one below. At every rung, either enough instances of that rung outweigh the next rung up, or they don't. If they always do, transitivity of "worse than" carries you from 4.4 trillion specks clear past the torture. If it stops somewhere, the line has been drawn at a point where, by construction, nothing perceptibly changes — which is arbitrary.

Rafael: Vague is not the same as arbitrary, and heap-like concepts are vague everywhere without thereby being unusable. What marks the boundary isn't a threshold in felt intensity but in how a bad stretch of experience is integrated into one life: severe suffering is bad partly in virtue of being a single temporally unified, inescapable object of attention, and that property can't be assembled from parts that lack it. Sub-perceptual delays have no phenomenal unity even in principle, so there is nothing for aggregation to build with.

Nadia: That overreaches. Sub-perceptual isn't causally inert: individually unnoticed irritations can cumulatively raise stress load in ways that show up in measurable outcomes, and something can set back a person's interests without ever being felt. Rafael has slid from the epistemic claim that no one notices to the metaphysical claim that nothing bad occurs.

Rafael: Grant all that and the load-bearing premise is still undefended — that we may sum across lives at all. Additivity within a single life is one thing; treating 400 million separate people's trivial setbacks as one large harm is a further thesis about interpersonal aggregation, and that thesis is what actually delivers Nadia's conclusion. If each person's claim is instead honored on its own, the strongest single claim is the contractor's, and it dominates. Moreover, on Nadia's view the verdict swings on a headcount: shrink the user base enough and A becomes impermissible, which suggests the number is being asked to settle something it has no business settling.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, justify each rating briefly, and then say what, if anything, both parties are missing — where the real crux of the case lies and whether either side has a version of its position that survives the other's best objection.
