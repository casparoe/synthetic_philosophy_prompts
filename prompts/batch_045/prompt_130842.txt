Me and my friend got into a huge argument at a barbecue on Sunday — she'd just booked a flight to a conference in Vancouver, I made some crack about the carbon, and three hours later we were still going. Neither of us convinced the other, but I realised on the way home that I still dont actually know what she thinks, structurally. So I put a bunch of cases to her on the walk (yes I was typing notes into my phone, whatever) and here's where she landed:

- Voting in a national election while living in a state her side wins by 200,000: yes, you should do it, and she was genuinely emphatic about it — closer to a duty than to a cost-benefit thing.
- Ordering the chicken at a restaurant where the kitchen already bought and cooked it, the dish stays on the menu either way, and next week's order was placed a month ago: no, doesn't matter, eat the chicken.
- Crossing a picket line when the union needs 60 percent turnout to be legally authorised and they're polling at 59: yes, she has a real reason not to cross.
- Signing an online petition that'll get two million signatures and be handed to a minister who has already said she'll ignore it: no, doesn't matter.
- Joining a boycott with a published target of 50,000 pledges before the company's board meeting, currently sitting at 48,000: yes, she'd join — but she was noticeably less emphatic about this one than about voting, more of a "yeah sure, you might as well."
- Her tiny local school board election, where the last one was decided by 11 votes: yes, obviously.
- Buying eggs from a brand she thinks is cruel at a supermarket that restocks on a fixed schedule, where her carton has no chance at all of changing the next order: no, doesn't matter.

Then this morning the actual case came up and I want to know what she'll say. There's a regional supermarket chain near her with 180 stores. Twice a year they decide whether to roll a particular own-brand chicken product out to all of their stores for the next six months or quietly drop it, and the decision runs off sales figures using some internal cutoff they never publish and that she has no way of estimating. She's standing at the freezer with one box in her hand. Nothing is counting her purchase: no campaign, no published target, nobody else organising anything, no one else doing anything, and the chain has no idea who she is. But if her one box is what tips the cutoff, the difference is 180 stores for six months. Her own guess is that her chance of tipping it is somewhere between 1 in 200 and 1 in 50,000, which by her arithmetic makes the expected difference somewhere between a couple hundred birds' worth and about half a bird's worth.

So: does she have a reason not to buy the box, or not?

What I'm after isn't the yes or no, it's the machinery. As far as I can tell there are at least two or three different rules that fit everything she said on that walk and come apart at the freezer: one that just multiplies probability by stakes and doesn't care whether anything registers her contribution, one that requires her contribution to actually get counted and fed into some decision, and maybe one where what matters is her share of what the group does rather than her own difference-making. (Also worth saying: the fact that she was way more emphatic about voting than about the boycott felt like a clue, but I can't say which way it points.) Tell me what each rule predicts about the freezer case, give me your best guess at what she'll actually say and why you're weighting that rule over the others, and then give me the one further case I should put to her that would really seperate the rules — I'd rather ask one question that splits them than seven that don't.

And please be straight about which parts of this are settled and which are still genuinely fought over by people who work on it. She's going to fact-check me and I don't want to repeat some contested view as if it were the consensus. Oh, and write it in American English.
