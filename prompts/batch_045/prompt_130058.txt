I write historical fiction, and I'm two books into a trilogy set in a made-up river-valley settlement I call Hallow Reach, somewhere in the late third millenium BC by the old dating (I've decided not to put a date in the text at all, which has already saved me a lot of grief). I did a big pile of reading before book one, and then someone in my writing group whose partner directs a dig said something that I can't shake: every time I let an object do something in a scene, I've already made an inference about what the people in that scene believed or what rules they were living under, and I ought to be able to say which of those inferences an archaeologist would actually sign off on and which ones are just mine. It's become a genuine problem now that I'm drafting the author's note, because I keep typing things like the people of the Reach held that... and then deleting the whole sentence.

So what I want is a procedure, not a lecture, and not a reading list (I have no library access for most of the year and I'm not going to chase down papers). Something I can run in half an hour, over and over, every time I catch myself about to put a belief or an institution into the story on the strength of objects alone: the questions in the order I should ask them, what counts as a good enough answer to each, the step where I stop and write some version of we don't know and I invented this, and the step where the question stops being about evidence and becomes a decision about what kind of book I'm writing. I'd like it grounded in whatever archaeology and the philosophy of inference actually say about getting from stuff in the ground to minds — the business of how a pattern in the debris is supposed to connect to a behaviour and then to a belief, what has to be true of an object before it can carry a meaning at all, why the gap keeps reopening — but in plain words I can use, and honest about which steps are really just taste wearing a lab coat. Constraints: I have one undergraduate course and a lot of enthusiasm, I can email the dig director roughly once with three questions so I should know which three to spend, I can't do any fieldwork, and the findings I already printed in book one are fixed and can't be retconned. Also, she used some phrase I half remember, middle-range something or other, that apparently names exactly the move I keep making badly; if that's a real thing, tell me what it is and what it would have me do differently.

Three cases to run it on, please. (1) The find I keep coming back to: around forty small fired-clay cones, found only in the thresholds of the bigger houses, always in pairs, points facing inward, and in three cases snapped on purpose before being set in the clay. I want a doorway belief — something at the threshold that keeps something out. Does the procedure let me have it, given that my first reader's suggestion was that they're just door sockets or loom weights? (2) What I've been privately calling the counting stones: about two hundred pebbles in a bag under a floor, in eleven distinct size grades, plus a flat stone with rows of matching pits cut into it. I want a grain levy and a person whose job it is to keep the tally, that is, an office and not just a habit — can the procedure get me from objects to an institution, or only to a practice, and what would I have to have found to get the office? (3) The one I already know you're going to spoil for me: my favourite scene in book two is a public judgement, the whole settlement watching while an accused person's hands are tied and they're put into the river, and everyone reading what happens as the river's answer. My evidence is a burial face-down in the channel silts with the wrists bound, and a second one with bound wrists and the hands removed. I have a horrible feeling the procedure lets me keep the burials and takes away the belief, and I'd rather be told that plainly than have it hedged. For each case say what the strongest sentence I can honestly put on the page is. And please flag the cases the procedure can't settle at all — the one where two different belief attributions fit the objects equally well, the one where everything that rotted would have carried the meaning and what survived didn't, and the one where a people three hundred miles away and four centuries later left written curses that sound a bit like what I want — plus tell me what the procedure simply cannot do, no matter how well I run it, so I stop asking it for that.
