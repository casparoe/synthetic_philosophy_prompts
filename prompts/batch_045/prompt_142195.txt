I'm a novelist — my last two books have both turned on a narrator who wakes up unable to tell which morning she has woken into, and I've agreed to run a single 90-minute session for about fourteen other writers at a residential retreat next month. The room will be fiction writers and a couple of poets, mostly smart and mostly unsympathetic to anything that looks like a puzzle with a right answer, and there is no budget and no projector, just a handful of adjoining bedrooms or breakout rooms, envelopes, index cards, a coin, and whatever I can carry in a tote bag. What I want them to come away with isn't a formula but a felt sense of why a person can know perfectly well that a coin is fair and still reasonably refuse to bet at even odds on it, and why the disagreement in the standard awakening case isn't a dispute about the coin at all but about what you're supposed to do with a credence whose truth is settled partly by where or when you are. They shouldn't hear the words "self-locating," "halfer," "thirder," or the name of the thought experiment until after the last round is scored.

Two things I'd like you to solve, because they're where my own drafts keep collapsing. First: I obviously cannot drug anyone or erase anyone's memory, so I've been toying with a between-subjects version — many "sleepers" awake simultaneously in separate rooms, twice as many of them on one outcome of the flip as on the other, each knowing the protocol but not which condition she is in — and I want you to tell me whether that substitution quietly destroys the thing that makes the case interesting, and how to stage it so the loss is visible to the participants themselves rather than something I have to admit in the debrief. Second, and this is my own pet idea, I want the same person to be scored on two different ledgers at once: an "experiment ledger" that counts her once per coin flip and an "awakening ledger" that counts her once per time she finds herself awake, with a small stake riding on each, and a moment partway through where she is allowed to re-lock which ledger she'll actually be paid on. My hope is that this forces people to notice that the two answers behave like correct answers to two different questions about what a credence is *for*, instead of like a squabble about one number — but I suspect the double-ledger trick also invites a cheap "it's just pragmatics" reading, so please tell me how to block that, and be honest if the gimmick is doing less work than I think. Please give me usable rules and materials, a timeline for the full ninety minutes, the payoff scheme (real but tiny: coffee, chocolate, the right to have your sentence read aloud at the end), a debrief that maps specific moments in the room onto the specific moves of the argument, an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — small samples, participants who try to solve it as a riddle instead of acting, the one person who insists the coin can't have been fair — and, just as important, the one feature of the problem the activity structurally cannot reproduce and that I will simply have to say out loud.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the single design decision the whole activity turns on — and then give me the rules, timeline, debrief, and caveats, with no preamble and no restating of what I've asked for. Please write in American English.
