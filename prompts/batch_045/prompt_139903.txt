You are an experienced philosophy interviewer and a specialist in contemporary moral epistemology and the debate between moral particularism and generalism. Before drafting anything, I want you to think carefully: spend a short preliminary pass identifying where a view like the one described below is genuinely vulnerable and where it is stronger than it looks, and only then write the questions. I am an MA student in a seminar on moral epistemology this term, and I have been asked to moderate the Q&A after the second of two talks by a visiting speaker who will also be recorded for our department podcast. After her first talk a few of us in the cohort stayed behind arguing about her position and could not settle whether the generalist objections we had read actually land or whether they simply assume what is in question, so I want the Q&A to do better than that.

The speaker defends a version of particularism. Her position, as I would summarise it, has these parts: first, holism about reasons, meaning that whether a consideration is a reason at all, and which way it points, can depend on what else is true in the case, so that a feature which favours an action in one case may be no reason at all, or a reason against, in another. Second, there are no true, finitely statable moral principles that codify the moral, and moral judgement is not the application of any such principle. Third, the competent judge's responsiveness is better modelled on perception, or on the kind of expertise a good diagnostician has, than on rule-following. Fourth, she allows that some considerations are default reasons, ones that favour an action unless something is present that defeats or silences them, but she insists that in a case where a defeater is present the default contributes no weight that has to be overcome. Fifth, she concedes supervenience in the minimal sense that there is no moral difference without some non-moral difference, while denying that this yields principles in the sense she rejects. Sixth, and this is the move she reaches for most often, when pressed to codify she turns the demand back on the questioner: any generalist principle still has to be applied, and application is not itself rule-governed on pain of regress, so either the demand for principles proves too much or it bottoms out in exactly the uncodified judgement she is talking about. She is also fond of analogies to jazz improvisation and to clinical diagnosis, and I would like the questions to force those analogies to do real work rather than let her restate them.

I want eight to ten questions, each one or two sentences, self-contained enough to be asked aloud without a long preamble, ordered so that they begin where she is comfortable and then progressively close off her usual escape routes. For each question, give me the reply you think she is most likely to make, and then one follow-up keyed to that specific reply. Please make sure the set covers the following pressure points, and do not ask her anything generic about her intellectual biography or about what drew her to the topic, and do not suggest readings or citations, since I only need the questions themselves.

The pressure points I want covered:

- A framing-problem worry I came up with: if the valence of a feature is holistically sensitive to the rest of the case, then competence in a new configuration seems to require knowing how each feature behaves in combination with every other, which threatens to make moral judgement computationally intractable. She will probably say that the skilled judge simply perceives which features are salient. Your follow-up should press on whether the trained perception of salience is anything other than a principle that is real but not accessible to the agent, and on what would in principle settle whether the content of her judgement is general.

- A case from my seminar that I want used: a clinic offers bereaved families a generative model trained on a dead relative's messages and voice, so that they can keep exchanging messages with a simulation of her. Assume the family consents and finds it genuinely helpful, and that there is no settled practice on either side. Ask her what her skill model says here, given that there is no shared trained disposition to perceive. If she says the view has no verdict yet, press the follow-up that particularism then looks conservative, able only to adjudicate inside practices that already exist; if she says it does have a verdict, ask where the verdict comes from if not from some general truth about what matters.

- A construction of mine that I would like tested: a community exactly like ours except that some feature that operates here as a default reason operates there as no reason at all, or as a reason pointing the other way, with everything else adjusted to compensate. Ask whether this is a possible moral practice, and what would make our arrangement the correct one and theirs incorrect. If she answers that the practice is impossible because reasons are what they are, the follow-up should ask for the source of that necessity and whether it is a general truth linking the feature to favouring; if she answers that it is possible, ask whether her view has the resources to say either practice is going wrong.

- The supervenience concession. If no moral difference comes without some non-moral difference, there appear to be indefinitely many truths of the form that anything with such-and-such a base is thus-and-so morally, which look at least principle-shaped. Ask her whether her quarrel with the generalist is therefore only about what counts as a principle, which would make the dispute verbal, or whether she must also deny that these truths play any explanatory or deliberative role. The follow-up should ask her what test would show the dispute is substantive rather than terminological.

- Her default reasons and silencing machinery. Press on whether the distinction between default and non-default reasons is itself doing the work of a hedged general principle, and on whether the difference between a reason's being silenced and its being outweighed can be marked without some account of what silences what. Her likely reply is that silencing is a fact about the individual case rather than a rule; the follow-up should ask what makes it the same phenomenon across cases and how we are to recognise it without a criterion.

- The explanatory question. When she says that the wrong-making features of an action are simply these features in this case, ask what work the making is doing, given that the very same feature can be inert or reversed in a near-identical case. If she distinguishes what makes an action wrong in this case from the claim that there is no difference in verdict without some difference in the base, the follow-up should ask whether the first notion can do any explanatory work at all, or whether it collapses into the second.

- Her anti-regress move. Ask her to say whether her claim concerns the content of moral judgement or only the psychology of applying rules, since a generalist can concede that application is uncodified while insisting that the content judged is general. If she says it is about content, the follow-up should ask what distinguishes the judge's judgement from a hunch, and why we should expect judges to converge if there is nothing general for them to be right or wrong about.

Close the set with one question about what would count as evidence against her view: ask her to name a concrete finding, armchair or empirical, that would raise the probability that generalism is true, for example the discovery that some feature's valence is stable across every context anyone can construct, or the discovery that it never is. If she says that nothing could count against it, your follow-up should ask whether that is a defect in the view or an honest consequence of it, and how she would reply to someone who took it to be the former.
