I'm in the Lower Sixth and I help run a small lunchtime reading group at school on Stoicism and 'modern life' — it started as a spin-off from our EPQ cohort, though most of the people who turn up now came for the self-help angle and stay for the arguments. We got properly stuck last week, and I'd like something I can take back to the group.

The sticking point is the one line everyone in the room already knew before they arrived: 'just focus on what you can control' — the modern version of the sentence that stands at the head of Epictetus's Enchiridion, which in the translation we were using reads, "Some things are in our control and others not. Things in our control are opinion, pursuit, desire, aversion, and, in a word, whatever are our own actions." In half an hour that sentence was made to do three separate jobs. One friend said that because a university's admissions decision is not up to her, it is irrational to care about it, and equally irrational to do anything aimed at shifting it — writing to a tutor, say — since the slogan shows both to be wasted motion. Another used it to argue that it is irrational to be upset about his grandfather's illness. And when I offered what I took to be a reductio — if my A-level grade is not up to me, why revise at all? — nobody could tell me where my argument went wrong, which makes me think the slogan is doing more work than anyone had checked it for. A Classics teacher who wandered in then muddied things further by suggesting that the Greek phrase lying behind 'in our control' means something closer to 'attributable to us' or 'up to us' than to anything like causal control, and that if that is right the whole sentence changes shape. I have been trying to sort the options myself under four labels — the guarantee reading (I control an outcome only if my willing it is enough to bring it about), the leverage reading (I control it if what I do alters its probability), the authorship reading (it is up to me if it is mine to answer for), and the jurisdiction reading (it is mine to deliberate about) — but I cannot tell whether these are four genuine theses, two theses in disguise, or one thesis with different mood music, and I suspect my list is missing something.

So please treat the line as a piece of language rather than as a doctrine and lay out, as a menu rather than as a single interpretation, the precise claims it could be making, from the trivial to the radical: which are platitudes that nobody denies, which are substantive and defensible, and which are straightforwardly false or self-undermining. For each, say whether it would actually deliver the conclusions my friends wanted from it — that caring is irrational, that attempting to influence is pointless — and say where, on the strongest reading, the real disagreement is located. Two constraints on the answer: please handle the case I keep returning to, where two pupils revise identically and one gets the higher grade only because an examiner misread a page, so that the outcome difference is in nobody's control although revising plainly still mattered; and please use British English throughout. I am less interested in what Epictetus himself had in mind than in which readings the sentence can honestly bear, and I'd rather have a one-page handout of readings with verdicts than an essay defending your favourite one.
