I've lived in the US for twelve years now but I grew up in Sweden, and I still can't tell which of my reactions to other people's success are principles and which are just a hangover from Jantelagen — the you are not to think you are anyone特别 stuff everyone quotes from that 1933 Sandemose novel. So I want to do this by cases instead of by theory. Here are my verdicts, gut level, and I want to know whether any single principle actually captures them.

1. A colleague gets promoted into a job she is genuinely good at and posts about it on LinkedIn with a paragraph of the usual humble-gratitude. Fine. I should congratulate her. The eye-roll I feel is my problem, not hers.
2. Her employer also puts out a press release about the same promotion, with a headshot. This one makes me wince in a way the post doesn't, and I can't fully explain why. It feels like something is being extracted from me.
3. A friend's startup sells for real money and he starts name-dropping and handing out advice nobody asked for. I'm entitled to cool on him. That isn't envy, that's a response to something he's doing.
4. Schadenfreude: when a tech founder who spent two years being smug on podcasts has his company implode right after a round of layoffs, I enjoy it and I don't feel guilty about enjoying it. When a rival in my own field's book quietly flops, enjoying it feels nasty and I try to stop. These feel different to me and I don't know why — the rival case is the one where I have the most to gain.
5. My kids' school stopped naming a valedictorian and now gives everyone a character award. Bad. It's defeatist and the kids can see straight through it.
6. My mother, on hearing about my raise: don't tell people about that, it isn't nice. Wrong, and I think it did me real damage. But I also notice I still half-believe her and I want to know if that half is just conditioning.
7. Billionaire funds a building and gets his name on it. The wealth doesn't bother me much. The fact that we are now required to walk past his name does. Mocking it is fine.
8. A nurse at my father's hospital, quietly excellent for thirty years, gets a plaque and a small local award. This moves me more than any of the big stuff, and I want to say it's the more legitimate kind of celebration.
9. Employee of the Month at a chain store. Creepy, and I'm pretty sure it's creepy because it's praise the employer is spending for its own purposes.
10. A pop star whose main achievement is being famous. The celebration around her doesn't make me resentful, it just reads as noise. I don't know what my verdict here even is.

My own stab, which I suspect is wrong: call it the audibility condition. Public celebration of an achievement is in order only if the people being asked to join in can, at least roughly, see what makes it an achievement. Praise that can only land as bare rank — this person is above you, admire them — is the thing the tall poppy instinct is properly tracking, and the thing the American habit of celebrating everything quietly inflates.

I also think there's a mechanism my rule doesn't model. Call it the ratchet: public praise compounds. Once someone has been publicly marked as a high achiever, later credit, invitations, benefit of the doubt and second chances flow to them out of proportion to anything they've done since, and the people who never got marked can't catch up. So a single act of celebration isn't a one-off gift, it's a small permanent transfer, and maybe that's what's really bothering me in case 2 and case 7.

What I want: test the audibility condition against the ten cases, repair it or replace it, and tell me which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and which ones I should give up. Be explicit about where the ratchet does the work and where it's just a story I like. I want a principle stated sharply enough that it gives verdicts on cases I haven't listed — whether I should post about my own good news, whether a Nobel prize and an Employee of the Month award differ in the way I think they do, whether I should praise my own kid's wins loudly, and what I owe a colleague who is genuinely excellent and also insufferable. And give me your confidence: which clause are you fairly sure about, which is doing no work, and which of my ten verdicts would you overturn even though I'll resist.

Also, plainly: if the whole setup is confused, say so rather than humoring me. If this is just envy wearing principle-shaped clothing, or if the audibility thing collapses into something trivial, or if celebrating and cutting down aren't two ends of one scale at all but three or four different things I've lumped together, I'd rather be told that than given a tidy formula for a pattern that isn't there.
