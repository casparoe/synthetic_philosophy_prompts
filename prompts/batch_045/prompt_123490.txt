Compose a satirical document: the closing site-visit report of an inspector from a national Office of Explanatory Standards to the vice-chancellor of a university whose research portfolio is under statutory review. Aim for 700–750 words, formatted as a memo with a header block (to, from, subject, file reference), numbered findings against audited projects, and a short closing section headed "Conditions of Continued Registration." The register is deadpan civil service throughout: no exclamation marks, no winking, no arch asides. Every joke should come from the gap between the bland administrative voice and the impossibility of the job it is performing.

The Office enforces a statute permitting only grounded explanations — cranes, in the vocabulary of its founding theorist, the late Ignatz Kranich, who set them against skyhooks, devices that appear to lift their load while resting on nothing. Two offences are on the books: unlicensed skyhooks, and greedy reduction under §4.2, defined as explaining a phenomenon by dropping to the lowest available level and skipping the intermediate structure that does the real work. The target of the satire is not the crane/skyhook distinction, which the report should treat as perfectly serviceable in ordinary hands, but the administrative fantasy of enforcing it: the idea that some bureau could certify groundedness case by case.

At least five audited projects must appear, and each must be diagnosed correctly in the technical sense even while the diagnosis is applied absurdly:

1. A group the inspector clears without difficulty, whose mechanism generates the appearance of design out of cumulative selection without any designer — the Office's own model of a well-built crane.
2. A group caught red-handed with a genuine skyhook that has merely been rebranded: a vital principle renamed a "sky-crane" and submitted for retrospective certification.
3. A group wrongly cited. They explain a chess program's refusal of a sacrifice by its evaluation of the positions that would follow; the inspector flags this as illicit teleology. The reader should see that the citation, not the model, is the mistake — the design-level story is exactly what makes it a crane rather than a spook. This is the one place the report is allowed to be quietly wrong in a way a specialist will enjoy.
4. A group that really does breach §4.2: having announced that the university's decision to reappoint a professor is "nothing but" a pattern of neural activity, they have dissolved the search committee, and cannot now say what the decision was a decision about.
5. A group that explains why drivers stop at red lights by appealing to a convention — a regularity held in place by many agents' expectations about one another's expectations — and which the inspector cannot classify, spending several paragraphs trying, since it appears to be made entirely of other agents and therefore to be floating.

Add one invented bureaucratic abbreviation used consistently (a certification grade, a filing code), and one piece of deadpan invented data — for instance, the proportion of cited mechanisms later found to be a crane resting on a crane resting on a crane.

The report should close by turning the statute on itself: the Office's own authority to certify cranes must be either grounded or a skyhook, and its recommendation that the Office license itself should be filed, without comment, as compliant. No character may state the moral; the piece should never say that the distinction is unenforceable, only show an office enforcing it.

Constraints: the only named authority is the invented Kranich — no real philosopher, no real book, no citations, and nothing that depends on bibliographic or historical knowledge the writer might misremember. The vocabulary of cranes and skyhooks may be used as though it were common property. The report should be legible to a reader who has never heard the terms: define each technical notion in the Office's own bureaucratic idiom the first time it appears.
