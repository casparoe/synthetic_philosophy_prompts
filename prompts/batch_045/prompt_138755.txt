I run a small reading group for graduate students and postdocs that meets every other week, and last session we spent an evening on anthropic reasoning — the self-sampling assumption, the self-indication assumption, doomsday-style arguments, the reference class problem, the presumptuous philosopher case. Someone made an offhand joke that I haven't been able to shake: the self-indication assumption is less a thesis in epistemology than a funding model. If noticing that you exist is evidence for hypotheses on which there are more observers, then a philosopher with a comfortable chair can outrun an entire field of observational cosmology, and the telescope budget can be retired. I'd like to turn that joke into a sustained piece of satire.

Write a parody of a private foundation's Request for Proposals, roughly 700 to 900 words, issued by a fictional organization announcing a new program in armchair cosmology. The RFP should invite applicants to settle empirical questions — the total number of observers the universe contains, whether it is spatially infinite, how many technological civilizations exist, when our species ends — using nothing but the fact of their own existence, and it should spell out the scoring rubric by which submissions are judged. The humor has to come from getting the actual mechanics right. Please include: a rubric that rewards hypotheses positing more observers, with the weighting made explicit (holding priors equal, a hypothesis with N times as many observers is N times more likely); a worked illustration in the presumptuous philosopher's style, where two theories are equally supported by ordinary evidence but differ enormously in observer count, so that the larger wins by a factor large enough to settle the question a priori; a mandatory prior-disclosure statement, in which applicants must state their priors and the foundation observes that priors in this program are reliably exactly as flat as the argument requires; a required reference-class declaration, where applicants choose whether to count only humans, only beings with roughly their evidence, all conscious beings, or all possible beings, disclose the choice in a footnote, and the foundation notes how much more work this choice does than any empirical evidence would; a bureaucratic workaround for the genuinely awkward case, in which a submitted hypothesis posits infinitely many observers, or in which merely possible observers are themselves infinite, so that the weighting no longer normalizes; and a short FAQ of three or four questions, including at least one in the spirit of whether a telescope is required and at least one answering what happens if the empirical evidence points the other way. The foundation should also note, in passing, that during an earlier funding cycle it briefly relied on the self-sampling assumption alone and that program was discontinued, with a correct explanation of why that assumption does not by itself license this one, and it should mention that the foundation's former existential-risk portfolio has quietly lapsed now that self-indication cancels the doomsday shift. Include at least one quote from a displaced observational cosmologist and at least one from the program officer defending the program.

Two constraints. First, the piece should be fair: exaggerate real features of the view rather than inventing failures, so that someone who knows this literature laughs rather than winces, and stay deadpan — no winking, no editorial asides telling the reader this is absurd, since the reductio should land on its own. Second, it should be legible to a reader who has never encountered anthropic reasoning and should not depend on any real citation: invent the foundation, the program officer, and any proponents or papers you cite, and do not attribute invented claims to real philosophers. By the end, a non-specialist should be able to say what the self-indication assumption claims and why someone would refuse to believe it, and a specialist should be unable to point to a single place where the satire rests on a misunderstanding.
