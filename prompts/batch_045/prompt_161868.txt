I should say at the outset that I have no academic background in physics or philosophy. I work in logistics. For about two years I've been reading on my own about the direction of time, and I've reached the stage where I can recite the standard story — the low-entropy condition at the Big Bang, the statistical character of the second law, the reason memory and records only work in one direction, the reason the symmetric fluctuation picture makes us out to be absurdly atypical observers — without being able to tell which parts of it I actually believe. So please don't spend your answer rehearsing that story back to me, and don't give me anything I'd have to look up: no papers, no authors, no bibliographies. What I want is a number, and I want to see the scaffolding that produced it.

The proposition I want your credence on, stated as tightly as I can manage:

The difference between the past and the future consists entirely in the direction of the entropy gradient. There is no further fact about which temporal direction is "earlier" over and above the fact that entropy is lower that way; if the gradient were absent or reversed, nothing about time's direction would be left over; and observers embedded in a reversed gradient would be mistaken about nothing whatsoever in calling their own low-entropy end the past.

I'd put myself at about 0.7 on this. A friend I argue with puts himself at about 0.35, and I can't locate the disagreement. Below is a test case I invented to force the issue, which I'd rather you use than the usual examples — I'll call it the Two-Bang Test, and I'm not claiming anyone else uses that name.

Take a universe whose large-scale history runs from a smooth, low-entropy Big Bang to a smooth, low-entropy Big Crunch, with entropy maximal in the middle. Suppose the recollapse is smooth enough that complexity and observers reappear during the contracting half. There are then two populations of observers, expanding-half and contracting-half. Each has memories, records, and expectations oriented toward the direction in which entropy increases for them; and in the time-ordering an external physicist would write down, those two directions point opposite ways. One question in the test is whether this setup is even physically coherent, and I want you to treat that as a live sub-question rather than granting it. But the real question is: is there one time containing two populations who disagree about which way it goes, or two times with nothing to choose between them? And if the latter, does that vindicate the entropy-only claim, or does it reveal that "the direction of time" was never the thing I was asking about?

Please decompose your credence into the sub-questions it actually depends on. The ones I can see, which you should feel free to replace, weight differently, or supplement:

1. Coverage: conditional on a single low-entropy boundary condition, how well does the entropic account explain the whole family of asymmetries we care about — memory, records, causation, deliberation, the fact that we know the past and not the future, counterfactual dependence?
2. Bruteness: how much does it cost the claim that the gradient itself is contingent and, in the standard story, simply posited rather than derived? Does having to bolt an unexplained boundary condition onto the account count against "nothing over and above the gradient," or is that a separate complaint?
3. Two-Bang: conditional on the test case being coherent and both halves containing observers, is there a residual fact about which half is really first? This is the term I expect to dominate, and I want it separated out explicitly.
4. Microphysics: how much, if anything, does the small time-asymmetry in the weak interaction contribute to the direction of time? My instinct is that it does no directional work at all, but I'd like your number rather than my instinct.
5. Self-location: whether the standard account's reliance on a low-entropy boundary condition, combined with symmetric fluctuation reasoning, puts our own status as typical observers under pressure — and whether that pressure lands on the proposition above or only on an auxiliary assumption.
6. Verbal vs. substantive: how much of the residual disagreement between people like my friend and me is a quarrel about what "the direction of time" means, as opposed to a disagreement about the world?

For each term give a probability and one line of justification, then show how you combine them, and state which single term your final number is most sensitive to. Then tell me, between me at 0.7 and my friend at 0.35, which of these sub-questions the disagreement most likely lives in, and what one question you'd put to both of us to find out. Finally: name the observation or argument that would move your number the most, and say roughly how far it would move it.
