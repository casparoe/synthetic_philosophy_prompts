I'm a postdoc in environmental economics, and last week I ended up sitting between two people at a dinner after a workshop on animal-welfare policy who spent most of the meal arguing past each other about whether insect suffering should dominate the funding portfolio. I stayed out of it, and I've been turning it over since without being able to settle it, so I'd like you to referee. Please act as a professional philosopher who works on population ethics, moral uncertainty, and animal cognition; take a moment before you write to work out for yourself which moves you think survive and which merely sound good, and then give me your verdict in continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you'd write an email to a colleague. I should also say that I'm writing this from a field station with no library access, so please don't point me to papers, authors, or reading lists; I want the reasoning itself.

Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it. (1) Owen opened with the scale argument: he put the standing insect population at roughly 10^18 — overwhelmingly immatures that die before reproducing — and said that even if the chance a beetle larva has valenced experience at all is only one in two hundred, the expected number of sentient individuals touched by a broad intervention still dwarfs the tens of billions of vertebrates we farm and kill annually, so expected value says the movement's money should mostly go to insects. (2) Nadia replied that Owen was treating the uncertainty as if it were distributed across individuals, like 10^18 independent coin flips, when it is really one hypothesis with a single shared truth value: either the type is sentient or it isn't. So the insect bet is all-or-nothing, and a funder facing many opportunities should prefer options whose value doesn't collapse to zero if one unresolved empirical question goes badly; on her view the rational response to a correlated, high-variance bet is to fund the cheap discriminating experiments first and scale up only once we know. (3) Owen said the correlation is real but changes only the shape of the bet, not its expected value, and that preferring lower variance is a fact about the funder's temperament rather than about how much suffering exists; he added that the asymmetry cuts his way, since the downside of acting is wasted donations while the downside of not acting is a possible moral catastrophe, and that her research-first principle proves too much because it would paralyse action on anything uncertain. (4) Nadia then pressed the sign problem: most insects are r-selected and nearly all die young, so if they are sentient their lives are plausibly net-negative, which means the scale argument delivers magnitude without direction — an intervention's worth is a product of two shaky quantities, whether they feel at all and whether what they feel is good or bad, and multiplying by an astronomical headcount mostly magnifies our ignorance, so the honest conclusion is that insects dominate in variance rather than in expected value. (5) Owen conceded the sign worry but said the numbers do their real work through cost-effectiveness rather than headcount, because a dollar moves conditions for enormously more individuals in the insect case, and because sign questions are empirically tractable: you can look for motivational trade-offs, grooming at treated wounds, or learned avoidance, so Nadia's objection is an objection about evidence, not about scale, and scale hasn't stopped mattering just because we are unsure. (6) Nadia's strongest move, I thought, was about population dynamics: insect populations are flows held near carrying capacity rather than stocks, so removing individuals now largely substitutes different deaths later through density-dependent compensation, which means the figure that enters the calculation is not 10^18 individuals affected but the net change in the equilibrium flow — possibly far smaller, possibly zero, possibly perversely positive — and the astronomical number is therefore being placed in the wrong slot, measuring a population instead of the size of the counterfactual difference an intervention actually makes. (7) Owen granted that for culling-style interventions but said it doesn't reach interventions that improve welfare per unit time across the whole flow without changing numbers, where the denominator stays astronomical regardless of compensation. (8) Nadia answered that if per-life welfare is negative, improving the quality of each unit of time while leaving the timing of death fixed buys little, since the gain would have to come from shortening lives, which reinstates the replacement problem; and she said that anyone who takes that route owes an estimate of how strong the compensating response is, which nobody in the room could supply.

Please adjudicate this move by move: for each of the eight moves, tell me whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, and why, and then say who has the better of the argument overall and what both of them are missing — whether that's a distinction neither drew, a hidden empirical assumption, or a question about how to aggregate expected welfare across an uncertain population that neither addressed. Don't ask me for clarification, and please don't retreat into saying merely that it depends: I want you to commit to a ranking of the moves.
