I'm a graduate TA with one fifty-minute section coming up — twenty first-year undergraduates, mixed maths and biology students — in an interdisciplinary course on minds, animals and machines. My job in that hour is to make it a live option for them that sentience admits of degrees: that between an insect and a thermostat there may be a graded series rather than a threshold a system is either over or under, and that moral status might grade along with it. The handout I've been told to use breaks the grading into five axes: the richness of what is present to a system at one moment (it calls this "grain"); how far a present state reaches backwards and forwards into the system's life ("depth"); the range and intensity of valenced states open to it; whether its states at a time belong to one subject or to several loosely coordinated ones; and whether the system represents itself at all. I don't want you to explain any of this to me. I want a plan for how I should explain it.

What I need, in this order of importance:

The sequence. What has to be in place before what, and why. My instinct is that "sentience" has to be prised apart from intelligence and from behavioural sophistication before anything else can happen, and that the harm of a painful minute has to be separated from the harm of a death before "moral status" is introduced at all — but I want the dependencies argued rather than asserted, including the places where I'll be tempted to open with a definition and shouldn't.

The carrying case. One example to build the hour around, with an account of what it must have and what it must lack. Two constraints: I'd rather it not be a real animal whose sentience is empirically contested, because once they start arguing about whether a crab is in pain the hour is gone; and I'd rather it not be a case in which all five axes move together, since the case should force the axes to come apart visibly.

The misconceptions, in the order they'll actually arise, each with the point in the sequence where it should be headed off. The ones I expect are the slide towards panpsychism — "so the thermostat has 0.004 units of sentience, and my coffee machine has slightly more" — the conflation of nociception with pain, and, the one I think will do the real damage, the assumption that if something comes in degrees then those degrees must lie on a single line, so that any two systems are comparable. That last one is the crux of the session: I want them to see that five axes may yield only a partial order, with systems that are genuinely incomparable, and I need to know where in the hour that has to be said so that it doesn't collapse in their hands into "it's all vague, so nothing follows."

What to refuse. Name the two or three things I should openly decline to explain in fifty minutes — the metaphysical machinery, presumably, and whatever else — and tell me how to refuse them without making the whole idea look arbitrary.

The check. One question for the last five minutes that would show me they've grasped the partial-order point rather than just picked up the vocabulary. I'd like the right answer to be their noticing that a question I've put to them is ill-posed, so build it so that noticing that is the natural thing for someone who has followed the hour, and tell me what a wrong-but-confident answer will look like so I can recognise it in the room.

If any of this won't fit in fifty minutes, say which piece you'd cut and what it costs.
