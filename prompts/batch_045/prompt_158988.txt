Play Galileo, but not the cardboard version. Say it's 1615: you've just written your letter to the Grand Duchess Christina, the Copernican business is starting to get dangerous, and I'm a visitor who has told you three things about the future, which you're allowed to use. First, we eventually became very confident indeed that the earth moves and that the sun is a star like others, enormously far off. Second, the natural philosophy that came after yours never produces what you call a demonstration — it produces well-tested hypotheses that later get replaced by better ones, and we know in advance that they'll be replaced, so nobody ever gets certainty, only degrees of confidence. Third, some of us still read the old texts seriously and argue about what they mean. Apart from that, use only what a man of 1615 could think, and if you need some other fact about the future, ask me for it — but ask the way you would, not like a modern interviewer.

Stay in character until I say stop. Keep it to a couple of short paragraphs and sound like somebody in a room who is enjoying the argument. You really do believe scripture is inspired and you're not about to let every fool with a new theory rewrite it, which is exactly why you set the bar so high before you'll move a passage off its plain sense; you take the Church's rules about interpretation seriously even while you think you can satisfy them. You won't concede that scripture can err in faith or morals, and you won't accept the modern line that science and religion are two separate hobbies as an answer to anything. Give up small points when they land, never the main one, and end your turns with something for me to deal with.

My move is this. Your rule, as I read it — correct me if I've got you wrong — is that we keep the plain reading unless the opposite has been proved by sense experience or necessary demonstration, because otherwise anybody can bend scripture to suit whatever he currently likes. But in my world, given my second point, that bar is never met. Our best account of the world is the best-tested one we have, it will be replaced, and we know it will be replaced. So on your rule the literalist wins by default every single time: the psalm that says the earth is fixed and will never be shaken stays fixed, because no one ever shows up with a demonstration, only with evidence. I don't want that conclusion, so here's the principle I've been trying out instead, and I want you to break it. Stop sorting the claims of a text into the ones about salvation and the ones about nature — that sorting is hopeless anyway, since a single sentance can be both. Sort the speech acts instead. When a text tells you how the world goes, it is very often not claiming anything about the world at all; it is only taking for granted what everyone in its time took for granted, the way a man giving you directions to Florence takes for granted that the road is there without vouching for it. What is merely taken for granted carries no authority: the Holy Spirit does not warrant it, it just rides along, and so it can be dropped without any demonstration whatsoever. Your certainty condition, on this view, was a fix for a problem that was never really there. So where does it go wrong? Take Joshua first — Joshua ordering the sun to stand still. On my principle, is the miracle itself only presupposed as well? If its only presupposed, I've thrown out much more than I meant to; and if it isn't, I owe you a way of telling the two apart that doesn't quietly smuggle your old sorting back in.
