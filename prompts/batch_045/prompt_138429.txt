been reading here and the old LessWrong sequences on and off for about a decade, and I still dont have a stable view on Bertrand's paradox, so I finally sat down and wrote out what I actually believe, and I want someone to check whether the pieces fit together before I post it anywhere. Here's the set:

(a) The chord case isn't a paradox, it's an under-specified problem statement. 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4 are each the correct answer to a different well-posed question about a different way of picking a chord, and once you tell me the picking procedure there's no disagreement left to have.

(b) When the statement doesn't tell me the procedure, the principled move is to find a symmetry group and take the measure invariant under it (the Jaynes-style fix, whatever you want to call it), and in the chord case that picks out 1/2 uniquely — so 1/2 is privileged, not merely one of three.

(c) But I also think probability is a fact about the actual physical chance setup, and the symmetry move is only a heuristic for guessing what that setup is.

(d) Homework problem I gave myself: same question, but chords of a unit square instead of a circle. Here the symmetry move seems to die, because the square only has a finite symmetry group and a whole lot of different measures on lines are invariant under those eight symmetries, so nothing gets picked out. Even so, I think there's a unique right answer for the square: take the unique translation-and-rotation invariant measure on all lines in the plane and condition on hitting the square.

(e) The cube-factory style case — a cube's edge is somewhere in [0,1]; what's the chance the edge is under a half, and what's the chance the face area is under a quarter (same event, since area is the square of the edge) — I want to say has no fact of the matter at all. Not "we weren't told the procedure," but there is nothing to be told; the question is just broken.

(f) Indifference for dice and cards: fine, symmetry earns it. Indifference over a continuous parametrization with nothing to pin down the measure: not fine.

(g) Except I do use scale-invariant (log-uniform) priors for quantities I'm ignorant about when they range over several orders of magnitude, and I think of that as the continuous analogue of indifference; and when there's no symmetry to hand at all, I max-ent over whatever coordinates I happen to be describing the problem in.

My own guesses at where the trouble is: (b) and (c) feel like they pull apart, since the most natural physical procedure I can think of for the chord case — throw two darts at the circumference, independently and uniformly — gives 1/3 and not the invariant 1/2, and I'm not sure what "privileged" means anymore if (c) is right. And (f) and (g) look like they contradict each other too. But I half suspect (c) and (g) only look inconsistent.

Format: exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no opening line and no summing-up line at the end. Rank the problems worst first; for each one say which of (a)-(g) are doing the work and which specific commitment has to bend or go and what that costs me. If one of the pairs I flagged only looks like a clash, use its item to say so and dissolve it instead of manufacturing a conflict.
