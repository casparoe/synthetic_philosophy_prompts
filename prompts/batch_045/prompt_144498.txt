So I got roped into chairing my university's curriculum committee this year, which I assumed would be pure busywork right up until I noticed this: we vote separately on whether a proposed course satisfies each of several requirements (writing-intensive, quantitative reasoning, whatever else), and then we vote on whether it counts toward the major — and the overall verdict sometimes flatly contradicts the votes on the parts. A colleague mentioned this thing has a name and a whole literature attached to it, and I've been falling down that rabbit hole in my spare time since.

Two wrinkles make it more than a textbook case for me. First, I'm the one who writes the ballot, so I choose the decomposition into sub-questions — and I've noticed I can pretty much steer the outcome by slicing a course's requirements differently, which is either a superpower or an ethical problem. Second, we've started using an LLM as a kind of clerk: I can ask it each sub-question separately and derive the verdict, or ask it for the verdict straight, and because it's stochastic and I sometimes rephrase, it hands back a little cloud of answers rather than one. So we're aggregating across a population of model-judgments as well as across seven humans, and nobody has any idea whether that thing should count as one more voter, as a tie-breaker, as a supplier of reasons, or not at all. It also matters that the sub-questions aren't all the same kind: on some everyone quietly defers to whichever member actually knows the subject matter, and on others it's straight-up politics.

Constraints: me, roughly one day a week for a semester, one grad assistant who is very good with spreadsheets, no software budget, and the output has to be a written protocol that seven tired faculty will actually follow and that the dean won't laugh at.

I'll write the protocol myself — what I want from you is a defended agenda for my limited thinking time: which questions about aggregating connected judgments I should actually grind on, in what order, and why that order. What's genuinely upstream of what, what's tractable for someone who is not a logician, what I should stop worrying about because the answer wouldn't change the protocol, and — the part I care most about — what I could dig out of our own past voting records that would move a question up or down the list. Also be honest about which bits of this literature people get excited about that are basically irrelevant to a committee like mine.
