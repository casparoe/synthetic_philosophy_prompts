hey — I'm a novelist and I'm stuck on a bit where I need a poem one of my characters actually wrote, and I'd rather get it written properly than fake it badly myself. Heres the setup.

The narrator, Ines, has just taken over her family's day book: one volume a year, unbroken for two centuries, every entry written the same evening, never written in advance. Her father, dead that spring, used to say "the volume is finished, we are only turning pages." She can't stand that. Her own view — it's more the family's than hers, and she's not sure she believes it — is that the written past is every bit as real as the present, because the entry is the thing that holds the day in being, and the future is not a dimmer version of now but simply nothing yet.

The wrinkle I want the poem to carry, and which I haven't seen anywhere: the old volumes are going. A wet cellar, mildew, one volume lost outright in a move. So on her own view the past can leak out of existence — not be forgotten, stop being. A truthmaker that can rot. I like that her view has this problem and her father's doesn't, and I want the poem to notice it without sermonising about it.

Form: a sestina. Six stanzas of six, end-words rotating, then the three-line envoi. I want the form to do the arguing: nothing is ever added or dropped across the whole poem, which is basically his picture, and "now" is only ever whichever word happens to sit at the end of the line you're reading. She's trying to write a presentist poem in a form that won't let her. The envoi is where it should either break her way — everything she has is here, in one breath — or go against her, since the whole poem turns out to have been all there the entire time. I'd rather it genuinely not settle, and leave her not knowing which she just wrote.

Accuracy bar: the presentist worry I actually care about is the truthmaker one — what is there, right now, in virtue of which it's true that her father said such-and-such last April — and on her view the answer is the page, which is a hostage to damp. I don't want any of the usual soft stuff: no rivers, no flowing, no quantum woo, no every-moment-is-forever Hallmark. And no citations or reading lists — I won't check them and they'd be ballast in a draft.

One extra thing if it fits: there's a seperate day book kept by an aunt two valleys over, and the poem might register — without naming Einstein or relativity — that two keepers can't agree on which entry is the one being written now. That's a real problem for her view and I'd like it in there, but only if it doesn't turn into a lecture.

Audience: it has to survive being read cold by someone who has never heard of presentism or eternalism — it should work as a poem about a woman keeping a ledger in a damp house, arguing with her dead father. But someone who does know the literature should be able to point at a stanza and say which view it belongs to, and say why the envoi doesn't settle it. Get the rotation right, I will check. 39 lines is fine. Pick the six end-words yourself, but at least two should be words a bookkeeper would genuinely use, and at least one should be doing double duty as an indexical.
