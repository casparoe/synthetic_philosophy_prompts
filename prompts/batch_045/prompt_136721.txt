**Two things we want from deliberation, and why HCH only gives us one**

*1. What an idealisation of deliberation is supposed to do*

Say that an idealisation of deliberation is an account of what a deliberator is up to, stated in terms of an ideal limit in which the ordinary limits on actual deliberation — time, memory, attention, partiality, ignorance — are removed. The model for this is the ideal gas law: describe the limit case and you have described what the real cases are imperfectly doing.

But not every removal is like removing friction. There is a condition on fruitful idealisation, which I will call the *preservation condition*: an idealisation is informative about an activity only if the features it strips away are mere limitations on that activity rather than partly constitutive of it. Removing friction from a pendulum is fine. Removing the pendulum is not. Most criticism of idealising moves in ethics and epistemology is, I think, best reconstructed as the charge that the preservation condition has been violated — that the ideal agent in the story is not doing the thing we do, only a nearby thing that looks better in writing.

*2. Two aims*

Deliberation, I want to say, has two distinguishable aims, and the interesting question is whether they can be pursued separately.

The first is *competence*: deliberation aims at arriving at the right, or at least the most defensible, answer to the question of what is to be done or believed here. Call failures of this kind of failure *error*.

The second is *authorship*: deliberation aims at the agent's being the source of the answer — at its being her answer, attributable to her, such that she can later answer for it and be asked why. Call the corresponding failure *alienation*: arriving at an excellent answer by a route that is not yours, or that leaves you unable to say where the answer came from or why you stand behind it.

*3. The target: HCH*

The idealisation I want to test is the one sometimes called HCH, which I will characterise as follows. Take a human-level question-answerer, H. Allow H, in answering any question, to ask further questions of copies of itself, to delegate subquestions, and to recombine the answers; allow this to proceed to any depth, with the subquestions chosen so that each is easier than the question that contains it. The result is a very large tree of question-answering, at the bottom of which sit questions bounded enough to be answered well. The whole tree can then be compressed — distilled — into a single system that answers the top-level question directly, without running the tree. The related empirical programme, factored cognition, asks whether real tasks of the kind we care about can be decomposed in this way by bounded agents and recombined without loss: whether the loss from decomposition is smaller than the gain from dividing the labour.

*4. The claim*

HCH is, I claim, an excellent idealisation of deliberation-as-inquiry. Competence is built into it by construction: the tree is a device for applying arbitrarily much cognitive labour to a question while pushing residual error down to the leaves, where it is small. If deliberation were nothing but the pursuit of competence, HCH would be the natural end point of the activity, and the distillation step would be the natural end point of learning to deliberate.

But HCH cannot satisfy the authorship condition, and not through any contingent feature that could be engineered away. Three structural features of it are responsible:

*(i) Interchangeability.* Every node is an instance of one procedure; no node is privileged. There is no element of the tree corresponding to the one who decides. The tree has a top and a bottom but no subject.

*(ii) No act of closure.* Decomposition has no principled terminus. The tree stops where it stops for reasons of budget, not because anyone judged that enough had been done. In human deliberation, by contrast, stopping is itself something the agent does, and it admits of internal criticism: one can stop too soon, out of haste or discomfort, or too late, out of a refusal to commit. The existence of those criticisms suggests that closure is internal to the activity rather than merely imposed on it from outside by a deadline.

*(iii) No stake.* No node bears the consequences of the answer it helps produce, and no node is affected by its own output. Yet a deliberator is: she reasons about what she will do, she knows that her own deliberation is evidence about her future action, and this self-reference makes some reasoning available to her and renders other reasoning unstable in a way that no purely third-personal enquiry exhibits. An agent can also have reason to remain ignorant of her own dispositions, or to arrange things so that she cannot reopen a question — strategies whose rationality depends on their being consequences that fall on her.

So the idealisation violates the preservation condition with respect to authorship. It removes, together with the limitations, features that partly constitute the activity. HCH idealises the *product* of deliberation and the *division of cognitive labour* that produces it, but it idealises away the *practical stance* of the deliberator.

*5. A repair and a prediction*

The natural repair is to add to the tree a node that is not an interchangeable copy: a decider at the root with irrevocable authority to stop and to commit, a stake in the outcome, and a possibly inaccurate model of itself that it uses in reasoning about what it will do. Call this *anchored HCH*.

My prediction is that at the ideal limit, anchored HCH will reproduce phenomena that look, from the standpoint of pure enquiry, like defects: that more information is not always better for the agent, because it can destroy what she is able to commit to and what others can rely on; that some deliberation is best conducted under self-imposed ignorance of one's own dispositions; and that the value of having made up one's mind is not reducible to the value of the answer arrived at. If those phenomena survive the removal of every ordinary limitation, they are not limitations. They belong to the activity, and an idealisation that cannot express them has not described deliberation but replaced it.

*6. The objection I most expect*

Someone will say that authorship is just competence plus a causal history: the answer is mine if it came out of my machinery, or out of a distillation of it, and nothing more is needed. My reply in brief: causal history does not do the work we want, because what authorship is for is answerability, and answerability requires that the agent could have stopped, could have committed, and bore the consequences. Those are exactly the features the tree lacks, which is why they have to be added back by hand in the repair.
