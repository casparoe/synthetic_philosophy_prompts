You are a senior political philosopher and family-law theorist who has served on two state law-reform commissions. The setting. A mid-sized U.S. state is rewriting its marriage statute from scratch. Two things are settled and are not to be relitigated. First, same-sex couples will continue to marry on the same terms as different-sex couples; that is the floor of the statute, not the question. Second, the statute may not rest on any religious or otherwise contested comprehensive doctrine: the state supreme court has signaled that it will strike down any legislative statement of purpose that cannot be defended in public-reason terms — reasons available to citizens generally, without appeal to a contested metaphysics of persons, bodies, or the human good.

What is unsettled is the heart of the statute: the account of what marriage is for. The commission has narrowed the field to six candidate accounts, described below in my own words.

A. Unreconstructed conjugal view. Marriage's point is to recognize and stabilize a union that is comprehensive in a specific sense: sexually consummable and of the generative type, exclusive, and intended as permanent. Other goods (companionship, pooling of resources) are byproducts of that union, not the reason the state is involved.

B. Revised comprehensive-union view. Same structure, loosened: the union is comprehensive in the sense of a whole shared life, oriented toward but not requiring children, and still expected to be exclusive and permanent. It extends to same-sex couples without residue.

C. Care and dependency view. Marriage exists to attach a default caregiver and default economic responsibility to people whose lives have become interdependent and who may become incapacitated. The state's interest is in who is answerable for whom when someone is sick, disabled, or old; affection is the usual cause, not the point.

D. Companionate view. Marriage recognizes the central intimate relationship in a person's life and gives it public support and stability. Deep devoted companionship is the good; sexual exclusivity, consummation, and permanence are common features of such relationships but not conditions on the recognition.

E. Domestic economic partnership view. Marriage is a joint household: pooling of income and assets, division of labor, economies of scale, default property and support rules. What the state regulates is the firm, not the feelings.

F. No-purpose view. Marriage has no single purpose; it is a bundle of default legal incidents — next of kin, inheritance, tax, immigration, medical decision-making, parental presumption — that citizens should be able to assemble à la carte. The statute should specify no purpose, or only an administrative one.
