I run a small reading group — six of us, mostly people I originally met through the old rationalist blogs, and we have spent three sessions on the sea-battle passage in Aristotle's *On Interpretation*. We are closing the last session with something short, and I would like it to be a villanelle. The group split along the lines these threads always split along: half of us think the puzzle dissolves into determinism plus indexical ignorance, and I have been insisting that this is a non sequitur unless you add a further premise to the effect that present states together with the laws make future-tense claims true — determinism by itself does not hand you that. Someone else keeps saying the whole thing is just the epistemic reading in a toga, i.e. that the only thing at issue is that nobody knows yet, and I want the poem to quietly rule that out: the question is whether there is a truth of the matter to be known, not whether we have found it. What actually grips me is the bit about deliberation — the thought that if both forecasts were already determinately true or false, the admiral's deciding whether to give the order would be a performance rather than a decision, and that this, not empirical openness, is what the argument is trying to protect.

So: a nineteen-line villanelle. The two refrains should be a single forecast's affirmative and negative forms — one saying the battle will be fought tomorrow, one saying it will not — and the wording is yours; please do not lift phrasing from a translation, I want no borrowed vocabulary. Both refrains must return verbatim, with no capitalization, italicization, or punctuation asymmetry anywhere, so that nothing on the page's surface ever privileges one over the other; the only asymmetry allowed is the order in which they first appear and, consequently, which of them gets the final line. The non-refrain lines carry the evening: the admiral on deck, the deliberation, the order not yet given. Those lines must stay scrupulously non-committal about the outcome — no imagery, weather, or omen that settles it — so that the only things in the poem that assert anything about tomorrow are the two refrains, which keep coming back unchanged while the situation around them shifts. Do not add a third, reconciling line or a softening hedge at the end: the closing two lines are the two refrains and nothing else.

On accuracy: someone who knows the literature should be able to tell which stanza is doing which work. I would like the poem to be structured so that a student can map one refrain onto the reading on which neither forecast is true yet and the future is genuinely open, and the other onto the reading that keeps bivalence and evaluates truth along a single privileged branch of future history that no one can identify from where they stand — with the ordering chosen so that the refrain that gets the last line is the one your mapping treats as already true on the second reading. Somewhere in the middle the poem should register that the disjunction is already settled while neither disjunct is, and that this gap is exactly what leaves the admiral's deliberation something other than theater. After the poem, add a paragraph of prose (150 words at most) stating the mapping, justifying which refrain got the final line, and naming what the poem is presupposing. Two people in the room will be partners who have done no philosophy, so it also has to stand on its own as a poem about a commander's night: archaic setting, plain diction, no jargon, nothing that reads as a lecture in verse.
