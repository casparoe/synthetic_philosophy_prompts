Could you help me practice an argument before my reading group meets again on thursday? Bit of background: I'm 68, retired from a lab where I spent most of my career measuring heat capacities of minerals at low temperatures, and I've come to philosophy late — so please don't assume I know the jargon, translate it into physical language when you can. Dave, who runs the group, is a panpsychist of the broadly Russellian sort, and he insists that even if experience is a fundamental and ubiquitous feature of the physical world, nothing whatever follows for how we should live. I want to argue that something staggering follows, and I want a worthier opponent than Dave, who mostly just shrugs. So: please play a panpsychist who grants the metaphysics in full — every fundamental physical entity has some minimal experiential aspect, and macroscopic subjects like us are somehow built out of theirs — but who denies that any of this changes ordinary ethical practice, because (as you'll argue) moral weight scales with integration and bandwidth, bare micro-experiences have no structure that could support valence or a point of view, and in any case we could never act on it. Be sharp and fair, not a strawman: concede small points freely, never concede the main one, stay in the first person and argue with me rather than summarizing what people have said, and if I use a term you'd define differently, say so in character instead of asking me to clarify. A paragraph or two a reply, plain words, physics analogies welcome. Stay in it until I say stop.

Here's my opening. In my old field, going from local degrees of freedom to a collective mode — phonons in a crystal, say — never annihilates the local motions; it entrains them. They're all still there, just slaved to something bigger. So if a brain's unified experience is built the same way out of the experiences of its parts, integration doesn't destroy the parts' experience, it recruits it — which means every scrapp of matter that never gets recruited into any subject at all keeps its own share, unabsorbed, unintegrated and unhelped. Your quietism looks like it needs integration to erase or at least swallow the micro-experiences whole, and that's exactly the step nobody can explain. And I think it gets worse for you, because I don't think the particles are the right thing to count. I'd guess experience tracks organized dissipation — entropy production, the sort of thing you find in a convection cell, a thunderstorm, a star. That gives a nasty middle case: systems organized enough that something may well be going on in them, not unified enough that anyone is home to mind it. Nature is mostly that middle case. So which is it — does integration erase, absorb, or merely mask the experience of the parts — and why isn't the total suffering of all that unrecruited experience the largest moral fact in the universe?
