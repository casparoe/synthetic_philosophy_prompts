Ok so I've been going through a pile of aquaculture welfare certification standards — the kind supermarkets slap on farmed salmon and shrimp — and there's a shape to them that I can't tell is an actual view about welfare or just an accident of what happens to be measurable. Let me lay out what I think they say, correct me if I've got any of it wrong:

- Farmed finfish have to be stunned before they're killed or bled out, and whether the stun worked is checked with behavioural proxies borrowed from mammal slaughter work: loss of equilibrium, no eye-roll reflex when the fish is flipped over, no response to handling or being pricked.
- For shrimp and prawns there's basically nothing comparable. The normal kills are immersion in ice slurry, or being shipped and cooked live.
- Every number in the standards is a rate or a density: kg of fish per cubic metre, cumulative mortality per production cycle, share of the batch with fin erosion or eye damage or lice counts above a threshold, feed conversion. Nothing anywhere depends on how many animals there are in total. Ten million shrimp that hit the thresholds certify the same as a hundred thousand.
- But the justification documents and the marketing for these schemes lead with headcount — billions of animals, the sheer scale of it — as the reason anyone should care or pay for this.
- Whole-cage or whole-site culling when a disease shows up is written into the same standards as a welfare and biosecurity measure, i.e. as a good thing, not as a cost.

Here's the part that's my own invention, tell me if it's a distraction. I can imagine someone getting fed up with checklists and proposing to replace the whole thing with a single number: estimated hours of moderate-or-worse pain per kilogram of edible protein produced, farmed out from the usual intensity-and-duration accounting. Farms get graded on that one figure. It has the appealing property that the number of animals finally shows up somewhere in the math.

So here's what I want: what does the checklist package presuppose about welfare? Does it strictly require that death itself isn't a harm to the animal, so that only a badly-performed death costs anything — or could a view on which being killed early is a loss of future good still license all of the above? Does the indifference to headcount commit the standards to something like per-capita or average welfare rather than total welfare, or is that just measurability, i.e. is the practice actually underdetermined between several theories here? What does it presuppose to settle whether an animal is insensible using mammalian behavioural proxies — some implicit account of what consciousness has to be for those proxies to be evidence — and what would break if you took seriously the possibility of an animal that's immobile from cold or shock but still having experiences? And on the shrimp exemption: what does it presuppose about sentience and about uncertainty, a threshold view or an evidentiary-burden view or just tractability, and why wouldn't you instead weight every welfare term by your credence that the animal is sentient and do expected value?

Then, two more things. Does the per-kilo pain-hour metric escape these problems or does it smuggle in worse ones? My vague worry is that it makes very short lives containing one sharp bad moment look fantastic, and I can't tell whether that's a bug in the metric or an honest implication of duration-times-intensity views. And concretely: if a retailer actually wanted a standard built on the view that being killed is a cost and that uncertain sentience deserves precautionary weight, what would have to change — caps on numbers, counting the culled fish, anesthesia before chilling, something I'm not thinking of? I'd also like to know which bits of the current standards would survive that shift unchanged, because I suspect a decent chunk of it is compatible with more than one theory and I keep over-reading it as committed.
