I have been working on the latent-knowledge problem in its vault form: a predictor is trained on sensor streams to forecast whether the diamond is still where it was left, and we want a reporter that turns whatever the predictor represents internally into honest text answers — including, and especially, on the novel inputs where the predictor might be scheming and where we could not check the answer ourselves. A colleague has sent me an argument that this cannot be done even in principle. I think it proves too much, but I cannot say precisely where it breaks, and I would like a diagnosis rather than reassurance. Here is the argument as I have reconstructed it, with my own labels.

(1) *Usefulness.* The reporter is worth having only if we can trust it on inputs whose correct answer we cannot independently verify. On inputs we can check we do not need it, and we do not need it to be honest either, since we can simply correct it.

(2) *Verifiability.* Any elicitation procedure we can actually run can only compare candidate reporters by their behaviour on inputs whose correct answer we can verify: labelled data, simulations we can construct, physical interventions we can perform, or answers we can check by other means. There is no other observable to optimise.

(3) *Underdetermination.* Take any finite body of verified behaviour V and any honest reporter H that tracks the predictor's latent knowledge. Then there is a distinct reporter H* which agrees with H on every input in V and which elsewhere reports what a competent human who had seen the same evidence would say — or, if you prefer the harsher version, whatever report would look best to us. Since H and H* agree on everything in V, no procedure satisfying (2) can distinguish them.

(4) *Non-discrimination.* If two hypotheses about the reporter fit all the evidence the procedure could gather equally well, then the procedure gives us no reason to prefer one to the other. The evidence does not discriminate, so nothing the procedure does could count as a reason for trusting one over the other.

(5) Therefore no elicitation procedure can give us any reason to trust its reporter on unverifiable inputs: a perfectly executed procedure still leaves the honest reporter and the evasive one on a par, and the evasive one is the one we were worried about.

(6) Therefore eliciting latent knowledge is impossible in principle, and not merely hard. The best achievable outcome of any method is a reporter we have no more reason to trust than one built to tell us what we would like to hear, and the problem does not get easier with better methods.

(7) *The parallel.* But (3) is an instance of a wholly general fact about finite evidence: for any finite evidence and any hypothesis about unobserved cases, there is another hypothesis that agrees on the observed cases and diverges elsewhere — the grue-style case for predicates, the curve-fitting case for functions, the sceptical case for other minds. If (4) is true, then no finite evidence ever supports any conclusion about any unobserved case: not that the bridge will bear the load, not that my colleague has beliefs, not that the sun will rise tomorrow. Some such inferences are plainly reasonable. So (4) is false, and with it (5) and (6).

That is my discomfort in outline: the argument seems to run on premises that hold quite generally for induction, and to yield a conclusion — that no finite evidence is ever evidence — that neither my colleague nor I accepts. Yet I cannot point at the step where the specificity of the latent-knowledge case is supposed to enter.

I keep going back and forth between (3) and (4), and I suspect they may be the same objection in different clothes, which worries me more than either does alone. On the one hand, (3) looks like it slides on quantifier order: the evasive H* is constructed *after* we know which inputs we verified, whereas the reporter a procedure actually hands us was fixed by the training process *before* we knew which inputs we would test, and "for every V there is an H*" is not "there is an H* for every V." On the other hand, (4) looks like it slides from *there is some hypothesis that also fits the evidence* to *fits the evidence equally well*, where the second is a claim about simplicity or about priors and not about the evidence at all. If the second is where the argument fails, then the whole question seems to relocate: it becomes a question about which reporter is antecedently more natural for gradient descent to find, and it is not obvious to me that the honest one is the natural one. And if the first is where it fails, I am not sure the point survives in the case I actually care about, since the predictor is trained to be accurate on roughly the inputs on which we will later query it, so the training process may in a sense anticipate the test distribution.

There is also my own attempted escape from (2), which I would like tested rather than indulged. Call it the swap test: take two inputs that differ only by an intervention we perform ourselves on the sensor channel — swapping two camera feeds, blanking a region, replaying a doctored stream — and require the reporter's answers to change in the corresponding way. This supplies checkable constraints on inputs whose true answer we do not know, since we know what we changed even though we do not know what is in the vault. It looks like verification without knowledge of the answer, and if it works then (2) is false. I suspect the argument has a reply, and I would rather hear it now than at the defence.

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look sound enough to bear the weight it is carrying? Then, please, price the alternatives. I want to know what each rejection commits me to about the problem itself — in particular, whether rejecting (3) or (4) turns latent-knowledge elicitation into a different problem than it first appeared to be: something about inductive priors over what training finds, or something about the design of training and test distributions rather than about the elicitation objective. If you think my swap test fails, say what it would have to show in order to succeed and whether anything like it could. And if you think the argument is sound and the conclusion is one I should learn to live with, tell me what accepting it would cost me elsewhere.
