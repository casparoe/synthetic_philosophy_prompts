Two people in my decision-theory reading group got into it after we went over the Hofstadter stuff on superrationality, and they asked me to settle it, which I definately can't. Here is roughly how it went.

The case Marta kept using was this. Two players, no communication, both perfect reasoners, each knows the other is a perfect reasoner, common knowledge all the way up. A fair coin decides which one is the "strong" player and which is the "weak" player, and each is told which one they are. Then they play a one-shot prisoners' dilemma. Temptation and sucker payoffs are the same for both: defect while the other cooperates and you get 5000, cooperate while the other defects and you get 0, both defect and you get 1000. The only difference is the reward for mutual cooperation: the strong player gets 3000, the weak player gets 1500. So it's still a PD for both of them, but the weak player gains only 500 from mutual cooperation over mutual defection, while the strong player gains 2000.

(1) Marta opened with: superrationality is either evidential decision theory in a costume or it's a mistake. The move where you say "I'm not choosing between cooperate and defect, I'm choosing between us-both-cooperating and us-both-defecting" assumes the very thing that needs arguing for, namely that my decision settles his. Causal decision theory says: whatever he does, I do better by defecting. The correlation is just evidence, and if you want to act on evidence you're an EDT person and you don't need a new word for it.

(2) Sascha: that misdescribes the view. What's chosen isn't an act, it's the output of a reasoning procedure that happens to be running in two places. That's why it doesn't collapse into EDT: EDT buys correlation wherever it finds it, which is why it tells you not to smoke in the smoking-lesion case — it manages the news instead of the world. Superrationality only cares about correlation that comes from the same reasoning being instantiated twice. And it's also why you don't get to defect once you learn you're the weak player: there's no later self who reopens the question.

(3) Marta: ok, take it at the level of procedures and apply it to my game. Two things happen. If superrationality is "do what you'd want every relevantly similar reasoner to do," it has no grip here at all — the players aren't mirrors, they're one procedure with different inputs, so there's no single act they must both land on. If instead you evaluate policies from behind the veil, before the coin, then mutual cooperation isn't the best one. Mutual cooperation is worth 2250 a head ex ante. The policy "whoever is strong defects, whoever is weak cooperates" is worth 2500, and so is its mirror image; and since we're running identical reasoning we can share a coin flip to choose between them, so 2500 each. Note that the exploited weak player ends up with 0, which is worse than the 1000 he'd get from mutual defection and worse than the 1500 from mutual cooperation. So superrationality taken seriously as policy choice tells us to flip a coin to decide which of us gets 5000 and which gets nothing. That's a reductio of the theory, not a solution to anything.

(4) Sascha: the craziness is an artifact of counting dollars. Under even mild concavity the ranking flips: with square-root utility, the coin policy is worth about 35 to each of us ex ante, against about 47 for mutual cooperation, because it puts half your probability on zero. So the answer depends on a parameter you left free, which is normal in decision theory, not a reductio. And it cuts both ways — a risk-seeking weak player likes the coin policy even more than I said.

(5) Marta: but that's just my point showing up in a new place. Now the answer depends on whose utility function the procedure is maximizing and how you combine them. In the symmetric case there's only one utility function in the room so nobody notices. Here the stakes differ, so the attitudes to risk can differ, and "what would we want the procedure to output" has no answer until you import a rule: sum the utilities, maximise the worst off, average behind a veil, pick a Nash bargaining point. The rule does all the work. So the superrational solution to the one-shot PD was never rationality alone — its rationality plus the assumption that everyone in the room wants the same thing, which is exactly what fails in any real conflict.

(6) Sascha: overstated. Even with divergent interests the framing still rules out the outcome ordinary decision theory walks straight into, and it relocates what's left over: not whether to cooperate but how to split. That's progress, not silence. And "you imported an aggregation rule" applies to every multi-agent decision problem there is, two people picking a restaurant included, so it doesn't tell us anything special about superrationality.
