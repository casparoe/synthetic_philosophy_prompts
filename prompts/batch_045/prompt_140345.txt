I'm assembling a handout for a graduate reading group on panpsychism and the combination problem, and I need a decision tree that sorts a reader into a named position on the basis of what she says about cases rather than on which label she finds attractive. Build it from the logical space instead of from a list of isms: start everyone at the single commitment that ordinary waking adult humans have conscious experiences, and then branch on eight to ten questions, each phrased as a concrete case or claim a student could answer without knowing where it leads. Please route the branching through at least these: whether a lone particle in an otherwise empty universe has any experience at all; whether a brick-sized lump of matter built out of such particles is itself a subject; whether the smallest experiences, if there are any, are drawn from the same palette as ours, the familiar colours and pains, or are of a kind we could neither recognise nor describe; whether a macro-subject formed from many micro-subjects can have a phenomenal quality that none of its parts has; whether the unity of such a macro-subject is something further over and above its parts or just the parts under a different description; whether the relation doing the unifying is itself conscious, and if so whether that merely restarts the problem one level up; whether a mind whose stream of consciousness is switched off and on again every second is one subject or many, and what that implies about combining subjects across time as opposed to at a time; whether a brain whose hemispheres are disconnected fibre by fibre contains one subject, two, or a vague and indeterminate number; and whether the fundamental conscious thing might instead be the cosmos as a whole, with the small flickers derived downward from it and carrying a decomposition-side difficulty of their own. The leaves must include constitutive micropsychism, panprotopsychism, the phenomenal-bonding view with its regress, the fusion or identity version of combination, strong emergence, cosmopsychism, and the option on which there is no deep unity of a subject to be explained in the first place; give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Wherever the space of answers permits a combination that no one in fact occupies, mark that cell as empty and say in a sentence whether it is empty because incoherent, because unattractive, or because merely unexplored, and flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot pry apart. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way one would write to the group in an email: no headings, bullet points, numbered nodes, bold, tables, or ASCII arrows, with the tree traversed in sentences from question to question and out to the leaves.
