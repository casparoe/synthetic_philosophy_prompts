I am a novelist, and I need a teaching instrument rather than an essay.

The book I am finishing is about a real woman: a herbalist tried for poisoning in 1893 and acquitted. Her archive is a single police deposition, a hostile obituary by the local vicar that is demonstrably wrong about two dates, and a bundle of letters whose recipients burned the replies. I want four things from her that the record does not give me: a sentence spoken aloud in a scene that appears in no document (a sentence I am fairly confident she thought); a childhood in a foundling ward for which there is no evidence at all; a private admission of something the archive makes probable but never states; and the use of her dead child, who is real, as the engine of my third act. Her great-granddaughter objects to one of these and is indifferent to the others; her great-grandson is delighted by precisely the one the great-granddaughter objects to. I have also been told that under English law the dead cannot be defamed — please assume nothing rests on that, or on any legal doctrine; I have counsel, and I do not want a legal map.

What I want for the workshop is a decision tree that sorts writers by what they are willing to let me do. The audience is a room of about twenty historical novelists, most of whom think of themselves as careful and none of whom will admit to caring about accuracy in the abstract. So build it from the logical space and not from a list of names: first the branch questions, phrased as concrete cases about my four requests and others like them, each answerable by someone who has never heard a label for any position; then locate the actual positions in the space the questions open up.

Eight branch questions. Five of them must be these, recast as cases rather than slogans: (i) whether the claim is held by the dead woman herself or only by the living who carry her name and her community's memory; (ii) whether what is owed concerns what happened, how she is made to appear, or the fact that she is being used for my book at all; (iii) whether anything she did — courting notice, burning papers, living two lives under two names — can waive or forfeit the claim; (iv) whether inventing words she never said, actions she never performed, thoughts she never recorded, and consequences she never lived to see are four different offences or one; (v) whether a fabricated particular is redeemed by being the vehicle of a true general pattern — my foundling ward is invented, but the shape of the childhood I need is, I think, what the record implies. Choose the remaining three yourself.

Include, as leaves, at least: a strict archivalism that permits nothing not documented; a view on which only the living hold claims, so the dead are raw material; a view on which the claim belongs to a community rather than to a person or a family; and — these are my own coinages, which I would like you to sharpen or correct rather than replace — the *mosaic licence* (any scene is permitted whose every element is documented, however the elements are arranged), the *counterfactual confession* (one may supply the interior act only when the archive makes it overwhelmingly likely and the supply is not exculpatory), and the *anachronism of interiority* (the wrong of giving a woman of 1893 a modern vocabulary of self, grief or trauma that she could not have had). No fewer than ten and no more than thirteen leaves. Each leaf should carry a name, or "unnamed — nobody defends this"; one sentence on its most awkward commitment; and one sentence telling me exactly what to do with the spoken-sentence scene.

Two further things. List the answer-combinations your questions permit that no leaf occupies, and for each say briefly whether the gap looks like incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. And flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot actually separate, rather than pretending the tree distinguishes them — I suspect at least one such pair, since two of these views may give identical verdicts on every case while disagreeing only about whom the wrong is done to.

Please do not illustrate with famous films or well-known literary controversies; if a case would help, invent one in two lines. No citations, no suggested reading, no bibliography. And write the whole thing in British English.
