Hey — I'm one of the people on our national stratigraphy committee that's been asked to give an opinion on where the Anthropocene should start, and we've got a resubmission to rule on and I'd like your help before the meeting.

Our track record so far, as best I can reconstruct it (the minutes are a mess and we've never actually wrote down a rule):

- The 1610 CO2 minimum, the "Orbis spike" from reforestation after the collapse of Native American populations: we said it's a real global anthropogenic signal but voted it down as the base, on the grounds that the base belongs where the transformation of the Earth system is most pronounced.
- ~1800, the Industrial Revolution / first sustained coal burning: rejected. The signal arrives centuries apart in different regions and even within Britain it smears over a century, so there's no single horizon to draw.
- The "early anthropogenic" hypothesis, CO2 and methane anomalies from deforestation and rice irrigation several thousand years ago: rejected. Gradual over millennia, no sharp horizon, and accepting it would make the unit more or less coextensive with the Holocene.
- Plastics and other technofossils: kept as auxiliary markers, rejected as the primary one, because first appearances are diachronous and depend on local deposition and preservation.
- Mid-20th century, radionuclide fallout from bomb testing plus the Great Acceleration: this is what we picked. Sharp, globally synchronous to within a year or two, and by far the biggest change.
- A proposal to simply date the start to 1492: rejected, 4-3, on the principle that a boundary is fixed by a marker in the rock, not by a date in human history. Two of us dissented and said that principle is itself a claim about who gets to decide what kind of thing this period is.

The new submission is a resubmission, cleverly done. A paleoecology team plus a historian propose a base around 1560–1620 defined in a nominated section of annually laminated lake sediment, with the primary criterion being the first appearance of Zea mays pollen (a New World cultigen) in Old World varves, matched by the first appearance of Old World livestock dung-fungal spores and commensal rat traces in New World sequences — a "Columbian Exchange horizon," as they call it. Their argument: unlike the CO2 dip, this was never reversed; it's the first time land use on one continent reorganized the biosphere of another, i.e. the first genuinely planetary human signal and the one that made everything later possible; and a first-appearance datum in varves is far more sharply and widely datable than a smooth CO2 trough. It's obviously weaker than the 1950s on magnitude, and its onset tracks trade routes, so it's diachronous by roughly 60–80 years depending on the site.

What I want from you: figure out what rules are actually consistent with all six of our verdicts, and where they come apart on this one. Then give me a definite recommendation — accept or reject this as the base — with your reasons, name the closest analog among our precedents, and be explicit about what principle we'd be committing ourselves to either way if our precedents genuinely underdetermine it. I'd rather you commit to one and tell me what would change your mind than hand me a menu. Also: is there a further case, or a piece of evidence we could commission, that would actually tell our candidate rules apart (not just a better version of this one)? And say something about whether picking between these rules is also a decision about who this period belongs to — the stratigraphers, the historians, the people it happened to.
