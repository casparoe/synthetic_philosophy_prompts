I write fiction, and over the past two years one of my characters has talked me into a position I originally gave her only to make her sound clever. Call it narrative panpsychism: micro-level subjects are real and ubiquitous, but the macro-subject I call myself is not a further subject made out of them — not a mereological composite, not a fragment of a cosmic subject, not the product of any phenomenal bonding relation. It is a representational achievement. The unity of a conscious life is something a stream of experience tells about itself, the way a novel makes one life out of scattered episodes, and the combination problem in its subject-summing form never gets off the ground because nothing was ever being summed.

I want to know what would count against it. Give me the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and mark which of them a defender of the view should concede rather than dismiss. I care most about the ones specific to my version: whether the narration silently presupposes the unified narrator it was supposed to explain; whether the view can describe cases where unity seems to persist without narrative, or narrative without unity — a divided brain, a creature with no narrative self-representation at all; whether it commits me to there being no fact of the matter how many subjects I am at a given moment; and whether it has quietly collapsed into the deflationary or illusionist view of selves that panpsychism was meant to be the alternative to. Say also whether it predicts anything its rivals do not, and whether it has been qualified into something no case could touch — and if it has, whether that is a defect in a view of this kind. For what it is worth, what would move me most is a case in which narrative unification demonstrably fails while a single unified consciousness plainly remains.
