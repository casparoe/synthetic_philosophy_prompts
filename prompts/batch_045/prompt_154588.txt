I help run an AI alignment reading group at my university — about sixteen people, mostly CS grad students plus a handful of philosophy undergrads, ninety minutes on a Tuesday evening, and we've now spent three sessions going in circles about the same thing. Half the room thinks debate is one of the few genuinely promising ideas in the alignment literature: you can't check the model's answer to a hard question yourself, but you can watch two models argue about it, and the honest one can win by pointing at the single step where the other's reasoning goes wrong, which turns a question you can't verify into a sequence of things you can. The other half thinks this is exactly where the scheme dies: the debaters are only rewarded for winning, so what you'll select for is arguments that are hard to check, not arguments that are sound, and the winner will be whichever model got better at burying a bad step where the judge won't look — the obfuscated-argument worry. Everyone is arguing about an abstract training setup nobody in the room has ever been inside of, so I want to stage it instead.

Please design one ninety-minute session — rules, materials, timeline, and debrief — in which participants actually play debaters and judges and run into both halves of that disagreement themselves. Constraints: the questions argued must have answers that are objectively right or wrong, that a judge cannot just compute in the time available, but that decompose into steps a judge can check one at a time (long arithmetic or logic chains, what a short program outputs, which of two small datasets has the higher median — that sort of thing, but pick and commit). Debaters should be assigned a side, true or false, and scored on whether they win rather than on whether they're right; judges should be scored on whether they pick the true side. Rotate people so everyone judges at least once, keep a visible scoreboard, and use points or chips rather than money. I want at least two variables deliberately manipulated across rounds so we can compare them afterwards — my own guesses would be (a) a hard check budget on the judge, e.g. they may verify at most one or two steps per round while the dishonest debater's chain is deliberately longer than that, and (b) whether the judge may challenge steps directly or may only listen to what the debaters volunteer to unfold. I've also been toying with a third: a round where the assigned-false debater is told the true answer anyway, so both sides know the truth and only the incentive differs. If any of those three is the wrong way to stage the problem, say so and build something better — but build one thing, not a menu.

What I care most about is that the debrief is honest in both directions. Map the moments of the game onto the actual argument for debate (a flawed chain can be defeated by isolating one bad step, so a weak judge can adjudicate a strong claim) and onto the failure modes: the asymmetry where the liar knows exactly which step is fake and has rehearsed its defense while the truth-teller has to go looking for it; the cost to a debater of challenging the wrong step and losing credibility for the rest of the round; and the way a pressed or passive judge ends up rewarding fluency and composure rather than soundness. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — judges who don't engage, debaters who lie unconvincingly, questions that turn out to be too easy, someone who meta-games the scoring instead of the arguments — and how to handle each. And be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce: that in the real proposal the debaters are models being optimized over a great many rounds, so obfuscation is something training selects for rather than something a participant chooses for an evening; that there may be questions whose answers are not decomposable into checkable steps at all; and that a judge may fail to recognize a correct step even when it is isolated and displayed. Then commit to a verdict: given what you expect to happen in the room, should we come out of the session more or less sanguine about debate as a path to honesty in systems stronger than the judge, and which single observation from the session should carry the most weight in that judgment? I'd rather have a definite answer with reasons than a survey of what everyone might conclude.
