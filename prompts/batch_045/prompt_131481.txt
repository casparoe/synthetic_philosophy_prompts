I am seventeen, in my last year of school, and I have noticed a pattern in how I make choices that I have no word for. I would be grateful for help identifying it — or, if it has no name, for a name and a careful definition.

The pattern. When I have to pick one thing out of a large pool and cannot inspect all of it — a used bicycle, a summer programme, an extracurricular I will be stuck with for two years — I have fallen into a routine. I look at a few options at the start, form a rough sense of what counts as good "around here", turn that sense into a cutoff, and then take the first later option that clears the cutoff. Three concrete instances.

First. Buying a used bicycle, I inspected three that were rusted and badly priced, concluded that bicycles "around here" are in poor shape, set a low bar, and bought the fourth one I saw. It was serviceable. A week later I came across two bicycles, both cheaper and in better condition, that I would clearly have preferred. My mistake was not that I stopped looking early; it was that the standard I stopped at had been manufactured out of three bad draws.

Second. Choosing a summer programme, I read three excellent descriptions first, set my bar at that level, and then rejected four perfectly good programmes for falling short of it. The deadline passed and I ended up with something weaker than the things I had turned down. Here the same routine produced the opposite error — and this is the part that interests me — for what seems to be the same structural reason: the few options I happened to see first were unrepresentative in the other direction.

Third, and hardest. A society I belong to fills a handful of positions by rolling admissions, and the pool visibly thins over the year: the strongest candidates are taken early. My cutoff was calibrated in September against a full pool. By March, holding to it means almost certainly ending up with nothing, while lowering it to match what remains means I will accept essentially whatever is left, since each downward adjustment redefines "good enough" in terms of what is still available. Either way the standard seems to have no independent grip on the choice: it is always just the pool I happen to be looking at, whether the pool I sampled or the pool I am stuck with.

Two features seem to me to be doing the work, and I want to know whether either has been singled out before.

(a) The cutoff is not given to me in advance, by my preferences or by the world. It is manufactured out of the same set of options it is then used to judge. It is a measurement taken with a ruler made of the thing being measured.

(b) The options I calibrate on are not a random sample of the pool. They are the first ones, and what comes first is not neutral: the three listings at the top of the page, the three a friend happened to send me, the three that were still available in September. So the error in the cutoff is not noise that averages out across many decisions; it points in whatever direction the ordering of the options happens to be biased.
