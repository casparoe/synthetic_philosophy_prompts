You're a professional political philosopher who's spent a lot of time on conservatism, institutional epistemology and the theory of reform — the kind of person who's read both the Burkean tradition and the modern decision-theory stuff and can move between them. Please think carefully and take your time: start by brainstorming freely about where a view like mine could come apart, list maybe six or eight candidate friction points before you settle on anything, and then give me the audit. Context: I'm writing an internal handbook for a platform/ops team on how to change things without breaking them, and I want the Chesterton's-fence idea (don't take down a fence until you know why it was put up) as the spine of it, but every time I write it down I feel like I'm smuggling something. Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. The fence itself: if a check, rule, or process is in place and nobody can say why, the default is keep it until somebody can state what it was for. Not knowing the rationale isn't evidence there isn't one.
2. Inherited practice carries real epistemic weight because the knowledge in it is tacit and distributed — more than any one person, or any committee, can articulate. That's the Burke-flavoured bit: the practice knows things the planners don't.
3. But I want the mechanism to be boring, not mystical. My story is that persistence is weak Bayesian evidence of function: stuff that did nothing tended to get dropped or outcompeted, so "it's still here" earns a modest prior bump and nothing more.
4. At the same time I think most of the institutional furniture around us is path-dependent junk — lock-in, prestige copying, QWERTY-ish accidents, conformist transmission — and that environments shift a lot faster than any filter operates. So the filter is weak, slow, and noisy.
5. Asymmetric burdens: I want evidence before we adopt anything new (show me it works), but I don't demand the same evidence for practices already in place. The fence shields those by default.
6. Legitimacy: a rule that binds people has to be justifiable to the people it binds, in terms they could accept. "It's traditional and nobody knows why" doesn't seem to meet that bar — even though (2) says the knowledge in it can't be articulated in the first place.
7. Reversibility matters more than most people think: I want reforms small and reversible where possible, and I hate big-bang reorgs.

Two things I've been chewing on that I can't place in the above. First, what I call the cut problem: a practice doesn't arrive pre-individuated, so the same act can be described as removing a fence or as trimming, relocating, or replacing one, and the fence's verdict seems to swing with how finely you cut. Second, what I call accidental fences: some practices were never put up for a reason at all, they're by-products or spandrels of something else — in which case the fence's instruction can't be carried out, and my worry is that the procedure then manufactures a rationale instead (fence laundering: someone invents a plausible genealogy, and now the practice is harder to remove than before because it has a story). Are those two just implementation annoyances, or do they bite the principle?
