I am defending a thesis about how large moral revolutions happen, and I would like you to map its exposure rather than restate it. The thesis, in my version: when a practice such as chattel slavery and the slave trade, elite dueling, or footbinding collapses, the engine is a shift in the prestige economy of the relevant community — what confers honor, whose regard the practitioners seek, what has become ridiculous — together with changes in material interest. Moral argument is largely epiphenomenal: a vocabulary participants reach for in order to say why they stopped, whose content is typically available in recognizable form well before the collapse and remains available, and unconvincing, in surviving enclaves long after. This is the Williams–Appiah line, sharpened in one direction.

My sharpening is a diagnostic I call the reverse-order test. For any revolution, ask whether you could have predicted the timing of the collapse from the prestige economy alone — who was admired, who had become contemptible, whose approval the practitioners needed — without knowing which arguments were circulating or how good they were. If yes, honor-first wins that case. I have added two corollaries I am less sure of: (i) that the volume or quality of moral argumentation should not predict the timing of collapse at all, so a revolution should look like a fashion cascade with a tipping point rather than a diffusion curve; and (ii) that in an honor-driven revolution there should be no identifiable argument that participants cite as the one that changed their minds.

What would count against this? I want defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want the ranking to track how much damage each does to the version I actually hold rather than to a weaker cousin. Please separate what a committed defender of the thesis would herself concede as counting against it from what only a critic would say. In particular:

- What does the thesis predict that an argument-centered rival does not, and are there cases where that differential prediction has already come out the wrong way?
- Which historical or hypothetical cases are decisive, and which are merely awkward — for instance, a practice that survived a full prestige shift, a practice that collapsed with no prestige shift in sight, or a revolution inside a small expert community where a demonstration rather than a change in honor seems to have done the work?
- Does the thesis have an unfalsifiability problem of its own? After a revolution, honor realigns with the moral verdict, so ex post every revolution looks honor-driven; and interest and honor can each be invoked flexibly when the other fails. Is my version insulated in a way that makes it uninteresting, and for an explanatory claim about history, is that insulation a defect?
- Where does my sharpening fail while the core thesis survives? I would separate the two.

I would personally be most moved by evidence about temporal ordering — whether the prestige shift precedes or follows the behavioral change — and least moved by participants' own stated reasons, which I take to be contaminated whatever the truth is. Say how confident you are in each ranked item and identify the single claim you are least sure of. Please work from historical cases you know, described loosely: no citations, quotations, or suggestions for further reading. Format: a ranked list, one or two sentences per item, followed by a short closing paragraph on insulation and confidence; total under 600 words.
