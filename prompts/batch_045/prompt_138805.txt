I'm a philosophy PhD student writing on decision-making under severe uncertainty, and what I want is a two-directional case analysis: the decision to fire the Trinity test on 16 July 1945, read through the lens of expected value fanaticism — the commitment, which strict expected-value maximization seems to carry, to trade any finite sure good for a sufficiently tiny chance of a sufficiently enormous one. You don't need to restate the standard dialectic (Pascal's wager, Pascal's mugging, the various trilemmas); assume I know it.

The case facts can be taken as stipulated. At a Los Alamos meeting in 1942 Edward Teller raised the possibility that the heat of a fission explosion might set off a self-sustaining fusion reaction among nitrogen nuclei in the atmosphere. Hans Bethe did the calculation by hand and concluded it could not happen, writing in the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists in 1975 that "Ignition is not a matter of probabilities; it is simply impossible." Arthur Compton, in a 1959 interview, said the odds had been put at slightly less than one in three million, a figure Bethe later denied had ever been on the table. Just before the detonation Fermi is reported to have offered bets on whether the bomb would ignite the atmosphere, and if so whether it would destroy only New Mexico or the entire world. Assume also, without arguing about the history, that the alternative to testing was not a modest sure thing but another gamble with large and uncertain stakes of its own.

The thing I actually want tested is a constraint I've been toying with, which I call the informational corral. The thought is that fanaticism is self-effacing in a benign way: because the expected value of information scales with the size of the stake rather than the size of the probability, an agent facing a prospect whose expected value is carried by an unresolved microscopic probability will almost always be told, by expected-value reasoning itself, to spend on inquiry rather than to act on the tail — to buy evidence until the probability is either driven to zero or shown to be irreducible. If that is right, then the objectionable form of fanaticism — drop everything and take the long shot — rarely survives contact with a fanatic's own recommendation. What I don't know is whether this is a real constraint on fanaticism or just fanaticism with a delay built into it, and whether the familiar worry that a theory which tells you not to follow it is unstable applies here.

So: five numbered items, each one or two sentences, nothing before item 1 and nothing after item 5.

1. Lay out the decision as the theory needs it — the options, the probability, the magnitude of the stakes — and say whether those stakes are genuinely unbounded or merely astronomically large but finite, and why the difference decides whether fanaticism gets any grip on the case at all.
2. Say what strict expected-value maximization with fanatical sensitivity to tiny probabilities would have directed the actors to do here, and whether their actually firing the device is a counterexample to it, a permissible result under some anti-fanatical view, or something the theory can absorb without strain.
3. Identify where the standard fanaticism framing misdescribes the episode: it is set up as a sure modest good against a long shot at an enormous one, whereas here both branches carried enormous stakes and the quantity in question was arguably model uncertainty rather than a sharp credence — and say what each of those two mismatches does to the argument.
4. Say what Fermi's wager can even mean, given that in the branch where he loses there is no one left to settle it, and what that tells us both about how the physicists were treating the tail and about the status of a loss that would have destroyed every observer positioned to record it.
5. Give your verdict on the informational corral: whether it constrains fanaticism or merely restates it in temporal dress, and specifically whether the Trinity case supports it — given that the inquiry returned impossibility rather than improbability, and that a calculation returning something like one in ten thousand would have left the corral with nothing further to recommend.
