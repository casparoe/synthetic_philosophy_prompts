On Sunday afternoons I sit in on a small reading group on rational choice that two friends run out of a back room in a coffee shop, and last week the two of them spent the entire session arguing about bet sizing, then turned to me to referee, and I honestly could not tell who had won. The toy case they were fighting over was this: you are offered the same favorable gamble sixty times in a row, and each round you may stake any fraction f of your current bankroll; with probability 0.6 your wealth is multiplied by (1 + f) and with probability 0.4 by (1 − f). They agree on all the arithmetic. The fraction that maximizes the expected logarithm, and hence the growth rate of the bankroll, is f = 0.2, worth a little over 2% per round in the log; f = 0.1 gives up roughly a quarter of that growth while halving the per-round standard deviation of log-wealth; and f = 1 maximizes the arithmetic expectation, so that after only twenty rounds the all-in bettor has an expected thirty-eight times the starting stake together with a 99.996% chance of holding nothing at all. They also agree on what makes the case more than a textbook exercise: the bankroll is a million dollars, its whole purpose is to fund a fixed-cost project that requires three million, the project is worthless if the money is not there within the sixty rounds, there is no extra credit for overshooting three million, and a lender withdraws if the bankroll ever dips below $250,000, at which point everything is lost. As it happens the target sits almost exactly on the median path of the 0.2 strategy, which ends near 3.3× after sixty rounds, and I suspect that coincidence is why they kept missing each other. What they disagree about is how to size the stake, and it went roughly like this.

1. Claire: The growth-rate answer does not mention sixty, or three million, or the quarter-million floor, which is a sign that it is answering a different question. What should be maximized here is the probability of reaching 3× before either hitting the floor or running out of rounds. With a horizon that short and a target that the 0.2 strategy does no better than tie at the median, you need more variance than that: a rule whose median just scrapes the target is a coin flip, and anyone who wants a real margin has to stake more than 0.2.

2. Reza: Two replies. First, buying a margin with variance is the same move that takes you to f = 1 — the largest right tail there is, paired with a chance of ending with nothing equal to 1 − 0.6^60. The standard objection to the arithmetic mean is that it is dominated by paths almost nobody travels, and that objection applies to any objective that reaches for the right tail. Second, the growth rate is not a limit theorem that ignores the horizon; it is the exponent, and it is also the reason a constant fraction is the right kind of object to be choosing at all, since the logarithm is the one scale on which the rounds add up, so that the myopic choice is also the long-run choice.

3. Claire: On the first, I said interior and above 0.2, not 1; 'don't chase tails' eliminates an endpoint, it does not locate the optimum at 0.2. On the second, 'long-run' is doing all the work. In this problem there is no long run, there are sixty rounds, and a criterion whose justification is asymptotic is being asked a question it was not built for. You have also quietly swapped in a different objective — reaching some target eventually — which nobody in the case actually has; mine has a date on it.

4. Reza: Fair, I should own that I am maximizing where you end up rather than the chance of a dated event. But notice what your objective does to the strategy space. Probability of hitting a target is not additive across rounds, so what maximizes it is not a constant fraction at all but a rule that depends on current wealth and rounds remaining. Once you grant that, 'Kelly versus more than Kelly' is not the real contrast; the contrast is constant fractions versus policies, and if you insist on a single number you should at least pick one that has not been fitted to one arbitrary deadline.

5. Claire: I accept that, and the honest version of my view is a policy: stake more when you are behind with time running out, less as you approach the target, and nothing at all once you have hit it. But that is still not the growth-rate rule, and it is not for the reason I gave at the outset: deadlines and absorbing barriers make this a different kind of problem. And the deeper point survives: the logarithm is a utility function. It is the one that happens to be additive under multiplication, which is elegant, but that is algebra, not a reason to want it. With a floor at a quarter of the bankroll, losing the last chunk is not somewhat worse than losing a smaller chunk, it is the end, and no smooth additive-in-logs criterion represents that. You derived the log from the dynamics and hoped no one would notice that the derivation only works if you were already maximizing the log.

6. Reza: On the barrier I concede: with an absorbing barrier the growth-rate rule has to be modified, and I would stake less than 0.2 when the bankroll is near it. But then the barrier pushes down while your deadline pushes up, and neither of us has said which dominates — you are using one feature of the case to argue for more than Kelly and I am using another to argue for less, and the case as stated contains both. On utility: the growth rate is not a taste, it is the almost-sure exponential rate at which the bankroll grows or shrinks, and 'this rule shrinks the bankroll by a few percent a round' is a fact about the process. What is a further choice is whether the thing you care about is the single trajectory you live through or an average over copies that never coexist — and the reason the arithmetic mean misleads is that nobody ever receives it.

7. Claire: 'Nobody receives the average' holds only when the decision-maker is one trajectory stretched over time. But the cases that draw people to this debate — a fund running many positions at once, a foundation paying out across many grantees, a portfolio of projects — are ensembles that genuinely exist in parallel, and for those the arithmetic mean is not an illusion at all. The time-average argument tells you which average belongs to which kind of entity and then stops; it does not say that the single-trajectory case is the one that matters. Here it is, so we are back to a dated target, a floor, sixty rounds, and a policy, and nothing in the growth-rate argument selects that policy for us.

Could you go through the exchange move by move — for each of the seven, say whether it is right, partly right, or wrong, and briefly why — and then tell me who is more right overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing? Two things in particular I could not settle on my own: whether the floor and the deadline really do pull in opposite directions or whether one of them dominates the other once you do the calculation, and whether 'the growth rate is a fact about the process, not a preference' is a genuine reply to Claire's last move or just a relocation of the same disagreement.
