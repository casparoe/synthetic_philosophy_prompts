My brother Tom is getting married next Saturday to Nadia, whose family comes from a place I'll leave unnamed here — a couple hundred kin, everybody with long-standing obligations to everybody else, where a wedding is something the whole network hosts rather than something two people throw. The six-month fight has been the guest list: Tom's side wants sixty people, "people we actually know," and thinks three hundred strangers is closer to a shakedown than a celebration; Nadia's side wants the full network and thinks cutting kin is a betrayal so plain that there's nothing to argue about. Neither side, and I want to stress this, experiences the other as having different customs — each one thinks the other is proposing to do something shabby, and both are completely certain. I'm giving the sibling toast at the reception, five minutes max, to a room that's half Toms crowd (nurses, his softball team, my parents' neighbours) and half Nadia's relatives, maybe a third of whom will be hearing it in translation, so jokes have to survive being translated.

Here's my problem, which is professional. I'm four years into a PhD on cross-cultural variation in folk morality and what its supposed to show about moral objectivity — the ultimatum game run in a dozen-odd small-scale societies with offers ranging from stingy to more than half and, as I remember it, at least one place where the generous offers got turned down; the moral foundations stuff; the trolley results that keep looking shakier the more countries get added. The wedding fight is basically my dissertation in miniature, and I keep catching myself reaching for lines I don't believe. I don't want to say love is bigger than all this, because it isn't an answer to anything. I don't want to say the two families "just have different values" with that soothing anthropological shrug, because from the inside neither of them is reporting a preference — they're reporting a requirement — and the shrug quietly reclassifies both of them as tourists in their own moral lives. And I obviously can't adjudicate; I'm the groom's sister, and I genuinely don't know who's right.

The thing I've been chewing on, and what I'd like you to try to build the toast around, is something like an object-of-inquiry picture: moral judgment is aimed at something the way inquiry into cholera is aimed at something, all of us start from whichever pile of parochial certainties we happened to be handed, and objectivity lives in the procedure of revising those under pressure rather than in any consensus we begin with. On that picture the variation data isn't evidence against objectivity at all — it's just what real disagreement looks like from the outside — and the honest thing to say to two families who disagree is something like: you're both doing the same thing, badly, at the same target, and you'll find out. But I have no idea how to say that in a wedding toast without it sounding like a seminar. So write me the toast: I want it to do one structurally clever thing (my thought was to build it around the guest list itself, or around one concrete object at the reception — the seating chart, the empty chairs, whatever you think works), and one joke that lands for both halves of the room, ideally one that turns on the WEIRD-subjects problem without anyone needing the acronym. And if you think my object-of-inquiry thought can't survive being said out loud in front of four hundred people, tell me that and tell me what I should actually be saying; I'd rather give a toast that's honest and a little flat than one that smuggles in a philosophy nobody in the room consented to.
