I'm on the tiny team that keeps the risk scores in our hospital system's electronic record alive — the 10-year cardiovascular number, the post-op complication estimate, that sort of thing — and the same fight keeps happening: a clinician looks at the number on screen for one patient and says "that's not about her, she's not an average." Compliance has now added a rule that every probability we display has to be traceable to a named reference class with at least 50 patients in it in our own data, so we can answer a patient who asks "who is this number about?" That helps with the "not me" complaint and creates a bunch of new ones.

Can you write me an actual procedure for choosing the reference class for an individual patient, something my team could run in about fifteen minutes per complaint? Questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops with "no number can be shown," and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts — I'm thinking of things like whether it's OK to define a class by race, sex or insurance status, or whether to prefer the number that's most useful for the patient's decision over the one that's most defensible if we get sued.

Three things make this harder than it looks and I want the procedure to survive them: (i) for a lot of patients there are two or three perfectly reasonable classes that disagree badly — 11% versus 26% for the same 62-year-old with diabetes, depending on whether you also condition on the prior stent and the kidney numbers; (ii) the narrowest, most "that's really her" classes are either empty, have n=1 and no frequency at all, or were found by somebody fishing through subgroups after the outcomes were in (we've caught ourselves doing this more than once, including a class quietly defined using the very outcome we then predicted); and (iii) most of our base rates come from a cohort recruited fifteen years ago, before the current drugs, so the class in the data isn't quite the class the patient is in now. I take the n=1 problem to be the actual reference class problem and not a defect in our database — say so in the procedure if you agree, and say where that leaves us practically.

Run it on three complaints we actually have open: (1) the 62-year-old diabetic, where cardiology wants the 26% and I suspect the right answer is the 11%; (2) a 34-year-old with a genotype that occurs 19 times in our registry; (3) a post-op pneumonia estimate whose only big-enough class was defined using the outcome. At least one of them should come out as "we shouldn't show a number here" even though the record will then just display a blank, and I want that stated as a real output of the procedure, not a failure of it. Also be explicit about what the procedure can't tell us no matter how well we run it.

End with \answer{...} containing the numbers of the cases (one or more of 1, 2, 3, or "none") where your procedure says we should show no number at all.
