Consider the following finitized variant of the St. Petersburg game. A fair coin is tossed until it lands heads; if the first heads occurs on the n-th toss the house pays 2^n dollars, except that no payout may exceed 2^40 dollars (roughly 1.1 trillion), so any run of more than forty tails is truncated at the cap.

I want to use this variant to argue that the St. Petersburg paradox is not really a problem for decision theory at all, but merely an artifact of allowing unbounded payoffs. The expected monetary value of the finitized game is finite and modest: every possible outcome contributes one dollar up to the cap, plus one further dollar from the truncated tail, so the total comes to about forty-one dollars. An expected-value maximizer should therefore pay at most roughly that amount, and there is nothing paradoxical in it — it is simply a very skewed but perfectly ordinary gamble. The original game looks paradoxical only because no house could ever cover an unbounded payout. On this reading the familiar move from expected monetary value to expected utility, with its diminishing marginal utility of money, is beside the point: finiteness alone already tames the gamble, and the paradox turns out to be a remark about the physics of casinos rather than about rational choice.

Two things nevertheless bother me when I try to make this precise.

First, if the finitized reply is correct, then the price one should pay is sensitive to the size of the cap — roughly one additional dollar per doubling of the house's bankroll — so that the value of one and the same gamble seems to depend on a fact about the house's finances rather than on the gamble itself. Is that genuinely the right conclusion, or does it indicate that finitizing is doing less explanatory work than it appears to?

Second, suppose I instead evaluate the finitized gamble in the way the expected-utility response recommends, using logarithmic utility over a total wealth of 100,000 dollars. My rough calculation suggests that the truncated tail beyond, say, 2^20 dollars contributes almost nothing to expected utility, so that the certainty equivalent comes out in the tens of dollars and is essentially unchanged whether the cap is a trillion dollars, a billion dollars, or removed altogether. If that is right, the cap is inert within the expected-utility calculation while being the entire explanation within the expected-value calculation, which suggests the two responses are not rival cures for one and the same disease.

So my question is this: is the St. Petersburg paradox fundamentally a puzzle about infinite, or merely very large, payoffs that dissolves as soon as payoffs are bounded, or is it a genuine constraint on any decision rule that ranks prospects by their expected value? And connectedly, if a finite bankroll is all it takes to dissolve it, why has the paradox continued to be treated as a serious objection to expected-value maximization rather than as a curiosity about gambles that could never be offered? If the way I have set the problem up involves a mistake, I would like to know exactly where.
