I'm a 5th-year PhD student in political theory and on thursday I'm giving a works-in-progress paper to a mixed seminar: it's mostly labour and public finance economists who do optimal tax and social insurance, plus two analytic political philosophers (one is a luck egalitarian, one works on intergenerational justice and the non-identity problem), and the chair is an american politics person who mostly wants to know what the empirical prediction is. Nobody there works on Rawls specifically, so I don't want to lean on the secondary literature.

The thesis is that the difference principle is better read as a constraint on transitions than on end-states: a reform of the basic structure is permissible iff the worst-off representative person's prospects improve relative to what would otherwise have happened. Two moves after that. First, that reading isn't extensionally equivalent to end-state maximin, because of path-dependence — in a toy model with three positions and a five-period horizon, you can have a sequence of worst-off-improving reforms that lands somewhere worse for the worst-off than another feasible sequence would have, and the set of reachable endpoints strictly grows with the horizon. Second, to stop that from licensing lock-in, I rank admissible sequences by the worst-off's lowest prospects at any point along the path (maximin over the path, not the endpoint), with time-integrated improvement as tie-breaker and no discounting.

What I'd like is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view. Give me the seven or eight objections I'll actually get, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way that audience would phrase it — the economists' version should sound like social welfare functions, social insurance and identification, the luck egalitarian's version should sound like responsibility and whose prospects count, the intergenerational person's should sound like people who exist at different times. For each: roughly a two-minute answer, and a note on where that answer is softest. Also tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes so it doesn't come back as an objection, which single reply has to land for the paper to survive at all, and the one question I'm hoping nobody asks (I think I know which one it is but I'd like to see whether you land on the same thing).
