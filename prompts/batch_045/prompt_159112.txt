I am a graduate student in a seminar on practical rationality, and I also sit as the student member of my university's dissertation fellowship committee. Each spring we read roughly forty shortlisted files and award eight fellowships. Applicants are scored on five dimensions, each on a one-to-five scale, and the sums produce exact ties several times per cycle — sometimes a clean pair, sometimes a five-way clump at the cutoff where no one believes the one-point gaps mean anything. Our meetings allow about ten minutes for a borderline case, the other members are faculty with no training in decision theory, and every decision goes into a minute that the dean reads and that an unsuccessful applicant could in principle appeal against. The chair has to sign off on anything we adopt.

I would like you to turn what the Buridan's ass problem, and the surrounding discussion of choice under genuine indifference, actually establishes into a written tie-break protocol my committee could vote to adopt at the start of a cycle. I have the philosophical reading; I do not need a summary of it or pointers to it. I need the operationalized version, and I need it to survive contact with people who have never heard the term "incommensurability."

Concretely, please give me:

1. An ordered sequence of tests, the first of which asks whether the case before us is a tie at all. I want the genuine symmetry case distinguished from its impostors: a rubric too coarse to resolve a real difference, evidence we simply do not have, and the inarticulate but possibly expert judgment of a member who says only that one file feels stronger. For each, say what counts as a sufficient answer and when we stop investigating.
2. A rule about when the protocol itself must be fixed. I suspect it matters that we adopt our tie-break method before we know which files will tie, because a member who prefers a candidate can always announce indifference and then break the tie his own way. Say what the protocol does about that.
3. Where the protocol terminates in a decision, where it escalates to a named person, and where it stops because the question is one of values rather than facts.
4. A clause forbidding invented reasons: we must not write down a discriminator we do not actually believe in order to make a tie look resolved. Tell me how a member is supposed to distinguish a fabricated discriminator from a real one, and tell me what the minute must say when the honest description is that we had no reason.
5. Guidance on the menu of exits — randomize openly, pick arbitrarily, defer to the chair, split the award between two applicants, or reopen the search rather than choose. Which is right when, and why. Include the case of repeated ties within one cycle and across consecutive years: if an applicant loses a coin flip twice, should the second flip be independent, or should the first loss count?
6. What to do when the two sides of the tie carry unequal downside — a wrong pick in one direction is much worse than in the other.
7. An honest account of what is lost in translating the philosophical problem into a committee rule, and which distinctions you deliberately dropped as unusable at ten minutes per case.

Then run the protocol on three cases from a cycle I can anticipate: two applicants with identical scores on all five dimensions and files of comparable depth; a five-way clump at the cutoff where the point differences are noise; and a tie between a candidate from my own department, about whom three members have substantial informal knowledge, and an external candidate about whom the file is everything anyone knows. At least one of the three should produce an answer my committee will not like, and I want you to say plainly why we won't like it and that we should follow it anyway. Finally, state what the protocol cannot do — the questions it is the wrong instrument for.
