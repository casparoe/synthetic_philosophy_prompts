Write a satirical press release of 650 to 750 words, headline and dateline included, announcing the launch of a consumer certification service whose selling point is that a measuring instrument can earn a certificate of reliability using nothing but its own readings. Invent a company name, a product name, and a founder; do not name or cite any real philosopher, paper, or book.

The target of the satire is a genuine argument in epistemology: the bootstrapping argument, which holds that an agent can come to know that a source is reliable by taking the source's outputs at face value, running ordinary deductive steps over them (the gauge reads that p; p; so this reading was accurate), and then projecting by simple enumerative induction from the accumulated instances to the general conclusion that the source is reliable. The procedure appears to deliver knowledge of reliability for free, with no independent corroboration of the source from outside it, and so appears to make knowledge of the external world far cheaper than it ought to be. Exaggerate the feature that actually makes this argument disturbing: each step in it looks impeccable on its own, and the absurdity emerges only from the concatenation.

Constraints on the content:

1. The voice is earnest corporate PR, deadpan throughout. No character says the words circular, question-begging, or too easy, and the narrator never signals that anything is amiss.

2. Every inferential step the company describes must really be one that epistemologists treat as truth-preserving or nearly so: modus ponens, conjunction introduction, a closure-style step, enumerative induction. The comedy should come from the fidelity of the description, not from a strawman.

3. Work in the problem of what class of processes the reliability claim covers. Give the service pricing tiers in which the covered class is gerrymandered ever more finely, topping out at a premium tier whose class contains a single occasion; a reader who knows the generality problem for reliabilism should recognize it in the fine print.

4. Include a flagship offering the company calls something like Relay Certification: a closed ring of instruments, each one certified by the next, with no external standard anywhere in the loop. The copy should present the total absence of an outside anchor as the luxury feature. Let an attentive reader see for themselves whether a mutually certifying loop differs in principle from one instrument certifying itself; the piece should not settle the question.

5. End by extending the same procedure to the customers: a subscription tier that certifies the reliability of a subscriber's own senses, announced in the same register as everything before it.

6. Include two short quotes: one from an invented epistemologist who is uneasy, whose objection must be recognizable as the real demand for independent support for a source together with the worry that the procedure would churn out its conclusion even in a scenario where all of the readings are systematically misleading; and one from a satisfied customer who reports that the service let him stop worrying about something he was worrying about.

The finished piece should be legible to a reader who has never heard of the debate and should strike a reader who has as fair rather than cheap.
