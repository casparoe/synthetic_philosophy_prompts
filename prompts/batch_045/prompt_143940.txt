I am assembling a short dossier of fictional primary-source documents for an undergraduate jurisprudence unit on self-reference in legal systems. The unit covers Hart's rule of recognition, entrenched clauses and amendment of the amending power, and the game Nomic, in which changing the rules is itself a move governed by the rules. The dossier already contains a court judgment, a constitutional-convention transcript, and a newspaper editorial; what it lacks is the bureaucratic document, and I would like you to write that one.

The piece: an internal guidance note of roughly 800 to 900 words, issued by the Office of the Registrar of Rules of the Republic of Skarn to the members of the newly elected Revision Assembly, explaining how an official is to determine which rules are currently in force. Format it as a memo — letterhead, subject line, numbered paragraphs — and set it in the following situation, which is mine and not historical.

Skarn's founding instrument contains Rule 9 (any rule may be amended by simple majority of the Assembly) and Rule 10 (Rule 9 may not be amended). Last spring the Assembly carried the Ninth Revision on a plurality rather than a majority; the Revision lowers the Rule 9 threshold to a plurality, repeals Rule 10, and declares itself validly enacted whatever Rule 10 may say. So the Revision is invalid under the rules it purports to change and valid under the rules it would bring into being, and there seems to be no standpoint from which to say which. The Assembly has gone on legislating; the courts have not ruled; the Registrar has gone on registering.

The Registrar's office has responded with a classificatory scheme, and I would like you to invent its details in the same spirit: rules are sorted into those in force, those in suspense, and — my favourite, and the one I most want to see handled — those in advance, i.e. rules whose validity awaits a future rule that only they themselves can authorize. There is also the Register: Entry 1 creates the Register, and Entry 1 was deleted by a rule passed under the procedure the Register alone records. Give the scheme a name, a form that officials must submit when acting under a rule of uncertain status, and at least one worked application to the Ninth Revision itself.

Two constraints on the voice. First, no winking: the memo must be deadpan, must never name the paradox it is drowning in, and must never resolve it by appeal to some higher rule the reader has not been shown — the comedy is that the guidance is perfectly usable in practice and legally empty. Second, the memo should turn on itself somewhere: one paragraph should purport to repeal or qualify an earlier paragraph, and the closing signature block should be from an office whose own authority to issue guidance is in suspense.

The target is the conceit that a legal order can settle questions about its own rules, and, alongside it, the bureaucratic compulsion to keep issuing guidance when it cannot. The humour has to come from getting the jurisprudence right: a reader who knows Hart and the self-amendment literature should find the satire fair, and a first-year should still be able to follow it.
