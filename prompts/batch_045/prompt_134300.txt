Hey — I'm in my final-year epistemology seminar and we each have to hand in a short "where I stand" note on the bootstrapping / easy knowledge stuff before we present, and I'd rather find out now that I've written myself into a corner. So: please act as a professional philosopher who works on epistemology, brainstorm through the package below carefully before you settle on anything, and tell me whether it hangs together. What I want is an audit: which of these actually conflict, which only look like they do, and for the real ones, what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me. Rank the problems by how much damage they do rather than listing them all as equally bad, and if one of the commitments is doing more work than I realise, say which. Also, no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables please — just continuous prose, the way you'd write it in an email.

Here's where I've landed:

(1) I'm a reliabilist about perceptual knowledge: a true belief that comes from a reliable belief-forming process is knowledge, and I don't require the subject to have any independent reason to think the process is reliable. That's the whole anti-sceptical point of the view, and I don't want to lose it.

(2) I want to keep closure for knowledge — if you know p, you know p entails q, and you infer q, then you know q. Denying closure feels like a last resort to me and I'd rather not.

(3) Perceptual justification is immediate: the visual experience as of a red wall gives you justification for believing there's a red wall straight away, not by way of some prior belief that your eyes work. You don't have to audit your faculties before they can give you anything.

(4) But I'm convinced by the bootstrapping cases. Someone who wanders round the room making colour judgements and then concludes "my colour vision is reliable" hasn't got knowledge — it's getting something for nothing. Same for the short version: inferring "I'm not hallucinating" from "there's a red wall" plus introspecting that I believe there's a red wall.

(5) And I think the standard reliabilist way out of (4) is a dodge: the move where you say the relevant process isn't colour vision but the whole composite procedure of looking-and-then-concluding, and that this procedure is unreliable because it spits out "reliable" even in a world where your eyes are broken. That feels like choosing the process description to get the answer you wanted. I think the generality problem is basically a pseudo-problem — individuate processes by the actual causal mechanism and move on.

(6) The bit I'm least sure about, and which I haven't managed to find discussed anywhere, is the negative version: I think self-bootstrapping to a negative conclusion does work. If my colour judgements keep clashing — same object, same light, I call it red on Monday and green on Tuesday, or I disagree with the paint chart every single time — then I can come to know that my colour vision is unreliable, and I got there using my own eyesight. That doesn't feel like something for nothing at all, but I can't tell whether it's the thing that saves the rest of my view or the thing that sinks it.

So: which pairs are genuinely in trouble, which are fine once you look properly, and what's the cheapest way to make the whole set consistent? Don't tell me everything is in tension with everything — I want to know which commitments I can keep and which one really has to go.
