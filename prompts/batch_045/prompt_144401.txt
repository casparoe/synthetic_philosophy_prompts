Context: I spent three hours last night arguing with a friend about expected value fanaticism, and I came away holding a position I would rather have stress-tested than defended. Here it is, stated as precisely as I can.

I accept fanaticism: for any positive probability p, however small, and any finite good G, there is some finite good G' large enough that a certainty of G is worse than a p-probability of G'; and correspondingly, a rational agent whose value function is unbounded above ought always to maximize expected value, however long the odds.

My argument for it, which I will call Inheritance, runs as follows. Take a large world: an agent facing a long run of decisions of the same shape, with outcomes that are probabilistically and causally independent of one another, and no borrowing or insurance constraints. In that setting maximizing expected value in each decision is not merely permissible but obviously right, since aggregate outcomes concentrate around the sum of expectations and any rival rule predictably leaves the agent worse off in the sense she herself accepts. Now take the small world: a single decision of exactly that shape. The only difference between the two cases is the existence, elsewhere, of further decisions that are independent of this one, that I have no control over, and whose number I may have no evidence about. I claim that what I ought to do here cannot depend on whether such decisions exist. So permissibility inherits downward from the sequence to the single case, and the one-shot fanatical verdicts are correct.

I have exactly one safety valve, which I will call Evidential Proportionality: the decision weight I give to a hypothesis should be discounted by the reliability of the process that produced my probability for it, so that probabilities generated by processes with no track record, no calibration data, or an unbounded space of rivals get heavily discounted. I invoke this to defuse mugging-style cases, and I invoke it to explain why real-world long-shot reasoning is not as crazy as the abstract fanatical verdicts sound.
