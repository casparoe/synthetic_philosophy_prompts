ok so I'm in this after-school ethics club thing (it's mostly just me, my friend Deja, and a librarian who lets us use the debate room) and last week I tried to build the strongest argument I could for the claim that we dont owe future people anything. Problem is it came out way stronger than I wanted and now I can't find the hole. Deja says it "proves too much" but she wont tell me where. Please help me find it:

(1) You can only wrong somebody if they end up worse off than they would of been otherwise. No victim, no wrongdoing.
(2) Whatever big policy we pick now — energy, taxes, whatever — reshuffles basically everything downstream: where people end up living, who meets who, which kids get conceived and when. So the set of people alive in, say, 2125 depends on which policy we pick.
(3) So pick any random person alive in 2125 under the "burn everything" policy. If we'd gone green instead, that specific person never would have existed at all — somebody else would be standing in their place.
(4) Even a pretty rough life — bad storms, heat, expensive food — is still way better for that person than never existing.
(5) So the burn-everything policy doesn't make anybody worse off than they would otherwise be.
(6) So it doesn't wrong anybody. So we owe future people nothing, no matter how bad we make it for them.

Here's why I think it's broken but I can't say why. Deja pointed out I can run the exact same six steps on a policy we both agree is monstrous: suppose we knew for sure our policy would leave everyone in 2125 with lives that totally suck but are still barely worth living, and we picked it anyway for no reason. Steps (3) and (4) still go through, so apparently nobody is wronged. And she says I can run it on stuff that affects people who are alive right now too, because anything we do changes who gets born later, which eventually means morality is empty and nothing anybody does ever wrongs anyone. That's obviously not right.

Her fix is what she calls the "contrast class rule": you only count as not-made-worse-off if there's no other policy that was actually available where *you* exist and you do better. So under burn-everything, the relevant question isn't "would this person exist under the green policy" but "is there any available policy where this same person exists and is better off" — and if there is (like, same energy mix but with way more money put into sea walls and AC and disaster relief), then the person IS made worse off and (1) still bites.

I kind of like her rule but I also feel like it just moves the bump, because whether you wrong somebody now depends on which other policies happened to be on the table, which seems like a weird thing for wrongness to depend on. Also I dont know if step (3) is even doing what I think it's doing, or if (2) and (3) are secretly saying different things.

So: which step do I actually give up, and what does giving it up cost me? I'd really rather keep (1), and I'd also rather not give up (2) since that seems like just a fact about how the world works. If the answer is "reject (1)," tell me honestly what I'm signing up for, because I tried that and it seemed like it committed me to saying we should be making as many sort-of-happy people as possible instead of fewer really happy ones, which I also hate. And does Deja's contrast class rule actually fix it, or does it just hide the problem one step over?
