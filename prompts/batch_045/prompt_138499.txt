I keep running into the one-liner "intent isn't magic," and I'm trying to work out what it actually commits people to. Two encounters prompted this. First, in a group chat, someone told a joke that landed badly; when he said "I didn't mean anything by it, it was just a joke," a friend replied "intent isn't magic" and treated that as settling whether he owed an apology. Second, in my seminar on speech acts we discussed whether the university's new advising chatbot — which has no mental states at all — can assert, promise, or warn when it outputs sentences like "I promise this form will be processed by Friday," and someone said "intent isn't magic" there too, in a way I took to support the opposite conclusion: that lack of intention on the machine's part doesn't stop the utterance from doing something. It struck me later that a single slogan was being used to settle both a case about uptake and a case about whether there was any speaker there at all, and that this probably means it was doing very different work in the two arguments.

So please lay out, as a list, the distinct precise theses "intent isn't magic" could be expressing, ordered roughly from the most platitudinous to the most radical — I have in mind, among others you should feel free to add: a claim about perlocutionary effects (that offense and harm don't track what the speaker meant to happen); a claim about the priority of conventional or community meaning over private speaker-meaning; a claim about uptake and context mattering more than intention in fixing illocutionary force; the much stronger claim that uptake alone fixes force and intention is never relevant; a claim about sincerity conditions (that an insincere promise is still a promise, so the speaker's intentions about the future are beside the point); a claim about illocutionary disablement, i.e. that some speakers are blocked from performing certain acts at all — a refusal that isn't heard as a refusal, a warning that isn't heard as a warning — whatever they intend; a claim about what makes an apology adequate rather than about what act was performed; and finally the wholesale reading that a speaker's mental states are irrelevant to the characterisation of any act she performs.
