I am giving a paper next month at a political theory workshop in a politics department: forty-five minutes plus twenty for questions. I want to walk in with a real Q&A prep sheet, not a vague sense of what might come up.

The thesis I am defending. Arrow's theorem is standardly read as showing that no satisfactory way of aggregating individual preferences into a collective ordering exists. I argue it should be read instead as a reductio on the preference profile as the sole input to aggregation: no rule that is a function of a single profile alone can be at once responsive to every person, fair among persons, and coherent as an ordering. My positive proposal is that a democratic rule should take a second argument, namely a public record of prior provisional decisions together with the complaints lodged against them. Call the resulting object a contested-ordering rule. It works roughly like this: the assembly takes provisional pairwise majority decisions; any coalition that loses a pairwise vote may lodge a complaint, and a complaint reopens the comparison unless and until it has been answered; a decision becomes final only when no unanswered complaint is outstanding. The independence condition I defend is not Arrow's independence of irrelevant alternatives but what I call post-audit independence: once an alternative has survived a complaint round, the social ranking of the surviving alternatives must not depend on whether some defeated alternative was also on the ballot. I claim there are contested-ordering rules that satisfy analogues of unrestricted domain, weak Pareto, and non-dictatorship together with post-audit independence; and I claim that the price of this — that the output is history-dependent, so that one and the same profile can yield different outcomes on different occasions — is not a defect to be minimised but the very feature that makes the procedure answerable to the people who lose.

The audience. About fifteen people. Eight or so are analytic political philosophers working on legitimacy, authority, and democratic theory, mostly in a broadly Rawlsian or public-reason idiom; they care about whether a procedure could be acceptable to those bound by it. Two are formal theorists: a social choice theorist who has taught Arrow's theorem many times and a game theorist who will immediately think about manipulation and agenda control. One is a deliberative democrat who thinks the whole framework's assumption of fixed exogenous preferences is the real scandal. One is a political psychologist who studies how ordinary people reason about collective decisions and who will want to know what happens if you actually run this thing in a lab with thirty undergraduates. Assume the formal people will grant me the theorem itself and will concentrate their fire on whether my mechanism escapes it or merely relocates it.

Do not pad the list with the objections I would get from an economics seminar or from a committee of practitioners; I want the ones this room will produce.
