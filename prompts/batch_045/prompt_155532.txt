So I'm on a volunteer research-prioritization group (tiny, unpaid, very online) and we go in circles about insect sentience. Every single time, someone drops "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence", someone else answers with "extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence", and a third person says "when in doubt, err on the side of caution" — and somehow all three get used to argue for opposite conclusions. The place it's doing real work is the scale argument: person A says we can't rule out sentience and the numbers people bandy about for insects are astronomical, so even a lowish probability times those numbers swamps everything else we could fund; person B says the same slogan, but insists we basically know nothing, the probability is properly tiny, and we should stick to what we can measure. I can never tell whose side the slogan is actually on, and I suspect it's being used as a claim about evidence and then spent as a claim about what to do.

Two wrinkles I'd like taken seriously rather than smoothed over. First: sentience might not be binary. Maybe what matters isn't P(insects are sentient) times N but something like an expected welfare range — and "no evidence" about the *degree* seems to do as much damage as "no evidence" about the existence. Second, the sign-flip worry: if a small probability times huge numbers is decisive, then a small probability that insect lives are on balance bad is also decisive and points the other way, so the reasoning looks like it can push any direction at all. Someone mentioned Pascal's mugging and got shouted down, but I didn't follow why this case is supposed to be different.

I'd like a disambiguation, not a verdict on insect sentience. Please give me:

1. "The readings" — 8–10 distinct precise things the slogan could be claiming, ordered from trivial to radical, one or two sentences each, each stating what it would actually license. This should be the bulk of the answer, ~half.
2. "Verdicts" — a short pass over that list sorting it into platitude / substantive-but-defensible / actually false, and flagging any reading that is true but doesn't say anything about insects. ~100 words.
3. "Which reading does which job" — for each of the three uses it gets in our meetings (precaution, dismissal, expected-value-plus-scale), say which reading is needed to get that conclusion and whether that reading survives section 2. ~200 words.
4. "Where the numbers come in" — whether huge N lets a weak reading do a strong reading's work, plus the graded-sentience and sign-flipping wrinkles above. ~250 words.
5. "What I say at the meeting" — 3 or 4 sentences, the honest thing to say instead of the slogan. ~60 words.

No citations or further reading, I just want the distinctions.
