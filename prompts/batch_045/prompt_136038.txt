You are a professional philosopher who works on value theory and the ethics of compromise, and who is used to being asked to pin down exactly which concept is in play. Please think this through carefully rather than reaching for the first label that fits: before you commit, spend a short paragraph listing the four or five candidate concepts you considered and why you set each aside, and only then give me one answer.

I sit on the seven-person grants committee of a small family foundation, and twice in the last two years we have escaped a deadlock with an arrangement I cannot name but have since noticed in other places. The committee divides between four members who think the foundation should fund structural work (impact litigation, organising) and three who think it should fund only interventions with measurable near-term welfare effects. Any payout needs five votes, and money not disbursed lapses back into the endowment, so a straight vote means nothing gets funded at all. Last spring the deadlock broke this way: each bloc's half of the payout went to the other bloc's priority, so the structuralists' money funded a cash-transfer programme and the measurers' money funded the litigation. Both blocs ended up with their own priority funded at a level no vote could have reached for them, and both walked out looking faintly ashamed. The other case is smaller and one-sided: a neighbour was going to grub out a hedgerow I think he has no business touching; I offered to pay him to leave it; he regards the hedge as worthless and my concern as sentimental; and he took the money, though with a look that suggested I had insulted him. A friend who tried the same manoeuvre on a colleague over a hiring decision was flatly refused and is no longer speaking to that colleague. What the two cases seem to share is that the gains are created by the disagreement itself — if my neighbour came round to my view he would keep the hedge for nothing, and if I came round to his I would stop caring, so on either conversion the deal evaporates.

I have tried labels and rejected them. Logrolling is close, but it is about votes, and both sides act. A friend who reads philosophy says there is a literature on 'moral trade', yet in the examples she described both parties do the other's favoured thing, whereas in my hedgerow case only one party does anything and the other only pays. Bribery seems wrong, because I take the thing I am paying for to be right rather than wrong; extortion and the Danegeld logic seem wrong for the mirror-image reason, and because nothing is being threatened. Coasean side-payments are structurally similar but turn on who holds a right, while our disagreement is about whether there is any duty at all. 'Commodification' is far too broad, and the half-remembered phrase 'rotten compromise' seems to concern dealing with genuinely evil parties. So: is there an established concept for what I am describing, and are my two cases one phenomenon or two? My brother also claims that if the two sides were exact mirror images — each valuing the other's cause to precisely the degree the other values theirs — the same structure would produce no gains whatsoever, and I cannot tell whether he is right; if he is, then disagreement alone does not do the work and I want to know what the extra ingredient is. And is the sullenness — the frequency with which these offers are refused with anger — part of the same phenomenon or a separate one?

Give me one answer, not a menu. Name the concept if there is one; say how confident you are that it is established in the literature under that name; and if nothing established fits, coin a term, define it tightly enough that I could tell whether a new case falls under it, and give me two near-miss cases that must be excluded and why. Flag explicitly which of the labels you mention you are sure about and which you are reconstructing as uncertainly as I am, and tell me which part of your account a specialist would be most likely to push back on. One last thing: I am writing from a field office with no library access, so I want your own sense of the conceptual terrain rather than citations or a reading list — I could not check them, and I would rather know what you actually think.
