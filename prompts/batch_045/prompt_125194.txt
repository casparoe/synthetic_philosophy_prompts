I'm putting together a 'which solution to Frege's puzzle do you actually hold?' quiz for my third-year language and mind seminar — 9 or 10 questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto named positions and flags the combinations nobody actually occupies.

Build it from the logical space rather than from the list of names: start with the questions that really separate views (is the informativeness of an identity statement a semantic matter at all; is the extra ingredient a property of the expression, a relation between token and object, or a hidden element in the report; does the difference show up outside belief reports, in action or emotion; does it survive when the speaker has both names fully coordinated; does it need a referent at all; does it need a subject with experience), then locate the positions in that space. Leaves should include at least: direct reference with a pragmatic story about cognitive significance, semantic relationism, classical descriptivism, a Fregean or neo-Fregean mode-of-presentation view, hidden-indexical views, two-dimensionalist views, and the metalinguistic/deflationary option — and mark 'unnamed' for the plausible-looking combos nobody defends, with one line on why.

Cases I want used: a lab specimen given two code names in a single naming ceremony by someone who is told they name the same sample and then uses both for years; a person who learns purely by testimony that two names pick out one individual and can never verify it; the empty-name case where neither name refers; the arithmetic-looking case where one side is a complex term and the other isn't; a first-person case where the identity is between me and someone I see in a video feed; and one where the 'subject' is a big language model or a database that behaves differently depending on which codename you query it with — I want students to have to say whether cognitive significance requires a subject at all. Lois-Lane-style belief cases are fine but only as one or two items, not the whole quiz.

Phrase everything so a student can answer without knowing any label, and explicitly flag any pair of leaves your quiz can't really separate (I suspect the pragmatic Millian and the implicature/impliciture stories collapse, and possibly relationism and some neo-Fregean views do too) rather than pretending the tree is cleaner than it is. Keep the whole thing under two pages.
