So I'm a PhD student in religious ethics (writing on Augustine and the will, which is hilarious given what follows) and I also work about 15 hours a week on a locked memory-care unit. I sit in on the weekly behaviour rounds and I'm the person the CNAs grab in the hallway when they want to know whether some bit of therapeutic fibbing is ok. This comes up several times a day, not once a semester: a resident with mid-stage dementia asks where her husband is and he died in 2019; a resident is convinced she's late for her shift in 1974; a daughter asks at handover 'did she have a good night?' when she didn't. The stakes are small officially — nobody is being disciplined — but inconsistent answers between shifts shred trust with families and leave new staff (mostly 19 or 20, no training in any of this) feeling like they're being asked to lie as a job requirement. I have maybe three minutes per conversation, whatever I say has to be sayable the same way to a CNA and to the nurse manager who signs the care plan, and it has to be runnable by someone who has never opened a philosophy book.

What I want is a decision procedure, in plain language, that takes one of these utterances as input and outputs an answer about whether it is a lie in the sense Augustine actually cares about — not 'is it false', not 'is it harmful', not 'would a reasonable person object'. It has to fit on one side of a laminated card. No footnotes, no 'Augustine says in chapter 3' — I've read the texts, I can't check anything from the unit anyway, and a CNA can't use it. If a step depends on a contested reading of him, say so in a parenthesis and tell me what changes under the other reading.

Things the procedure has to handle, in this order, with questions in a fixed sequence and, for each, what counts as a good enough answer and where it stops with a no or hands off to a person:

1. Whether there's an assertion at all. Playing along with a resident who is in 1974 is not the same act as informing her; I keep failing to explain why. Give me a test.
2. The will-to-deceive bit. What question does the CNA actually ask herself, and what's a sufficient answer when the honest answer is 'I don't know, I was just trying to get her to stop crying'?
3. The thing I really can't get straight: a true sentence said in order to get someone to believe something false, versus a false sentence said in a register where nobody is being asked to believe anything. Does the will do all the work?
4. Nested will. The nurse doesn't want the resident deceived as such — she wants her to stop trying to elbow the exit door, and the false belief is the means. Is 'willed as a means' inside the will to deceive or outside it? Pick a side, say what it costs you.
5. Concealment vs. assertion: silence, changing the subject, answering a different question, letting a resident's existing false belief stand. Is 'letting it stand' one act or does it renew every time she asks again, which she will, twelve times an hour?
6. Mixed audience — the daughter is standing behind the resident. Third party — the nurse tells the *family* something false about the resident. Self-deception — 'I said it because I believed it at the time'.

Mark explicitly the points where no procedure can help and a human has to decide as a matter of values, and say which human (me, the family, the care-plan meeting). I'd also like a corridor test or something like it — one question that catches a hidden will without anyone needing the theory.

Then run it on three: (a) a CNA tells a resident her dead husband 'got held up, he'll be here after dinner' to stop her from trying to leave the unit; (b) staff tell a resident who is living in 1974 that they'll clock her out at five; (c) the nurse tells the daughter 'she was fine last night' knowing the daughter will hear 'she slept and ate' and having decided the daughter would only be upset for nothing. Case (a) is the one where I think the procedure is going to give me an answer I don't like — that the grading of lies changes how bad it is but not whether it's permitted — and I want it to give me that answer anyway and then tell me where I get to push back. Finish by saying plainly what this procedure cannot do: which questions it looks like it answers and doesn't.
