I teach an upper-level undergrad epistemology seminar — 16 students, one 75-minute block — and were coming up on the accuracy-first material: veritism, scoring rules, the argument that incoherent credences get dominated in accuracy. I want them to play something rather than get another handout, and I want the experience to do the work, not me: nobody should hear the words strict propriety or dominance until the debrief.

Rough plan I have, so tell me if im building it wrong: a bunch of short rounds, each resolved immediately in the room (no phones, no internet — deck of cards, dice, or questions about the room itself settled by a show of hands), and students report credences cell by cell on a 0-100 scale for each cell of a partition, with no chip budget that forces them to add to 100, so incoherence shows up on its own. The payoff is trivial — chocolate, or picking next weeks reading — but it has to feel like a payoff.

The twist I want the session built around: run one block with the ordinary quadratic score computed over the fine cells (twice the credence in the true cell, minus the sum of squares over all cells, scaled to something readable), then run a second block with a rule that is strictly proper only at a coarser level — sum their credences within each of three groups of cells and apply the same quadratic formula to those three totals, so anything they do inside a group is invisible to their score. My claim is that the second rule is proper but not strictly proper, so the dominance argument delivers coherence over the coarse algebra and nothing at all inside the groups, and students will feel this as: they can reshuffle credence inside a group for free and nothing happens. Is that right, or does my rule break in some way that will bite me in class? And which block should run first, and why?

What I need from you: rules, materials, the payoff scheme, how many rounds and how to keep single-round luck from swamping the lesson, and a debrief that maps two or three moments in the game onto veritism, onto propriety, and onto the gap between dominating in every world and merely doing better in expectation. Also say honestly what the game cannot reproduce — I assume it cant reproduce the actual theorem, and that having a prize quietly contaminates the non-pragmatic point. And tell me what goes wrong the first time: I expect at least one student extremizing forever, one splitting a cell in two just to see what happens, and a pile of arithmetic errors.
