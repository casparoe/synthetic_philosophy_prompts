I am writing the position statement for a small workshop on the wrong kind of reasons problem, and before I circulate it I want an audit of whether the package I have assembled is actually coherent. Here are the commitments I want to make, stated roughly in the order I would defend them.

1. The right-kind/wrong-kind distinction is genuine and uniform across attitude-types, and it is not a distinction in weight. A wrong-kind consideration gives me no reason at all for the attitude itself — not an outweighed one, not a pro tanto one — though it can give me a reason to bring it about that I have the attitude. So when a demon threatens to punish me unless I admire a saucer, I have a strong reason to get myself into a state of admiring it and no reason whatsoever to admire it.

2. The distinction is to be drawn as follows, in what I'll call the fittingness account: a consideration is a right-kind reason for an attitude A toward an object o just in case it bears on whether A fits o, on whether A is the correct response to o; a wrong-kind reason bears only on whether it would be good, useful or prudent for me to be in the state of A-ing. This is the object-given versus state-given distinction, and I want it to do all the work.

3. I am a buck-passer about the relevant value properties: for the saucer to be admirable just is for there to be right-kind reasons to admire it, and similarly for fearsome, desirable and the rest. I also treat it as a non-negotiable constraint that the account in 2 must not itself mention admirability, value or their cognates, on pain of circularity.

4. For belief, the right kind of reason is evidence and only evidence: considerations of practical stake, and considerations about the moral quality of holding the belief, are wrong-kind. I reject both pragmatic and moral encroachment on what one has reason to believe, and I think their appeal comes from conflating reasons to believe with reasons to act as if, or with reasons to bring it about that one believes. Note what this commits me to: evidential bearing is exactly what makes a consideration a right-kind reason for belief. A consideration is a reason to believe that p in virtue of indicating that p is true.

5. The verdict in 1 does not change when the threat is informative. Suppose the demon issues his threat exactly when the object really is admirable, so that the threat is a perfectly reliable indicator of admirability; call this an indicator threat. Even then I want to say that the threat is not a reason to admire, but at most a reason to believe that there is one, and I deny that a reason to believe that there is a reason to A is itself a reason to A.

6. Some attitudes admit self-fulfilling reasons, and those are right-kind. Trust is the clearest case: that my trusting you would partly constitute a good relationship between us, or would make it likelier that you prove trustworthy, is a reason to trust you, and not merely a reason to manipulate myself into trusting. I want to say something similar about hope, where hoping sustains the effort that makes the hoped-for outcome likelier.

7. But not every self-fulfilling consideration is right-kind, and this is the case I keep getting stuck on. My colleague has a spotty record. If I trust her with the task she will very likely rise to it; and if I do not trust her she will notice, the working relationship will sour, and she will be unreliable thereafter. I want to say the first is a right-kind reason to trust her and the second is not — that the second is the demon's threat in a nicer suit — but I cannot see how the fittingness account in 2 separates them, since both are considerations about the effects of my being in the state, and both bear on the relationship, which plausibly belongs to trust's object.

8. Wrong-kind reasons are genuine practical reasons to bring it about that one has the attitude, and I am untroubled by the fact that one cannot do that directly: indirect routes — arranging one's circumstances, attention and company — seem to me enough for these to be reasons for me.

9. Whether it would be all-things-considered rational for me to end up with an attitude is one question, settled by weighing both kinds of reason together; whether the attitude fits its object is another, settled by right-kind reasons alone. I rely on the independence of these questions: it is what lets me say of the demon's victim that she is rationally required to get herself to admire the saucer and that admiration is nevertheless unfitting.

What I want from you: treat this set as the object. Go through the combinations and tell me which are in tension, ordered by severity rather than listed as if equally bad. For each tension, say precisely which commitments generate it, what exactly I would have to give up, and what that would cost me somewhere else in the set — I care more about the costs than about the fix. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Separately, flag any pair that only looks inconsistent and say what makes it dissolve, and if any apparent tension turns out to rest on a word I am using in two senses, say which word. Where a commitment survives only because of a distinction I have not drawn explicitly, draw it for me. I do not need references or names of views; I can supply those myself. Keep the whole audit under about nine hundred words.
