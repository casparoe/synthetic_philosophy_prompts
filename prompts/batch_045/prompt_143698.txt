I am the TA for a second-year seminar called Persons, Selves, and Stories, and I have just finished marking questions 1–3 and 5 of the final exam; question 4 is the one I keep going back and forth on, and another TA who glanced at it came to a different conclusion than I did, so I would like an independent read before I enter the marks. The exam was closed-book and I have told students explicitly that no citations, page references, or secondary reading are required — everything they need is stated in the question itself — so please grade the conceptual content and the reasoning only, and do not reward or penalize anything for style, length, or polish.

The question (worth 20 marks) was as follows:

Question 4. Dennett proposes that a self is not a further constituent of a person — not a Cartesian ego, not an organ, not a homunculus — but an abstraction: the center of narrative gravity of a creature, posited for roughly the reason a physicist posits a center of gravity, because doing so makes prediction and explanation tractable. Compare this with two neighbors we discussed in the seminar: (a) Parfit-style reductionism, on which there is no further fact of personal identity over and above physical and psychological facts, and identity is not what matters; (b) the attributed-narrative variant, or AN, which I introduced in week 6 — the self of a creature is the center of narrative gravity not of the narratives the creature tells about itself but of the narratives other people tell about it. Now consider the Case of the Two Memoirs. N. has, since adolescence, kept a careful and internally coherent diary-narrative in which she is the person who protected her younger brother through their parents' long illness. Her brother, two of her long-term employers, and her therapist, drawing on records and on her observed behavior across the same decades, tell a stable and mutually consistent story in which she was dependent on her brother and often cruel to him. Both narratives are stable over thirty years; both are acted on (hers by her, theirs by them); and both, in practice, predict her behavior about equally well. Both are coherent enough to have a center of narrative gravity. In no more than 600 words: (i) Does N. have one self, two selves, or no determinate self? Say how each of Dennett's account, reductionism, and AN would answer, and whether those answers really differ. (ii) Is a center of narrative gravity discovered or stipulated — once the narratives are fixed, is there, on Dennett's own terms, a fact of the matter about where it lies? (iii) Does the analogy with center of gravity guarantee that a single life has at most one self? Justify each answer.

One student's answer (reproduced verbatim; I have numbered the paragraphs so you can key your comments to them):

[1] Dennett's proposal is that the self is not an extra thing inside the person. It is an abstraction, comparable to an object's center of gravity: not one of the object's parts, not something with a location of its own among the parts, and yet perfectly real, because without it a great deal of prediction and explanation would be impossible. The theory that posits it is not particle physics but a kind of people-physics, a hermeneutic or interpretive discipline. This is how Dennett can say both that a self is a theorist's fiction and that selves are real: the fiction marks a genuine pattern in the behavior of a creature, and patterns are real enough, even though they are not additional bits of matter.

[2] Parfit's reductionism is close to this but not the same thing. Reductionism holds that personal identity consists in nothing over and above physical and psychological facts, and that identity is not what matters; what matters is psychological continuity and connectedness. Dennett's account is not really a competitor to that claim so much as a view pitched one level up: granted the psychological facts, the self is the interpretive abstraction we erect over them. The two are therefore consistent, and the Case of the Two Memoirs does not tell between them. Both positions deny that there is a further fact of identity waiting to be discovered, and both say the question of who N. is has to be answered at the level of the facts about her psychology rather than by looking for a further entity.

[3] The case does, however, tell against AN. For Dennett, a self is the center of narrative gravity of the creature's own self-narrative: each of us is the author of the autobiography that makes us who we are, and it is the first-person perspective that supplies the narrative in the first place. N.'s self is therefore the protagonist of her diary. The story her brother and her therapist tell is their construction, and however useful it is to them, it is not her self — at most it is a character in their story about her. AN mistakes a third-personal summary, however predictive, for the thing itself. The verdict is accordingly: one self, N.'s own, described with varying degrees of accuracy by other people.

[4] Nor can Dennett allow two centers of narrative gravity in one life. A physical object has exactly one center of gravity, fixed by the distribution of its matter; in the same way, a person's self is fixed by the total narrative material available, and there is exactly one. The indeterminacy in the case is therefore epistemic rather than metaphysical. Because the self is not a further constituent of the world, there is no fact of the matter about which of the two stories is the true one; there is only a question about which is more useful to whom.

[5] It follows that the three accounts agree on the case. Reductionism says the same thing in its own vocabulary. AN, if it were accepted, would simply redescribe the same situation from the outside, choosing the other center of gravity and calling it the self; the difference between it and Dennett's view is verbal, a matter of which standpoint one prefers. N. has one self, and the interesting questions in the case are practical ones about which narrative it is better to work with.
