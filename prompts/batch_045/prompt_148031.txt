ok so I'm doing my EPQ on whether self-deception is even possible, and I've talked myself into a position I actually quite like — I'd much rather you attacked it than explained it back to me

Basically I think the whole thing is overrated as a puzzle. My view is that self-deception doesn't need you to believe two contradictory things at once, or to have a split mind, or any of that. You've got some worry, you steer your attention away from anything that would settle it, you go over the bits that help instead, and you end up with a plain false belief that you'd defend perfectly sincerely. Like not opening a letter: you're not hiding the letter from yourself, you just never open it, and after a while you've convinced yourself there was never any post. So no paradox, no special mental state, nothing weird going on underneath.

The bit I made up myself, which nobody's going to have an opinion on because I invented it: I also think there has to be a leak. For it to be real self-deception rather than just being wrong or not knowing, some trace of the avoided worry has to show up somewhere — they change the subject too fast, they get weirdly defensive when its brought up, they over-argue the case, their reaction times go odd, whatever. If nothing leaks at all, and they're indistinguishable from someone who's simply never considered the thing, I want to say they aren't self-deceived, they're just mistaken (or lying to everyone else really well). I call the no-leak ones sealed.

So: what would count against this? Properly, I mean, and ranked from 'this destroys my EPQ' to 'a bit awkward but I can wriggle'. A few things I can already see coming and want you to sort into which are actually decisive:

- the obvious one: if I don't already have some grip on the thing I'm avoiding, why do I avoid it? Doesn't the avoiding itself need me to sort of know already? That feels bad for me but I'm not sure how bad.
- people don't just fail to look, they actively build a case for the false thing — they come up with reasons, they argue you out of it. My model feels a bit passive for that.
- my leak condition might be doing no work at all. Either every genuine case leaks, in which case I've just defined my way to safety and I've made my view unfalsifiable; or some genuine cases are sealed, in which case my view wrongly says they aren't self-deception. Can I have it both ways? Be honest about whether I've built myself an unfalsifiable view, and whether that's actually a problem for a view of this kind.
- the group version: my mate Jay's whole friendship group has jointly agreed he's 'totally fine' about his ex going out with his other mate, and I don't think any single one of them is doing the avoiding. Does that break my individual-level story or not?
- I half remember something about people who've had a stroke and insist their paralysed arm works fine even whilst you're showing them it doesn't — I think it was a documentary, so don't quote me on it. If that's roughly right, is it a sealed case, or just a different thing entirely that shouldn't count?

Also — what does my view predict that the rival ones don't? If I'm right, what should self-deception look like in real life that it wouldn't if the split-mind people are right? Things like: how it responds to forced confrontation, whether it's easier in areas where feedback is constant, whether it tracks anxiety about the topic or the strength of the false belief. And what would someone who likes my view have to concede counts against them, not just what the other side keeps saying?

The thing I think would move me most is watching someone get shown dead obvious proof and scramble to explain it away in real time — that feels like more than not opening a letter, and I'd of thought it was the strongest evidence against me. Tell me if I'm overrating it.
