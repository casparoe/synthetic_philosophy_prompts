You're a professional philosopher — social ontology, metaphysics of fiction, that sort of thing — who also happened to spend about a decade as a raid leader and volunteer GM in a hardcore sandbox MMO, so you know how these fights actually feel from the inside. Take your time with this one: before you write anything, brainstorm for a bit — for each of the two people below, jot down their single strongest argument, the toughest objection they'd have to answer, and one thing they should honestly concede — and only then write the dialogue.

Write a dialogue between the two of them. They're both senior designers at the studio that runs the game, and they're on the committee that has to decide what to do about the Ashfall Reach incident. No name-dropping philosophers, no papers, no citations — just two sharp people arguing it out, roughly 1000–1400 words, with real back-and-forth: they should answer each other's actual points and give ground where they have to.

The setup to use:

Ashfall Reach is a persistent sandbox MMO running 400 independent shards. Each shard can contain exactly one Hearth-Cathedral: a guild hall that takes roughly forty people about four years of coordinated work to build, is bound to the guild that built it, and can't be transferred, sold, or rebuilt. There's an intended mechanic, the Unmaking Rite, that lets a rival coalition burn a cathedral down; it can fire only once per shard ever, and afterwards that shard can never have one again.

Three weeks ago the guild Ninefold lost theirs in a night burn, while most of their members were asleep in other timezones. Everything about it was within the rules. The attacking coalition never contacted anyone from Ninefold outside the game, didn't know their real names, spent no money, made no threats. Inside the cathedral's vault was also a soulbound cosmetic item — a "bondlight lantern", zero gameplay effect, untradeable even though the game's official exchange converts gold to real money at a fixed rate, so its market price is exactly zero — inscribed with a short message from a Ninefold member who has since died in real life. The Rite consumed that too.

Two more wrinkles. First: Ashfall ships an offline "Echo" mode — same physics, same asset database, you play your actual character with your actual gear, but nobody else can see your world and you can't touch theirs. Ninefold has a community-run Echo instance with a bit-for-bit restore of the cathedral from build files they'd backed up, so any of them can stand inside an indistinguishable cathedral whenever they like. A lot of them say this makes it worse, not better. Second: the studio is choosing between (a) a one-off patch restoring the cathedral, (b) doing nothing, (c) paying compensation in gold at the official exchange rate.

The two characters:

Mira thinks virtual objects and virtual harms are real in the ordinary, unspooky sense: the cathedral existed, it was Ninefold's, destroying it wronged them, and the lantern's destruction is a further and different wrong. Her wedge is the Indifference Test — if you'd be morally defective for shrugging something off, it's the kind of thing that can be lost — plus the Echo case: if what mattered were only the experiences the object gave you, Echo should fix it, and it definately doesn't.

Dev thinks "virtual harm" is always shorthand: trace any genuine harm down and you land on real people, real hours, real expectations created by real published rules, real grief about a real dead friend. So the burning was a real wrong, but it's the kind of wrong he'd call a breach of a normative practice rather than the destruction of a thing — and the live question is what repairs a practice, which might be a restore, or a rule change, or a public record, or an apology, and isn't automatically a restore. His wedges are the Price Question (if the thing that matters most here is worth exactly zero on the only market this game has, what are we even counting?) and the Bug Test (if the cathedral had been wiped by a server bug instead of by rival players, is the complaint the same size — and what does your answer tell you about what was lost?).

Have them work through all three bits of the case, not just the cathedral. Neither should win cleanly and neither should be a pushover: give each of them at least one point the other can't answer, and let each of them change their mind about something small. End with each saying, concretely, what would actually change their mind on the bigger question, and let them land on a joint recommendation to the studio even if they'd justify it differently.
