I am trying to put a number on a structural claim about commitment races, and I want the number decomposed rather than defended in prose.

The setup. Two parties contest a prize; each values exclusive control at 1, and either may at any time concede, in which case the conceder gets 0 and the other gets 1. Separately, at any real time t ≥ 0, either party may lock: install a public, irreversible, verifiable commitment to accept no settlement giving them less than a demand d, with d chosen at the moment of locking and d ∈ [0, 1]. Locking costs each party the same small c > 0 and cannot be revised or undone. Outcomes once the dust settles: if exactly one party is locked at d, the other concedes and the locked party gets d − c (conceding, worth 0, is assumed better for the unlocked party than fighting, worth −k with k > 0); if both are locked and d_A + d_B ≤ 1, each gets d_i − c and any surplus is discarded; if both are locked and d_A + d_B > 1, the demands are incompatible and the dispute escalates, each getting −k. There is no discounting and no cost to delay other than the risk of being preempted. No enforceable pre-play agreement is available — that is the point of the question — though non-binding communication is. Assume complete information, common knowledge of rationality, and exact symmetry in costs, speed of installation, and access.

Note the two-dimensional structure, since I am not certain I am carving it correctly: the timing dimension looks like a preemption game, where each wants to lock an instant before the other and there is no earliest instant at which to do it; the demand dimension looks like a game of chicken, since a greedy lock wins big against an unlocked opponent and is catastrophic against another greedy lock, while a moderate lock is safe only if the other is also moderate. The interesting question is what these two dimensions do to each other.

The claim. Call a settlement with no commitment technology available to either party the baseline, and stipulate that it gives each 1/2. The claim I want a credence on is this: under symmetric access to locking, the preemption rent is competed away entirely, so that each party's expected payoff is at most the baseline 1/2 minus the deadweight cost of the devices and minus the expected cost of conflict on simultaneous locking — that is, the power to bind oneself is worth a great deal to whoever holds it exclusively and is worth nothing, or less than nothing, when it is held symmetrically. Equally important is the branch in which this question has no determinate answer: if the continuous-time game has no equilibrium in the relevant class and the fine-grid limit fails to converge, say so and assign that branch a probability rather than declining the number.

I would count the question as resolved by a rigorous treatment of the specified game — existence and characterization of an equilibrium, or a clean non-existence result plus a characterization of the discrete-time equilibria as the period length shrinks and of what happens to the tie probability and to each player's value in that limit — together with agreement that the specification captures the phenomenon. Absent that, I want a credence.

So: give me your probability that the claim is true. Then the decomposition. State in one line which solution concept or limiting argument you are reading the claim under — I do not want it depends on the equilibrium notion as a stopping point; pick one and make the sensitivity to that choice a term in the decomposition. Assign a rough probability and a one-line justification to each of the following, and add any term you think belongs:

whether the game has a determinate solution at all under that reading, or whether the no-earliest-instant problem makes it ill-posed; whether simultaneous locking retains probability bounded away from zero in the fine-grid limit, so that the conflict branch is real rather than measure-zero; whether, conditional on no simultaneous locking, the preemption rent is fully dissipated or the winner still captures something close to 1 − c; whether moderately demanding locks are selected often enough to matter, given that a moderate lock is only safe if the opponent is also moderate and is strictly dominated if the opponent locks greedily; whether the technology that permits a lock also permits a restraint lock — an irreversible commitment not to lock, or not to lock above some ceiling — and whether that generates a second race running in the opposite direction which terminates in a stable outcome or regresses; whether the result is knife-edge in exact symmetry, or survives small asymmetries in cost, speed of installation, or patience; and, if installation is visible in progress with a completion lag, giving the second mover a window in which to respond, whether the race stabilizes or inverts.

Then the combination, with a note on whether these terms multiply, screen off one another, or are partly redundant; which single sub-question, if it went the other way, would move the final number most; and which term you are least confident in, together with the argument or result that would most change it.
