So I'm in this tiny reading group — four of us, meeting in the back of a pub — and we've been chewing on Parfit's hitchhiker for about a year now and I think we've officially stalled. Quick version in case it helps: you're dying in the desert, a driver stops and offers to take you to town in exchange for $1000 once you get there, the driver is an excellent judge of character and only stops for people they predict will actually pay, and once you're in town paying costs you $1000 and gets you nothing. Everyone in the group agrees the hitchhiker should pay if they can somehow be the kind of person who pays; the fight is about whether it's rational to pay once you're already in town, whether rationality even permits trying to become that kind of person, and whether "rational irrationality" names a real phenomenon or is basically a pun. Nobody has moved since roughly February and it's starting to feel like we're just restating ourselves in slightly different words.

Two wrinkles I keep throwing in that nobody can settle, and I'd like you to actually grapple with them instead of politely ignoring them. First, the variant we've started calling the noisy tell: suppose the driver doesn't read your character directly, but reads some behavioural signal — a microexpression, whatever — that correlates with your disposition with reliability r, somewhere strictly between chance and perfection. My intuition is that there's a threshold r* below which the whole rational-irrationality manoeuvre collapses, because the expected return from cultivating the disposition stops covering the cost of the times you end up actually having to pay, but I can't work out whether where that threshold sits is a substantive fact about the case or just an artifact of how you're slicing up "rational". Second, the legibility constraint: I don't think we can simply decide, on the spot, to be the sort of person who pays, and I genuinely don't know whether that's a psychological fact about us, a fact about which dispositions are acquirable at will at all, or something smuggled in by the setup. It feels like an empirical question that the debate keeps treating as either settled or irrelevant.

My working theory is that the two camps are answering different questions under one word — one about what you ought to do at the moment of action given that you're in town already, one about which dispositions or decision procedures it would be rational to install in advance — and that once you pry those apart there's nothing left to argue about. But I definately could be wrong: maybe there's a real substantive disagreement underneath about whether rationality is the kind of thing that can be self-effacing or self-undermining, or a hidden question about what prediction and legibility even amount to. Give me a diagnosis rather than a verdict, and ground it in what the sides here actually say — the CDT-ish people, the EDT-ish people, the people who say the rational choice is just the output of whichever decision procedure you'd have wanted to be running, and the Kavka's-toxin parallel. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a formal result, a distinction, a psychology experiment, a change of question — or tell me honestly that nothing would, and why.
