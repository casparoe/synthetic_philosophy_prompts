I'm a high school student in a philosophy of mind elective, and I'm trying to get clearer on the Malcolm–Dennett debate about whether dreams are experiences. My rough understanding is that Malcolm argued dreams are not experiences at all, because the only thing we ever really have to go on is the waking report rather than some inner show taking place during sleep. Dennett replied with the cassette theory: as I understood it, the brain composes a dream narrative during sleep, but the experience of the dream happens only later, when that narrative is played back at or just after awakening, so dreams are experiences even though they are not experiences one has while in the middle of sleep. What I want to ask about is a real practice that seems to take sides on exactly this. Sleep medicine treats recurrent frightening dreams as a clinical problem, sometimes called nightmare disorder, and a common non-drug treatment (I think it is called imagery rehearsal therapy) works like this: while fully awake, the patient recalls a repeated nightmare, writes it down or holds it in mind, changes the ending or some other feature so the story is no longer threatening, and then rehearses this rescripted version in imagination for a few minutes each day. To decide whether the treatment worked, the clinic relies on the patient's own morning reports: each day the patient records whether the nightmare occurred and how frightening or distressing it was, and the frequency and emotional intensity of those reported nightmares are supposed to decline; similar morning reports are also used to diagnose the problem initially and to justify calling it a disorder of sleep. What puzzles me is that the clinic classifies this as a sleep disorder and talks as if the target is something that happens during sleep, even though all of the evidence is waking reports and the therapy itself is performed while the patient is awake, and on Dennett's cassette theory the experience of the dream looks like something that happens at awakening rather than during sleep.

My question is what philosophical commitments about dreams, and about the relation between a waking report and a dream, this whole practice presupposes. Does it strictly presuppose that dreams are experiences, either frightening experiences during sleep or, on Dennett's cassette theory, experiences at the moment of awakening? Or could Malcolm's view that dreams are not experiences also underwrite the practice, perhaps by reinterpreting the therapy as changing a waking imaginative or linguistic habit rather than altering a nighttime experience? I'd like the answer to separate the parts of the practice that really force a commitment from the parts that fit several theories equally well: for instance, does diagnosing a sleep disorder from recalled content commit one to more than using awake rescripting to change future dreams does, and does measuring success by later reports commit one to something else again? If a clinic explicitly adopted Malcolm's denial or Dennett's cassette theory, what would have to change in the diagnosis, the therapy, the outcome measures, or merely in the philosophical story told about the same procedures? Please also tell me where the practice is underdetermined, so that rival theories of dreaming would license the identical clinical routine.

Finally, please be explicit about how confident I should be in the reconstruction, and mark which claims are forced by the practice itself and which are only one interpretation among others. Please push back plainly if my thumbnail sketch of Malcolm or Dennett is wrong, or if the question rests on a confused premise—for example, if the clinical routine is actually neutral on whether dreams are experiences and I have manufactured a tension that isn't there. I would much rather be told that my framing is mistaken than be given a polite answer that accepts it without challenge.
