hey — I'm in a phil of science seminar this term (mostly philosophy of physics and cosmology stuff) and I keep bumping into the same analogy and I cant tell whether its doing real work or just sounding persuasive. The analogy is the anthropic one: finding that our universe is fine-tuned for life is like a lottery winner being astonished that they won. Of course the winner is surprised, but from outside the draw there's nothing to explain, because somebody had to win and you only ever hear about it from the person who did. In seminar it gets used to license two things: first, that the fine-tuning of the constants is no longer evidence for anything beyond a multiverse (design, a deeper theory, whatever), and second, more strongly, that the multiverse is actually confirmed by the single fact that we find ourselves here — that self-locating evidence from a sample of one is enough, so we dont have to sit around waiting for a direct empirical test like bubble-collision signatures in the CMB. Our TA says the analogy is carrying both conclusions and that the only reason it feels compelling is that it sneaks in a well-behaved probability distribution over universes that nobody actually has.

Our prof then added a homebrew constraint he calls the ticket-count test: an anthropic explanation is allowed to move your credence only if, for any two candidate universes in the ensemble, you can say how many observers each contains — actual finite numbers, no infinities, no arbitrary cutoff on spacetime volume, no hand-waving about regulators. His claim is that every version of the analogy we've looked at flunks this once inflation makes the ensemble infinite, and that the two popular ways of fixing it go bad in opposite directions: one gives you a measure on which almost all observers are Boltzmann brains, the other gives you something like a youngness paradox where a typical observer should be absurdly early. I half buy it, but I'm not sure the test is fair — maybe it demands something no probabilistic explanation could ever deliver, in which case it isnt really a constraint on anthropic reasoning, it's just skepticism about infinity wearing a lab coat.

So can you audit the analogy properly? I want the correspondence table — what plays the role of the lottery commission, the tickets, the draw, the announcement that there was a winner, the reference class, the you — and then the disanalogies, sorted into the ones that matter for those two conclusions and the ones that are noise. Concretely: is the selection here a random assignment of an already-existing person to a universe, which is what a lottery models, or is it more like the universe causing the observer, and does that difference do any work? Does the picture smuggle in that a winner's existence was guaranteed, or that you could have turned out to be any of the observers in the ensemble rather than necessarily this one? And what exactly does it assume about you being typical? Separate but related: one of our readings argues that string theory gets confirmed non-empirically partly because decades of trying havent produced a genuine alternative, and someone in seminar said that's just the lottery move again — confirmation from an absence, whether it's the absence of surprise or the absence of rivals. Are those really the same argument in different clothes, or do they stand and fall independently? And if the lottery picture is beyond repair when the ensemble is infinite, is there a better analogy that does the same job (urn models, mark-and-recapture, the German tank problem), or is the honest answer that anthropic confirmation has no good everyday analogue and that this very fact is a reason for suspicion? I'm most likely to be moved by whatever tells me which step of the argument actually breaks if the measure problem never gets solved.
