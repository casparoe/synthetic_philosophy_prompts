Hey — I'm a CS PhD student (multi-agent RL, drifting steadily into decision theory and not really coming back) and our reading group is doing a session on cooperation in one-shot games: the setup where instead of picking an action, each player submits a program that gets the other player's source as input and outputs cooperate or defect, so that mutual cooperation becomes available in the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma. I've been asked to circulate a one-page "here's where I stand" beforehand so people have something concrete to attack, and I'd much rather find the holes now than in front of nine people who've all read more proof theory than me. Here's roughly the package:

(1) I think the program-equilibrium move is a genuine answer to the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, not a relabeling trick: submitting a program that reads the other's source and cooperates iff the other program cooperates with me is available, it's rational, and the usual "rationality requires defection" conclusion is an artifact of modelling agents as choosing actions rather than policies. I'm also unbothered that mutual defection is an equilibrium too — I pick among equilibria by Pareto.

(2) I don't count the identity-matching version as the real thing. A program that cooperates only with a character-for-character copy of itself is doing source comparison, not conditional cooperation. What I want is cooperation with functional equivalents: renamed variables, reordered branches, dead code tacked on, whatever.

(3) And I want to not be a sucker: my program should defect against anything that defects against it, and unconditional cooperation is straightforwardly irrational.

(4) My route to (2) is the proof-based one — cooperate iff you can prove, in some fixed formal system, that the other program cooperates with you. I know there's a Löb/self-reference wrinkle here and I've heard it described both ways: that self-reference is what makes two proof-searching programs able to cooperate at all, and that it makes cooperation brittle because it depends delicately on which proof system each side uses and how much meta-level trust you allow. I've never followed the argument properly either way, and I'm currently treating it as a technical complication rather than an objection.

(5) I think transparency is doing all the work: if the players can't actually read each other's source — say they only get a hash, or an obfuscated binary, or a k-bit summary — then all of this collapses back into the ordinary Prisoner's Dilemma, and I don't believe there's any residual "acausal" cooperation between agents who can't inspect each other.

(6) I want there to be, at least in principle, a best program to submit — maximally cooperative subject to being unexploitable — and I'd like to believe the differences between candidate "nice" programs are just engineering detail.

(7) But someone at a workshop sketched me a diagonal argument I can't shake: for any program P you can build a "bully" B_P that simulates P on B_P's own source (via the usual quining trick) and cooperates exactly when P defects against B_P, so that either P gets exploited or P defects against a program that was willing to cooperate with it — hence nothing is best and (6) is dead. I've never seen it written down, and I half think its cheating: it seems to lean on unbounded simulation time, and on the opponent being built after seeing P, which isn't how simultaneous submission is supposed to work.

Can you audit this before I send it round? I want the tensions, ranked by how bad they actually are, with what would have to give in each case and what that would cost me — and I want you to flag separately anything that only looks like a conflict but dissolves when you spell it out, because I don't want to spend the session defending a position against an objection that isn't real. Specifically: does (7) really kill (6), or does it stop biting once you bound runtime, or bound source length, or require both programs to come from the same design process — and if (6) survives only in some restricted form, does it still deliver what (1) wants? Do (2) and (3) really pull against each other, or can one program have both? Is (5) doing any work inside the package or is it just an independently plausible thing I've bolted on? And is my (4) even coherent with (2), given that proof search is a syntactic procedure and syntactic variants are exactly what (2) says I should see through? Please don't just tell me which commitment to drop — give me the map and the cheapest fixes, and I'll decide which one hurts least.
