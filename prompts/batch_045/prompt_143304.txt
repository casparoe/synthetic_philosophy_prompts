I work at a twelve person software shop, and somehow I've ended up as the keeper of the decision log. Every time we make a call that costs more than about a week of work — rewrite or patch, migrate or leave it alone, build or buy — I write down what we decided and the chain of reasons we gave. Then on Monday mornings I run a forty five minute meeting where we go through the open entries; the room is two seniors, a bunch of juniors with no theory background at all, and the founder, who gets visibly impatient when I keep asking "why" one more time. I'm the one who signs off. So I need a procedure I can actually run in that meeting, not an essay about one decision.

Here's where I got stuck. I started reading about the old regress problem on my own a few weeks ago — the thing where if every belief needs a reason, you either go back forever, or you go in a circle, or you just declare some beliefs to be basic and stop. As far as I can tell the three serious answers are: foundationalism, that you eventually hit bedrock claims that don't need support from anything else; coherentism, that nothing is bedrock and a claim is fine when it fits tightly enough with everything else you hold; and the third one, infinitism I think it's called, which says a chain of reasons is only genuinely justified if it never stops and never repeats itself, so every finite chain is strictly speaking unfinished. I find all three half convincing and none of them usable as they stand, so I want to turn them into a checklist my team can run without knowing any of this.

A few half baked ideas of my own that I want you to either use or tell me are junk. First: count kinds of support, not links. A log line, a measurement, a person's testimony, a general pattern ("this has always worked before"), a definition or stipulation, or just "it hangs together with the rest of what we believe." My thought is that if by the third level you're reusing a kind of support you already used — measurement, then the tool that produced the measurement, then a second reading from the same tool — you're not going deeper, you're going in a circle, and a circle should score as zero extra justification no matter how many links long it is. Second: I want to distinguish closing a chain from sealing it. We can close a chain and act on it, but never seal it; every closed entry should carry one recorded reopen condition (the specific thing that, if it happened, means we look again) and one named person with standing to reopen it. That's my attempt to steal what's right about the "never finished" view without actually doing infinite work. Third: a depth budget. Three levels by default, four if the decision is hard to undo, and the depth gets spent where the room disagrees most rather than where it's easiest to dig. Fourth: a separate gate where I ask the person pushing the claim what would change their mind, and if they can't answer, it fails no matter how good the chain looked — my worry is that this one is just vibes dressed up as a test.

So: give me the procedure itself, in the order I'd run it in a Monday meeting. The questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it says no and stops, where it hands the decision to a person because that question is about how much risk or cost we're willing to wear rather than about facts, and how deep it's allowed to go given forty five minutes and four or five items. Tell me which of my four ideas is actually load bearing and which I should drop. Be honest about where a coherent looking web of beliefs can be internally tight and still detached from anything real, and about where "we just treat this as bedrock" is really a social fact about our team rather than a fact about the world.

Then run it on three real shaped cases from my log. One: "we should do the checkout rewrite mobile first, because seventy percent of abandoned carts are on mobile," where the whole thing comes from our analytics dashboard, which is fed by tracking code that one of our own juniors wrote. Two: "we can retire the nightly backup verification job, the provider's snapshots have never once failed us." Three, the one I'm dreading: the founder wants to delay a security patch that'll cause an outage, and his reason is that our biggest customer is happy and won't mind, because he had lunch with their CTO and the CTO said so. Yes, that's his entire chain, and yes, he is going to be annoyed if the procedure comes back no — so tell me straight what your procedure says there even if the answer is unwelcome, and equally tell me if it lets it through and on what step, because I'd want to know that too.

Finally, say plainly what the procedure can't do: which of these questions it can't settle, and which hard cases need me as a person rather than the checklist.

One writing thing: please reply as continuous prose, like a long email. No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. I read these on my phone before the meeting and formatting just falls apart.
