I'm a PhD student in ancient philosophy, and the chapter I'm currently drafting asks how far the Epicurean argument that death is not bad for the person who dies can be carried beyond the case it was written for. Rather than testing it against contemporary deprivationist theories, I want to put Epicurus himself in front of three situations that Hellenistic medicine could not produce, and that therefore never entered his therapy of the fear of death:

1. Temporary suspensions of the subject — general anaesthesia, deep hypothermic circulatory arrest with successful resuscitation, reversible coma lasting months.
2. The indefinite maintenance of a living human organism after the destruction of whatever supports experience — brain death, and the persistent vegetative state, with ventilation and artificial nutrition continued for years.
3. The gradual pre-mortem dissolution of the psychological subject in advanced dementia, where there is no sharp boundary between the person's being there and not being there.

Take his commitments as I will stipulate them for the exercise, so that we are reasoning from the same starting point:

- The soul is a material compound that is dispersed at death; there is no survival, no post-mortem subject, and no post-mortem punishment.
- The good for a person is a state of a subject — freedom from pain, and from disturbance of the soul. Nothing can be good or bad for a thing at a time at which that thing does not exist.
- Death is nothing to us, argued in two steps: while we are, death is not present, and when death is present, we are not; and what has been dissolved has no sensation, and what has no sensation is nothing to us.
- The symmetry argument we find in Lucretius: the eternity before our birth harms us not at all, and the eternity after death is of the same kind.
- The therapeutic aim: the fear of death, not death itself, is the present evil. Correct reasoning about death is supposed to dissolve that fear and thereby restore tranquility, and a therapy that fails to calm anyone is not doing its job.

What I would like from you is a verdict on each of the following five questions, naming in each case which of the commitments above is doing the work, and how much of your answer is genuinely forced by those commitments as opposed to being a plausible reconstruction.

First, the interval problem. If the argument works because there is no subject at the time in question, it should generalise to any interval in which there is no subject — which would make a year in reversible coma, or an hour of clinical death followed by resuscitation, equally nothing to the patient. That seems to be the wrong result, and it is the wrong result in a way Epicurus has the resources to notice, since the person who wakes up is in a position to be deprived. So: would he accept the generalisation and the result, or does he need to restrict the conclusion to final absences? If he restricts it, what is actually carrying the argument — the absence of a subject, or the finality of the absence? And if finality is doing the work, what is left of the two-step argument, which appears to mention only presence and absence?

Second, the criterion of death. In cases of brain death and the persistent vegetative state, the subject is gone while the organism is maintained. Does his argument require a higher-brain, whole-brain, or organismic criterion of death, and can that requirement be derived from his materialist psychology rather than imported from modern bioethics? Concretely: would he say that everything that happens to a ventilated body after the loss of the capacity for experience happens to no one at all, and if so, is there anything in his position that explains why we should have stopped?

Third, the vagueness wrinkle. The two-step argument seems to quantify over times at which the person either is or is not there. Advanced dementia makes the subject's presence a matter of degree with no determinate boundary. Does the argument presuppose that there is always a fact of the matter about whether the subject exists at a given time, and if that presupposition fails, can the argument be reformulated — or is dementia simply outside its scope, and if so is that an idle limitation or a damaging one?

Fourth, the therapy. He allows that while death is nothing, the fear of it is a present disturbance, and his therapy targets the fear. Does that apparatus transfer to the fear of being kept alive for years in a state in which one is no longer there, or to the fear of one's own gradual dissolution in dementia? There is an awkward asymmetry here that I want you to press on: the first fear has an object that, on his own view, one cannot be in a position to undergo, so it is not obvious that it is even coherent as a fear, while the second has an object one may well undergo while still a subject, and the prospect of undergoing it is a present evil. What, on his principles, is the correct emotional posture toward each, and does the asymmetry tell against the therapy's claim to generality?

Fifth, and this is the part I most want flagged rather than smoothed over: where does the extrapolation outrun the texts? For each of your answers, say whether it follows from commitments he states, or requires a premise he never formulates and might have resisted when presented with it. In particular I would like to know whether any of these cases forces him toward something like a deprivation account of harm, which on the commitments above he should reject outright.

Please reason from the commitments as I have stated them rather than from secondary literature; I am interested in the argumentative structure, not in who has defended what. Where you think Epicurus himself would have been genuinely torn, say so and explain what the competing pulls are.
