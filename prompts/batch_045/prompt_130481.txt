Hi — please take this as a philosopher of science who has also spent a lot of time around how archaeologists actually argue from objects (put on both hats, or argue with yourself, I don't mind which). Think carefully and don't rush it: before you write anything, I'd like you to do some open brainstormin — say, a page of notes to yourself listing the distinct ways an inference from a thing someone left behind to a belief or an institution can come apart — and only after that start assembling the procedure. I want to see the reasoning that produced the checklist, not just the checklist.

Bit of background so you know who you're talking to. I'm retired: thirty-five years as a hydrologist and geochemist, mostly sediment transport and a lot of arguing about how much you can read off a core sample. Two years ago I started sitting in on the philosophy seminars at the university nearby, and I've got hooked. I also volunteer two days a week at our small regional museum, and somehow I've ended up responsible for rewriting the display text for the local Iron Age material. That means I'm in the middle of the same fight every month: someone wants a label that says the people who made this object believed X, or that there was an institution of Y, and I can't tell whether that's warranted or just a good story. I have real statistics and measurement theory, and almost no philosophy vocabulary, so keep it plain.

What I want from you is a written decision procedure — a flowchart in words — that my little team can actually run. Constraints: four volunteers, none with archaeological training, no library access, about half an hour per claim, and whatever we write has to be signed off by our curator, who is an art historian and will kill anything that reads like jargon. The claims we get look like: this cache of deliberately broken disks means they believed in household-protecting spirits; this hoard shows a priest class; these standardized weights show there was a regulated market.

Here's my half-formed idea for the spine, which you should feel free to tear apart. In my old field we tracked error propagation: every inference step multiplies uncertainty, and you report a result with its propagated uncertainty rather than a bare yes/no. So I was thinking of the chain as object → depositional history → action that produced it → repeated practice → shared norm → specific belief content → institution with authority, and having the procedure output something like: the material supports this claim up to step four and no further. Is that a useful frame, or does it distort things — maybe the steps aren't a chain but a web, or maybe some steps are actually the secure ones, or maybe the step from practice to norm isn't a step at all. Say so if it's wrong.

What I need in the procedure itself: the questions in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each (with what counts as *no answer available*, since we often just won't know); where the procedure stops with a hard no; and where it has to hand the decision to a person because it's a values call, not an evidence call. Tell me honestly which distinctions have to survive the simplification for volunteers and which ones I should drop as too fine-grained for our purposes, and where the procedure will systematically misfire.

Then run it, in some detail, on three of our actual cases — I've renamed the sites, but treat them as real:

1. Twelve fired-clay disks, all from the same batch, each deliberately snapped in half, buried in a shallow pit under the doorway of a house that later burnt down. The label currently says they were a charm to protect the household.
2. About two hundred unused socketed bronze axeheads, most apparently from three molds, recovered from a peat bog roughly two kilometres from the nearest known settlement, with no other material nearby. Somebody here wants to say they were offerings to a water deity.
3. My own pet theory, so please be blunt with me. Forty-one ground stone weights from a thirty-kilometre stretch of river valley, spanning maybe three centuries, and the masses cluster tightly — within about three per cent of two or three values — and they turn up in ordinary domestic contexts, not in anything that looks official. I want to write on the label that there must have been an institution that maintained the standard. I suspect the answer is no, and that mutual copying or a single workshop would produce the same pattern, and I'd rather be told that now than in a letter to the local paper.

Finally: what can't the procedure do? If the answer is that it can't settle whether we're allowed to reason from much later farming practice in the same valley to what these objects meant, tell me that's a hole and say what we should do instead — put a question mark on the label, hedge in one clause, or leave the meaning out entirely.

Length: something I can turn into a two-page sheet for the volunteers plus a page of rationale for the curator. Thanks.
