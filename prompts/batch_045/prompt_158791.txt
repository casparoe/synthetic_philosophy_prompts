I'm TA-ing an undergrad seminar on Mill's harm principle and the limits of free speech, and I put together this argument to torture my students, but now it's torturing me. It seems to show that Mill's own framework either let's the state ban basically every controversial newspaper opinion, or that his famous distinction between the newspaper case and the mob-placard case is totally ad hoc. I can't accept either conclusion: I'd really like to keep (a) the harm principle as Mill states it, and (b) his distinction between publishing an opinion in a newspaper versus addressing the same opinion to an excited mob outside a grain merchant's house (or handing out a placard saying it to that mob), where he thinks the latter can be justly punished but the former must be free.

Here's the argument I came up with:

(1) Mill's harm principle says the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over someone, against their will, is to prevent harm to others; a person's own good is not enough.
(2) Mill applies this to expression. He holds that circulating the opinion that grain merchants are starving the poor in a newspaper is to be unmolested, but that delivering that very opinion orally to an excited mob assembled outside a grain merchant's house, or handing it out among that mob as a placard, can justly be punished.
(3) The opinion expressed in the two cases is the same proposition, and lets assume the speaker's ultimate aim is also the same: to get others to believe the opinion. So the difference in treatment can't be a difference in the content of the opinion or in the bare intent to persuade an audience of it.
(4) So the only relevant difference must be causal: in the mob case the act of expression is likely to cause harm to others (the crowd may attack the merchant or his property), whereas in the newspaper case it is not (or only very remotely and indirectly).
(5) But for just about any controversial opinion, it is foreseeable that some readers or hearers will later be influenced by it and perform harmful actions they wouldn't have done otherwise. For example, publishing arguments that vaccines are unsafe foreseeably leads some readers to refuse vaccination, which contributes to harm.
(6) Therefore, by the very same causal standard, publishing any controversial opinion in a newspaper also foreseeably causes harm through the subsequent voluntary actions of others.
(7) So if causal contribution to harm is what justifies punishing the mob speech, the harm principle also permits punishing the newspaper publication of that opinion—and, by extension, virtually all controversial political speech.
(8) Mill obviously does not think the harm principle lets the state suppress ordinary newspaper opinion; he defends liberty of the press and discussion almost absolutely.
(9) So it looks like Mill's harm principle plus his own mob example either proves far too much, licensing the censorship of almost all controversial speech, or the newspaper/mob distinction is an ad hoc exception with no basis in the principle itself.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I feel like something slipped between (4) and (7), but I can't name it. Please name the exact step you'd reject, explain why that step looked innocent, and price the alternatives: what does a Millian have to give up (or add) to block the slide to (7) while still keeping both the harm principle and the newspaper-vs-mob distinction?
