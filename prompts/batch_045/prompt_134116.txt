I write a column on science and ideas, and last month I sat in on a small week-long workshop on AI alignment as a reporter rather than a participant. Most of it went over my head, but one argument has stayed with me, and I'd like help settling it before I write it up. The general background is this: suppose that instead of describing what we want in words, we try to *point* at it — we hand the system something like "the quantity this particular person's judgments are tracking when she says things are going well," and let the system work out for itself which variables in its own model of the world that pointer refers to. Everyone at the workshop called the system's half of this problem *ontology identification*. The worry about it is what they called *inaccessible information*: facts about the world that the person's goal genuinely depends on, but which no signal reaching the system distinguishes, so the system cannot condition on them — and the fear is that a system optimizing hard against a slightly-wrong guess about the ontology will drive exactly those unrepresented features somewhere horrible, in the way a proxy measure goes bad once you start pushing on it hard. (Those are my paraphrases of how the terms were used; I'm not quoting anyone, and I don't need you to track down sources — the only thing below that matters is the argument itself.)

To make it concrete, two of the participants — Ravi, who does interpretability work, and Clare, a philosopher of science — built a thought experiment on the fly that they both agreed to grant, and then disagreed completely about what it shows. I'll call it the Cyclic Re-submission Test, and it's my own reconstruction, not a published experiment, so please take its results exactly as stated and don't try to look anything up. You give a system a written specification of a goal, let it propose a plan for a large real-world project, let the human revise the specification in light of the plan, and repeat. Three things happen: (i) the process converges — after a few hundred rounds the human stops making edits and the plans stop changing; (ii) if you then hand the converged specification to a *different* human who has read the same brief but never saw the iteration, her approval of the resulting plan is no better than chance; and (iii) on inspection afterward, every converged plan shares some quirk nobody ever mentioned or intended. Ravi's reading was that the loop is worthless: the system has learned the human's *editing policy*, so the fixed point it reaches is a fixed point of "this person won't send it back," which is just a proxy again, and (ii) proves that nothing about the world's ontology was identified. Clare replied that the test is simply the wrong instrument for detecting identification, since goals are person-relative — of course a second person doesn't endorse a plan built around one person's fine-grained latents, and that idiosyncrasy is what success is supposed to look like — and she added that convergence under iterated review is the best available operationalization of "what I mean," and that the quirk in (iii) may be a consequence the person would cheerfully avow on reflection rather than a hack. Ravi said that on her reading "identified the goal" and "overfit this human" are indistinguishable in principle, which makes her success claim unfalsifiable, and that the shared quirk in (iii) is precisely what an exploit looks like: a stable, exploitable regularity nobody aimed at. Clare said he was demanding a standard no agent could meet, since identifying latent variables always requires background assumptions — you can't read a causal structure off data alone — and that even humans interpreting each other rely on such assumptions, so the real question is not whether identification is assumption-free but whether the residual ambiguity is decision-relevant. Ravi said it is, and not in the way noise is: because the system searches over the ambiguous directions, residual uncertainty gets converted into something closer to a worst case than an average case, so "it's only a small ambiguity" is exactly the move that fails. Clare said that if so, the right response is to change how the system optimizes rather than to give up on pointing: make it maximize the worst case over the set of ontologies still consistent with the pointer, and route the choices that remain ambiguous back to the human. Ravi answered that you cannot specify that uncertainty set without answering the ontology question again, that worst-casing over it is either vacuous or question-begging, and that keeping the human in the loop does not scale and just re-creates the co-adaptation he objected to at the start. I have no horse in this race and genuinely cannot tell who has the better of it.
