I teach a second-year course on rational choice and game theory to philosophy and PPE students: 24 enrolled, one 80-minute session, a projector, index cards, one coin per student, and no budget for cash payoffs (a chocolate bar for the winner of a stage is the ceiling). By this point in the term they can compute Nash equilibria including mixed ones, and they have had one session on iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies in which every dominator was a pure strategy. They have never seen a mixed strategy used as a dominator, and I want them to reach that idea by failing to do something, not by being told it.

Design a single-session activity around the following game. The row player chooses U, M or D; the column player chooses L, C or R. Row player's payoffs, rows U / M / D against columns L / C / R:

U: 4, 0, 0
M: 1, 1, 5
D: 0, 4, 1

Column player's payoffs, columns L / C / R against rows U / M / D:

L: 3, 0, 2
C: 0, 3, 2
R: 1, 1, 1

So (U, L) pays (4, 3), (M, R) pays (5, 1), and so on.

What I want the game for. Please check each of these five claims against the numbers before you design anything, and if one of them is wrong, tell me which one and repair the matrix with the smallest change that restores it, rather than quietly building the session on a different game:

1. In the full game no row strategy is strictly dominated, and M is not even weakly dominated there.
2. R is strictly dominated, but not by L and not by C: only by a mixture.
3. Nevertheless R is the column player's best pure strategy by the worst-case criterion, and the best worst-case guarantee obtainable from any mixture is 1.5, delivered by the very mixture that dominates R.
4. Once R has been deleted, M becomes strictly dominated by a mixture of U and D, though by neither pure strategy on its own.
5. Then the procedure stops under strict dominance. In the reduced game L weakly dominates C, and if you follow weak dominance through to the end you arrive at (U, L).

What the session has to accomplish:

- Two things should be felt rather than said. First, that a strategy can be perfectly defensible against every single alternative and still be strictly dominated, because the alternative that beats it is a coin flip; and that the coin flip is not the risky option here but the safer one, since it raises the guarantee rather than lowering it. Second, that deleting a strategy can unlock a deletion that was unavailable before, so that iterating is doing real work rather than tidying up: M cannot be touched at the start, and can be touched only once the class has already concluded something about the opponent.
- Participants must make real choices under real if trivial stakes and produce the mixture themselves. My own idea, which you may use or replace with something better, is to score column players by their worst case in the first stage, so that they converge on R and can then be shown a coin flip that guarantees more. The hands-on part must not open with a lecture, and the terms mixed strategy and dominated should not be spoken by me until the debrief.
- Give me: the handout exactly as participants would receive it, including the payoff table each side is allowed to see at each stage and when the second table is revealed; the facilitator's moves and the handful of sentences the facilitator is permitted to say; a timeline for the 80 minutes; and the scoring scheme in points.
- The debrief should map concrete moments in the session onto the five claims; state the general test for whether a strategy is dominated by some mixture, in the best-response-to-beliefs form, precisely enough that a student could apply it to a fresh three-by-three game without enumerating mixtures; and mark clearly what the session demonstrates as opposed to what I will simply have to assert. It should also show the class that the procedure stops while they still want it to continue: the surviving set contains profiles nobody would predict, and the tempting next step, weak dominance, is a different and more dangerous move that the session should flag rather than take.
- Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and what to do in the room when it does. Include at least the case where someone spots the mixture in the first minute and says it aloud, and the case where a student insists that a deliberate coin flip is not a strategy at all but a failure to decide.
- Be frank about what this session cannot reproduce: which feature of iterated elimination under mixed dominance it leaves out and which I must supply in the debrief. In particular, say whether the order in which deletions are made could change where the class ends up, and if so under what conditions (weak dominance, games that are not finite), and if you are not certain, say you are not certain rather than producing an example you cannot stand behind.

Begin with one sentence naming the activity and the thing participants will discover, then go straight into the materials and the handout. No preamble, no restatement of what I asked for, and no background on game theory before the session itself.
