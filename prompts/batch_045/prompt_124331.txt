I'm putting together a reading-night thing and I need a piece of satire, mock-official-document style, on Fitch's paradox of knowability. Here's the setup you can use if you like it: in an imaginary jurisdiction the parliament has passed the Knowability Act, whose operative section says roughly that every proposition that is true is capable of being known. The Office of Knowability Compliance is the poor body that has to implement it: it vets official statements, citizen filings and departmental reports for compliance with the Act. Everyone in the Office has quietly noticed the Act has a problem, which is that if there is even one truth nobody in fact knows, say whether the 1987 codicil is still sitting in a locked drawer in the Wrenley file, then the compound claim that the codicil is in the drawer and nobody knows it is there is also true, so under the Act it too has to be knowable; but anybody who knew that conjunction would know the codicil is there and would also know that nobody knows it is there, and since knowing something requires it to be true, you end up with someone who both knows and doesn't know the same thing. So taken straight the Act either rules out unknown truths, which is insane, or it's just false. The Office's actual daily work is therefore not applying the Act but keeping it from collapsing.

What I want is an onboarding guidance note for new compliance officers, written by a deputy director who's been there far too long and who completely believes in the Act. Around 700 to 800 words. It should walk one concrete filing, the Wrenley codicil, through review and have that file come back at least twice in different forms so the reader can track the moves. The comedy has to come from the logic being right: the Office's daily practices should be the real repair manoeuvres philosophers have actually proposed for the paradox, with bureaucracy bolted on. Pick at least three, and if you can think of a different real one use that instead, but please don't invent fake philosophy. Candidates I had in mind: the Closed Conjunction Doctrine, where the Office simply declines to accept any submission that joins a claim to a claim about that same claim's epistemic status, ruling such sentences malformed rather than true-but-unknowable, and trains officers to spot smuggled conjunctions like "and the drawer has never been opened"; the Rota, where knowability is cashed out as some merely possible examiner somewhere could find it out, so omniscience gets spread very thinly across infinitely many hypothetical inspectors and nobody in particular has to be omniscient, and the guidance has to say how idealised a Rota member may be, unlimited memory, unlimited time, allowed to know where and when they are, because cranking up the idealisation is the only way hard cases clear; pre-clearance, where the Act is read as applying only to claims that stay consistent when you add that somebody knows them, with the catch that you have to establish whether a claim is stable under being known before you can know whether it's covered; and the certificate regime, where the Office quietly stops assuming that knowing something makes it true, or lets an officer know a whole dossier without knowing either half of it, and starts issuing provisional knowledge certificates. Bonus if you can work in the register where a claim is filed as both verified and unverified.

The tone should be deadpan throughout: the narrator never winks, never says anything is absurd, and is gently annoyed at the public for not understanding the Act. No symbols or formalism, do it all in prose, and it should be followable by a smart person who's never taken a logic class. The thing I want a reader to feel by the end, without anybody ever saying it, is that every one of these repairs quietly requires a notion of truth that can outrun anything anybody could ever find out, which is exactly what the Act was passed to deny — the Office has become the machine that preserves the letter of the Act by destroying the reason anyone wanted it. Also worth a line or two on Office compliance statistics, since of course the numbers are fantastic. And please write it as continuous prose only: no headings, no To/From block, no numbered clauses, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — just paragraphs, like a long slightly passive-aggressive email.
