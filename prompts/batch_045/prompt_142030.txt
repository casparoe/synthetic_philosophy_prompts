Hey — I'm a research-methods PhD student who somehow got pulled into a tiny side project with two clinicians and a sociologist, and we've been going back and foward for weeks on the same stupid question. The plan is to pick one psychiatric diagnosis and follow it for five years to see whether we can catch a Hacking-style looping effect actually happening in the wild: clinicians and manuals sort people into a category, the people so sorted start experiencing, describing and acting differently (and sometimes organise, demand, or refuse the label), and that feeds back into how clinicians and the manual apply the category the next round. We have registry/diagnosis-code data for a whole health region plus funding for about sixty interviews, and we can't randomise who gets diagnosed, so I care much less about which category is metaphysically juicy and much more about which one would let us *detect* mutual reshaping inside five years. Also note two of us want different things: my sociologist wants maximum loopiness, my clinician wants a category where following people around for five years doesn't itself do harm, and I want something where a statistic has a chance of moving.

So: rate these eight on expected total loopiness over roughly a decade, 0 to 100, and for each one split it into two numbers — individual looping (people change once they have the label, or once they know the label exists) and expert/institutional looping (clinicians, criteria, billing codes, advocacy groups, employers, app companies, pharma marketing change how the category gets applied). The list: dissociative identity disorder; adult-diagnosed ADHD; avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder; the newer prolonged grief diagnosis; ME/CFS (chronic fatigue syndrome); hoarding disorder; and two my group invented as synthetic test cases — "Compulsive Self-Monitoring Disorder", where people can't sleep or exercise without their wearable and keep escalating their tracking, and "Adolescent Attentional Fragmentation Syndrome", which is basically ADHD-shaped behaviour in teenagers under a brand-new name nobody has ever heard. Then rank all eight overall with a sentence of justification each.

Things I actually want you to use in the scoring rather than wave at: whether the classified person ever hears the diagnostic word at all; whether people seek the label or have it imposed on them; how much of the criteria is self-report versus observation or lab value; whether there's already a patient community, a professional guild, or an industry with money in it; whether the label hooks into identity or only into access to services, insurance, or workplace accommodation; which direction the stigma gradient runs; whether the threshold sits on a continuum with a huge crowd just below it; and whether the category is new enough that the criteria themselves are still visibly moving. Say explicitly where the asymmetry between a prestigious label people want and a feared label people dodge changes what we'd be able to *measure* as opposed to what's true, and flag any score where you're really extrapolating because the category is invented or informal. Finally: your top two picks overall, which of the three of us each pick is for, and one concrete thing you'd look for in the registry data that would count as evidence of looping rather than of more people simply being diagnosed.
