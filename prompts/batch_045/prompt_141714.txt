I serve on the programmes committee of a small Buddhist-inspired humanitarian foundation. We currently fund three things: a hospice, a Buddhist chaplaincy in two prisons, and a deworming programme in a district where our field staff already work. One of our trustees, an economist by training, has been arguing that Buddhist ethics is simply consequentialism in religious dress, and he has a picture he uses to make it vivid: karma is a ledger, merit accrues to the agent like interest on a deposit, and the familiar practice of dedicating or transferring merit to others works like a wire transfer. His conclusion is that our duty is to buy the largest quantity of welfare per pound and then dedicate the resulting merit to whoever we were supposed to be serving, and that on that basis we should close the chaplaincy and move its budget to deworming, since the two are, morally speaking, the same act under different descriptions. I suspect the money picture is carrying the argument rather than illustrating it, but I cannot say where.

What I want is an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on deworming. Please set out, in continuous prose and with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables — I know a correspondence table would be the natural format here, so I am asking you to spell the correspondences out in sentences instead — what would have to map onto what for the ledger picture to be apt: what plays the role of the currency, the payer, the payee, the exchange rate, the institution holding the account, and the reckoning at the end. Then say where the mapping breaks, and separate the breaks that merely make the picture imprecise from the ones that matter for the conclusion he draws from it.

Three features of the tradition seem to me to be the pressure points, though I would like you to tell me which of them really bite and why: the teaching that it is intention, rather than the outcome actually produced, that makes an act karmically significant; the teaching that the fruit of a gift varies with the quality of the recipient, so that merit is not a fixed quantity per unit of welfare delivered; and the teaching that an act done without clinging or craving generates no new karma at all. For each, say whether it tells against a consequentialist reading of Buddhist ethics, or only against a welfarist and aggregative version of one, or against neither.

One of our Buddhist advisers prefers a different analogy and thinks ours is a category error: the Buddha as physician, the four noble truths as diagnosis, aetiology, prognosis and treatment, and the moral life as a course of therapy whose point is the patient's cure rather than a balance of outcomes. Please say whether that analogy can support anything the ledger cannot, what it in turn conceals, and what, if anything, either of them can actually bear the weight of my trustee's conclusion about the chaplaincy budget. If both fail, tell me what a better analogy would be.
