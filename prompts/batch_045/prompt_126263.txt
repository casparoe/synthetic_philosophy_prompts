I am a third-year PhD student, and my supervisor is finishing a monograph on hedonism, of which the central chapter answers the heterogeneity objection. Over two years of supervisions I have accumulated a long trail of her verdicts on individual cases, and before I decide whether to build my own chapter around her account I want to know which unifying claim she is actually committed to. She has never stated it in the abstract, only ruled on cases, and I suspect the cases underdetermine it.

Her verdicts so far, on whether a pleasure is occurring in the episode:

- the first swallow of ice water when one is desperately thirsty: yes;
- the malicious pleasure taken in a rival's public humiliation: yes, because the nastiness belongs to what the pleasure is about rather than to the pleasure itself;
- three absorbed hours spent solving a proof, during which there is no noticeable feeling of any kind, followed immediately by an unprompted report of having loved it, and by a return to the proof the next morning: yes;
- a pleasant bodily sensation produced by a medication, which the subject finds intrusive, wants stopped, and takes no enjoyment in whatsoever: yes;
- the moment at which a miserable headache ceases: no, she denies that relief is itself pleasant;
- an addict who craves and compulsively seeks the drug but reports no enjoyment of it at all: no;
- a subject who reports enjoying a taste, and shows the usual outward signs of enjoying it, but has no inclination whatever to repeat it: yes;
- and she is happy to compare intensities across kinds, saying that the pleasure of the proof can exceed the pleasure of the water. She treats the two as commensurable on a single scale and has no sympathy with Mill's distinction between higher and lower pleasures as a distinction between the pleasures themselves.

The case I need a prediction about: a subject is repeatedly exposed, below the threshold at which she can detect anything, to a stimulus she never notices. She reports no difference in her experience, and detects none even when asked to attend carefully to whether anything has changed. Everything else is as it is in an ordinary case of pleasure: the exposures reinforce learning, bias her subsequent choices, make a neutral drink taste better, lift her mood for the following half hour, and produce neural activity indistinguishable from that of a felt pleasure. Nothing at all has been added to what her experience is like for her.

What I want from you: identify the rule about what all pleasures have in common that best fits the verdicts above; set out the other rules that fit them equally well; say what each of those rules predicts about the unnoticed-stimulus case, and why you prefer the prediction you give; and tell me which further case would most cleanly separate the surviving rules. If the rule you attribute to her is in tension with any of her own verdicts, say which verdict she should give up. Finally, I want to know whether that rule genuinely answers the heterogeneity objection or merely relocates it: in particular, whether it can underwrite her own willingness to place the pleasure of the proof and the pleasure of the water on one scale, and what it commits her to about pleasures in subjects whose experience is nothing like ours.
