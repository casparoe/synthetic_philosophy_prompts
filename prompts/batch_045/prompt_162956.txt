I am producing the central handout for a first-year course, Mathematics, Truth and Proof, and I want it to take the form of a decision tree: a student begins from a commitment almost everyone in the room already has — that a great deal of mathematical discourse is genuinely truth-apt, and that mathematicians have by and large got things right — and is led, by questions they can answer before they have heard the labels, to a named position in contemporary philosophy of mathematics together with the argument that is supposed to force the choice. The organising problem throughout should be Benacerraf's dilemma: the apparent incompatibility between a semantics on which mathematical sentences are true in virtue of how things stand with abstract objects, and an epistemology on which a belief counts as knowledge only if the believer's state can be suitably linked, causally or at least naturalistically, to whatever makes it true. The companion complaint — that nothing in mathematical practice settles which objects the numerals pick out, since rival set-theoretic reductions serve the practice equally well — should appear as a node of its own rather than being folded into the horns, and I want the tree to be honest about whether the two complaints pull in the same direction.

Please observe the following constraints.

1. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a catalogue of names. Set out the branch questions first — nine or ten of them — and the combinations of answers they permit, and only then locate the actual views within that space. The leaves I want represented are: object platonism, including a plenitudinous or full-blooded variant on which every consistent mathematical theory describes some realm or other; ante rem structuralism; modal or eliminative structuralism; Field-style fictionalism; the paraphrase or figuralist view associated with Yablo; neo-Fregean abstractionism; and a quietist or deflationary stopping-place on which the demand for an ontology and an epistemology both is simply misfiring. Add a leaf if you think the space requires one, and say plainly if you think any of these is not really a distinct resting-place.

2. Every branch node must be phrased as a question a student could answer without knowing which position it leads to. No node may ask, in effect, whether one is a platonist; no node may turn on technical machinery a student would only have encountered inside one school — no appeal by name to abstraction principles, to conservativeness results, or to second-order modal logic. Where a node needs a concrete case, supply one.

3. Two of the branch nodes should be material of my own devising, and I want them placed where they genuinely bite rather than bolted on at the end.

   (a) A node on the reliability horn and machine-learned mathematics. A system trained only on human-authored text and corpora of formal proofs comes to conjecture results no one had proved, and to produce machine-checkable derivations of them; reliance on automated proof-checking of this kind is now routine. Ask the student whether they are willing to count such a system as knowing those results, and, if not, what they take the difference to be between its epistemic situation and theirs. The node needs teeth for someone who has already accepted that knowledge requires a naturalistically explicable link to the truth-makers, since the system plainly has no causal contact with abstract objects while being reliable in precisely the way causal epistemologies care about.

   (b) A node on the identification complaint, set in a case where the choice seems to matter. A mathematician working in a foundational framework finds that the two rival reductions genuinely diverge on some further question, or a student who has just been shown both reductions asks which one is really correct. Ask whether that question has an answer, and route the different answers to different leaves — including, if that is where it belongs, a leaf on which the question has no answer because there are many structures answering equally well to the same description.

4. For each unoccupied combination of answers, mark the cell as empty and give a one-line diagnosis: incoherent given the other commitments, merely unattractive, or unoccupied simply because no one has worked it out. I would rather have visible gaps than a tree with no spaces in it.

5. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate. I suspect the two paraphrase-style nominalisms will prove hard to prise apart without a node about nominalistic reconstruction; either find a question that does separate them, or say plainly that the tree bottoms out there and state what further question would be needed.

6. Keep the whole handout to one side of A4 if you can: a short note at the top on how a student is meant to work through it, the tree itself, and one sentence per leaf on that position's most uncomfortable commitment. The prose introduction should be no longer than a paragraph.

Finally, add a brief closing note on which single node does the most work in the tree and why, so that I know where to spend seminar time.
