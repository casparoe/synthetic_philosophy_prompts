Hi — I'm on a four-person team at a state agency that converts chunks of our administrative code into automated eligibility rules for a benefits system. Basically we take statutory sentences like "the claimant's authorized representative may request a hearing" or "if the county caseworker assigned to the case has not approved the request within 30 days, the request is denied" and turn them into database checks. A lawyer on the team signs off; the two devs have no philosophy background at all and mostly want something they can turn into pseudocode. We do maybe 15–25 of these a week. A bad rule means someone loses benefits or an appeal gets overturned, so the stakes aren't nothing, but we also can't spend an hour per sentence — think five to ten minutes per rule, and then it goes to the lawyer.

The thing that keeps biting us is definite descriptions: "the county caseworker assigned to the case", "the date the agency received the request", "the current benefit rate", "the notice described in subsection (c)". Half the time the database has no row that matches, or has two, or has one that matches under an old job title after a reorg. Right now we wing it, so we're inconsistent week to week, and I want something my team can actually run.

I've read a bit about Russell's theory of descriptions — the present-King-of-France style example, the idea that a definite description packs an existence-and-uniqueness claim into the content of the sentence rather than carrying it as a separate presupposition, and the scope distinction in negated sentences — and it felt like exactly the right tool, but I can't turn it into a procedure. So: please write me a decision procedure we can run on each description in a draft rule, in a fixed order, that terminates in one of a small number of runtime actions (something like: apply the rule, treat the condition as unmet, halt and route to a human, reject the rule as unautomatable). For each step, say what question we're asking and what counts as an answer good enough to move on. It has to handle:

- existence failure — nothing in the data satisfies the description;
- uniqueness failure — more than one candidate, or one live and one expired record;
- near-miss cases where everyone in the office knows which person or document is meant but the words don't strictly fit them (the "caseworker" is a supervisor covering the caseload, the record is under a maiden name, the job title changed in the last reorg);
- incomplete or elliptical descriptions — "the deadline", "the rate", "the notice" — where the intended domain has to be pulled from surrounding text;
- negated and conditional contexts where it matters whether the description sits inside or outside the negation: is "the assigned caseworker has not approved the request" saying there's no such caseworker or no approval on file, or that there is one and they didn't approve? Russell's scope point suggests those are two different rules and we have been writing one;
- descriptions that only get satisfied later or hypothetically, like "the first payment received after the date of death";
- and the versioning problem: "the current rate" or "the assigned caseworker" as of which date, when the rule runs months after the facts.

I need you to mark, explicitly and separately, which steps are real theory and which are us making a policy call dressed up as procedure; which distinctions have to survive the simplification and which we can drop without anything breaking; and the points where no procedure can settle it and a human has to decide because the question is about values, not facts — deny vs. hold, fail loudly vs. fail quietly, automate vs. leave it to a caseworker.

Then run it on three from our backlog:

1. "If the county caseworker assigned to the case has not approved the request within 30 days, deny." The case has no assigned caseworker at all — the worker left and the caseload is unassigned.
2. "The claimant's authorized representative may request a hearing." Two representative designation forms on file, one of them expired last year.
3. "Notice is effective on the date the agency received the request." Receipt date is missing in roughly a third of records.

I suspect the procedure is going to give me an answer on one of those that I don't like and that creates a lot of work for us — please don't soften it if so, just tell me which one and why.

Also tell me plainly what the procedure can't do, and how confident you are in each part: flag which steps are genuinely Russell's account or the standard follow-up views and which are your own invention, and how much the literature actually supports them. And if I've got this set up wrong — if Russell's theory isn't the right tool for spec-writing, or if the difference between "false" and "presupposition failure" doesn't actually cash out as different runtime behavior once you're writing code, or if my three cases aren't the hard ones — say that straight out instead of politely building me a procedure on a bad premise. Answer in American English, please.
