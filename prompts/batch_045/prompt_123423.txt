The problem of verisimilitude is the problem of saying what it is for one false theory to be closer to the truth than another. Below are three accounts of what that closeness consists in. Compare them.

**The content account** (the approach pioneered by Karl Popper). Closeness to the truth is a matter of the balance of a theory's true and false logical content: a theory is more verisimilar than another when it has more of the truth and less of the falsity, understood so that T₂ is at least as verisimilar as T₁ just in case every true consequence of T₁ is also a consequence of T₂ and every false consequence of T₂ is also a consequence of T₁, with at least one of these containments strict. It is a familiar objection, pressed from the mid-1970s onward, that on this definition no false theory can be closer to the truth than any other — which would make it unusable for the purpose it was designed for, that of describing a sequence of false theories as progressive.

**The likeness account.** Closeness to the truth is a matter of global similarity between the way a theory says the world is and the way the world in fact is: a theory is closer the smaller the departure from actuality that would be needed for it to be exactly true. On this account, saying that the number of planets is nine is closer to the truth than saying that it is nine billion, and closeness can be compared across theories that are logically unrelated.

**The reliance account.** Closeness to the truth is not a property of a theory alone, nor of a theory together with the actual world, but of a theory relative to a body of questions and a range of counterfactual situations. For a set Q of questions an inquirer needs answered and a range R of situations the inquirer might have been in, a theory's verisimilitude is the expected graded accuracy of the answers it would yield to the questions in Q across the situations in R — where a near miss counts as better than a wild miss, and an error that is systematic and therefore recoverable from the theory's other answers counts as better than an error of the same size that is unsystematic and unrecoverable. Truth is the limiting case: as Q expands to every question and R contracts to the actual situation, reliance-conditional verisimilitude converges on truth itself.

Test the three accounts against the following cases.

1. *The two atlases.* Cartographer A produces an atlas in which every coastline and every relative position is correct, but every stated distance is uniformly ten per cent too large. Cartographer B produces an atlas whose table of inter-city distances is exactly correct, but whose relative positions are scrambled, so that no single coherent map realizes the table, and whose errors admit no correction function. Which atlas is closer to the truth?

2. *Precision against safety.* The truth is that there are eight planets. Compare the precise falsehood *there are nine planets* with the vague truth *there are between seven and nine planets*. Which is closer to the truth, and does the answer differ if the only question in play is *exactly how many planets are there?*

3. *Adequacy condition.* Any account should avoid ranking a bare tautology, which has every true consequence and no false one, above a substantive theory that is false but nearly right. Say how each account meets this condition and at what cost.

In your answer: say where the three accounts agree and where they diverge on these cases, and explain why the divergences arise from the accounts' structural commitments rather than from arbitrary choices of metric. Then address the central issue directly. The reliance account makes verisimilitude interest-relative, whereas the content and likeness accounts make it a property a theory has independently of what anyone wants to know. Is that relativization a genuine insight, or is it a change of subject? In particular, those who want to claim that later scientific theories are closer to the truth than earlier ones typically want that claim not to depend on which questions we happen to care about; if verisimilitude is reliance-conditional, can that claim survive, or does it collapse into a pragmatist identification of progress with problem-solving effectiveness? Relatedly, if a theory's reliability for our questions no longer needs to be explained by its nearness to the truth but is partly constitutive of it, has the reliance account given up something the other two accounts were built to preserve?

Finally, argue for one of the three accounts as the most defensible overall, and state the strongest objection to the account you have chosen.
