hey — I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy (broadly contractualist corner, though I keep drifting) and I've ended up defending a particular version of the practice-cum-assurance story about promising, and I'd like it stress-tested before I commit another chapter to it.

The rough view: you're bound by a promise neither because you intended to bind yourself nor because someone gets hurt if you wriggle out. You're bound because promising is a conventional normative power whose point is to let people hand each other insurance against their own future change of mind. What I keep calling the lockbox feature: a promise is supposed to keep its force after every reason you had for making it has evaporated, and even in cases where nobody was relying on you in any ordinary sense. And I want to bolt on a second, sharper claim that I think falls out of it: the stringency of a given promise tracks how much assurance the promisee actually needed — how exposed they are to your changing your mind — and *not* how solemn the framing was. So a mumbled, unwitnessed promise to do something the promisee has already reorganised their life around binds harder than a formal signed promise to do something that costs them nothing if it lapses. That's the bit I take to be a real prediction rather than a restatement.

My supervisor says I've built something unfalsifiable: any pattern of intuitions can be soaked up by redescribing what "the practice" is, and any case where stringency fails to track exposure can be explained away by saying the practice happens to have a formality convention too. I want to know if that's fair or if it's just a cheap shot at any view with a social practice in it.

So: what would count against this package? Along the lines of —

- cases where the practice seems to run out: promises to beings who can't have expectations in the relevant sense (very small children, someone permanently unconscious), promises to a group whose membership has completely turned over between the promise and the performance date, and at the limit a promise to an artificial agent that can model your future behaviour but can't plan or suffer. Does my story have to say these don't bind, or can it say they bind for a different reason — and if it says the latter, what has it given up?
- the fact that release is built into the practice: the promisee can let you off. Doesn't the easy waivability of promissory obligation sit badly with the idea that I've created a claim by exercising a power? What would the practice look like if waivability *weren't* there, and would that be evidence for or against me?
- the shape-of-the-norm worry, which is the one I care about most: if the function of the practice is assurance, why does the practice seem to rule out promising to do something bad, rather than permitting it subject to a side-constraint that's imported from elsewhere? That looks like the norm isn't purely assurance-shaped, and I'm not sure my view can explain the shape from the function.

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and — importantly — be explicit about which ones someone in my position should *concede* count against me rather than dismiss as a misdescription. I'd rather have a defeater than a defence. And tell me honestly whether the unfalsifiability charge sticks, and if it does, whether that's actually a problem for a view whose job is to explain a practice, or just the cost of doing business. Concrete cases and what they'd show, please; I don't need a map of the literature.
