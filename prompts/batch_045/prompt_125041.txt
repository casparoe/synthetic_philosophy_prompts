THE QUESTION

(a) What does it mean to say that a person's preferences are revealed by their choices, and why would someone think this lets us stop treating utility as a feeling or as a quantity going on inside the person?

(b) Be careful here: if a shopper picks bundle x while bundle y was also affordable, exactly what have we learned? And what does consistency (the weak axiom, the strong axiom) actually rule out?

(c) Suppose you can only watch this shopper during a long inflation: her budget set shrinks every month, and whatever she bought last month is never affordable again. Can you still test whether her choices are consistent with maximizing some utility function? And could you use data like that to say how much better or worse off she is in month 6 than in month 1?

CLARA'S ANSWER

Utility used to be thought of as a feeling — pleasure, happiness — that caused people to choose what they chose. Samuelson's move was to cut the feeling out of the middle: we never observe anyone's happiness, we only observe what they pick when prices and income put a budget in front of them. So a preference just is a disposition to choose, and utility is only a convenient numbering of the choices, not a quantity of anything.

That's also why the whole thing is testable instead of speculative. If she picks x while y was also affordable, she has revealed that she prefers x to y: she had y and didn't take it. The weak axiom says that once you've seen x chosen with y available, you should never see y chosen with x available, and the strong axiom says the same thing for chains of choices, so no cycles. Choices that pass those tests can be thought of as maximizing a utility function; choices that fail them can't be thought of that way at all.

The function you recover isn't unique, but it isn't arbitrary either: any two utility functions that fit the same choices differ only by a positive affine transformation, u' = a + b·u with b > 0. So the absolute numbers mean nothing, but ratios of utility differences are meaningful, and that's what makes utility comparisons across people possible in principle.

On (c): I think you can still test for consistency even with shrinking budget sets. A rational shopper has to buy less of a good when its relative price goes up — that's just the substitution effect, and you read it straight off the quantities. So if in month 4 eggs get relatively more expensive and she buys more eggs, she's revealed an inconsistency; no utility function could generate that. What you do lose is the direct comparison: because last month's bundle is never affordable again, the utility levels aren't linked, so you can't say whether she's better or worse off in month 6 than in month 1. But the shape of the indifference curves is still recoverable, because at each observation the budget line is tangent to the indifference curve through the chosen bundle and the prices hand you the slope at that point.
