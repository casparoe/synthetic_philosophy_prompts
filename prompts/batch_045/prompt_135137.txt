Hey — I'm putting together a seminar handout on standing to blame, and I keep hitting the same wall: every case I can come up with where a hypocrite blames someone goes the expected direction, and my students walk out convinced the non-hypocrisy condition is obviously true. So I want a case built the other way round, and built to spec rather than just gestured at.

What I need is one ordinary, realistic scenario — a workplace, a family, a sports club; no trolleys, no kings, no official roles like judges or referees, no war crimes — in which a hypocrite's blame is, all things considered, in order: she has standing, the target really does deserve it, and the target has reason to take it to heart. Constraints, and I'm strict about these: her own wrong is the same generic kind as the target's (say, both are deceptions for gain), it's at least as serious and at least as recent, she's never acknowledged, repented, reformed, been caught, or been punished, and she isn't self-deceived — she knows exactly what she's done. She isn't the victim of the target's wrong herself, she holds no office, and she has no special relationship to the target (a relationship to the victim is fine, and is probably where this has to go). The target knows about her record, so "and you too" is right there on the table for him. The blame has to be addressed to him, not muttered to a third party, and it can't be motivated by wanting to draw attention away from her own thing. No stipulating that she has standing, and no sneaking in one of the exit routes — if you can only make it work by loosing one of those constraints, say which one and tell me what that shows about the condition.

Some scaffolding that might help, it's how I've started thinking about the problem. There are three defects that usually travel together and that I want pulled apart: hypocrisy proper (she's done the same kind of thing), self-exemption (she holds him to a standard she doesn't apply to herself), and insincerity (she doesn't really care about the value she's invoking). Make at least one of them absent and say which, don't quietly drop all three. I've also been toying with treating standing as scalar and domain-scoped rather than binary — partial standing, or standing to make the judgement but not to demand the apology — and with the idea of second-hand standing, where the blamer's standing is borrowed from the victim she's speaking up for. That second move raises what I've been calling the laundering objection: if a hypocrite can launder her blame by borrowing someone else's standing, it looks like no hypocrite ever lacks standing and the forfeiture condition is empty.

Deliverable: the paragraph of case, then the walkthrough. Establish first that the target deserves the blame and that the blame is otherwise apt — right belief, fitting attitude, addressed to him, no excuse in play. Then run the verdicts: what the forfeiture view says and why, what a relationship / second-personal view says and why (does borrowed standing actually transmit, or does the borrower's own record contaminate it?), and what the "hypocrisy is a fact about her character, not about the propriety of the blame" view says. Then the honest part: which single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens when you delete that feature (I want to watch it collapse back into a bog-standard hypocrite case), and how a forfeiture theorist would attack it — the laundering objection at minimum, plus whatever else you can think of. If you think the spec can't be met without cheating, argue that instead; that's a useful answer too. Please skip citations and reading lists entirely — I've got the literature side covered, I just want the case and the argument around it.
