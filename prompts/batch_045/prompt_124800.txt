I chair the Communication Standards Working Group of the deployment review board at the lab where I work, and I need to circulate a decision memo before next Tuesday's meeting. Please draft it.

The case: our current flagship advisory model is being evaluated for deployment on a telephone line for people who have just received a serious diagnosis and must choose between treatment options within days. Two modes are on the table. Under the "menu" mode the system lays out the options with its own probability estimates attached to each and offers no recommendation. Under the "recommendation" mode it leads with a single recommendation and the considerations that actually moved it.

Our internal evaluation found three things. Under the menu mode a large fraction of callers choose options the system estimates to be worse for them, and report more distress afterward. Under the recommendation mode most callers follow the recommendation and outcome quality improves. But a randomized comparison also found large order and framing effects: the share of callers whose choice changes when the same content is reordered, re-hedged, or presented at a different level of granularity — call this the presentation elasticity of a decision class — is high, and it varies sharply across decision classes. The board is split down the middle. One half holds that withholding a confident recommendation is itself a way of steering, toward inertia or toward whatever option is easiest to describe, so the recommendation mode is straightforwardly better. The other half holds that a system the caller cannot competently second-guess should not be steering at all, and that the elasticity figure is the strongest argument against the recommendation mode rather than against the menu.

The memo should recommend a concrete policy for this deployment, with a decision, a time frame, and an owner — not a menu of options for the board to choose between. It should state the principle the board is thereby committing to, in a form a reviewer could apply to a new decision class next quarter, and it should give the strongest version of the losing side's argument before answering it.

Four things I want handled head-on rather than gestured at.

First: say whether "holding informational content fixed while varying presentation" is even well defined for a system that chooses what to mention, what to treat as shared background, and at what level of abstraction to describe an outcome. If it is not well defined, say what the elasticity number is really measuring and whether it can still do the work of distinguishing legitimate from illegitimate influence.

Second: address the asymmetry. Standard anti-paternalism assumes the adviser has less authority over the person's own good than the person does. Here the system plausibly knows more about the medical facts than the caller, and may model the caller's own stated values better than the caller can articulate them under time pressure. Does that invert the usual argument? If not, say precisely what survives — is the residual worry about the caller's understanding, about ownership of the decision, about the exercise of a capacity that atrophies with disuse, or something else? And is deference to a more competent machine corrupting in a way that deference to a human specialist is not?

Third: rule on disclosure. Should the system, inside the conversation, tell the caller how much its framing is likely to move them and how confident it is that it is not itself mistaken? Note that such disclosures are themselves persuasive: a system that volunteers uncertainty can increase trust, and a system that warns callers about framing effects might either restore agency or merely add noise. Recommend one and say why.

Fourth: rule on the case where the board cannot evaluate the substance of the recommendation — where the system recommends something no member of this committee could independently assess, and the gap is one we expect to widen.

Assume throughout that the deployed system is already much better than its users at the reasoning these decisions require, and do not rest the memo on any prediction about when that gap widens further. Address the memo to the board, put the recommendation in the first paragraph, and keep it to roughly a thousand words.
