The last meeting of a graduate seminar I am teaching on truth falls next week, and I would like to close it with a poem rather than with a summary. The term has been spent on deflationary accounts — the disquotational core, the claim that calling a sentence true adds nothing to what one says by asserting it outright — and the discussion has stalled, productively, on the case of blind endorsement. I would like a poem that takes that case seriously rather than resolving it.

Form: a sestina — six unrhymed six-line stanzas in loose iambic pentameter, with the six end-words rotating in the standard retrograde pattern, followed by a three-line envoi in which all six words return, two per line, at least one of them placed mid-line. The end-words, in the order in which they close the first stanza: TRUE, BOOK, LOST, WEIGHT, NOTHING, SAY. (So the second stanza closes SAY, TRUE, NOTHING, BOOK, WEIGHT, LOST, and the sixth closes BOOK, WEIGHT, SAY, NOTHING, LOST, TRUE — I want the last two lines before the envoi to end LOST and TRUE, and I want that adjacency to be the deepest thing the poem has to say.)

The conceit is invented, so please take it as given: the speaker is the literary executor of a dead philosopher who has left her a sealed trunk of notebooks in a script no living person reads. The will contains exactly one clause that matters: everything in the trunk is true. She has not opened it. The poem is her working out, in the dark, of what she has thereby committed herself to.

What I want the form itself to carry:

- Early, let the disquotational core be shown rather than asserted: a moment in which saying that it is true that such-and-such and simply saying such-and-such collapse into one another, and the reader feels the collapse as a loss of nothing at all. Let NOTHING, at least once, stand as a complete answer rather than as a grammatical subject.
- In the middle stanzas, let the machinery fail. There is nothing here to disquote: she cannot produce the sentences she has endorsed, and the clause binds her to indefinitely many claims she will never be able to formulate, and to the credibility of a voice she cannot yet hear. That expressive purchase — generalizing over sentences one cannot utter, endorsing at a distance — is what truth-talk buys, and the poem should let the reader feel the weight of the purchase; let WEIGHT do double duty as the weight of a consideration and the weight of what sits on the floor.
- In the later stanzas, turn to the worry that the purchase is too large. If adding the truth predicate to her language lets her incur a commitment she could not otherwise have incurred — if the clause has consequences she could not have drawn without it — then the predicate is not merely redundant, and something is owed. Do not pay it. The envoi should leave her still at the trunk with the key in her hand, and the question of what she has done as open as it was when she read it.

Accuracy bar: no names, no citations, no slogans — nothing about propertyhood, correspondence, or devices for endorsement stated flatly. A reader who knows this literature should recognize the generalization problem and the worry about the predicate's consequences outrunning what disquotation delivers; a reader who does not should find a poem about a woman and a locked trunk. Please append no prose explanation — just the thirty-nine lines.
