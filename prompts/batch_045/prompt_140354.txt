My grad reading group has been going round on the internal vs external reasons debate for like eight weeks and we've pretty much reached the point where every remaining move is one somebody already made last week. Quick version so you know which fight I mean: the internalist says a consideration is a reason for an agent only if there's a sound deliberative route from that agent's actual subjective motivational set to her being moved by it; the externalist says some reasons hold for an agent whether or not her S gives her any route at all, and if she has no route then so much the worse for her S. The externalists say the internalists have confused a thesis about what moves people with a thesis about what there is reason to do. The internalists say the externalists have kept the sentence "you have a reason" while dropping everything that could connect it to the agents own practical reasoning, which they think makes it either false or a kind of bluff. Nobody is confused about the other side anymore and still nobody moves.

The diagnosis I'm toying with, which I want you to attack or improve: the real fight is about what a deliberative *route* is allowed to do. Call a route conservative if it can only fix the agent's factual and logical mistakes, and generative if it can also leave her with an end she didn't have going in. On that reading internalism is obviously true about conservative routes and externalism is obviously true about generative ones, and the classic dispute is at least partly verbal. Except I don't think it fully dissolves, and one reason is a case I keep throwing at people: take an agent whose S contains nothing but one procedural motive — she wants, above all else, to end up doing whatever there is most reason to do, and she has no first-order desires at all. Internalism looks committed to saying she has a route to literally any consideration, which makes it extensionally equivalent to externalism while still insisting it's a different view. The externalist can say: right, so the route-from-S test was tracking the wrong thing all along. But then I want to know what makes their claim about her true, if not something about her.

So: what kind of stalemate is this actually — verbal, substantive, two different questions under one name, or a hidden empirical question about how deliberation works dressed up as a conceptual one? Give me a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of the positions, and tell me honestly whether anything would count as progress here (my distinction, a better one, a real psychology of deliberation, changing the question) or whether the honest answer is that this one shouldn't be expected to resolve. Also: say how confident you are in the diagnosis you give, and single out the bit you're least sure of.
