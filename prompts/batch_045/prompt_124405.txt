For most of my working life I was an experimental physicist — nearly forty years, mostly in solid-state spectroscopy — and since retiring I have been sitting in on a graduate reading group in the philosophy department of the university where I used to work. We have just spent three sessions on the use of Gödel's incompleteness theorems to argue that human mathematical reasoning is not Turing-computable, the line of thought associated with Lucas and with Penrose. I finished those sessions less confident than I began, and, more to the point, unclear about where the disagreement in the room actually sits. So I would like a number, and I would like to see how it was assembled.

The proposition I want your credence on is this:

(P) The incompleteness theorems, combined with premises about human mathematical capacity that neither side of the dispute contests, give a reason of non-trivial strength to believe that human mathematical reasoning is not Turing-computable.

By non-trivial strength I mean a reason that would still look like a reason when stated to a competent and unsympathetic logician — enough, say, to move a reflective person's credence in mechanism by ten points or more, though nowhere near enough to settle the matter. For calibration: my own credence in (P) is about 0.3. Two other members of the group, both far better read in the literature than I am, sit near 0.08 and near 0.6.

Please give me your credence in (P) to two decimal places, and then the decomposition. Assume throughout, by stipulation, that human mathematicians are reliable about elementary arithmetic, so that the discussion does not collapse into global scepticism. Here are the places where I think the argument can fail; use them, reorder them, and add any term you think belongs.

1. The insight premise. Handed an explicit specification of an arbitrary consistent, recursively axiomatizable system S strong enough for arithmetic, a competent mathematician can, on reflection, come to know the truth of the Gödel sentence for S. What is your credence that this is a real, reliable, truth-tracking capacity, rather than a posture we adopt because the sentence has been constructed to look self-evident?

2. The indexical gap — the term I find least discussed in the popular presentations. For any single fixed S, the system S supplemented by the assertion of its own consistency already proves the Gödel sentence of S, so a machine can do for that one system what the mathematician is supposed to do. What the anti-mechanist seems to need instead is a uniform capacity: for every S you might be handed, you can recognise its Gödel sentence as true. But mechanism denies only that my total mathematical dispositions are the dispositions of some single machine; it does not deny that, being one machine among many, I can be handed the specification of another and comment upon it. So the argument needs the uniform reading, and the mechanist has an account of why a machine might display the uniform reading anyway. Please split this term: how probable is it that the argument requires the uniform capacity, and how probable is it, if required, that we have it?

3. The certification premise. I cannot come to know, with the warrant the argument requires, both which formal system I am and that that system is consistent; and if I cannot certify my own consistency, then applying the construction to myself yields no sentence whose truth I am entitled to. This is the familiar consistency objection. How much of the argument's force is lost here?

4. The sloppy-machine reply. The best computational model of actual human mathematical output may be an inconsistent system, or a partial and noisy one. Incompleteness guarantees a gap only for consistent systems, so on the first option there may be no gap for the argument to exploit at all. Give me your credence that the correct model of our output is inconsistent or merely partial-and-noisy, and, separately, your credence that the anti-mechanist can salvage something by substituting a reliability assumption for a consistency assumption.

5. The bootstrap worry. Does the argument presuppose what it concludes? Is the premise that I can see the Gödel sentence to be true available to someone who has not already assumed that her own mathematical insight outruns any machine? Give me your credence that the argument is not covertly circular.

6. The disjunctive reading. Gödel himself is usually read as drawing only a disjunction: either the human mind outruns every finite machine, or there are absolutely undecidable mathematical problems. Even granting every step, how probable is it that what has been established is at best this disjunction? And conditional on that, which disjunct is the cheaper one to accept — how do you weigh the cost of absolute undecidability against the cost of non-mechanism?

7. Scope transfer. Suppose the conclusion about mathematical reasoning were granted outright. How much does it support the further claims that draw people to this argument — about consciousness, about agency, about the need for a non-computable physics in the brain? I want this as a separate term even though it lies outside (P), because I suspect much of the temperature in the room is generated here rather than by the logic.

Then show me how the terms combine — whether you are treating them as conjuncts in a single argument, as independent routes to the conclusion, or as a noisy-OR — and do the arithmetic explicitly. Tell me which single term your final number is most sensitive to, in the sense that moving that one term to 0.5 would move your final number furthest. Then give me your credence in (P) under each of two stipulations: first, the Certified Mirror case, in which a trustworthy external source tells you exactly which program you are and certifies that it is consistent; second, the case in which the sloppy-machine reply is simply true. If your number barely moves under either stipulation, say why.

Finally: one member of my group is at roughly 0.08 and another at roughly 0.6. Which of these seven terms is most likely where their disagreement actually lives, and what single question would you put to the two of them to find out whether I have located it correctly?
