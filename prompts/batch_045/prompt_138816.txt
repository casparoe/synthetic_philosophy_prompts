I sit on the clinical ethics committee of a mid-sized community hospital, and over the past two years I have kept running into the same shape of case, for which I have no word. The categories we were trained to reach for — ask what the patient would have wanted, ask what serves her interests now, ask who has been appointed to speak for her — each seem to slice slightly off the mark. Three cases, all involving patients in roughly the same condition: advanced dementia, no real conversation, apparently content, no prospect of recovery. First: a woman who, while still sharp, signed a document saying that if she ever reached such a state she did not want antibiotics or a transfer to hospital for an infection. We honored it, and the reasoning felt settled. Second: a man who, while still sharp, signed a document saying that if he ever reached such a state he wanted everything done, and that he did not want anyone to tell him his life was not worth living. We treated it as evidence about his values and not much more; the clinicians still spoke of treatment that was not beneficial. Third: a woman who, while still sharp and knowing her values might shift, signed a document saying that whatever she seemed to want at the time should settle the matter, as though she had appointed her later self to decide for her, and that she gave up in advance any claim to override that later self. Nobody would touch it. Had she named her sister instead, the sister's word would have bound us.

The pattern I think I am seeing is that the authority here runs in one direction only. A person can bind her later self, and the binding survives even the later person's explicit repudiation — the demented woman who says, I don't know who wrote that, that isn't me, I want to live, is still overruled by the document, and we do not find that monstrous. What she apparently cannot do is release her later self, or hand her the decision. Notice that this is not a refusal to let the document refer to the later person at all: clauses that make her later *condition* the trigger — if I am in pain, if I no longer respond to music, if there is no reasonable hope — are perfectly ordinary and perfectly honored. It is specifically the later person's *say-so* that cannot be made load-bearing. A colleague's explanation is that the later self simply lacks the capacity to hold authority, whereas a named proxy has it, and that is all there is to it. That cannot be the whole story: we do let people in this condition settle plenty of matters by their current say-so (whether to get out of bed, whether to see a visitor, whether to finish a meal), so the later self is not voiceless; the whole point of these documents is to extend a person's say past the point where she can speak for herself, so "she cannot speak for herself" is the puzzle rather than the solution; and it does not explain why the same incapacity blocks a clause deferring to her choice while permitting a clause deferring to her condition.

So: does this asymmetry have a name? I mean both the one-way flow (the earlier self can restrict and cannot empower) and the condition-versus-say-so contrast, if they are indeed one phenomenon and not two. If there is an established concept, I would want to know exactly how it is delimited and how it differs from the neighbouring ideas it is likely to be confused with — the general device of arranging in advance not to be given what you will later ask for, the doctrine that the person who signed is the one whose values should govern even after she has changed, and the practice of reconstructing what she would have wanted. If there is none, I would want a term coined with a definition and some boundary cases, including this one: whether the answer changes when the later person's say-so is precisely about whether the earlier person was her. A bare label will not help me; I need to know what the phenomenon is, what it is not, where its edges lie, and whether the right frame for thinking about it is rights, or authority, or something more like a trust held for someone else's benefit. And I would much rather have the analysis than a reading list — I can find the literature myself, but I cannot find the distinction if I do not know what it is called.
