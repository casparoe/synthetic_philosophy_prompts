I'm grading a short logic/epistemology problem set and want a second pass before I commit to marks. Here's the question I set, and one student's answer to it.

Question. On Sunday a teacher tells a class of perfect reasoners: call the announcement A — "There will be exactly one exam next week, on some day from Monday to Friday, and on the evening before the exam you will not be able to deduce, from A together with the exams that have already failed to happen, which day it is." The class is separately and reliably told that the date was fixed in advance by rolling a fair five-sided die, that the outcome is hidden from them, and that it cannot be changed. Assume the class reasons from that mechanism plus what they observe as the week goes by, and from nothing else — they do not simply take the teacher's word for the surprisingness clause.

(a) Which days could the exam fall on consistently with A being true? Set out which eliminations actually hold and what each of them assumes.
(b) What is the probability that A is true?
(c) A classmate objects: the die is irrelevant, the backward induction eliminates every day, so no schedule makes A true. In three or four sentences, say what this objection gets right and where it goes wrong.

Student's answer:

(a) Friday is out. On Thursday evening, with no exam Mon–Thu, only Friday is left, and the class can deduce that using just the mechanism — the die guarantees exactly one exam that week — without leaning on the surprisingness clause at all. So a Friday exam would make A false.

The same move then knocks out Thursday: once Friday is excluded, Wednesday evening leaves only Thursday, so the class deduces Thursday; and likewise for Wednesday. So A can only be true if the exam is Monday, Tuesday or Wednesday.

(b) 3/5, since three of the five die outcomes are admissible.

(c) The objection is right that the class cannot use A as a premise in its own reasoning — that's the self-referential part — and right that the die changes nothing day by day, since how the date was chosen has no bearing on what the class can work out. But it is wrong that nothing satisfies A: Monday to Wednesday still do. The real moral is that A is true but unknowable, a blindspot for anyone: even the teacher cannot coherently assert it, because the teacher does not know what the class will manage to deduce, so asserting A is incoherent in the Moore-style raining-but-I-dont-believe-it way.

Give it a mark out of 20 and a letter grade, with the comments that justify it. I want this point by point rather than a general impression: which claims are right and why, which are wrong and exactly where the reasoning breaks, and anything that should have been said but wasn't. Lead with the grade in your first sentence, then go straight into the comments, no preamble and no restating the question.
