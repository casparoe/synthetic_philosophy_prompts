I do a lot of committee work at my university and read a bit of philosophy on the side, and there's a pattern I keep seeing that I don't have a word for.

Two instances. A junior colleague who's the first person in his family to go to grad school has started pre-loading everything he says with hedges and citations: "this is just a hunch, but", "there's some literature on this I think" — for observations that other people in the room just state flatly. And he gets taken seriously only in that hedged form. Seperately, a friend who's the only woman in her department told me she has trained herself to describe problems at work in a completely flat, affectless register, and that the flat version is now the only version she can produce: she says she knows something sharper is true but she can't get it out anymore.

The thing I keep noticing isn't the obvious wrong of not being believed. It's that expecting to be disbelieved reshapes what someone says, and after a while it seems to shrink what they can say — the hedged or flattened version is the only one they've had practice making, and it's a worse report of what they know. People who were never going to be challenged don't have this problem, they just say the thing.

Is there an established concept for this? I've seen "epistemic injustice" used, but it seems to cover everything from being ignored to being stereotyped, so it doesn't pin this down. I'm not after citations or a reading list — I want to know whether this is its own thing or a species of something, and where the edges are. Does it still count when the hedging works, i.e. he is taken seriously precisely because he hedged? Is it the same phenomenon if it's not about identity at all but about being the only qualitative person in a room full of quantitative ones? And what separates it from plain self-censorship or from someone just being modest?
