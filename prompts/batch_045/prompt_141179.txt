hey — I'm in a philosophy of science seminar this term, we just did a couple weeks on falsification, ceteris paribus clauses and Lakatos-style research programmes, and I'm writing my final paper on Malthus.

The position I want to defend, and want tested, is: Malthus was right but early is an immunizing move, not a real defence. His 1798 prediction — population tends to grow exponentially while food supply grows only linearly, so gains in subsistence per head get eaten back up — hasn't happened in the two centuries-plus since, and not in the places it was supposed to hit first. The standard rescues (you just haven't waited long enough; he couldn't have foreseen modern agriculture or the collapse in fertility that comes with wealth) either have no fixed deadline or repair the theory by bolting on auxiliaries after the fact.

The bit I'm actually pleased with is a distinction I came up with for separating honest delay from evasion. Call it clock-early vs shield-early. A prediction is clock-early if it came with (1) a trigger condition stated in advance that we can check right now, (2) some rough timescale given in advance, and (3) auxiliary assumptions with support independent of the prediction's failing so far. It's shield-early if the deadline keeps sliding, the mechanism gets re-described to fit whatever actually happened, or the only evidence for the auxiliaries is that the prediction hasn't come true yet. I want to argue Malthus is shield-early on all three, and that if you run the same test on a failed doomsday prediction everybody already laughs at, it comes out shield-early in the same way — so the Malthus case only looks respectable because of its prestige and hindsight.

What would count against this? Specifically:

- Which of my three conditions is doing the real work, and is any of them one a Malthus defender would reasonably reject as the wrong standard for a two-hundred-year prediction? Maybe nobody states timescales in centuries, and demanding one is just anachronistic.
- What would a defender themselves concede as counting against them, as opposed to what only critics say? Rank the defeaters fatal down to merely awkward.
- Whether my own test is insulated: am I set up so that any long-run prediction that eventually comes true gets retro-classified as clock-early, while any that hasn't yet is automatically shield-early? If so, say so plainly.
- What would genuinely move me: an actual case of a long-run prediction that looks shield-early by my lights and later turned out right anyway (resource-depletion and climate stuff is the obvious hunting ground, but don't just assert one), or an argument that Malthus's implicit timescale was always something like once cheap fossil-fuel inputs stop propping up yields — in which case maybe the deadline really hasn't arrived and I'm just punishing him for being vague.

My prof's offhand remark was that all this is just Duhem–Quine with a longer fuse; if that's all it is I'd rather find out now. Give me an actual verdict — is Malthus clock-early or shield-early, and is my test worth building a paper around or should I bin it? Commit to one answer rather than laying out both options, tell me why, and say how confident you'd be.
