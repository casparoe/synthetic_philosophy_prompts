I'm a teaching assistant for an upper-level philosophy of mind course, and we've just finished the stretch on Dennett's three stances — physical, design, intentional — and the companion idea about when a pattern is real (roughly: when describing the data as an instance of the pattern plus noise buys you compression you couldn't get by describing it point by point, which is meant to make the pattern no less real than anything else while denying that it has to show up from the physical stance). A student pushed back in office hours with something I haven't been able to shake: she says that for contemporary machine-learning systems we now routinely adopt a *fourth* stance, which she calls the corpus stance — you don't predict the system by asking what it's made of, what it was engineered to do, or what it believes and desires; you predict it by asking what it was trained on, and letting the statistics of that corpus do the work. Her claim is that this is genuinely new, because in practice it succeeds where the design stance fails badly: the design story says gradient descent on next-token prediction should yield a diligent, truthful assistant, and that prediction is worthless, while "what would this look like if it were sampled from the internet" is uncannily good. My own counter is that this is just the design stance with the dataset treated as a design component, and that we shouldn't multiply stances to accommodate a case where our design-stance assumptions happened to be wrong about which design was in play.

So I want a joke that would land with people who have actually read this material — the philosophy-of-mind pub-quiz crowd, not a generic "ha ha, philosophers" audience. Give me something structurally dry rather than pun-based: a dialogue, a shaggy-dog setup, a fake exam question, a bar scene with a twist — your call. The setup should put someone trying to predict a system's behavior and getting it wrong, and the punchline should turn on the actual content of the view: that the stances are prediction strategies rather than rival metaphysical theses about what's really in the box, that there's a characteristic mistake in treating whichever stance you currently can't give up as the one that tells you what's fundamentally there, and that a pattern can be perfectly real — exploitable, compressible, not merely projected by a bored observer — while remaining invisible from the physical stance. Working the fourth-stance idea into the machinery of the joke rather than bolting it on as a tag would be ideal, and I don't mind if the joke quietly takes a side on whether the corpus stance is a new stance or the design stance wearing a lab coat.

After the joke, give me three or four sentences on why the punchline is a joke about the view and not just a joke with vocabulary sprinkled on it — which feature does the laughter depend on, and what would someone who had only skimmed the stances miss? I'd rather have a joke that a specialist would groan at approvingly than one that a general reader would find merely pleasant.
