I'm a science-and-ideas journalist working on a feature about the replication crisis, and I keep tripping over a line that functions in this debate as a conversation-stopper. In a recent interview, the author of a much-publicised small-sample study — the familiar shape: an implausible mechanism, a large headline, and a later preregistered multi-lab replication that came back close to zero — used it symmetrically. "Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. But the claim that my effect doesn't exist is itself extraordinary, so that replication had better be extraordinary too, and one null result isn't."

I don't think that move works, but I can't yet say precisely where it fails, and I'd like to print something more careful than "the line is vague." So please treat the slogan the way you'd treat an argument that has to carry weight: separate the precise theses it could be expressing, and give verdicts.

Two words are doing all the work and neither is marked. "Extraordinary" might mean low prior probability given well-confirmed background theory; low prior probability given what the field currently believes (an orthodoxy-relative rather than evidence-relative notion); threatening to a lot of auxiliary commitments at once; merely unfamiliar or newsworthy; or simply consequential if acted on. "Require" might mean: a more stringent statistical threshold before the claim counts as supported; more evidence of the same kind (bigger samples, more studies); evidence of a different kind (mechanistic, independently measured, preregistered, open); a shifted burden of proof in a dialectical or editorial sense; or a heavier penalty for being wrong, i.e. an asymmetric loss function.

Lay the resulting combinations out as a menu rather than defending one. For each: what it actually says, whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible methodological norm, or false, and — the part I most need — whether it would license the use my interviewee made of it. In particular I want to know: whether any reading makes the principle genuinely symmetric as between a surprising positive result and a surprising failure to reproduce one; whether the Bayesian-looking reading is even coherent as stated, or whether a fixed evidential standard plus a low prior already does the work the slogan misdescribes; and whether, inside a strict error-rate framework, the slogan is inexpressible, collapses into a uniform recommendation about thresholds, or survives as something like a claim about a literature's false-discovery budget rather than about any single hypothesis.

My own provisional taxonomy is prior-relative, loss-relative and dialectical readings, plus that last "field-level error budget" one as a fourth. Tell me if the fourth is a real reading and if the taxonomy misses any. Please keep the general Bayesian-versus-frequentist quarrel in the background, except where it bears on which readings are available at all. If nothing in the menu survives, give me the one-sentence version I could responsibly print instead.
