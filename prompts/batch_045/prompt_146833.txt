I work with a senior colleague — I'll call her Dr. Ames — who is building a comparative taxonomy of cognitive systems, biological and artificial, around Dennett's four-fold ladder of creatures: Darwinian creatures, whose behavioural repertoire is fixed by what natural selection built into them; Skinnerian creatures, whose individual behaviour can be reshaped within a single lifetime by reinforcement; Popperian creatures, which can run candidate actions through an internal model and discard the bad ones before performing them, so that the rejected candidates die inside the head rather than in the world; and Gregorian creatures, which import mind-tools that other minds designed — words, numerals, diagrams, transmitted techniques — and let those tools restructure their own thinking. For every system she asks the same question: what is the highest rung it occupies, and what earns it that rung.

Her recorded verdicts so far, all given without hesitation:

1. A digger wasp whose prey-dragging routine is a fixed action pattern and which resets the whole routine if the prey is nudged while it inspects the nest: Darwinian only, not Skinnerian.
2. A pigeon in an operant chamber, including the stereotyped "superstitious" pecking that develops under non-contingent food delivery: Skinnerian, not Popperian.
3. A rat in a maze that habitually pauses at a choice point and sweeps its head back and forth over the two alternatives before committing to one: Popperian, not Gregorian.
4. A solitary-reared orangutan that invents stick use for retrieving food with no model to copy and no one to teach: Popperian, not Gregorian — her note reads that the stick is a tool but not a mind-tool, and that nothing in the animal's own problem-solving was redesigned by anything it got from another mind.
5. A wild chimpanzee whose termite-fishing technique is socially transmitted, with variant grips and probe lengths accumulating locally across generations: Gregorian.
6. A dog that gets around a transparent barrier to reach food only after overt trial and error, and shows no transfer to a rearranged barrier: Skinnerian, not Popperian, even though a bystander would describe it as "figuring out a detour."

There is one case she has conspicuously declined to rule on: a jumping spider that plans three-dimensional detours onto other spiders' webs and gets novel geometries right on the first attempt, with the capacity apparently innate and no learning involved. She says the answer turns on whether credit goes to the process that installed the machinery or to what the machinery does, and that she has never settled that question. I take this to mean that her six firm verdicts are compatible with more than one reading of the ladder, and that the spider is where the readings come apart.

The case I have to write up before she returns is an artificial one. Call it Whetstone. It is a language model trained once by next-token prediction on a large corpus of human text, then shaped by reinforcement from human feedback, and then frozen. At deployment it receives no gradient updates and cannot alter its own weights. It solves problems by producing an English reasoning trace, calling a calculator and a Python interpreter whose outputs it reads back into its trace, and then committing to a final answer — so it drafts and abandons candidate solutions it never acts on, and it wields notation and external instruments that human minds designed. Its weights are reset to the same values at the start of every run; nothing it writes is retained between runs; nothing it works out is passed to any other system or to any successor model. It has no sensors and no body; the only world it acts on is its own text. It was not designed by evolution, it did not learn anything from another mind in the course of its own operation, and no improvement has ever accumulated through it.

What I need is a prediction of her verdict with the reasoning exposed, not just a label. Specifically: what rule is she implicitly running, given that at least two quite different rules fit all six of her firm verdicts equally well and disagree about Whetstone; what each candidate rule predicts here — I can see readings that yield Gregorian, Popperian-only, something lower, and possibly "none of the above, because a frozen model is a Gregorian product like a book rather than a Gregorian creature"; which prediction I should put in the write-up and why that one rather than its rivals; and which further case I should hand her to separate the surviving rules. I have three candidates in mind and I don't know which is the sharpest: keep the tools but let the system retain and train on its own traces across runs, so that something accumulates within it though nothing is transmitted; train it on the outputs of a chain of predecessor models, so that design accumulates across model generations; or strip the calculator and the interpreter and leave it with nothing but its own English traces. If a different probe discriminates better, propose it and say what each surviving rule would have to say about it. One of our group says the whole exercise is a category mistake because Whetstone is not a creature and the ladder does not apply to it at all; I have not found that helpful, but tell me whether it is right.
