A disagreement at work has turned, unexpectedly, into a problem in the philosophy of disposition ascriptions, and I would like help getting clear about its structure before I have to write something down.

I work on reliability documentation for safety-critical equipment. Our specifications say of a given unit that it is, or is not, prone to a particular failure mode under a specified stress. Recently we shipped a mitigation layer alongside the unit: a monitor that detects the precursor signature of the failure and shuts the unit down before the failure completes. Half my colleagues now say the unit is no longer prone to that failure — the mitigation is part of the unit as delivered, and under the specified stress the failure does not occur. The other half say the underlying mechanism is untouched and that all we have done is interfere with the manifestation. Two further complications are on the table: in the field the monitor catches the precursor on only a fraction of occasions, and every actuation slightly degrades the substrate, so that the unit's tendency to fail is not constant across repeated exposures.

Reading a little on my own, I have got as far as the following picture, which I would like corrected where it is wrong. The naive conditional analysis says that a thing is disposed to manifest M under condition C just in case, were it in C, it would M. It is undermined by two standard kinds of counterexample: the fink, in which the stimulus itself removes the disposition, so that the counterfactual fails though the disposition is intuitively present; and the mask, in which the disposition survives but something triggered by, or present alongside, the stimulus prevents the manifestation. Lewis's repair, as I understand it, evaluates the counterfactual with the object's intrinsic basis of the disposition held fixed in the antecedent, allows some time to elapse between stimulus and manifestation, and requires only that the stimulus together with that basis would be a cause of the manifestation that is complete apart from contributions extrinsic to the object.

What I cannot work out is how any of this handles the cases my colleagues are actually arguing about, which are not clean finks or clean masks.

First, graded interference. Suppose the interfering mechanism operatures on only some occasions, so that under repeated exposure to C the failure occurs on, say, thirty percent of trials. Is the unit disposed to fail or not? The single-case counterfactual seems either false or indeterminate, and yet it seems wrong to say the disposition has simply been removed.

Second, built-in versus external interference. Does it matter whether the interfering mechanism is one of the object's own parts, as with a breaker wired into the unit, or something outside it, as with packaging around a fragile item? My sense is that the causal clause in the repaired analysis is doing work here, and that it may treat the two differently — but I cannot tell whether that is a feature of the analysis or a symptom of its arbitrariness.

Third, interference that changes the object. Each activation of the mitigation leaves the unit slightly different, so the disposition at one time is not the disposition at the next. A counterfactual evaluated once, at a single time, seems to miss something that matters to us practically, and I want to know whether the right response is to index dispositions to times, to move to a multi-trial or propensity-style account, or to identify the disposition with its causal basis and let the basis drift.

So, concretely, I would like an explanation of the following. What, at bottom, is the distinction between a fink and a mask supposed to consist in — is it just a claim about whether the disposition is retained, and by what independent test could we tell, given that the retention claim and the counterfactual seem to be settled by the same considerations? How does the repaired conditional analysis fare on graded, built-in, and self-altering interference, and does handling these require giving up the single-case counterfactual altogether in favour of a propensity or basis-identity account? And is the engineering predicate prone to even the same notion as disposed to, or is ours irreducibly a matter of degree and of frequencies across trials, in which case the whole fink-and-mask apparatus may be addressed to a question we were never asking?

I want to be able to explain to my colleagues why this disagreement is not merely verbal, and what the genuine options are. I do not need pointers to the literature and I have no way to look anything up at the moment; what I am after is the conceptual map and the pressure points in it.
