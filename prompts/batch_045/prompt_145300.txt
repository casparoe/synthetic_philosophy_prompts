I run a weekly 80-minute discussion section for a first-year Ethics and Everyday Life course: 18 students, one flat-ish room with movable chairs, no AV, and I'd rather not have them on their phones. Next week's topic is everyday conventions — tipping, queueing, jaywalking — whether these are moral obligations or just etiquette with gossip attached, and in particular whether a visitor has to follow the local version.

The problem is they've all heard when in Rome about a thousand times. Left to talk, they say "it depends on the culture lol" and we're finished in ten minutes. So I want them to run into the thing instead of discussing it.

My half-formed plan: invent a small made-up town (so nobody's actual knowledge about, say, tipping norms in Japan does any work), split them into locals and visitors, hand the locals a private sheet with three or four local practices — around crossing the street, queueing at a bakery, and leaving money at a café — and let the visitors blunder through a few staged encounters. Then swap some people over and debrief. That's as far as I've got and it feels thin.

What I want them to feel rather than be told:

- that these rules are arbitrary in content and still genuinely binding. Not "it's polite to," but: you cannot fail to conform without thereby saying something. There's no neutral option.
- that the very same physical behaviour means different things depending on who does it. This is the one I most want and my plan doesn't get near it. A local who jumps the queue in a jokey, stylised, everyone-knows-it's-a-joke way is doing something different from a visitor doing the identical thing, and I want that to land as a difference in the action, not just in how people react.
- that conforming can also be the offence. Doing the local thing too exactly, or doing it visibly as a guest, can read as mockery or as slumming — so when in Rome isn't automatically the safe answer.
- that locals pay the cost of keeping the practice going and visitors can just leave, and whether that changes what the visitor owes.

Two more things I'd like built in if you can: one practice that the locals themselves beleive is a moral rule rather than a custom (I want the gap between "we do this" and "this is right" to show up in the play rather than in the discussion), and one where a newcomer genuinely can't tell whether it's a rule of the town or just how this one particular person happens to behave.

Constraints: no lecture up front; they shouldn't hear the words convention, coordination, or the Roman proverb from me until after the debrief. Locals need some way of sanctioning visitors that isn't just them being rude — some scoring thing so the visitors actually feel the cost — but no real money and nothing that leaves anyone genuinely embarrassed in front of 17 other people. I'd also rather be a bystander during the play than a referee.

So: the town and its practices, the roles and what each role knows at the start, a timeline with minutes on it, and a debrief that maps specific moments back onto those four points. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which part of the "are conventions obligations" question a simulation like this just can't reach, so I know what I have to say out loud myself.
