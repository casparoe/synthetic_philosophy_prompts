I sit on a small advisory group that a finance ministry set up to comment on the population assumptions behind long-run fiscal projections, and the work has made me realise that I have no settled view about whether a larger population is, in itself, a good thing. I can recite the labels — total against average, the repugnant conclusion, the non-identity problem — but they don't deliver verdicts on the cases I actually face, and it's the cases where my confidence lives. So I want to start there. Priya and Daniel have one child and are deciding about a second; the second child would be clearly happy, but Mira's share of their time and money would fall, their own well-being would fall, and average well-being in the family would end up lower. I think they should go ahead, and that the outcome is better — though I don't want to say better by much, and I don't want to say they're required to. Nina could have a child who would affect no one else at all: no siblings, no transfers, no strain on anything, and the child's life would be mildly worth living but well below the level of everyone around her. My verdict here is different: I can't see this as an improvement that gives Nina any reason, and yet I also don't want to call it worse, and I resist saying it would be wrong. And when I compare a world of three billion people living rich lives with a world of those same people plus twenty-seven billion additional people whose lives are only just worth living, I don't think the bigger world is better; pressed, I'd say the smaller one is, or that they aren't comparable. I take it those three verdicts can't all survive together, and I want to know which one goes and what the surviving principle costs.

The cases that actually come up in my job are messier, and they're the ones I care about. In one valley there are about nine hundred speakers of a minority language, and it takes somewhere around twelve hundred to support a school, a newspaper and a teacher-training programme; below that floor the language is gone within two generations. Five hundred extra people, each of them living only a mildly good life, would cross the floor — and I find myself thinking that the larger population is genuinely better, not merely instrumentally good for the nine hundred, because something comes into existence that couldn't exist at the smaller size. That verdict worries me: is it just a threshold in a shared good, or am I valuing a culture over the people in it, and if so doesn't the same logic tell me that a population big enough to sustain something I thoroughly disapprove of — a sect that needs a thousand members to keep a doctrine alive, or a demographic majority needed to keep a nasty party in power — is better too? There's also the question of the route: crossing that floor requires the women now of childbearing age to have more children than they want and to carry nearly all the cost, while the benefit lands on their grandchildren, and this makes me much less willing to call the change better all things considered, even holding the endpoint fixed — yet I can't see how to build that in without sliding into a view on which Priya and Daniel's second child is impermissible as well. Then there's the couple who want a fourth child partly because the country will need taxpayers to pay pensions, which strikes me as a bad reason, though I can't tell whether that's a point about population ethics or about what kind of consideration may move an agent at all. And the case I keep reversing on: a couple who very much want a child and know that the child will live through serious climate damage, with a life still worth living but much harder than their own — sometimes I think of course it's fine, our own lives are hard and fine, sometimes I think they'd be handing someone a burden they helped create.

The best I can do by way of a principle is this: adding a person makes the outcome better just when their life is (i) worth living at all, (ii) above some critical level well above zero — I want to say, a life you could be glad to have given someone — and (iii) compatible with no existing person being pushed below the level at which their own life stops being worth living; and (iv) a larger population is additionally better when it crosses a floor at which goods like a language, a school, a clinic or a research community first become possible at all. Separately, I want to claim that even when a larger population is better in that sense, the fact is only sometimes a reason for anyone in particular, and never a reason that overrides what the people who would bear the costs of getting there actually want. Please test that against my cases and repair it where it breaks: I suspect clauses (ii) and (iii) are doing incompatible work, and that (iv) is either redundant — just (i) applied to future people's well-being — or smuggles in a non-welfarist value I haven't owned up to. Give me one principle, or at most two or three rivals, each clause tied to the case that motivates it; be explicit about whether you're giving me a betterness relation over outcomes or a view about reasons and permissibility, since I keep sliding between the two; tell me plainly which of my verdicts each candidate forces me to give up; and then tell me what it predicts about two cases I haven't put to you — a state that stops subsidising births and instead admits a large number of immigrants, and a couple who have a third child mainly so that their two existing children will have a sibling and the family name will continue. I would rather be told that one of my verdicts has to go than be handed a principle with six adjustable dials.
