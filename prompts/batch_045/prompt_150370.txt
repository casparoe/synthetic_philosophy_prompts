I've been arguing about AI-generated images and music with the same group of people for roughly two years now, and I'm finally writing up where I've landed as a short sequence of posts. Before I publish it I'd like an audit of the package rather than a defence of it. Here are the things I take myself to believe, stated a little more crisply than I actually believe them:

1. Creativity is a functional property. A process is creative to the extent that it produces artefacts that are novel, valuable, and not reachable by any short mechanical recipe. No consciousness, understanding, intention, or felt struggle is required. On this view a good image generator is creative, and so are evolution and a decent search algorithm.

2. I am an anti-intentionalist about artistic meaning. There is no privileged interpretation that the maker's intentions fix, and the death-of-the-author point was correct before generative models existed; I don't want to abandon it now that it has inconvenient consequences.

3. I am a realist about achievement value: part of what we properly value in a work is that a person actually did something difficult, and that the object is the surviving record of a mind having solved a real problem. I don't think this reduces to snobbery or status competition, though I concede that in lots of particular cases it does.

4. I take the readymades to show that labour and craft are not where the value of art lies. But I don't think they show that causal history is irrelevant: in that case the achievement was conceptual rather than manual, which is still an achievement.

5. Aesthetic supervenience: two artefacts that are perceptually indistinguishable to a competent viewer cannot differ in aesthetic value. My sealed-portfolio case is the one I lean on — two portfolios, identical to me across many sittings, one of which I'm told was made by a person. I think the right answer is that there is nothing aesthetically at stake in which one you pick, and that the residual feeling that there is I call provenance leakage, on a par with the premium people put on authentic replicas.

6. Methodologically, I want to be someone whose aesthetic responses track the object rather than the label. When I notice myself caring about provenance I treat it as evidence that I am consuming art as a status game rather than for its own sake, and I try to discount it in myself.

7. I think the strongest of the standard objections to AI art is the training-data one — consent, appropriation, who gets paid — and I hold that it is a separate question from the aesthetic one, so that my aesthetic view need not wait on it.

I have no reliable sense of which of these is load-bearing; if I had to guess which one a critic would target, I'd probably guess wrong. So please do the following:

Go through the pairs and tell me which are genuinely in tension, ranked by severity rather than listed as equally bad, with what would have to give in each case and what the cheapest restatement would cost me elsewhere in the set — which other commitments I would then have to rephrase, and whether anything downstream in my argument collapses. Tell me separately which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection and why, since two of my interlocutors have told me that 2 and 3 cannot both be true and I have never been able to see why. Flag any commitment that is doing no work at all. And finish by naming the single revision that would clean up the most, and the one objection you'd expect from someone who shares 1 and 5 but not 3.

I'd rather you didn't spend the answer telling me which named position in the literature this most resembles; I want the machinery, not the map. Please write in British English.
