I help allocate a pretty small health budget at work — clinics, two outreach vans, some vaccination stuff — and I'm constantly making calls about who to prioritise with nothing I can actually state as a reason. So I sat down, wrote out a bunch of paired cases with my gut verdicts, and now I want to know what I believe, if it adds up to anything at all. In the reading group I go to, one person says I'm obviously a prioritarian and should just own it; another says my verdicts are really egalitarian and I'm going to run into the levelling-down objection sooner or later. Here they are, welfare on a rough 0–100 scale so we're not arguing about units:

1. Same total, different spread: a group at 50/50 versus a group at 90/10. I prefer 50/50, and not because of diminishing marginal money or anything like that — I just think it's the better outcome.

2. Who to help when the gains differ: lifting someone at 30 up to 40, versus lifting someone at 80 up to 55. I take the worse off person, even though the second option buys more welfare overall.

3. Straight levelling down: one group at 90 and another at 45, versus everyone at 40. I think the second is worse, clearly, and I don't think "at least no one is worse off than anyone else" is really a consideration. But I keep having the thought anyway and I can't tell whether that's a judgment I should own or a feeling I should discount.

4. The one that worries me: the person at 90 drops to 60 so that the person at 20 can go to 40. Total goes down by 10. My gut says yes, do it — at least when the person at 20 is in chronic pain with kids going hungry. My friend says that's the levelling-down objection in a prioritarian hat, and I don't have a reply.

5. Numbers: 100 people at 15 versus 10 people at 70. I take the ten, definately, and I don't even feel torn. But if priority to the worst off is what's doing the work in cases 1 and 2, I don't see how I avoid saying the hundred are better — they're all badly off, which is supposed to count for more, not less.

6. Lives versus moments: someone who is miserable from twenty to twenty-five and then does fine for sixty years, versus someone who is flatly mediocre the whole way through. Judging whole lives I'd rather have the first life. Deciding who to help today, I also want to help the first person. I can't tell whether those two verdicts come from the same principle or whether I'm switching units mid-thought.

7. Fault: two people at the same low level, one because of a disability she's had since birth, one because he quit a decent job to write a novel. I want to help both at the same weight, and I feel slightly bad about that but not enough to change it.

So: is there one account that gets these, or gets almost all of them? I'd rather have the principle than a tour of the literature. Specifically: (a) does the weighting track how badly off someone is in absolute terms, or how badly off they are compared with everyone else, and does that choice matter for case 1; (b) does it apply to whole lives or to person-moments, and what does that do to case 6; (c) which of my verdicts does it force me to drop, and is 3-or-4 the pair where I have to give something up; (d) what does the resulting view say about cases I haven't listed — a badly off person who's perfectly content and doesn't know they're badly off, and the same question about a badly off animal.

And honestly: if I am some kind of prioritarian, does the levelling-down objection touch me at all, or is the real objection the one in case 4 — loosing 30 so that someone else can gain 20? And if I need priority plus a separate concern about equality in order to save case 1, am I just back in case 3 with no way out?
