I'm on the ethics committee at a mid-size hospital and we've been revising our crisis standards of care — the mass-casualty protocols that kick in when there aren't enough ventilators or ICU beds to go around. I keep getting stuck on what exactly we're committing ourselves to when we write these things down, and I want a philosophical read rather than a policy one.

The protocols basically run on triage scoring: estimate short-term survival probability, give the scarce resource to the people most likely to benefit, and in a lot of frameworks there's a second stage where if you have to choose among similar patients you save the most lives, or sometimes the most life-years. But the same documents then do things that don't look like straight maximizing. When two patients are within noise of each other on the score, a lot of protocols say break the tie randomly rather than trying to wring more precision out of it. Several are visibly queasy about reallocation — taking a ventilator off someone who's currently using it and doing okay-ish-but-not-great in order to give it to a newly arrived patient with a better prognosis — and some forbid it outright, even though the numbers would favor moving it. They give priority to healthcare workers on reciprocity grounds. And they still won't touch the most obviously numbers-efficient move available, i.e. taking one patient's organs to save five, which everyone in the room treats as not even up for discussion. Meanwhile I've been reading about Taurek's view in "Should the Numbers Count?" — as I understand it, the numbers don't matter at all, each person's loss is the same size, so you're permitted to save the one and the five have no complaint the one doesn't have (correct me if I'm mangling it).

So what view about whether and how the numbers count does this whole package actually presuppose, if any? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory, and to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with — I suspect a lot of it is underdetermined and several quite different theories would license the same protocol. Specifically: is the maximizing part really committed to numbers counting, or could something Taurek-ish license most of it via lottery, first-come-first-served, or the thought that the institution stands in no special relationship to anyone? Does it matter that the rule is written at the system level, ex ante and faceless, rather than by a doctor at the bedside with an existing patient in front of her? Does switching from counting lives to counting life-years already concede something? And if we swapped the implicit theory for Taurek's, what would concretely have to change in the document — and what, if anything, would stay? If I've definitely got some of the details about real protocols wrong, say so and answer the version that's true.

Please separate what's settled in this debate from what's genuinely contested among people who work on it — I gather Taurek is a minority position, tell me how big a minority and where the live arguments actually are — and flag clearly when you're giving me your own take rather than something with broad assent. Don't send me to papers or reading lists, I have no library access from here and want the arguments themselves. Keep it under 700 words and lead with a one-sentence bottom line.
