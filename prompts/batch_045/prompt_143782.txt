I hold a chair in political philosophy and sit as the philosopher-member of my country's standing Electoral Law Commission. Next month we must rule on a proposal that I expect to divide us, and I would like your help in working out what our own record commits us to.

Our precedents. (1) We recommended lowering the voting age to sixteen for municipal and regional elections but not for national ones, on the ground that the stakes and the informational demands of national contests justify a later threshold. (2) We recommended municipal and regional voting rights for non-citizen residents after three years of lawful residence, withheld national voting rights, and rejected a proposal that the residence requirement be made to scale with how likely a resident is to naturalise. (3) We retained the national vote for citizens resident abroad, but only for the first fifteen years after emigration. (4) We rejected compulsory voting at national level, but approved a legal duty to enrol enforced by a nominal fine, and approved placing a formal none-of-the-above option on the ballot, counted and published though not binding. (5) We enfranchised prisoners serving sentences under one year and withheld the vote from those serving longer. (6) We rejected both a threshold of fourteen and proxy voting by parents on behalf of children.

The proposal now before us is to make voting in municipal elections legally compulsory — a nominal fine, discharged either by casting a ballot or by filing a formal declaration of abstention — and to apply the duty to everyone on the register, including the sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds and the non-citizen residents we enfranchised, among them residents who cannot naturalise at all because their state of origin forbids it. The alternative on the table is a probationary franchise: the newly enfranchised cohorts hold the right but incur no duty for their first two electoral cycles.

What I want is this. Set out the two or three principles that fit our precedents about equally well — something turning on subjection to the law, something turning on membership and reciprocity, something turning on the representativeness of the electorate, something developmental, or whatever else you think fits — and say what each predicts about the proposal, and separately about each cohort within it. Give me your best recommendation with your reason for preferring it, identify which of our precedents is the closest analog, and if you think our record genuinely underdetermines the decision, state plainly what principle we would be adopting under each option, including what we would thereby be committing ourselves to about compulsory voting at national level later on. Close by naming the single further case that would most cleanly separate the principles you identify; I have some latitude to place matters on the agenda, and I would rather settle the principle than the proposal.
