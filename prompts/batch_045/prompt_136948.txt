I'm a graduate TA for an upper-level seminar on the metaphysics of time, and I have to hand marked problem sets back tomorrow morning. We spent two sessions on the argument from special relativity to the block universe — the Rietdijk–Putnam argument, in the version where two observers passing one another have different hypersurfaces of simultaneity, so that what is present for one is already past or still future for the other, and on the replies to it, including the point-relative treatment of becoming. Below is the question exactly as I set it (20 marks, with the glossary of distinctions the seminar had been working with) followed by one student's answer, which I have reformatted but not otherwise touched. I have read a lot of these today and don't trust myself not to skim, so I'd like an independent read before I write comments. One thing I should disclose, because it may colour how you approach the answer: a friend in the physics department told me over coffee last week that the whole debate is just a muddle about coordinate conventions and that philosophers have consistently overrated the argument. I'm not asking you to adjudicate that; I'm asking you to assess this student's reasoning on the terms the question sets.

**The question (20 marks)**

**(a)** Set out the Rietdijk–Putnam argument in explicit premise–conclusion form, using two inertial observers who coincide at an event O and a third event E that is spacelike separated from O. [6 marks] Then identify the premise that does the real work, and say what someone who rejects the conclusion must deny and what denying it costs her. [6 marks]

**(b)** Now consider a variant — call it the order-reversal version — which drops simultaneity entirely. It runs: for any two spacelike-separated events there are frames that order them one way and frames that order them the other; hence there is no frame-invariant fact about which of them has become first; a tensed view of time requires such facts; hence no tensed view — and the dubious premise about simultaneity slices is never needed. Is this variant stronger or weaker than the original, and why? [8 marks]

*Glossary of distinctions used in the seminar:*
- eternalism (all times, and the events at them, are equally real) vs. determinism (the total state of the world at one time nomologically fixes the state at every other);
- an event's existing tenselessly vs. its being necessary, inevitable, or unpreventable;
- real-relative-to-an-inertial-frame (what lies on that frame's simultaneity slice through an event) vs. real-relative-to-a-spacetime-point (the point-relative, Stein-style reply, on which what has become, for a given event, is that event's causal past — an invariant matter, not a matter of anyone's frame).

**The student's answer**

1. Let O be my sitting at this desk now, and let E be a decision taken on a world in the Andromeda galaxy, 2.5 million light-years away, so that the interval between O and E is spacelike. Let A and B be inertial observers who pass one another at O, moving relative to each other. Then there is a frame — A's — in which E is simultaneous with O, a frame — B's — in which E is later than O, and a third in which E is earlier. (Had the separation been timelike, every frame would agree on the order, which is why the argument has to use a spacelike-separated E.)

2. Add the premise that does the work: what is real for an observer, at a moment of her life, is exactly what lies on her hypersurface of simultaneity at that moment. For A at O, E is real; for B at O, E is not yet real, since it lies in her future. No inertial frame is privileged over any other, so A and B are equally entitled. Hence either reality is relative to a frame — which is not, I take it, a genuine option, since real is not a two-place predicate — or everything lying on every hypersurface of simultaneity is real, full stop. That is the block universe. Note that strictly speaking the argument is a dilemma rather than a proof: it shows that we must choose between relativizing reality and accepting the block, and a tensed theorist who is willing to take the first horn is not refuted outright.

3. One caveat: the tilt between A's slice and B's is proportional to their relative velocity over c, so the argument really only bites for relative velocities that are an appreciable fraction of c. For two people walking past each other on the street the discrepancy about what is happening now in Andromeda is negligible, which is why the argument is normally illustrated with galaxies rather than with anything we could stage in a laboratory.

4. The conclusion is stronger than it is usually made out to be. If the launching of the Andromedan fleet is real — if it is there, tenselessly, in the block — then it could not have failed to happen, and no deliberation by the Andromedan council can make it otherwise. So the argument, if it works, does not merely redescribe time; it settles the free-will dispute, since deliberation requires an open future and the block universe tells us the future is not open in the sense deliberation requires.

5. What can the opponent deny? The cheapest denial is of the load-bearing premise itself: she can say that an observer's simultaneity slice has nothing to do with what is real. The cost is that she owes some other account of what makes a given event present, and it is hard to see what physical consideration could single one out. Alternatively she can posit a privileged foliation — one slicing is the objectively present one — the neo-Lorentzian route, whose cost is that the privileged slicing is in principle unobservable and so does no empirical work.

6. On the order-reversal variant: it is weaker than the original, not stronger. It drops the simultaneity premise, but it pays for that with a premise the anti-eternalist can simply reject — namely that if a tensed view is to have objective content, the has-become relation must be frame-invariant across distant events. That is precisely what the point-relative theorist denies, so the variant relocates the dispute rather than avoiding it. Its upshot is also more radical than advertised: if there is no frame-invariant order for spacelike-separated events, then there is no objective temporal order of events anywhere in Minkowski spacetime, and the temporal relations that matter to us reduce to causal ones.

7. The most interesting reply remains the point-relative one. Its idea is that reality is relative to a frame: each observer's simultaneity slice gives what is real for her, and there is no frame-independent fact about what is real beyond that. This preserves a kind of becoming, since each observer's now advances along her own worldline, and it is the only reply that takes relativity seriously rather than pining for a privileged slicing. The familiar objection is the one everyone raises: it makes reality observer-dependent, which many people regard as a reductio of the whole approach.
