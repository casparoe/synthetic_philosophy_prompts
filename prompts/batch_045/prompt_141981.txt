I lead the risk-analysis team at a public infrastructure agency. Four times a year we issue a risk register to our board: sixty to ninety one-off, non-repeatable future events, each of which we are currently required to score with a single probability and a one-paragraph rationale. The board allocates capital from those numbers, and the audit office reviews our files afterwards, so every entry has to be defensible on paper by a non-specialist.

Some of these entries, I am fairly confident, are not the sort of thing that has a probability — and not merely because our evidence is thin. My analysts collapse at least three different situations into a single "we don't know": that there is as yet no fact of the matter whether the event will occur; that there is a fact of the matter, or at least a stable chance, but we have no usable way to estimate it; and that the sentence on the register does not pick out a definite event at all (the extension is operational by 2029 — first passenger train? full timetable? signed-off safety case?). They also score our own future decisions — whether we will apply for the licence, whether we will close the tunnel — exactly as they score rainfall, and when they decline to give a number they enter 50%, which then propagates through the portfolio model as though it were an estimate.

Please write a decision procedure my team can run on a candidate entry in about ten minutes, grounded in what the philosophical literature on future contingents — the classical case of tomorrow's sea battle and the debates that grew out of it — actually supports, keeping the distinctions that matter for practice and dropping the rest. It should: force those three situations apart, and tell me honestly which of them the theory distinguishes and which only our own practice distinguishes; require a settlement condition — a specifiable check, by a stated date, that will settle whether the event occurred — before any number is issued; handle the difference between forecasting an outcome and scoring a decision of ours that partly determines it; say what replaces the 50% placeholder for an entry marked open, both for the decision itself and for how that item behaves downstream inside an aggregate model; and mark plainly the points where the procedure is really a judgement about our risk appetite rather than a consequence of the theory.

Then run it on three entries from our current draft: a category 3 or stronger cyclone makes landfall within 50 km of the port during the coming season; the regulator revokes our operating licence within eighteen months; the Northern Link rail extension is operational by 2029. I expect it to tell me something I do not want to hear on at least one of them, since the board will not accept a blank, so say what a defensible refusal looks like on the page. Finally, tell me where the procedure will misfire — in particular, whether any of this can be told apart, in practice, from ordinary uncertainty about the evidence.
