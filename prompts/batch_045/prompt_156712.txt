Almost every wealthy legal system has converged on the same strangely shaped set of rules about the human body, and I want to know what a person would have to believe for that shape to make sense.

You may give a kidney away for nothing: the law permits it, hospitals facilitate it, and public bodies encourage it with medals, tax credits, or priority on transplant waiting lists. You may not sell one. Attaching a price turns the identical transfer into a crime, and the informed, uncoerced, mutually beneficial consent of both parties is not even a defence — donors are prosecuted alongside brokers. Yet the same legal order will let you be paid for plasma, for eggs or sperm, for hair, for breast milk, and for the risks of a phase-one drug trial; will let you risk your life for money as a logger, a boxer, or a security contractor; and, in a number of jurisdictions, will hold that tissue removed during your surgery is not your property at all — the Supreme Court of California so held in 1990 — so that clinicians may patent a cell line derived from it while you may not sell it. Meanwhile the state may reward the gift with reimbursement of travel, lost wages, and childcare, or with honours and tax benefits, provided no price is ever negotiated between the parties. And the same codes retain a small residue of offences with no complainant whatever: sexual activity with a corpse, commerce in human remains, and, in many jurisdictions, paid surrogacy.

A friend tells me all of this is of a piece with what moral-foundations psychologists call purity or sanctity, and that the opposition to such markets is psychologically distinctive — quick, affect-laden, hard to argue people out of even after their stated reasons have been knocked down — and distributed very unevenly across the political spectrum. I don't know what to do with that. So, four questions.

First, reconstruct the implicit moral theory. What view of persons, bodies, or value does this pattern of rules only make sense under: that the body is inalienable rather than owned, that some treatments of it are degradations independent of any harm, that certain things must not enter the market at all, or something about the meaning of the gift? Be precise about the difference between what the practice strictly presupposes, what it merely fits well with, and where it is underdetermined — where several quite different theories would each license exactly these rules.

Second, apply your reconstruction to a test case I have been turning over. Suppose a state sets up a monopsony: it alone buys kidneys from living donors at a single fixed statutory price, with no negotiation, no intermediaries, mandatory independent counselling, a six-month cooling-off period during which the donor may withdraw, donor and recipient anonymity, allocation by medical criteria alone, and state-funded lifetime follow-up care for the donor. Which of your candidate theories forbids this, which permits it, and why? Then remove the features one at a time — allow negotiation, allow private buyers, drop the cooling-off period — and say where each theory flips, since that tells me which feature is doing the real work in your reconstruction.

Third, take the reconstruction's own consistency seriously: name one currently permitted practice that the theory, honestly applied, seems to condemn, and say what the law would have to change if the theory were adopted as stated. If the theory cannot condemn anything else, say why that is not a sign you have gerrymandered it.

Fourth, and most important to me: does the psychological story about these intuitions tell us anything about whether the rules are right? Suppose the opposition really is carried by disgust sensitivity rather than by any harm judgement, that people abandon their reasons for it as soon as those reasons are shown not to apply, and that the sensitivity tracks political identity and is partly heritable. Does that support the prohibition, undermine it, or is it beside the point? Deal with the symmetry worry: harm-based and fairness-based responses also have evolutionary and psychological etiologies, are also triggered by cues that track nothing morally important, and are also politically patterned — so why doesn't the same debunking move consume them? And finally, distinguish the intuitions from the institution: if the prohibition had an independent rationale — the transplant system's dependence on trust, or the need to keep the poor from being harvested — does it matter that the appetite for enforcing it is psychological in origin, and what does a legal system presuppose about its own authority when it criminalises conduct on a rationale that roughly half the population does not share?
