Me and three mates have been going round in circles on this for about a month and I want something that settles it, or at least shows us where we actually disagree rather than where we think we disagree.

The thing we keep hitting is this. Me and my partner might try for a baby in the next year or so, my sister's doing IVF and has been asked about screening embryos, and one of the group works on a forty-year flood defence plan for the council. In every one of those, whatever we decide changes *which* people end up existing, not just how good their lives are. My mate says that means nobody is made worse off by the bad choice, so nothing is wrong with it. I think that's obviously dodgy but I can't say why without going in a loop.

So: write me a quiz that sorts someone by what they say about cases, not by which theory they think they like. About ten questions, each one a concrete situation with three or four answers to pick from, then a scoring key at the end that maps patterns of answers onto named positions, with a one-line plain-English gloss of each position and its most awkward consequence. Everyone in my group is happy to answer things like "that's wrong", "that's not wrong", "you can't compare those two", or "that's wrong but not because anyone's worse off" — so make the options that sort of verdict, not names of views. No question should mention a philosopher or ask anyone to identify a theory; if I wanted that I'd just read the wikipedia page.

Cases I want in there: a teenager who has a baby now when waiting a few years would mean a different, better-off child; a factory that leaks something which both causes a birth defect and, by luck, is also what led to that particular child being conceived at all; two hospital programmes with the same budget where one helps children who already exist and one changes which children get born; a flood defence or energy policy that changes which people are alive in fifty years; picking between embryos; and one where the choice changes how many people there are rather than just who.

But I also want the questions the usual versions of this never ask, because those are the ones we keep tripping over:

- Does it matter how *similar* the alternative person would have been? Say the other child would have had the same parents, born the same month, and a life that's 95% the same. Most of us feel that makes a difference and none of us can say why.
- Which "compared to what" is the right one: what you'd have done instead, or what would have happened if nobody had done anything? Our flood defence argument flips completely depending on this and we only noticed last week.
- What if you don't know whether your choice changes who exists — say you're about 50/50 on it?
- Does it matter if the badness is spread really thin across a million people instead of concentrated in one kid?

The leaves I want named (or marked "unnamed" if it's literally just us): the narrow view where it only counts if it's worse for the very same person; the view that identity doesn't matter at all; the in-between one where identity doesn't matter but how many people there are does; the full add-it-all-up impersonal one; the averaging one; the "those two outcomes just aren't comparable" one; the "it's wrong but not because anyone's worse off, more like it used them" one; and the asymmetric one where you've got a reason not to create a child who'd be miserable but no reason to create one who'd be happy.

And this is the bit I'm most curious about: build it out of the combinations of answers first, then tell me which combinations nobody actually holds, and for each empty one say whether it's empty because its incoherent, because it's just unattractive, or because nobody's bothered to work it out. Also be straight with me about which pairs of positions your quiz genuinely can't seperate — I'd rather know than get a tidy-looking key that fudges it.

Keep each question to a short paragraph, don't assume anyone's read any philosophy, and no reading lists, I've got enough of those already. British spellings please.
