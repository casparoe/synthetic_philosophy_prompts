Hey — I need a consistency check on my own view. I run a small philosophy-of-biology reading group with two people from the biology department and a postdoc who does protein design, and I promised them a two-page here's-what-I-actually-think-about-functions handout by thursday, so I finally sat down and wrote my own position down, and it looks considerably less tidy on paper than it does in my head. I've been teaching this material on and off for years and I always end up saying something to the students I can't quite defend when they push, which is presumably what writing it down is for. Here's the set, roughly in the order I'd defend it:

(1) Proper functions are etiological: a trait's function is the effect for which items of its type were selected, and designed artefacts get proper functions as a special case of the same historical story rather than as a rival kind of thing. (2) I'm also a pluralist in practice — I think the Cummins-style what-does-this-part-contribute-to-the-containing-system attributions do real explanatory work, I use them constantly when I talk to physiologists, and I don't think they're genuinely competing with (1), though I have never been able to say clearly why not. (3) Dysfunction is a real, normative, biologically load-bearing notion: that kidney isn't doing what it's supposed to do has to amount to more than that's statistically unusual or I don't like the downstream consequences, and functions are what are supposed to supply the supposed to. (4) I want function attributions to explain why the trait is there, and I want the account to vindiate what biologists actually do rather than correct them — including annotating a freshly sequenced open reading frame as the function of this is to methylate X on the strength of sequence similarity and a single assay, when nobody knows anything whatsoever about its selection history. (5) Three case verdicts I'm attached to: an enzyme that came out of twenty rounds of directed evolution in a lab, selected for binding some target, does have a proper function and not merely a current-contribution one; a protein that a generative model produces in a single pass from sequence data, with no round of selection and no one intending that particular molecule to do anything, does not have a proper function — call it a zero-history protein — even though it binds the target beautifully and would be indistinguishable from the first one in the tube; and the function of feathers in a modern bird is fixed by what selection has recently been doing, not by whatever the original selected effect was. I want all three to fall out of the account rather than be stipulated, and in particular I don't want to lean on something cheap like lab stuff doesn't count. (6) I want to allow that a trait can have E as its function even though no token of the type has ever actually produced E — the very first bearer of a beneficial variant, before selection has had time to act on it, already has the function as far as I'm concerned.

What I'd like is an audit, not a survey: go through the pairs, tell me which are genuinely in tension and how badly, which only look like they are, and for each real tension what exactly would have to give and what giving it up would cost me. Rank them by severity instead of listing them all as equally bad, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most at once. I'm not attached to any one of these — I'd much rather find the soft spot now than on thursday in front of a protein designer who has annoyingly good intuitions about this stuff.
