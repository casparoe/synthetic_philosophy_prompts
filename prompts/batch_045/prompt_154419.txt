I was arguing about this with a friend who does priority-setting at a different animal-advocacy org (I volunteer on the research side of a small one — we write grant recommendations, we don't run programs), and she said something I think is wrong but couldn't answer, and since then I've been trying to figure out what I actually believe by going case by case. My gut says: improving the housing of 10,000 broiler chickens beats preventing ~10 million fly larvae in a manure pit from existing and living out their three weeks, and by a lot, even though the numbers are a thousand times bigger and the chicken ask is only a marginal improvement. My gut also says: shrimp and prawns I'm happy to fund interventions on directly, there's some behavioural and physiological evidence there, but farmed black soldier fly larvae I basically ignore, even though those numbers are three orders of magnitude larger still. My gut gets queasy on a pesticide reform that would spare something like 10^12 insects a few seconds of distress each (order of magnitude, don't hold me to it) — the expected-value arithmetic seems to dominate everything else we could fund, but it feels like I'm being mugged, and I can't say why case three is a mug and case one isn't. I also think the probability question is one hypothesis — 'insects feel pain' — that's true or false for insects generally, not a per-species or per-animal lottery; my friend says it should be a base rate across the taxa we've actually bothered to study. I don't think we're even using the same kind of probability, and I suspect which one you use changes what the huge numbers do to the calculation. Two more verdicts: I count each adult body once — a fly that lives three weeks is one subject, not twenty-one, and a colony isn't one superorganism — and I'm much keener to fund research on insect sentience than direct insect interventions at the same low credence, which seems to imply the numbers matter somewhere but only through what we'd learn.

My tentative rule, which I don't trust at all: treat the sentience probability as a single all-or-nothing hypothesis, so the giant numbers scale one stake rather than getting multiplied by a pile of independent small chances, and then just apply expected value. But I can't explain why that should make the pesticide case feel less like a mugging, and it doesn't obviously deliver the shrimp-yes / soldier-fly-no split either, and it says nothing about why I count bodies the way I do.

Can you reconstruct a principle that gets most of these? I'd like it stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing which case, and I want you to be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to drop — I'd rather have a simple rule that overturns one of my gut calls than a baroque one that saves the the lot, but tell me both if there's a real tradeoff. Also tell me which of the two probability pictures is right, or whether that distinction is doing less work than I think, and what your principle says about two cases I haven't listed: a gene-drive program that collapses one pest species by 90% over a couple of decades (the individuals spared are future ones, and spread over time in a way I find hard to picture), and an insect species discovered to retain learned associations through metamorphosis.
