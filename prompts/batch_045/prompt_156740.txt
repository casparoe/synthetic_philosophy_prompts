hey — I've been chewing on this by myself for a couple of months. I have no philosophy background and nobody to talk to about it, so I'd really like blunt comments rather than encouragement. I got into it after an argument with my brother. He said it's fine to deliberately work yourself up into feeling grateful toward your in-laws, because it keeps the peace and makes you happier. I said that isn't what gratitude is for — you're manufacturing it — and if the reason you feel it is that it pays, then it isn't really gratitude, it's something else wearing gratitude's clothes. He said I was being precious. Later I found out people online call this general area the wrong kind of reasons problem, but I havent read any of the actual papers and probably can't get hold of them, so please don't give me a reading list — I literally can't follow up on one. Treat everything below as my own attempt and tell me where it collapses.

Here's how I'd set the problem up.

Some attitudes seem to have correctness conditions: fear fits what's dangerous, admiration fits what's admirable, belief fits what's true, resentment fits an actual offense. Call the features in the world that make the attitude fit its merit-making features — the danger, the admirability, the offense.

But you can also have perfectly ordinary practical reasons to get yourself into an attitude. A demon will give you a penny if you admire him. Believing your partner didn't cheat will hold the marriage together. Staying afraid of the dark keeps you out of the ravine at night. These feel like they aren't answering the same question. If I ask is he admirable? and you say admiring him will get you a penny, you've changed the subject. So there's a gap between reasons to have an attitude and reasons the attitude is correct, and that gap is what I'm interested in.

Now the parts I think are actually mine.

1. Three families of reasons that crowd into that gap.

(a) Payoff reasons: having the attitude produces some independent good — the penny, the marriage, the safety.

(b) Stewardship reasons: reasons that come from your history with your own attitude. You've admired your old teacher for thirty years; giving that up now would make those thirty years look wasted and would be a small betrayal of the person you were. Or you promised a dying friend you'd go on thinking well of someone.

(c) Calibration reasons: reasons drawn from facts about your own reliability. You find out you judge your ex-colleagues far more harshly than everyone else, so you have reason to dial the contempt down — not because they deserve less, but because your dial is off.

2. My claim is that these aren't one phenomenon. (a) is the easy case and I think everyone agrees it's the wrong kind. (c) looks like it might secretly be a right-kind reason wearing a disguise, because the fact that your dial is off is itself a fact about the fit between your attitude and the world. (b) is the genuinely interesting one, because it seems to have real authority over you in a way (a) doesn't, and yet it says nothing at all about the object.

3. The test I want to propose instead of the usual one. I don't like saying wrong-kind reasons are just reasons to bring it about that you have the attitude, because I can act on stewardship reasons too and that doesn't feel like the same thing as acting on payoff reasons. So: a reason R is of the right kind for attitude A toward object o only if R could show up in a good explanation of why someone who has A toward o has it — meaning R is the sort of thing that makes the attitude intelligible from the inside, from the point of view of the person who has it, without that person having to mention what the attitude does for them or where they've been. Penny-reasons and stewardship reasons fail this. Calibration reasons pass it, sort of, because I was overrating the threat is something I can say from inside the fear.

4. The consequence I'm not sure I like: on this test, the difference between the right kind and the wrong kind is a difference in what can explain, not a difference in reasons. So maybe the honest thing to say is that there aren't two kinds of reasons at all. There's one kind of reason, plus a separate question about which reasons are allowed to appear in the explanation of the attitude. Which would make the wrong kind of reasons problem close to a pseudo-problem, and move all the real work onto the notion of fittingness.

5. Where I think this bites: hope. For hope, (b) and (c) look like they might be most of the reasons there are. You keep hoping because stopping would dishonor something, or because your hope-meter is broken, not because the evidence supports the thing hoped for. If my test is right, then either hope doesn't have correctness conditions of the relevant sort, or a lot of what passes for reasons to hope is the wrong kind, which seems like a harsh result for an attitude people value.
