I want to stress-test a principle I keep relying on about how ontology is fixed. Call it the accessibility constraint: necessarily, if two candidate total descriptions of the world agree on every piece of information that any inquirer could in principle obtain — every possible observation, measurement outcome, or inference from such — then they do not differ in what there is; where rival ontologies diverge only over facts that are in principle inaccessible, there is no fact of the matter, and the appropriate response is not suspense but rejection of the question as indeterminate.

The wrinkle I cannot resolve is that the accessible information may not come in a single maximal package. In quantum mechanics there are pairs of quantities such that either one could be obtained but no procedure yields both together, and there are distinct preparations that no measurement can distinguish with certainty; so the phrase "every piece of information that could in principle be obtained" seems to range over a collection with no largest member, leaving no single body of accessible information against which two ontologies can be compared. Does the constraint then collapse into vacuity — never able to identify an ontology because it can never be applied — or must it posit a privileged specification of what is accessible, and is that itself a cost, given that nothing in the accessible information appears to single one out?

Give me the map of what would count against the constraint, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and separate what its defenders would themselves concede as counting against it from what only its critics assert. Include the structuralist version of the worry: if what is accessible is exhausted by relational or causal-nomological structure, the principle may identify no ontology at all rather than identify one. Note where the elasticity of "in principle" insulates the principle from any test, whether that insulation is a defect for a claim of this kind, and whether my use of it — against disputes whose resolution would leave no trace in any obtainable record, such as claims about what occupies regions from which no signal can ever reach us — is overreach. Distinguish what is settled among people who work on these questions from what is genuinely contested, mark your own confidence, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
