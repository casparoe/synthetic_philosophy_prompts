I'm a second-year undergraduate, and this term I'm taking a seminar that has recently been working through an ancient argument turning on whether it was already the case yesterday that a certain event would take place tomorrow. I think that argument is structurally identical to something my flatmate is using to justify a decision I'm worried about, but I can't tell whether the seminar material is meant to refute that style of reasoning or to vindicate some careful version of it, so I'd like your help before I talk to him again.

My flatmate is in his final year. He has a professional entrance examination in nine weeks, and he needs a high mark to keep the scholarship that lets him stay in the programme. Three weeks ago he stopped revising and stopped attending the revision sessions. His reasoning runs roughly like this. First version: it is already the case that he will pass, or already the case that he will fail; whichever of those holds already held before he stopped working, so his stopping cannot make a difference to which of them holds. Second version, which he reaches for when I object: yesterday it was already the case that he would spend this evening on the sofa, that is now a fact about yesterday, and facts about the past cannot be changed, so he could not have been doing anything else tonight — and the same will be true of every evening until the exam.

What makes this hard to answer is that he concedes more than I expected. He agrees that revising raises a student's chances, and he agrees that his chances have fallen. But he says the chances only live in his tutor's spreadsheet, which puts him at roughly forty percent; in reality, he says, there is already a single outcome, and the number merely records how little the spreadsheet knows. So he distinguishes two claims I can't pull apart: that his result depends on what he does, and that it is already settled what he will do. He insists he can hold both at once, and that holding both at once is exactly why there is no point in his trying to make himself revise, since the trying is either already part of the story or it isn't.

Two further details. Our study group has a room booked for the next eight weeks, and I have to decide whether to keep pressing him to use it or to accept his framing and stop; I've noticed that when I half accept it myself my own work slips, which makes me suspect the view has consequences I should weigh, not just truth conditions I should check. And it has occurred to me that the stakes look lopsided: if he is right then nothing I do matters, while if he is wrong then what I do might matter a great deal, which seems like a reason to ignore him even if I can't find the flaw.

Where exactly does his argument go wrong, if it does? I'd be grateful if you could separate the version that runs through the fixity of the past from the version that runs through what is already the case about the future, tell me whether the distinction he draws between the outcome's depending on his effort and the outcome's being already settled is coherent, and say whether the lopsided-stakes consideration is a good reply to him or only a way of changing the subject.
