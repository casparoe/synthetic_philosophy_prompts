I'm helping a small funder decide how to spend a pretty small pot — call it five researchers for two years — on moral trade: the idea that parties who disagree about what's valuable can each do what the other wants and both come out ahead by their own lights. The end goal isn't a survey, it's deciding whether this is actually a big lever or a cute idea that doesn't scale. So no reading lists, no "who has written on this"; I want a defended agenda.

Two bits of framing so we skip the standard version. First, I want the intrapersonal case taken seriously as part of the same agenda: one agent, genuinely uncertain between two moral views, whose "parts" reach a compromise policy instead of one side winning. Is that usefully modelled as trade at all, or does it collapse into ordinary moral uncertainty and the whole gains-from-trade story quietly evaporate? Second, I care a lot about counterparties you can't verify. Concretely: a future AI system whose goals overlap with ours in some regions and not others, where the only enforcement available is that we can shut it down, and we can't audit whether it held up its end. Treat that as the case the agenda is ultimately for.

Given that: rank the open questions those ten person-years should go on, and defend the ordering. Which are upstream, in the sense that other questions can't even be posed properly until they're settled? Which are actually tractable in two years for people with decision theory and some econ and no ability to run empirical work on what real agents value? Which would you defund — either unanswerable with the tools we have, or not load-bearing, i.e. the answer wouldn't change what anyone does? And what evidence showing up in the next couple of years would move something up or down? I'm much more interested in your criteria and how you trade off importance, tractability and neglect than in the list itself.
