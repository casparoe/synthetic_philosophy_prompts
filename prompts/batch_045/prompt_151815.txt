At my epistemology study group on tuesday we got into an argument I can't shake, and I've written it out below because I can't find where it cheats. My flatmate says the whole thing is really about assertion and not about belief at all; I think he's changing the subjet, but I'm not 100% sure why, and the more I look at it the wierder it gets.

(1) Both of these are absurd to say: It's raining but I don't believe it, and It's raining but I don't know that it's raining. In each case the two halves could both be true — a thoroughly self-deceived person could be in exactly the state the first sentence describes, and somebody with a Gettier-style justified true belief is in exactly the state the second one describes. (2) So the absurdity isn't just a psychological fact about how rarely those combinations come up. It has to come either from something about the attitudes themselves or from something about what you're doing when you say it. (3) It isn't about what you're doing when you say it. I tested this at 1am in my private diary — nobody reads it, I wasn't telling anyone anything — and the sentence still felt wrong, and so did just sitting there judging it to myself. Whatever is going wrong doesn't need an audience. (4) So it's about belief: believing has a constitutive standard, a standard a belief has to meet to be in good standing as a belief, and it's incoherent to judge that p while denying that you meet that standard. (5) The standard for belief that everybody accepts is truth: a belief is correct iff things are as it says they are. (6) But the knowledge version is every bit as bad as the belief version, and if truth were the whole standard then It's raining but I don't know it should be perfectly fine, since you can meet a truth standard without meeting any knowledge standard. So the standard constitutive of belief has to be knowledge rather than truth: a belief is in good standing only if it amounts to knowledge. (7) That means there is no such thing as a belief that is fine as a belief but falls short of knowledge — every non-defective belief is knowledge. But that can't be right. We plainly do criticise people for believing on bad evidence even when what they believe happens to be true, and that criticism only makes sense if what is demanded of a belief is something weaker than knowledge. (8) So something above has to go.

I'd rather keep (4) and (5) than (7) — I want Moore's paradox to be telling us something about the mind rather than about the etiquette of talking — but I can't say what keeping them would cost me. So: which step is the rotten one, exactly, and why does it look innocent? And if the right move is to give up (3) and let my flatmate's assertion story back in, what does that story say about the diary case, and about the feeling that the Moorean sentence isn't merely unassertable but is something you couldn't even bring yourself to believe? I'd also like to know what the other standard diagnoses — the truth-is-the-aim-of-belief one, and the one that says first-person avowals aren't reports of inner states at all — each have to pay.
