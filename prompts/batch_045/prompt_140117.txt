I direct the methods unit of a national health technology assessment body. About thirty times a year we convene an ad hoc panel of between nine and fifteen people — mostly practising clinicians, plus a patient representative, a health economist, and an epidemiologist when we can secure one — and ask it to reach a verdict, in a single day, on whether the evidence supports one of three recommendations for a screening or diagnostic technology: recommend it, recommend it only within a research programme, or recommend against it. Every panelist receives the same single dossier, which is our own systematic review and meta-analysis, circulated a week in advance. The panel hears presentations, deliberates as a group, and then votes by show of hands. A simple majority carries; the chair breaks ties. The verdict is published with a written rationale and is subject to appeal by the manufacturer or by a professional body.

I have been reading about the Condorcet jury theorem and the work around it, and I take the headline result to be this: where a question has a correct answer, and each member independently has a better-than-even chance of getting it right, majority accuracy rises with the size of the group and tends to certainty; where each member is worse than even, enlarging the group drives accuracy towards zero and the best jury is a single person. That result seems to me both obviously relevant and obviously inapplicable as stated, and I cannot work out how to turn it into something an office can actually run. Five difficulties:

First, we hand every panelist the same dossier and then let them talk to one another. Their errors are correlated through a common evidence base and through an open discussion. I have no idea how many genuinely independent assessments a fourteen-person panel represents, or whether a fifteenth person of the same kind and reading the same review is worth anything at all.

Second, competence is wildly uneven and I am forbidden from acting on it. A colleague in decision science tells me that when members are independent but unequal, the accuracy-maximising rule is a weighted majority in which each person's weight is the logarithm of their odds of being right, so that a member at eighty per cent carries roughly seven times the weight of a member at fifty-five. Is that right, and does it survive once the errors are correlated? Either way I cannot implement differential weights: our panelists are volunteers who would resign, and the appeal panel would strike a weighted vote down as undemocratic. So what should I do instead that captures whatever is true in that result — staged votes, a documented expert assessment treated as evidence rather than as a vote, a smaller panel, abstention by the least-informed members?

Third, our verdict space has three options, and I gather that majority rule over more than two options can cycle, so that which option wins can depend on the order in which votes are taken. I do not know whether that is a real risk for us or a theorist's worry, and if it is real, how a procedure should handle it: an agenda fixed in advance, a sequence of binary votes, some scoring rule, or something else.

Fourth, the theorem assumes there is a correct answer. Many of our questions have a large component that is not like that at all — how much avoidable anxiety and overtreatment we are willing to impose for a small reduction in mortality. I suspect a procedure has to sort the epistemic parts of a decision from the evaluative ones before any jury theorem applies to it, and I want that sorting done explicitly rather than left to the chair's instincts.

Fifth, we deliberate in the open and then vote by show of hands, in that order. I have seen it argued that this both pools information and destroys independence through herding, and that private judgments should be collected first and aggregated separately. Should they? And what is the status of a private straw poll once the discussion has moved everyone — evidence about the group, or a contaminant?

What I want from you is a written procedure, not a reading list: something I can paste into our methods manual and hand to a panel chair. It must be runnable in under an hour by a methods officer who is not a statistician; it must use only information we actually have, which is each panelist's specialty, their prior service on our panels, and for a minority of them a thin track record on earlier panels whose verdicts were later borne out or not; it must be publishable in the rationale; it must not allocate unequal voting weights; and every branch of it must terminate, either in a recommendation about how to constitute and run the panel or in an explicit hand to a named person on the ground that what remains is a judgment about values rather than a question of accuracy. Give me the questions in order, state what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and mark where the procedure returns a no.

Then run it on three panels I have to constitute in the next two months.

One: fourteen people deciding whether to lower the starting age for a cancer screening programme by five years. The evidence is a single meta-analysis with wide confidence intervals and substantial heterogeneity; two panelists have published on the modality and sit on the relevant specialist body; all fourteen have read the same dossier; the patient representative has not.

Two: eleven people deciding on a diagnostic test for a rare condition. One member has actually run the test; eight have never encountered the condition in practice; the patient representative has lived experience of it and strong views. The spread of relevant competence here is about as extreme as it gets.

Three: nine people where the factual evidence is unusually clear and the value trade-off is not — the technology detects indolent disease that would never have harmed anyone, producing measurable anxiety and a modest rate of complications from follow-up procedures against a small mortality benefit. My own expectation is that no truth-tracking question survives here at all, and I want to see how the procedure marks that, what it tells us to do with the majority once it does, and what we would be doing if we presented a value judgment as an evidence call in order to make it appeal-proof.

Finally, tell me where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment however hard we try, what it is incapable of telling us in principle, and — the part I most want — the case in which applying it faithfully would give me an answer I will not like.
