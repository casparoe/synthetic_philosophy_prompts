Write a satirical press release of 600–750 words in a deadpan corporate voice, announcing general availability of the "Non-Turing Tier": on-demand hypercomputation from a fictional Series B cloud startup. The piece should consist of a headline and dateline; two or three short paragraphs of quotable executive statement (a CEO and, say, a Chief Ontology Officer); a numbered FAQ of five or six entries; and one closing reaction quote from an unnamed, exhausted professor of computer science. The register is straight-faced throughout: no winking, no nudging, no footnotes admitting the joke.

The comedy has to come from the epistemology, not from gadget jokes. Get the following distinction right and let the company exploit it. The Church–Turing thesis in its mathematical form identifies an informal, pre-theoretic notion — an effective procedure, the kind of finite, mechanical, step-by-step method a careful human could carry out with pencil and paper — with what a Turing machine can do. Because one side of that identification is informal, the thesis cannot be proved; and because the machines we actually build and the runs we actually perform are finite, it is not straightforwardly an empirical hypothesis either, so no experiment settles it. There is also a physical version: the claim that any finitely realizable physical system can be simulated to arbitrary accuracy by a universal machine. That one really is a claim about the world, and in principle the world could say no. The company's entire business is the slide between the two — marketing the mathematical thesis as though it were the empirical one it has just overthrown.

Each thing the company sells must be a real resource drawn from the actual hypercomputation literature, recognizable to a specialist as a bad-faith reading of a proposal someone has made, and each must be billed as though it were merely an engineering achievement:

1. A "Zeno" accelerator: the machine performs its nth step in half the time of the (n−1)th, so that infinitely many steps complete within a bounded interval, with the result read off at the endpoint. The FAQ should handle, without ever naming the difficulty, the question of who or what observes the halt bit, what happens to a job whose answer is that the program does not halt, and how a customer is meant to distinguish a machine that finished from one that is still running.

2. An "analog co-processor" that stores a genuine real number — infinitely many significant digits — in one physical quantity, so that a non-recursive amount of information can be deposited at configuration time. Give this the structure of a premium service tier with a name like "Oracle Deposition," in which the customer supplies the initial condition and the company supplies everything else, and in which billing is per digit of a parameter that the hardware cannot in principle verify.

3. An "availability zone" sited in a region of spacetime whose entire infinite future lies in the causal past of the single event at which results are returned — so the answer is available before, in the customer's own frame, the job was submitted. Let the service-level agreement, the invoice dating, and the audit trail all be written from inside the machine's frame.

Requirements on the whole: every advertised speedup must trace to exactly one of those three resources; the reader should never be told in so many words that each is an infinity smuggled in rather than a discovery about computation, but should be able to say so after reading. At least one FAQ entry should explain what happens when a customer reports a wrong answer — support's position being that a failure refutes only the physical thesis for that particular hardware, leaving the mathematical thesis exactly as it was, and that this is covered under the standard SLA. The piece should close on the observation that all results are still independently re-checked on an ordinary machine, so that the only measurable difference between the Non-Turing Tier and the free tier is the invoice. It is fine to make the company unfalsifiable; it is not fine to make it correct about the mathematics — the undecidability of the halting problem stays standing, and no character should be made to deny a genuine theorem. Aim for something a philosopher of computation would recognize as fair to what is being mocked and a general reader can follow without a reference list.
