So a postmortem at work turned into a philosophy argument and I've been losing sleep over it. Here's what happened and here's where I'm stuck.

We had a bug in the payments service. A helper called `add_amounts(a, b)` took two signed 32-bit ints. Every test we had covered small values. In production a merchant's balance crossed 2,147,483,647 and the thing wrapped around to a negative number and we paid out garbage for about forty minutes. In the review afterwards everyone said the same thing: nobody *meant* wrap-around addition, the spec was ordinary addition, the implementation was just wrong. Dave, who's on the platform team and reads a lot of stuff that has nothing to do with his job, said: how do you know what anyone meant?

He then explained the Kripke/Wittgenstein thing to me. As I understand it, you take "plus" and you invent a deviant rule — I think he said Kripke called it quus, and the cutoff was 57 or something, doesn't matter — where quus agrees with plus on every pair small enough that a human being would actually have computed it, and returns 5 (or whatever) for anything bigger. My entire history of adding numbers is consistent with my having meant quus all along. So my history doesn't determine which rule I meant. And that's supposed to generalize: no fact about me settles any question about what I mean by anything.

Fine, I followed that. But then Dave pushed it further and I've reconstructed his argument as best I can, and I'm now pretty sure it proves too much — possibly including proving that Dave's own argument doesn't say anything. Here's the version I wrote down:

(1) What a rule *is*, for the purpose of what I mean by a word, is just the function it determines: this input, that output. Two rules that differ somewhere are different rules; two rules that never differ are the same rule.

(2) Everything that could possibly be evidence of what I meant by "plus" is finite: every sum I've ever computed, every time I've said "yes, that's right" or "no, you made an error", every brain state, every disposition I have. I'm mortal, I've computed finitely many sums, and a disposition is a disposition to produce some specific output for some specific input.

(3) For any finite body of evidence E and any rule R that fits E, there are infinitely many other rules that fit E just as well and disagree with R somewhere outside E. (quus, and the grue-style cases are the same shape.)

(4) So nothing about me — nothing in my behavior, my brain, my dispositions, or even that feeling of "grasping" the rule — singles out one rule as the one I meant.

(5) If nothing about me singles one out, there's no fact of the matter about what I meant. So when the review said "you meant normal addition, not wrap-around addition", that claim isn't true and it isn't false. It's just something we're licensed to say because our community lets us get away with it.

(6) But (2) through (5) isn't an argument about addition. It's an argument about rules in general, so it applies to every expression in it — "rule", "same", "all", "not", "finite", "if...then", "fact".

(7) And that seems to eat the argument itself. If there's no fact about what those words mean, then the sentence "there is no fact of the matter about what I mean" doesn't determinately say anything either, and doesn't rule out the deviant reading of itself. Which means (2)–(5) can't be asserted as an argument at all — it's like saying "every sentence is meaningless" out loud.

(8) But it obviously has force, and it clearly isn't just word salad, so something above has to go.

Dave's answer to (7) is that it's a cheap trick and a real skeptic can just shrug it off, which I don't find satisfying but I also can't say why.

What I want from you:

- Name the step where it goes wrong, and explain why that step looked airtight when I wrote it down. I want the diagnosis, not a tour of the topic.
- Then price the alternatives. If I reject (1) — say because meaning isn't the input/output function but something like the internal representation, the "source code" rather than the behavior — what do I actually gain? My worry is that the source code is just more symbols that need something to interpret them, so I've moved the problem one level down and not fixed it. Is that the right worry, and does it have a name or a standard answer? If I reject (4) or (5), I'm back to needing some straight story about what the meaning-fact consists in, and I don't see one that (2) and (3) don't chew up. If I reject (6), I need to know whether you can even *construct* a deviant rival for a word like "not" or "all" — I sort of suspect you can't, but I also suspect that saying so is just special pleading to save logic from an argument that works fine on arithmetic. If I reject (7), I want to know what the skeptic is doing if they aren't asserting a thesis.
- And bring it back down, please: on whatever reading you end up with, what happens to "you implemented the wrong spec" in that code review? Is it false, or neither true nor false, or licensed rather than true? Does the "what matters is what the community lets you get away with" move actually buy me anything at work, given that my community is six engineers with a flaky test suite and a manager who was on vacation?
