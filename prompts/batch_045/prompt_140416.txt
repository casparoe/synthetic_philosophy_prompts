So — I'm finally writing up the paper on idealized-preference accounts of welfare I've been threatening to write since grad school, the CEV-ish family of views: roughly, what's good for a person is what she'd end up wanting if she knew more, imagined the alternatives more vividly, and made fewer instrumental mistakes. I'm presenting it at a small workshop in a few weeks, and my department runs these brutal "state your commitments in five bullets and let everyone else find the contradiction" sessions that I'd rather not walk into cold. I've been arguing about this with a friend in political theory and we're not getting anywhere, so here's where I think I've landed:

(1) Welfare is desire-based but idealized: what's good for me is what I'd want under full relevant information, full imaginative vividness, and perfect instrumental rationality. No moral or evaluative improvement gets built into the idealization itself — it's purely epistemic plus instrumental, because the moment you let "better person" in through the back door you've got a perfectionist theory pretending to be a desire theory.

(2) Some kind of no-replacement condition: the output has to stay recognizably mine. If the procedure hands back a motivational set I'd now repudiate wholesale, it's not describing me at all, it's describing somebody else who happens to be reachable from me. I know this is vague — I don't have a clean statement of what counts as "wholesale" — but I'm attached to it.

(3) Preferences I have mainly because of how I was socialized get no weight — the standard adaptive-preference cases — even when the person endorses them wholeheartedly, isn't depressed, and has no false beliefs.

(4) The idealization gets iterated to a fixed point: idealize, then idealize the result, and keep going until it stops changing, and whatever that fixed point says is authoritative for me even if it's a long way from where I started. (I'm also, maybe inconsistently, relaxed about there being more than one fixed point depending on what order you apply the improvements in — renovate the kitchen before the bathroom and you get a different house — I've been assuming any old fixed point is as good as another.)

(5) It gets run on one person at a time. I dont want any of that business where you idealize the community or the group and let the interpersonal dynamic do real work; that seems to me to smuggle in a substantive moral ideal under cover of a procedural one, and avoiding exactly that is most of why I like this family of views.

(6) A case verdict I'm weirdly attached to: someone raised from birth to want nothing but to serve her older brother. Full information about other ways of living, two years away from home that she hated, cheerful, no false beliefs, no irrationality I can name, zero disposition to revise. I want to say (a) being her brother's servant is not what's good for her, and (b) the procedure still isn't allowed to just delete her entire motivational set and install a different one, because that's replacement, not extrapolation.

My political-theory friend says (3) is doing all the work and can't sit with (1) and (5) together; another colleague says (2) and (4) are straightforwardly incompatible. I don't know who's right and I'm not defending any particular bullet — I'd give up whichever one costs me least. So: which pairs of these are genuinely in tension, and which only look like they are on a quick read? Where exactly does (6) stop being deliverable? Rank whatever tensions you find by severity, tell me for each one what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me, and then commit to the single revision that cleans up the most. And please actually take a side rather than laying out the options for me to choose between — including telling me where you think your own recommendation is most likely to be wrong. (No literature survey needed; I've read the stuff, I want the argument.)
