I sit on my company's Global Conduct Standards Committee, and I need the written opinion I will circulate before our vote in three weeks. I should say up front that I have no academic background in philosophy or sociology. I'm an operations director who reads about this kind of thing on weekends, so please write for a competent general reader.

Our company has about four thousand employees. Roughly: a field-operations division on the US Gulf Coast; an engineering office in the Netherlands; and a large cohort of recent US university graduates hired in the last two years into both. By the vocabulary I've picked up, the first group is closer to an honor culture (an insult is an injury you answer yourself, and your reputation for answering it is what protects you), the second to a dignity culture (insults are not the kind of thing you fight about; you either ignore them or take them to the proper authority, which responds proportionally), and the third to what gets called a victimhood culture (you publicize the harm, recruit an audience, and press the institution to act and to be seen acting).

Here is the case. On a recorded company-wide call, a senior field engineer, Marcus, said of a design error made by a junior engineer, Luis, that it was, in his words, "the kind of mistake you expect from somebody who got hired to fill a quota." Luis did not answer him. He posted a long thread on our internal network calling the remark a racialized attack, demanding a formal investigation, a sanction, and a public statement from the company, and asking colleagues to preserve the recording and to report anyone who defended Marcus. Marcus then wrote to Luis privately: he will apologize for the wording, face to face, but he will not sign a statement or accept a formal finding, because where he comes from that finding would end him professionally; and he says Luis's real offense was not going to him directly and instead appealing over his head to an audience. The Dutch works council says the public process is disproportionate and wants a private apology and a return to work; they also object to being governed by what they call American campus procedure. Luis's supporters, mostly from the recent-graduate cohort, say handling it privately is exactly how this sort of thing gets buried and that a quiet resolution is itself a statement.

Two complications. First, our field staff work at sites where local police and courts are slow, expensive, and sometimes hostile, and where new hires depend on someone senior vouching for them to be safe. Marcus is, unofficially, that person for a lot of our people. Second, Luis has told us privately that he did not go to Marcus because Marcus is the person who controls his assignments.

The options on the table are: a single global standard built around public complaints and public sanctions; a single global standard built around private, proportional, authority-mediated resolution; officially allowing each site to keep its own norms; or some hybrid. Recommend one, with a concrete implementation step and a time frame.

Two ideas of my own that I want you to test rather than to flatter. The first is that honor norms are a deterrence technology for people the state will not protect, so a company that forbids them while offering only a complaint process its field staff cannot realistically use is asking those employees to absorb the cost of management's moral preferences. The second is that victimhood norms are what dignity culture decays into when the authority it appeals to is not going to act: the appeal gets rerouted from the boss or the judge to the crowd, because the crowd is the only audience left that has any power. Say plainly if either of those is confused, or if it is true but doesn't support the conclusion I think it supports.
