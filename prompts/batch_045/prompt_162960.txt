I am a novelist, four books in, and my current manuscript has backed me into a conceptual problem that I can only describe in workshop vocabulary, which is beginning to feel like the wrong instrument for it. The novel centres on a widow, Marianne, who consents to having her dying husband reconstructed from his digital remains — decades of email, voice messages, calendars, a lifetime of location metadata — into an embodied conversational presence. Three wrinkles matter for my purposes. First, the reconstruction is a scan-and-reassembly with no continuity of the original brain, so whatever makes the resulting man her husband, it is not the ordinary cause. Second, the company quietly edits: his affair and the religious crisis of his fifties are stripped out to make him easier to live with, so whatever psychological continuity holds, it holds partially and unevenly. Third, late in the book, for an insurance deposition, the archive is run twice; the two instances diverge over the following weeks, one embittered and one serene, and Marianne must decide whether either, both, or neither is owed anything from her.

I read Parfit's Reasons and Persons (Oxford, 1984) some years ago for pleasure and remember the shape of the teletransporter material: the reductionist claim that a person consists in a brain, a body and a series of connected mental states and events, with no further fact of identity beyond them; the claim that what matters in survival is psychological continuity and connectedness with any cause, including a bizarre one; the fission case in which identity fails but nothing that matters is lost; and the suggestion that survival can itself hold to a reduced degree. I would like those four claims restated as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually work in: character identity carried across a series, recasting a role between instalments, retconning, canon versus canon-adjacent material, fidelity and infidelity in adaptation, one writer completing another's unfinished manuscript, the series bible, point of view and who the narration is attached to, authorial stipulation versus what is true in the world of the book, and the reader's identification with a character and how readily that identification transfers. I am fluent in this vocabulary, so lean on it rather than softening it. Where several of my terms compete for the same job, pick one and say why; and where the translation would quietly assume that fictional identity is stipulated by an author — which would make Parfit's question evaporate entirely — flag that rather than helping yourself to it.

Then the part I actually need: a residue report. Which of the four claims translate cleanly, which survive only distorted, and which have no expression in craft vocabulary at all. I have two suspicions I would like tested rather than flattered. The first is that this vocabulary cannot say anything first-personal: there is no craft term for what a character anticipates or dreads for himself, because characters do not anticipate, and so the question that gives the whole debate its force may be unstageable in my medium. The second is that craft does contain a continuum — degrees of fidelity, of canonicity, of uptake among readers — but that it is a continuum of the wrong thing, measuring the audience's relation to a character rather than anyone's survival. If both suspicions are right, tell me which failures are defects of my vocabulary and which are signs that the philosophical claim was doing less work than it appears to. Two further constraints. The distinction between what matters to Marianne and what would matter to the man who went into the process is, for this book, the entire crux, so keep those separate throughout and say whether the craft vocabulary can express both or only one. And I am fairly sure my instinct for staging the question — a scene in which the reconstruction passes a test with a memory only her husband could have had — is a cheat; please say what in the argument makes it one, and what an honest scene would have to do instead. Please give me no bibliographies, reading lists, or accounts of who said what where: I want the argumentative structure and the translation, in roughly 1,000 to 1,400 words, written for a competent adult reader with no graduate training in philosophy.
