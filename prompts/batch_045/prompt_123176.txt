The chapter runs roughly like this:

Malthus's argument was that population grows geometrically while food production can only grow arithmetically, so human numbers will always end up overshooting what the land can feed. When they do, nature restores the balance through famine, war and disease — the positive checks — which knock the population back down to a sustainable level. That's why Malthus opposed the Poor Laws and any organised relief for the poor: keeping people alive who can't support themselves just lets more of them survive and pushes the day of reckoning further off, making it worse when it comes. As it turned out he got the timing badly wrong — the Industrial Revolution and then the Green Revolution made food output grow far faster than he thought possible, so the famines he expected never arrived in the rich world. But he was right about the mechanism, just early: for the whole pre-industrial period up to around 1800, the data show living standards stuck near subsistence while population crept up, exactly as the trap predicts. The trap only stopped biting once modern growth and falling fertility severed the link between higher income and bigger families.
