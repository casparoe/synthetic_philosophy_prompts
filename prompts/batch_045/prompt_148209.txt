For about thirty years I've made my living moving other people's data from one system into another, and somewhere along the way I picked up a question I haven't been able to put down. Years ago I read a well-known popular book about minds and machines that staged the question with a picture I still remember: some kind of tank — I think the author called it a careenium — full of little floating markers, simmballs perhaps, with patterns at the top that are supposed to mean something. The puzzle was whether the meanings can ever be said to shove the markers around, or whether only the markers ever shove anything. I don't remember what answer the book gave, if it gave one.

What I keep running into at work is that puzzle with a twist I have never seen described, and it comes in three shapes.

First: I rename a field in a schema — not the values, only the name and type — and a nightly job moves several million bytes around on disk. Those bytes moved because of what the symbol means; had the field carried a different name with the same stored value, nothing would have moved. And yet the engineer's account of which electrons went where is entirely complete and mentions no meanings whatsoever. Two complete accounts, neither one apparently needing the other.

Second, and this is the one that bothers me: a source system sends a value the target system's specification declares impossible — a sixth code where only five exist. Nothing crashes. The reporting layer goes on producing clean reports; it quietly files the sixth code under whichever of the five its bit pattern sits closest to. The fine level did something the coarse level forbade, and the coarse level did not fail. It got re-fitted. Note what this is not: it isn't vagueness, since the five codes are perfectly exact, and it isn't error handling, since nobody anywhere decided to call it an error.

Third, the same thing running the other way. A disk image rots by a single bit; the program goes on running and prints nonsense, and we all say the program is broken rather than that a different, equally valid program is now running. But when a host recompiles an emulated program's hot loops into machine code sharing no structure with it, we all say the same program is still running. Sometimes the coarse description survives a deviation in the substrate by being redrawn; sometimes it dies of the very same kind of deviation. I cannot state the rule that decides which.

I have been calling this map drift, which I know is inadequate. What I want to know is whether there is an established concept for the relation I am describing: a coarse and meaningful description whose mapping onto the fine-grained stuff is renegotiated after the fact, so that the coarse level can neither be refuted by the fine level nor shown to be doing any work of its own. I have seen people online say that the higher level is nothing but the lower level described differently, but that slogan says the coarse level adds nothing, and my worry runs the other way — it seems to add something that no amount of inspecting the fine level could ever confirm or disconfirm. I have also seen people talk as if the coarse level obviously does push the fine level around; but in my first case it looks like it does, and in my second case it looks like it cannot fail to, which amounts to the same as not doing anything at all.

So: does this have a name, and if so which of the nearby notions actually fits and how does mine differ from the others that merely look like it? If it has no name, coin one, define it, and give me the boundary cases where it shades into something else. I won't be satisfied with a bare label — I want to know where the thing's edges are. And please keep clear what is settled in this area from what people who actually know it disagree about, and tell me when you are giving me your own position rather than the lay of the land. Don't send me to papers or reading lists; I have no library access and wouldn't know how to read them. Argue it out in your own words.
