You are a professional philosopher of biology who has also spent years in the rationalist blogosphere — you know the Sequences, you are comfortable steelmanning both Dennett-flavoured reverse-engineering optimism and Gould-flavoured pluralism, and you know how a good diagnostic question differs from a vibe check. Please think carefully and work in stages rather than answering in one pass; brainstorm the bare logical space first and only then hang names on it.

What I want: I run a small reading group — twelve or so people, mostly long-time readers of LessWrong and Astral Codex Ten, a couple with undergraduate biology, nobody with graduate training — and we are working through the adaptationism wars: the spandrels critique of the Panglossian paradigm, the optimality-modelling defence, exaptation, developmental and structural constraint, drift and near-neutrality as a null, niche construction, and the various "it was a methodology all along" moves. I want to end the unit with a written diagnostic — part decision tree, part scored quiz — titled something like "Which kind of adaptationist (or anti-adaptationist) are you actually?" The point, as always with these instruments, is that it should sort people by what they say about concrete cases rather than by which label they find flattering. So an absolute hard rule: the answer options must never contain the words "adaptation," "spandrel," "exaptation," "constraint," "drift," or "just-so story" in any form. Taboo them in the options; you may use them only in the scoring key.

Format and size: ten questions, three options each, then a scoring key mapping answer patterns (not individual answers) onto ten to fourteen named leaves. Where two of my leaves would produce the same answers across all ten questions, say so explicitly in a short "the instrument cannot separate these" note rather than inventing a question that fakes a difference — I would rather have two flagged collapses than a fake discriminator.

Build the branching structure from the logical space, not from a name list. Work out, before you write any leaf labels, which three-to-five yes/no-or-three-way distinctions actually carve things up, and how many of their combinations are live. The distinctions I care most about, and which I want doing the real work, are:

1. Whether "T is for doing X" is a historical claim about what T was selected for (a selected-effects notion) or a claim about what T currently does in the economy of the organism (a causal-role notion) — because this decides whether you need any evidence about the past at all.
2. Which is the default: is drift/near-neutrality the null that a selection story has to beat, or is selection the default that you abandon only after an optimality model has conspicuously failed?
3. Whether an optimality model is to be read literally (organisms really are at or near a local optimum given the constraints) or instrumentally (the model is a measuring device: its failures are informative precisely because they tell you where to look for a constraint you didn't know about).
4. Whether developmental, architectural and mutational biases have independent causal standing, or whether they are themselves downstream products of past selection and so not a rival research programme at all.
5. What kind of "why" is being asked: why the trait arose, why it spread, or why it persists now — and whether the dispute even has the same answer at the molecular, developmental and morphological grains of description.
6. Whether the whole thing is in principle empirically adjudicable (comparative method, phylogenetic controls, ancient DNA, experimental evolution) or is a choice of research programme that evidence can only slowly reward.

Leaves I want represented if the space allows: strong empirical adaptationism (selection is powerful enough that most traits are locally optimal); the optimality-model instrumentalist; the "adaptationism as heuristic reverse engineering" view; the "test adaptationism by trying to break it" view; Gould–Lewontin-style pluralism with spandrels and architectural by-products; an exaptation-first view; a structuralist / developmental-bias view on the "arrival of the fittest" side; a niche-construction or developmental-systems pluralism; a neutralist or drift-default position that treats morphology and molecules very differently; a deflationist who thinks function-talk is just current-utility talk and the historical question is ill-posed; and a sceptic who thinks the debate is mostly untestable storytelling on both sides. If you think any of these is really the same position as another, merge them and tell me why.

Cases: each question must be a concrete case with the relevant facts stated inline, since my readers cannot be assumed to recall them, and each question should be posed where possible as a prediction rather than a verdict — "before you look, what do you expect the record to show, and what would you count as having looked?" Please draw on at least six of: the panda's radial sesamoid thumb; the human chin; the giraffe's recurrent laryngeal nerve detour; the inverted vertebrate retina against the octopus eye that has no blind spot; the near-invariance of seven cervical vertebrae in mammals; eye and pigment loss in cave populations; the fraction of a large genome that looks functionally inert; the sickle-cell allele and malaria; the peacock's train; and the independent evolution of camera eyes in cephalopods and vertebrates.

The one thing I would like you to add rather than recall: I want at least two "compound" cases that no encyclopedia entry covers, where the single-axis answers pull in opposite directions — for instance a trait that is currently under selection for a function it did not originally have, whose achievable variant space is skewed by a mutational or developmental bias, and where the comparative method is unavailable because every close relative is extinct. Construct these yourself, state their stipulations crisply, and say in the key why a compound case is diagnostic: which leaves it forces into an uncomfortable answer, and which leaves it genuinely cannot sort.

Finally: at least four cells of your logical space should come out empty, and for each I want one line on why — incoherent, merely unattractive, or so far unexplored. Give each occupied leaf one sentence naming its most awkward commitment, the thing its holder has to swallow that the neighbouring leaf doesn't. Do not cite anything: no papers, no authors, no dates. I am aware that the empirical/methodological/explanatory three-way split is usually attributed to a particular philosopher of biology and I do not need the genealogy — if a distinction is standard, use it without footnoting it. Write the whole thing in polished but readable prose, with the tree or flowchart rendered as indented plain text, and lead with your stage-one brainstorming of the logical space before you present the finished instrument.
