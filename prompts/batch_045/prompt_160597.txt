Our city council is toying with a small-scale futarchy pilot for housing policy, and I got volunteered onto the little advisory group for it, so I'm trying to get my head around the details before the next meeting.

The setup on the table: first a vote to fix a welfare index for the city (say median rent, vacancy rate and a count of displacement complaints, weighted 50/30/20), then two conditional prediction markets — one that pays out only if the council adopts the upzoning package, one that pays out only if it doesn't — and the package gets adopted iff the first market prices the index higher five years out.

Two things I can't get straight, and I think they're connected.

First: everyone keeps telling me the beauty of this is that the market hands you an unbiased estimate of the policy's effect. But a conditional market price is just an expectation over whatever actually happens in the branch where the policy passed. If upzoning only ever gets passed when rents were already about to flatten — because that's when its politically easy — then the "passed" branch looks great no matter what the upzoning does. One person on the group suggested fixing this by resolving the "didn't pass" branch against a basket of similar peer cities rather than against our own counterfactual. That seems to me to basically solve it, and cheaply. Is that right, or am I missing somthing?

Second, and this is the bit I actually care about: if that peer-city trick works, do we even need the values vote? Couldn't we skip the fight about the 50/30/20 weights, run markets on maybe eight or ten candidate indices, and then run a second-tier market betting on which weighting best predicts how satisfied residents report being five years later? Then the market tells us the right values too, which seems definately better than nine people arguing about it in a chamber.

Something here feels wrong and I can't tell whether its that the whole thing is circular or that it just pushes the same argument one level down. Which is it?
