You are a political philosopher who has spent years on real grant panels and knows how these things actually go wrong -- please think carefully, and do some brainstorming on the failure modes of each metric before you start writing; I would rather have a slow careful answer than a fast tidy one.

I am on a volunteer committee for our city that hands out about $400k a year to local programs for disabled and long-term-unemployed residents. We meet twice a month, ninety minutes, maybe six proposals a session, no staff, no statistician, no ability to commission our own evaluation, and every yes has to come with a one-page justification the city council can read without wincing. We keep having the same argument: someone says a program makes people happier, someone says it gives them more real options, someone says what matters is the money in their pocket, and we have no way of telling whether we are even disagreeing about facts or about what we are trying to equalize.

I need a decision procedure we can actually run in about forty minutes per proposal, by people who have never taken a philosophy class. I know the slogan-level version of the welfare / resources / capabilities debate from the usual blog reading, so please do not just explain the three views -- I need them turned into ordered questions, with what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure just stops and says no, and above all where it hands the question back to us because it is a values call that no amount of procedure settles. It has to say what to do when the number we want does not exist, which is most of the time, and it should be honest about which steps are judgement calls wearing a lab coat.

Then run it on three real ones. (1) A ten-week confidence-and-goals course for long-term unemployed twentysomethings: a small local evaluation found a big jump in self-reported wellbeing right after, most of it gone at six months, and no detectable change in employment, earnings or skills. Cheap per head. Half the committee thinks it changes what people want rather than what they can do; the other half thinks that is the point. (2) A home-and-transport modifications budget -- ramps, vehicle adaptions, subsidised rides. Same cash to everyone, but a ramp does wildly different things depending on the building, and applicants hate the means-test interview enough that some drop out. (3) Weekly music sessions for adults with profound intellectual disabilities: very high cost per head, no measurable change in anything anyone can name as a functioning, staff and some families say participants are plainly happier during and after, and the same money would buy mobility equipment for roughly forty other people.

Two more constraints, sorry: we are not allowed to ask applicants about diagnoses or home life beyond what they volunteer, and about a fifth of the pot is donor-restricted to programs that can show improved wellbeing on a survey the donor supplies and administers.

Be straight with me about which of these the procedure kills that we will not want to kill. And end by telling me plainly what this procedure cannot do -- especially whether it can ever tell us that we picked the wrong currency in the first place.
