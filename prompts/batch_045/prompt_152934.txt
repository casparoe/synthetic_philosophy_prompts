Im writing a novel with a splintered timeline and I keep coming back to Slaughterhouse-Five, so I want to get clear on what Vonnegut is actually committed to. I've been reading about Godel's rotating-universe cosmology too: a universe that turns, closed timelike curves so you could in principle loop back into your own past, and his conclusion that there's no objective lapse of time and that time is ideal in roughly Kant's sense.

Can you read the Tralfamadorians through that? They say every moment exists at once and always has, so nothing can be changed and grief is beside the point — the so-it-goes refrain over every death, especially Dresden. My sense is that's just the block universe, which is weaker than Godel's claim: plenty of people hold that all times are equally real and still think there's a fact about what time it is now. Godel wants more than that. So am I right that Vonnegut never reaches Godel's real target? And does the resignation even follow? Even if the future is already there, that doesn't by itself make anything anyone does pointless — so is the book quietly cheating, or is the comfort the whole point? Does it matter that Billy isn't reasoning from physics at all, just unstuck in time and perceiving moments out of order, and that our own universe doesnt globally rotate anyway? What I want is a reading fair to both sides: what the novel shows about the gap between all moments being real and passage being an illusion, and where the Godel analogy genuinely breaks.
