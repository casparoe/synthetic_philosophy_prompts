I want the implicit theory behind a pair of opposed social practices reconstructed rather than defended, and I would like the reconstruction to take my rough sociology as given.

The celebratory practice, stereotypically American: business magazines tag each large fortune as inherited or self-made and treat the self-made tag as the honorable one; universities name buildings after donors and profile alumni who made it; commencement speakers urge graduates to make their mark; a founder is publicly thanked for the products and jobs that exist because of her.

The leveling practice: Jantelagen in the Nordic countries and tall-poppy cutting in Australia and New Zealand. The successful are expected not to presume they are anybody special; visible display of prosperity draws disapproval; public figures who boast get knocked down in the press, at the ballot box, in everyday gossip.

Neither practice is applied uniformly, and that is what I want explained. Leveling cultures do not level their Olympic champions, chess prodigies or Nobel laureates — they celebrate those at least as loudly. Celebratory cultures do not celebrate inherited wealth, and they turn on the successful who display their success too conspicuously. Both cultures talk about desert: one says she earned it, the other says she had help and luck and owes the rest of us modesty.

So: what view of desert, credit and status does each practice actually presuppose, and which rival views does it quietly rule out? Please distinguish a desert view on which credit tracks what the person made happen that would not otherwise have happened — relative, say, to what the next-best available replacement would have achieved — from views on which the objection to celebration is not about desert at all but about status: public esteem as a positional good, the distribution of esteem as something we are jointly responsible for, or admiration as a force that distorts our beliefs about how much the admired person contributed. Can both practices be captured by one desert-based view plus differing empirical assumptions about how legible and how replaceable market success is compared with athletic or artistic success? For each practice, say what it strictly presupposes, what it merely fits well with, and where it is underdetermined by several theories. Finally, tell me what a public culture built consistently on the rival theory would do differently: whom it would honor, whom it would cut down, and whether either culture as it stands is coherent.
