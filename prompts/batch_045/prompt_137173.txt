I teach a graduate seminar on personal identity, and one of my doctoral students has produced an argument that I have been unable to fault and whose conclusion I cannot accept. I set it out below, in my own reconstruction, together with two cases of my own that I have been using as tests. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

The set-up is Parfit's, from *Reasons and Persons*: the teletransporter scans my cerebral state, destroys my body on Earth, and reassembles a replica on Mars from local matter. In the Branch Line variant the scanner works, the replica is created, and my terrestrial body is *not* destroyed: I walk out of the booth in Oxford and go on with my life, while someone psychologically continuous and strongly connected with me as I was at the moment of scanning, produced by the right kind of cause, wakes on Mars and goes on with a life of equal quality. Call the underlying relation *R*. Because *R* now holds one-to-many, neither successor is identical with me. The reductionist's claim is that in such a case nothing important is lost: what matters in survival is preserved, and the absence of identity detracts nothing.

My student's argument:

(1) Reductionism: what personal identity over time consists in is the holding of *R* in its non-branching form; there is no further fact of identity over and above facts about brains, bodies and psychological continuity.

(2) Identity is not what matters: what matters in survival is simply the holding of *R*, with any cause, together with the continuation of conscious life.

(3) So in the Branch Line case what matters is fully preserved on both branches, and, by (2), the failure of identity costs nothing.

(4) The received verdict — Parfit's, as I read him; and if I misread him, then treat this as a claim my student thinks any reductionist is committed to — is that division is *as good as* ordinary survival: not worse, but emphatically not better.

(5) Yet the Branch Line case contains twice as much of what matters as ordinary survival does. Ordinary survival yields one life continuing my mental life; division yields two lives, each as fully related to me as the single survivor would have been, with no overlap between them, no competition for the same experiences, and equal quality.

(6) Additivity: other things being equal, more of a good is better than less of it — more precisely, if one state of affairs contains all the good-making features of a second and, in addition, a further complete instance of just those features, then the first is better than the second.

(7) Therefore division is twice as good as ordinary survival, and division into a thousand successors a thousand times as good.

(8) If (7) is true, then each of us has overwhelming reason to be divided, and to be divided as often as possible. Division is a form of life-extension incomparably cheaper and more reliable than any medicine: press the button and what matters in survival is multiplied. By contrast, ordinary survival squanders an opportunity, and a reductionist who accepts (7) and declines to press the button is, by his own lights, being irrational.

(9) That is absurd. No reductionist I know recommends routine mass fission, and the judgement that division is merely as good as survival strikes me as a fixed point that no account of what matters should be allowed to overturn.

(10) My student's sting: the only way to block the argument at (5)–(7) is to say that the two successors are not both *me*, that their claims compete, that one life cannot be lived twice over by two people — and that is to say that identity does matter after all, contrary to (2). So the reductionist package is self-refuting: it either collapses back into the non-reductionist view it was meant to replace, or it hands us a recommendation that nobody, including its own defenders, will take seriously.

Two cases I use to test any diagnosis.

*The Unequal Division.* The scanner's output is split: one successor receives the full cerebral structure and lives a life of the same quality mine would have had; the other receives a degraded copy and lives a bare, impoverished, though not miserable, mental life for the same span. Is the outcome as good as ordinary survival, better, or worse? And does the answer depend on the sum, the average, or the best of the two lives? Whatever your account says here, I want to know why it says it.

*The Iterated Division.* Each successor is divided again at the end of every day for a month, yielding something of the order of a billion people at the close, each with a life of the quality mine would have had, each as fully related to me as any single survivor would have been. Does the outcome contain a billion times as much of what matters? If not, what caps it, and is that cap anything other than the identity thought in (10)?

What I want from you is a verdict, not a survey. Tell me which step you reject — or, if you think the argument is valid and one of the premises must go, which one — and give me your reasons. Price the alternatives honestly: I should say that I am attached to (1), (2) and (9), and that I would much sooner give up (5) or (6); but I also hold, in population ethics, that a world containing more well-being is better than one containing less, other things equal, and I suspect that whichever of those two I surrender will cost me at least part of that. The usual reassurances — that the successors share a past, or that I survive as both — have not helped me, since they strike me as restatements of the difficulty rather than solutions. So: where does the argument break, what does the repair cost, and how confident are you, and what would it take to change your mind?

Please write in British English.
