Okay so I've more or less become an updateless decision theory person and I want someone to try to take it apart, or at least tell me where the view is actually exposed. Quick statement of what I mean so we don't talk past eachother: at the moment of choice I shouldn't rank my available actions conditional on everything I now know; I should ask which output of the decision procedure I'm running would have looked best from the standpoint of my original prior, treating my own decision as one more logical/structural fact about the world to be conditioned on rather than as a free causal lever I get to wiggle. So I one-box in Newcomb's problem, and I pay up in that counterfactual-mugging case that circulates in this corner of the internet (predictor flips a coin, only asks you for money if it lands tails, and would have handed you ten times as much if you turned out to be the sort of agent that pays). I've basically stopped arguing about it with my CDT friend, which is probably a sign I've stopped being careful.

So: what would count against it? Not "here's a case where UDT gets the wrong answer because I stipulated that it does" — I want defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I especially want to know which ones a committed UDT person would concede as counting against them rather than dismiss as a formalization artifact. Here's my own worry-list, please add to it and sort it: (1) updatelessness might be two ideas bolted together — refusing to condition on which branch/copy/time-slice you turn out to be (the anthropic, Sleeping-Beauty-ish stuff), and refusing to treat your own deliberation as causally free (the Newcomb stuff) — and if the indexical half collapses for lack of any principled prior over "which copy is me," does the logical half survive untouched, or was the unity only ever apparent because both kinds of case happen to feature a predictor? (2) the logical-counterfactual machinery: if what does the work is the ability to say what would be true if a fact about my own output were different, and nobody has a clean account of that, is UDT a research program with a hole where the engine goes — and is the usual proof-theoretic/modal reply a solution or a promissory note, and how would we tell? (3) one I made up and can't get out of my head, call it computational updatelessness: a bounded agent deciding now can't run the deliberation its earlier, worse-informed self ran, so if UDT says to act as your prior self would have chosen, then for anyone who isn't ideal it seems to say act as your prior self would have chosen *with less computation*, i.e. act as if you were dumber than you actually are. That reads like a reductio to me, and it's the thing I'd personally find most damaging if there's no answer — so what picks out the relevant counterfactual prior self, with what information budget and what time budget?

And then the insulation worry, which is what I actually keep circling back to. Every time UDT gets an uncomfortable verdict — the $5/$10 thing where the agent can't even get past the first option, the mugging-farm cases where anyone who can cheaply simulate you can extract money from you — the response I hear is "that's an artifact of this implementation, the ideal is fine." So: is there anything UDT predicts that its rivals don't? Some class of environments where a UDT agent does systematically better, or some pattern of outcomes such that if the world came out the other way the view would genuinely be in trouble? Or has it been qualified into something that can't lose? And if it has, is that a problem for a *normative* theory specifically, or is "the ideal is unfalsifiable and only implementations can be wrong" a perfectly respectable place to end up — same way nobody thinks expected utility theory is refuted by the Allais responses? Last thing: my friend says UDT is either evidential decision theory with extra steps or CDT plus a precommitment device in a trenchcoat. I'd like to know what would have to be true for that to be right, and whether I should be embarrassed if it is.
