I'm a high school senior doing an independent reading project on logic and AI safety — I came in by way of Gödel and then spent most of the fall stuck on Löb — and I've ended up defending a position that someone I argue with online says is either trivially false or trivially true depending on how I phrase it, which I take to mean I'm phrasing it badly. So I don't want the position explained back to me; I want it attacked. Here is the set-up, stated precisely enough that we aren't talking past each other. Löb's theorem, due to Martin Löb in 1955, says that for a formal system T containing enough arithmetic and satisfying the usual Hilbert–Bernays provability conditions, if T proves the conditional that takes a proof of φ to φ itself, then T already proves φ. The standard consequence people draw is that an agent whose reasoning is exhausted by such a T cannot, from inside T, certify its own outputs as trustworthy, and in particular cannot hand a successor or a modified version of itself a general license to trust the thing it came from: the one-shot case is exactly what the theorem blocks, and the corollary for a contradiction shows the agent cannot even vouch for its own consistency.

My position is that this is a budgeting problem being dressed up as a paradox. What Löb's theorem forbids is uniform, unbounded self-trust; it does not obviously forbid bounded self-trust purchased at a price, and bounded self-trust is all any agent that actually has to do something needs — enough to check that the successor it just built preserves what it cares about for the next while, not a timeless warrant for every possible successor. The mechanism I've been defending I call degradation with a summable budget. Fix a total error budget b, say one quarter, and a schedule of discounts, say an error of b·2⁻ⁿ for the nth nested use of reflection along a single derivation path. The agent is permitted to lean on reflection, but only in degraded form: from a proof of φ it infers, not φ, but that φ holds with probability at least 1 minus the scheduled error, and it may not exceed the total budget across a path. Because the discounts are summable, a derivation that uses reflection finitely many times still ends in a guarantee that is weaker than certainty but bounded away from useless, never worse than 1 − b; because the budget is fixed in advance and cannot be topped up, the agent never obtains the unrestricted reflection principle the theorem rules out. The part I actually care about, and the part I think is new rather than merely cosmetic, is that the cost of self-trust is now a number attached to individual inferences, so the agent can trade it against other goods — decide how much of the budget to spend vetting a candidate successor versus getting on with the task — which the familiar observation that a theory can vouch for each of its finite fragments without vouching for itself does not by itself give you.

So: what would count against this, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and sorted by which failures my opponent could actually demonstrate rather than assert? I want the distinctions to be sharp.
