I direct commercial strategy at a mid-sized medical device manufacturer, and three or four times a year my group hits the same structural situation: two or three parties are each able to make a costly, hard-to-reverse commitment, and whoever makes it first captures most of the value — not because they were in the better position, but because moving first changes what everyone else can still do. An exclusive deal with a sole supplier. A public adoption of an interoperability standard. A binding pledge volunteered ahead of a rulemaking. In almost every case nobody involved wanted the race; it appeared because each side could see the other reaching for the same commitment. I need a procedure, not a discussion, and I want it grounded in whatever the theory of commitment actually supports rather than in negotiation folklore.

What I have so far is a rough three-way taxonomy my team uses, which I suspect is wrong in places: preclusive races, where I commit in order to deny you an option you would otherwise have; positional races, where I commit in order to put my preferred outcome on the table as the thing others coordinate around; and signalling races, where the content of the commitment matters less than what my willingness to be bound tells the other side about me.

Two further things I want you to test rather than assume. First: we keep observing that the party who commits first tends to win the framing and lose the terms — they set the axis on which everyone then competes, and they pay for it in price, because committing early reveals how much they wanted it. If that is a real structural feature and not an artifact of how we remember these deals, the procedure should exploit or hedge it rather than treat going first as simply good or bad. Second: I suspect our ability to make credible commitments is a depleting asset. We have won several of these by being the side everyone believed would not walk away, and I worry that each use spends some of that, and that we are near the point where our next public commitment is read as theatre. If the procedure cannot represent that, say so plainly and tell me what to do instead.

What I want from you is a decision procedure my analysts — MBAs, no formal game theory — can run in about two hours, in a fixed order, on anything we think might be a commitment race. It needs:

1. A screen that throws out situations which only look like races, including the ones where there is no premium on moving first at all, or where the premium is really on moving second, so that rushing is a pure loss. I especially want it to catch the case where both sides are racing because each believes the other has information it lacks.
2. For what passes the screen, a classification step: which parts of my taxonomy are worth keeping, and which distinctions have I collapsed that actually change what we should do.
3. The questions to answer before committing, in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each — and, this matters most, what to do when the answer cannot be had. We usually cannot estimate what being locked out is worth to the other side, how likely they are to follow our lead, or how fast they could commit if they knew we were about to.
4. Hard stops: the conditions under which the procedure returns "do not commit," and we leave the race rather than compete in it.
5. The moves that are not "commit faster": making our commitment conditional on theirs, simultaneous or escrowed exchange, deliberately making it impossible for us to commit so that we cannot be rushed, or removing the prize that makes going first worth anything at all. When each is available and what it costs us.
6. An escalation rule: which questions are value judgments that have to come to me or to the board rather than being settled by the checklist, and how to tell the difference.
7. Where the procedure will misfire, including what it structurally cannot handle: more than two parties; races inside our own company between two business units; and races where the counterpart may have no one who can actually commit.

Then run it end to end on three live-shaped situations.

(a) The sole supplier of a coated component used in roughly eight percent of our revenue is in talks with our main competitor about a five-year exclusive. Whoever signs gets the capacity; the other is shut out for around two years. We could sign this week. Nobody has told us the competitor is close.
(b) A consortium chooses between two data formats in eleven weeks. If we announce at a trade show in three weeks that our next product line will be built on Format A, and pre-pay certification, the other large members will probably follow and A becomes the standard on terms that suit our architecture. If they do not follow, we have committed a product line to a dead end.
(c) A regulator is drafting marketing rules for our category and several firms are volunteering binding public commitments to get ahead of it. Our chief executive wants us to be first, and being first has worked for us before.

Case (c) is the one where I expect an answer I will not like. If the procedure says what I think it says, tell me directly and show your work, including whether our record of winning these is evidence of skill or of something else.

Constraints: runnable in about two hours by someone without the theory; no step may depend on a number we cannot get; it must leave a written trail, because commitments above a certain size or duration go to the board. Some of it will inevitably be judgment dressed up as procedure — mark those steps as such rather than hiding them.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of what I have asked. Give me the procedure before the worked cases.
