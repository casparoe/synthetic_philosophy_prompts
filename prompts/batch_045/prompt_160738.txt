Last week I sat in on a reading group in another department that was working through Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals. Two of the members, Marta and Devin, spent most of the session disagreeing about how much those results actually show, and when they realized I was the only person in the room who wasn't invested in either position they asked me to settle it. I couldn't, since I don't work on this myself, but I promised to write something up. Let me reconstruct the exchange as faithfully as I can.

The background they agreed on: the suppositional or Adams–Stalnaker thesis that the probability of an indicative conditional is the corresponding conditional probability, V(if A, C) = P(C | A), and Lewis's argument that this cannot hold in full generality. Their joint reconstruction of the argument was that the collapse needs three things: the thesis itself, for arbitrary A and C; the assumption that a conditional is a proposition in the same algebra as A and C, so that the law of total probability applies to it; and two constraints tying the conditional to its antecedent, roughly that P(C | A and (if A, C)) = 1 and that P(if A, C | not-A) = 1. Substituting into total probability then forces P(C | A) = 1 for every A whose probability is less than 1, which is absurd.

(1) Marta said that this makes the result a reductio of a package rather than a refutation of the thesis, and that the Adams line is simply the decision to give up the second premise: conditionals are not propositions in a Boolean algebra closed under embedding, so total probability never applies to them, and the thesis can be retained for simple, unembedded conditionals.

(2) Devin replied that this retreat is more expensive than she made out, because embedding is both ordinary and action-guiding. People routinely evaluate negations of conditionals, disjunctions of conditionals with different antecedents, and conditionals nested in the antecedent of another conditional, and they stake plans on such compounds; a theory that refuses to assign them probabilities leaves all of that unaccounted for, and buys its consistency by declaring a large, well-behaved body of ordinary reasoning unevaluable.

(3) Marta then offered a positive proposal she called the question-relative mixture rule. Fix a partition of possibilities into the alternatives that the conversation is currently treating as live, say Q and not-Q, and set V(if A, C) = P(Q)·P(C | A and Q) + P(not-Q)·P(C | A and not-Q), weighting the cells by their unconditional probabilities rather than by their probabilities updated on A. Her worked example: with P(Q) = 0.4 but P(Q | A) = 0.8, and P(C | A and Q) = 0.9 while P(C | A and not-Q) = 0.2, the thesis yields 0.76 and her rule yields 0.48. She claimed three things for it: that it escapes the collapse, because the value is not the probability of any element of the algebra and so Lewis's second and third premises are never instantiated; that it is not ad hoc, because for a fixed antecedent the map from consequents to values is still a probability function, being a weighted average of conditional distributions, so complement and finite additivity over exclusive consequents survive — probabilistic coherence is retained one antecedent at a time; and that it makes a novel, testable prediction, namely that conditional confidence should shift when the salient partition shifts even if nothing else about the agent's credences changes.

(4) Devin objected twice. First, that the coherence she retains is not the kind anyone needs: because the weights are not updated on A, the identity P(C) = P(A)·V(if A, C) + P(not-A)·V(if not-A, C) fails, and that identity is exactly how you compute the probability of an outcome by summing over a partition of scenarios, which is what planning, expected-utility calculation, and updating on a learned conditional all require; so the rule preserves the form of probabilism while discarding its function. Second, that the value now depends on a conversational parameter for which she has given no principle: two agents with identical credences over the same possibilities could assign different values to the same conditional with neither at fault, and unless she can say what fixes the partition, the view is either incomplete or a quiet non-factualism about conditionals, which is a large bullet to have bitten without argument.

(5) Marta answered that the lost identity cannot be a decisive objection in particular, since Lewis's own result shows that no assignment can have it together with the rest of the package, so the only question is which loss is smaller, and she would rather lose a bookkeeping identity than lose embeddability and truth-evaluability, which her rule keeps at least for compounds sharing an antecedent. On the second objection she said context-sensitivity is a datum rather than a cost — indicative conditionals already shift with what is being held fixed — that established contextualist and restrictor treatments of if say something similar, and that needing the partition to be supplied is a research program with checkable predictions, not a refutation.

Please adjudicate. I want your verdict as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion, in this order: (1) whether Marta's diagnosis of what the triviality argument actually assumes is correct; (2) whether Devin's embedding objection is as damaging as he claims; (3) whether the mixture rule really does escape the collapse, naming the premise it gives up; (4) whether Devin's two objections land and whether Marta's replies meet them; (5) what, if anything, both of them are missing — and if the answer is nothing important, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a point. Within each point, separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, mark any claim that rests on a contested assumption or a contested reading of the mathematics, and do not present a contested position as the consensus one.
