Ok so I've been chewing on this inequality thing for a while and I've landed somewhere I'm suspicious of, so please try to knock me off it. Short version: I think the American attitude is more defensible than the European one, but not for the usual liberty/taxation reasons — I think the European instinct makes a specific category error, treating the *shape* of the outcome distribution as the thing that can be unjust, when really only the process that generated it can be. On my version, the questions that matter are whether people could enter the process on roughly comparable terms, whether the people at the bottom are better off in absolute terms than they'd be under the alternatives, and whether the inequality reproduces itself across generations. If the answers are yes, yes and no, I struggle to see what the objection is supposed to be, and I think a lot of European-style redistribution is aimed at a target that isn't actually the complaint.

The refinement I've added lately, which I think is where the real action is: my view quietly assumes that luck is legible after the fact — that we can tell, well enough, how much of someone's income is effort and how much is circumstance, so that rewarding effort isn't just a lottery with a morality story stapled to it. Call it the legibility condition. If it fails, I think my view collapses on its own terms, because "was the process fair" stops being a question we can answer and the European skepticism about rewarding observed outcomes starts looking like the epistemically modest position instead of the moralistic one. There's a second wrinkle I can't shake: my view gives verdicts about a single country at a time, but if process-fairness is what matters, the relevant process is arguably global, and then the view seems to push somewhere I didn't expect it to push.

So: what would actually count against the position I've described? I want defeaters ranked, fatal first, merely awkward last, and I want you to separate the ones I'd have to concede from inside the view — stuff that turns my own premises against me, like legibility failing, or persistence turning out to be much stronger than I assume, or the absolute-gains benchmark being the wrong baseline — from the ones I'd probably wave off as changing the subject. What does my view predict that the European one doesn't, so that there's something to check rather than just two vibes? Be honest about whether I've insulated it: can any observed inequality be retro-fitted as either the product of a fair process or of an unfair one, and if so is that a bug or is it fine for a view like this? I'd be most moved by anything about kids and persistence, since that's where my own criteria are least comfortable. And tell me where your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong, plus which single consideration would make you flip to the other side.
