Dear Dr. Halloway,

I am in the lower sixth, I help edit our school's politics magazine, and I have got stuck on an argument I keep losing. I would rather find out that I am losing it because I am wrong than because I simply cannot find the words.

Our school is consulting on a new expression policy. At the open meeting, after someone raised a case about a group chat in which boys in the year above had shared posts mocking a classmate's religion, the chair of the governors closed the discussion with the line "sticks and stones may break my bones, but words will never hurt me." I do not think he meant it as a joke. He used it to license two conclusions: first, that no restriction on what pupils say to one another can ever be justified by appeal to harm; and second, that the harm principle itself, which we had been taught in the liberalism unit, simply has no application to speech, because speech is not the sort of thing that can harm anybody.

I have tried to argue back and I end up flailing, because the sentence seems to be doing several different jobs at once and I cannot separate them. Sometimes it looks like an empirical claim about causation, sometimes like a claim about who is responsible when someone is upset, sometimes like a claim about what should count as harm for the purposes of the principle, and sometimes like a claim about the right response to bad speech being more speech rather than force. My instinct is that the version which is obviously true is too weak to support the chair's conclusions, and the version strong enough to support them is false, but I cannot lay that out cleanly.

Two further things are tangled up in it and I would like them dealt with rather than smoothed over. Our deputy head has started separating what she calls targeted speech, addressed at a named person, from ambient speech, posted where anyone might see it, and she says the principle only reaches the first; that distinction feels invented to me, but I cannot say what is wrong with it. And a friend of mine offers what he calls the broken-windows account: a slur is not like a punch, he says, it is like graffiti left up, because what hurts is not the marks themselves but what everyone else reasonably reads them as signalling about what will be tolerated here. If he is right, the harm seems to materialise only by way of other people's reactions, which raises the worry that any audience that reacts badly can silence a speaker, and also the worry that the speaker can disclaim responsibility for what others do with his words.

Could you set out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, from the trivial to the strong, and say for each whether it is platitudinous, substantive and defensible, or false, and whether it would actually do the work the chair needed of it? I also want to know whether the harm principle, read carefully, has resources to count harms that pass through other people's uptake, or whether accepting my friend's account collapses into something like a heckler's veto.

One request about form. I know a menu like this would normally be a list, but I am going to read your answer aloud to our society, so please write it as continuous prose, the way you would write an email, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text and no tables.

Yours sincerely,
Priya Raman
Lower Sixth
