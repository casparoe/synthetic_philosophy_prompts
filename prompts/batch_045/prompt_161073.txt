You are a professional philosopher who works on causation and on the metaphysics of omissions. Please think carefully and, before you settle on an answer, sketch the range of candidate concepts this phenomenon might fall under, try each one against all three of my cases, and explicitly discard the ones that do not fit. I am not after a label. I am after the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, where its edges are, and whether it is one thing or several.

I write serious-incident reports for a hospital trust, and a recurring problem in that work is how to fill in the "causal factors" box when every candidate factor is an absence rather than an event. I keep hitting the following pattern and I have no word for it.

Case 1. A plant in a hospital atrium dies over a long weekend. It needed both water and shade. The automatic watering was switched off for maintenance and nobody restored it, and nobody replaced the shade cloth that had been removed. Either deficiency on its own would have been fatal. So for each failure, it is false that if it had not occurred the plant would have survived. Yet the natural report is that the plant died because it was neither watered nor shaded — the two failures together seem to cause the death, although neither does on its own, and there is nothing at all connecting either of them to the plant's dying that anyone could point to or trace.

Case 2. A patient deteriorates overnight. The covering doctor never wrote the instruction up, and so the nurse never administered the drug. Here both failures do pass the but-for test: each on its own is such that, had it not occurred, the patient would have recovered. But the deterioration gets attributed to the doctor's omission and not the nurse's; it sounds wrong to say the nurse's omission caused it, and equally wrong to say the two of them jointly did.

Case 3. Indefinitely many further people's failures to act also pass the but-for test in these cases, including people with no connection to the incident whatsoever. Something is therefore filtering the candidates, and I cannot tell whether that filter is doing causal work or merely explanatory, normative, or blame-attributing work.

So: is there an established concept or literature for this configuration — failures to act that seem to be causes only collectively, or only relative to what was supposed to happen, or only in virtue of some other failure upstream of them, with nothing connecting any of them to the outcome? What are the nearest established notions, how does what I have described differ from each of them, and which fits best? If nothing established fits, coin a term, define it precisely, and give me the boundary cases that show where it stops applying.
