Hi — I work at a mid-sized municipal archive in a former mill town of about 200k, and over the last couple of years I've drifted into a position I now realize I hold pretty strongly, so I'd like it stress-tested before I build my whole accessioning policy on it. Roughly: the characteristic ethical failure of an archive is omission — the people and practices that never got recorded at all, or that survive only as objects of someone else's paperwork (police blotters, relief case files, hospital admission ledgers, the landlord's copy of the tenancy), so that the archive's silences line up almost perfectly with who had power — and therefore the right default for someone in my job is maximal capture: take in as much of the present as we can, especially from groups the current holdings barely mention, push it all through digitization, and treat weeding-by-appraisal as something that needs a positive justification rather than as routine hygiene. I've started calling this the plenitude principle, which I realize is mostly a way of making my own workflow sound like a moral theory.

What I want is the strongest case against it, and I want it sorted — fatal defeaters first, then merely awkward ones. Skip the budget-and-staffing objection; I know it, it's real, but it's a resource argument and not what I'm asking about. The version I can't get past is the one where capture and control are the same act: making a group legible to the archive is exactly what makes it available to whatever power comes looking later, so the people who are currently unrecorded may be unrecorded partly because being unrecorded is what's keeping them safe, and my collecting reads as rescue to me and as exposure to them. I keep circling around the thought that some people have a claim not to be made legible to institutions at all — I think there's a French Caribbean writer with a phrase like a right to opacity, Glissant maybe? — but I can't reconstruct the argument. Please don't send me to a reading list; I don't care who said it first, I want the mechanics.

Two structural worries of my own that I'd rather you take seriously than talk me out of. First, a colleague points out that if we keep everything, discoverability becomes the actual selection mechanism: what matters is what gets catalogued well, what's full-text searchable, what sits in a collection somebody has already written about — so plenitude doesn't abolish gatekeeping, it relocates it somewhere harder to see, and probably somewhere more captured by existing citational hierarchy. That's the best objection I know and I don't have a reply. Second, I worry the principle is insulated in a specific way: its subject matter is absence, and you can't produce a counterexample to there being more missing than we've noticed, so any harm I cause can always be redescribed as capturing wrongly rather than capturing too much. Is that a genuine defect in a view of this kind, or just what a default looks like? So: give me the defeaters ranked, and for each say whether someone committed to plenitude should concede it as really counting against the view or can reasonably shrug it off; where a defeater is empirical, say what observation would settle it and whether we're anywhere near having it, arguing from cases and mechanisms you actually know rather than anything you'd have to look up. If the core insight survives but my formulation is too crude, tell me what the careful version says instead. Formatting thing: lead with a single sentence giving your overall verdict on how exposed the view is — your actual bottom line, not a restatement of what I asked — and only then give me the ranking and the reasoning.
