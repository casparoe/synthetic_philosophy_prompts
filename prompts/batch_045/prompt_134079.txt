Putting together a two-page position paper for a decision-theory reading group and I want someone to audit my package for consistency before I hand it out. On Allais and the independence axiom, here's roughly where I've landed: (1) violating independence is genuinely irrational, not merely non-standard or inconvenient for modelling — I want to treat it as a normative requirement, not a simplifying assumption. (2) But I also think Allais's original example is a real descriptive result: most reflective people take the sure million in the first problem and the 11% shot in the second, they go on taking them after the inconsistancy is pointed out, and I don't think they're making an arithmetic slip or failing to grasp the case. (3) I don't accept the money-pump / dynamic arguments for independence — the sequential versions of these problems look to me like different decision problems, since part of what you're choosing under is that you choose once and nobody reruns the lottery. (4) At the same time I think regret is a perfectly respectable thing to take into account: it isn't a bias that you'd feel worse getting nothing when you could have had the million. (5) I think the rank-dependent / probability-weighting stuff is descriptive only and rescues nothing normatively. (6) And I want my rationality requirements to have teeth — if I'm going to call a stable, well-understood preference irrational, I want it to be the sort of thing the person could in principle be argued out of.

So: which of these actually collide? My sense is that most of them sit fine together and one or two quietly don't, but I can't tell which. Rank the genuine tensions by severity, say in each case what would have to give and what that costs me, and flag anything that only looks like a conflict but dissolves once you think about it. Please don't just tell me to drop (1) or reassure me that it's all coherent.
