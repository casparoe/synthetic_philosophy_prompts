I work in the economics office of a regulatory agency, and I am drafting an internal methods note on our benefit–cost practice. I do not want a verdict on whether the practice is good; I want the theory it is implicitly committed to reconstructed, and I want the places where it is philosophically underdetermined flagged as underdetermined.

The practice, as it actually operates. To score a proposed rule we monetize every consequence in dollars and add: households' willingness to pay for each benefit, firms' compliance costs, and mortality risk reductions valued at a single figure per statistical life applied to the expected number of deaths averted. A reduction of one-in-ten-thousand in the annual risk of each of ten thousand people is costed exactly as one life saved, and exactly as the certain avoidance of one identifiable death. Rules are ranked by net benefits with no weights attached to whose benefits and costs they are; when a rule's costs fall on one income group and its benefits on another, our guidance asks for a separate distributional statement but instructs us not to fold equity weights into the net-benefit calculation. The value-of-life figure itself is revised upward over time as real incomes rise, on the ground that willingness to pay for safety increases with income.

I have been reading about Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, and the practice looks as though it were built to match that result's conclusion — social welfare as a weighted sum of individual expected utilities — while quietly failing to match its premises. I want that suspicion tested piece by piece, in the following order.

First, reconstruct the social welfare function the practice must be using if it is coherent, and tell me what it strictly presupposes as opposed to what merely fits it. In particular, does the practice need each of the theorem's premises — expected-utility rationality for individuals, expected-utility rationality for the social ranking itself, the unanimity condition, and the impartiality or equiprobability step that converts free weights into equal ones — or does it need only the conclusion? My own suspicion is that the instruction not to use equity weights is doing work that nothing in the aggregation result requires, since the result leaves the weights undetermined; confirm or dislodge that.

Second, the currency problem. The weights in the theorem are weights on von Neumann–Morgenstern utility functions, and those functions are determined only up to positive affine transformation, with the weights absorbing any rescaling, so that the resulting ranking is invariant to how each person's utility is normalized. Our currency is dollars, and a person's utility is an affine function of dollars only if that person is risk-neutral with respect to money. Work out what the practice therefore implies: if a dollar is counted as worth the same wherever it lands, what is being said about the weight on each person's well-being? I think the answer is that equal treatment per dollar amounts to unequal treatment per unit of utility in a determinate direction, and that the direction is the opposite of what the impartial framing of the practice suggests — but I want the argument made properly, including whether the practice might instead be defended as a weighted sum with unequal yet principled weights rather than dismissed as a botched equal-weight sum.

Third, a diagnostic I would like you to apply. Because the Harsanyian sum is invariant to positive affine rescaling of each individual's utility, any aggregation method whose rankings shift when you change units or baselines is not a Harsanyian sum. Is dollar-metric aggregation invariant in that sense, given that the dollar value assigned to a person's gain depends on the income or consumption level at which the conversion into money is made? If two analysts who convert at different reference levels can reverse the ranking of the same pair of rules, say what that establishes: is the practice committed to universal risk-neutrality in money, or only to a particular cardinalization that it never explicitly chose?

Fourth, ex ante or ex post. Under the practice as described, a prospect that imposes certain death on one person and one that imposes a one-in-ten-thousand risk on each of ten thousand people are costed identically. Is the practice therefore committed to evaluating prospects by the sum of expected utilities rather than by how outcomes look once the uncertainty is resolved, and to indifference between concentrated and dispersed fatal risk at equal expected value? Tell me what the best-known objection to that indifference is, what it would take for the practice to register it, whether any scoring rule we actually use would have to change, and whether our separate handling of identifiable lives — the refusal to score a named worker facing a known hazard the way we score a statistical one — is a genuine inconsistency or a defensible division of labor.

Fifth, suppose an internal review found that stated willingness to pay per unit of risk reduction is systematically larger for large risk reductions than for small ones, so that the single value-of-life figure is not a constant marginal rate of substitution at all. Which premise of the reconstruction would that break, and would we then still be aggregating expected utilities, or something that merely resembles them? Does anything in the argument for the aggregation result tell us which way to go once that premise fails?

Finally, and most usefully for my note: if we took the result seriously as a constraint on our practice, what exactly would have to change? Separate the changes that are forced by the aggregation result itself, the changes that follow only if one adds an equal-weight or impartial-observer premise, and the changes that follow only if one rejects ex ante evaluation in favour of an ex post one. And be explicit about where the practice is underdetermined: if several different welfare functions would generate the same scores, the note should say so rather than announcing that we have uncovered a hidden utilitarianism in our spreadsheets.
