Hi — I'm a journalist working on a piece about what markets "know," and I keep tripping over the no-trade theorem. A chapter in a popular econ book I read (not naming it) puts it like this: "If two rational traders start out with the same prior, they can't both expect to profit from trading with each other just because they've seen different evidence — the fact that the other guy is willing to deal tells you what he knows. So once it's common knowledge that everyone is rational and every trade is voluntary, there's no trade at all. That's why every trade you actually see on Wall Street has to come from irrationality, from different priors, or from someone who just needs cash — and it's basically the theoretical backbone of the claim that you can't beat the market." The chapter glosses common knowledge in passing as "everyone knows it and everyone knows that everyone knows it." Then it pushes further: it cites a recent result it nicknames the "thin-wedge corollary," which supposedly shows the no-trade conclusion survives even when traders' priors differ a little, and concludes that the enormous daily volume in something like S&P futures is therefore proof that big institutional traders must have wildly different priors about where prices are going, not merely different information.

I recieved emails from a few economists and came away more confused: one called this the Milgrom–Stokey result, another said it's Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree, a third muttered something about Grossman and Stiglitz. Are those even the same thing?

That last bit about volume feels like a leap to me, but I don't know where the leap is. Please go through the chapter's account and tell me what's right, what's subtly off, and whether the conclusion about futures volume follows. And separate what's settled among people who actually work on this from what's genuinely contested — I don't want to print a contested reading as if it were the standard view, so give me a sense of how confident I should be in each of your corrections.
