In the small effective-altruism-adjacent reading group I'm in, the sentence "only the absence of craving and suffering matters" — we call it tranquilism — functions as a conversation-stopper. At our last meeting it settled two debates at once: that a future in which nobody suffers but nobody accomplishes, creates, or loves anything demanding would be a full success rather than a waste of potential, and that an intervention permanently removing the capacity to want while leaving the capacity to enjoy whatever is present would be an unqualified benefit, even for someone who currently wants to spend ten years writing a novel. Both times the slogan did all the work, and nobody said what it meant.

I want a menu of precisifications, not a defense of the view. What are the distinct theses this sentence could be asserting, and for each: trivial, substantive and defensible, or false? I am especially after three axes. First, is it a thesis about what makes an individual life go well, about what we owe other people, or about how good a world is impersonally — and do the two conclusions above even require the same reading? Second, does craving count against a life only when it is felt as restlessness or discomfort, or is a standing disposition to crave that never surfaces as an unpleasant experience also bad in itself? Third, is the relevant quantity indifferent to how suffering is spread across times and persons, is it aggregated, or does the worst episode take lexical priority?

Judge each reading against two cases: a person whose wanting has been pharmacologically removed while their capacity for enjoyment is untouched, and a person whose desires are always satisfied before they are ever experienced as lacks. Then say, for each reading, whether it actually supports the two conclusions my group drew, whether a universe containing no sentient beings would on that reading be the best possible one, and which reading people who repeat the slogan are probably relying on.
