my flatmate's brother is doing a philosophy minor, and over dinner last night he went off on one about Kant. his claim was that the universal law formula is just the thing your mum says when you're six — what if everybody did that? — with the serial numbers filed off, and that it's secretly consequentialist anyway, because whenever anyone actually runs it on a real case the badness always cashes out in consequences: if everyone lied, promising collapses, trust breaks down, nobody gets lent money, etc. his conclusion was that there's nothing specifically Kantian going on, so why wade through 200 pages of Kant to get there.

i have some sympathy with that but it feels like he's sliding something. can you audit the analogy properly?

- the correspondence table first: what plays the role of "everybody", of "that", of the bad outcome, of the mum's frown, of "you wouldn't like it, would you". and what's the Kant-side analogue of the "what if" — universal adoption of the maxim, or universal legislation of the law? does that difference do any work, or is it just branding?
- where the mapping breaks. the bit i half remember: Kant apparently splits things into a contradiction in conception and a contradiction in will, and the four examples he runs in the Groundwork (suicide, the lying promise, letting your talents rot, not helping anyone) aren't all meant to work the same way. so: does the mum-test fit one branch and not the other? my guess is it fits the contradiction-in-will ones, where the verdict does look like "a world like that would be unwishable", and completly misfires on the contradiction-in-conception ones — but i can't make that precise and i might have it backwards.
- the case that actually bothers me. take the maxim: when keeping a promise has become inconvenient, i'll quietly not keep it. if literally everyone always did that, promising is dead and the test gives the right answer for the boring reason. but that isn't the interesting case. the interesting case is partial: everybody holds that maxim but acts on it only sometimes, or 5% of people hold it. at low defection rates promising as a practice survives just fine, so the crude consequence story doesn't obviously deliver "don't do it" — and partial defection is exactly where deontologists and consequentialists are supposed to come apart. so does Kant's test say anything there that the mum-test doesn't? does the maxim's condition get universalized along with it? and does the answer depend on how fine-grained we make the description, which brings me to:
- the gerrymandering worry. i can apparently make anything pass by adding details ("i'll lie only to buy milk on tuesdays from red-haired shopkeepers") or make anything fail by stripping them out. is that fatal, or is there a principled story about what the unit of assessment is — maxim, policy, principle of volition — and who sets the level of generality?
- and the other direction, where the mum-test over-generates: i'll train as a concert pianist and grow no food. universalize that and we starve, so on the mum-test it's wrong, which is absurd. does Kant have a fix that isn't just consequences sneaking back in? (something about a world where no one cultivates talents being conceivable but not willable?) and if that fix needs the formula of humanity to do the work, does that mean the universal law formula on its own is empty — which is the objection my flatmate says one of the Germans who came after Kant already made, about nothing following from mere non-contradiction?

end by telling me whether the brother's conclusion survives: vacuous, crypto-consequentialist, or neither. and if "what if everybody" is the wrong analogy, is there a better one? someone suggested the constitutive rules of a game: you can't have "bishop moves one square diagonally when it suits me" as a universal rule of chess, not because the game would go badly but because it wouldn't be chess any more. that sounds right to me, but i want to know what it imports that the mum-test doesn't, and which cases it can't handle.
