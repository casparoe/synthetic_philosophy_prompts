we're closing out a reading group on the species problem next week and instead of a summary I want to end with a villanelle. Here's the case I want it built on — it's invented, so dont go looking it up:

a chain of salamander populations runs around the rim of a desert basin, every population interbreeding freely with the ones adjacent to it, and the two ends of the chain come back together on the far side of the basin and don't interbreed — not because they couldn't produce offspring, they just don't, and the few hybrids they do produce don't do well. Then, thirty-odd years ago, a wildfire wiped out the population at the middle of the northern arm. So the ring is a horseshoe now: the two ends still meet and still don't mix, and there's no longer any actual gene flow around the chain, only the fact that there used to be.

I want the two refrains to be the two ends of the chain: one line in the voice of the western end insisting on its continuity with everything that lies around to the east of it, one in the voice of the place where the ends touch and don't mix. Have them take turns through the poem and not land in the same stanza until the last one — and even there they should stay two lines, not fuse into one. That adjacency without fusion is the whole point; someone who knows the ring-species puzzle should be able to hear which refrain is which end, and why the form can't do anything else with them.

Somewher in the middle stanzas I'd like the gap itself to get a voice — the burned-out population — because whether the chain is one species or two seems to hang on whether "could still interbreed" means anything once the link is gone, and on whether that fire split one species into two in an afternoon or merely revealed that it had never really been one.

Please don't resolve it: no "species are just conventions" shrug, no tidy "so there were two all along", no moral about how nature refuses our boxes. Leave it open — that's honestly where our discussion ended up. Jargon stays out of the poem itself (no concept names, no binomials; I'll say those out loud before we read it), so the idea has to do structural work rather than vocabulary work. Slant rhyme is completely fine, and I'd much rather it be speakable out loud in under a minute than intricate.
