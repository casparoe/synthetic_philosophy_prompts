Here is the proposition on which I want your credence. Call it P:

P — There exists a reformulation of Pascal's Wager satisfying all three of the following constraints: (i) it invokes only finite, though possibly astronomically large, payoffs, making no use of infinite utilities or infinitesimal probabilities; (ii) it distributes priors over theistic hypotheses in a way that respects reversal symmetry, meaning that for every hypothesis on which religious belief is rewarded it includes a mirror hypothesis on which wager-motivated belief is punished or non-belief rewarded; (iii) it employs no decision rule that discounts or truncates an outcome merely for being very improbable. And this reformulation nonetheless yields a determinate practical recommendation which an initially agnostic but fully rational and fully informed agent would be rationally required to follow, and which is not simply the recommendation that agent would have reached anyway on independent, non-Pascalian grounds.

Give me your credence in P as a single number, together with the decomposition that produces it. I want at minimum the following sub-questions each assigned a rough probability and a one-line justification, laid out as a table:

Q1 — Does the retreat from infinite to astronomically large finite payoffs preserve the argument's force, or does the Wager depend essentially on the infinite (for instance because the infinite version conceals a sensitivity to the prior that the finite version exposes)?

Q2 — Is reversal symmetry a genuine structural constraint on rational priors, on a par with a principle of indifference, or is it an ad hoc device that rigs the hypothesis space against the Wager?

Q3 — Grain sensitivity. The Wager's verdict appears to depend on how finely deities are individuated: describe the space coarsely and the theistic hypotheses carry substantial credence mass; refine it until each hypothesis is maximally specific and that mass spreads thin. Is there a non-arbitrary grain at which to run the calculation, and does the fate of P turn on whether there is one?

Q4 — Self-defeating payoffs. On many otherwise eligible theistic hypotheses the reward is conditional on the belief not having been adopted for wager-like reasons, so that acting on the argument can destroy the payoff it advertises. Is this a distinct objection from the familiar point that belief is not directly voluntary, and how much weight does it bear?

Q5 — Fanaticism. Is the Wager simply an instance of the general difficulty that expected-utility maximization over tiny probabilities and huge payoffs collapses into fanaticism, so that it stands or falls with that general problem rather than with anything specific to religion?

Q6 — The evidential leak. Under evidential decision theory the act of wagering can be evidence of a favorable nature or of election; under causal decision theory it is not. How much of the Wager's apparent force comes from that leak, and does the leak survive inside the reformulation P describes?
