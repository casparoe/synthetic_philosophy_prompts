I'm in a tiny reading group — two logicians, one philosopher of maths, and me; I'm taking a course on philosophy of mathematics and this was supposed to be the bit I'd actually follow — and we've now spent something like eight weeks on the classical versus intuitionistic/constructive logic dispute, and it has completely stalled. Nobody has moved an inch. The pattern is always the same: the constructivist says that asserting an existential carries an obligation to produce a witness and that asserting a disjunction carries an obligation to say which side holds, so excluded middle isn't something you get for free, it's a claim about having a general decision method; the classicist says fine, but that's an epistemic gloss bolted onto an ordinary quantifier, denying that every number either has the property or lacks it is just false, and the constructive reading is a different notion wearing the same symbols. Then someone waves at the fact that the two systems can be translated into each other (one of the logicians sketched a negative or double-negation translation on the board, I think it's called the Godel-Gentzen translation but I may be garbling it), someone else says that just proves it's a verbal dispute about the meaning of the connectives, a la Quine's point that a change of logic is a change of subject, and we go round again. I don't want another survey of the positions — I've read the encyclopedia entries and I can't get library access from where I am anyway, so please don't bother with references.

My own hypothesis, which I'm not at all confident about, is that the fight is downstream of a disagreement about what a proof is *for*: whether mathematics is in the business of describing a determinate structure that's already out there, or of codifying procedures and computations we can actually carry out. If that's right then the logic dispute can't be settled by arguing about logic, because each side's connective-meanings are picked to fit their prior answer about the point of the enterprise, and neither answer is something anybody in my group has argued for — they've just got different starting pictures. But I also suspect there's a partly factual or formal question hiding inside it that we keep talking past: how much of ordinary mathematics actually changes when you insist on constructive proofs. The reason I think this is that one member of the group keeps insisting that for a large class of sentences the two systems prove exactly the same theorems, so the whole thing must be verbal, and I've never been able to tell whether that's right or whether it only holds for sentences of some restricted shape and everything interesting falls outside it.

So here's what I'd like. Diagnose the impasse using what each side actually says and actually denies — tell me honestly how much of it is verbal, how much is a real difference about standards of evidence or about which cases count as data, how much is bedrock starting intuition that neither side can argue for, and whether there's a hidden formal or quasi-empirical question doing the work. As a concrete progress-criterion I've been toying with something I call the witness test: take a contested theorem, ask whether the classical proof can be converted into one that hands you an algorithm producing the object it says exists, and sort theorems into ones where the witness comes out anyway and ones where it genuinely doesn't; my guess is that if we actually ran that audit across a chunk of standard mathematics, the big dispute would dissolve into a pile of local technical questions, and that dissolution would itself be the diagnosis. Is the witness test a good idea, is it circular, is it just the constructivist's position smuggled in as a method, or is there something better — a distinction, a formal result, a reframing of the question — that would count as progress? And if your conclusion is that no progress should be expected here, I want the argument for that too.
