Next month our philosophy of science reading group is doing a short unit on natural kinds, and two of its members — let me call them Marcus and Priya — have been arguing about it, inconclusively, for a year. Marcus holds something close to the Kripke–Putnam picture: a genuine natural kind has an essence, normally a microstructural one, discovered empirically rather than known in advance; kind terms designate rigidly; the classificatory successes of the special sciences are, where they are not simply reducible to the underlying kinds, useful but interest-relative groupings that do not track the joints. Priya defends a homeostatic property cluster view: a kind is a family of properties that reliably co-occur because some mechanism or mechanisms keep them together, so that the clustering supports induction and explanation; on her view the paradigm natural kinds include cell types, species, and kinds studied in the human sciences, they come in degrees of naturalness, and nothing requires a single microstructure or a unique correct taxonomy. They have agreed, at my suggestion, to try an adversarial collaboration, and I have been asked to produce the draft document that both of them could put their names to. Please write it, in American English, as a numbered section of agreed claims — substantive ones, not the kind of thing nobody would deny, covering at least what both accept about reference and externalism, about why description-based or purely operational definitions of kind terms fail, about disjunctive and otherwise gerrymandered classes that do not support induction, and about why some scientific classifications are plainly shaped by convenience — followed by a table of the residual disagreements, each stated in wording that its own side would accept rather than in the other side's caricature of it, and then a section on the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them.

Two things I want built into the document. First, Marcus's stock examples are water, gold, jade, and the familiar thought experiment about a superficially identical liquid with a different microstructure on a duplicate planet; Priya's are cell types, species, and psychiatric or economic kinds; each accuses the other of picking examples that rig the question. So I want at least one neutral case, designed in advance of the outcome, that neither has used, together with a statement of what each side predicts about it and what finding would in their own eyes count against them. The case I have drafted is this: astronomers identify a class of persistent atmospheric structures, call them ribbon vortices, on three very different worlds — a gas giant, a dusty terrestrial planet with a thick atmosphere, and a tidally locked ocean world. They share a morphology, a distribution of lifetimes, and a role in heat transport; a single dynamical model of stratification and shear predicts their behavior, to within observational error, in all three settings; and compositionally they have nothing in common — ammonia ice, mineral dust, and water droplets respectively. Is that a natural kind, and what would settle it? Second, I have my own hunches, and I would rather the document not simply ratify them: that the metaphor of carving nature at its joints is doing different work for each of them; that their apparent disagreement about microstructure in the simple cases is largely verbal while their disagreement about whether many cross-cutting classifications can be equally natural is not; and that part of what is really at issue is what a scientific taxonomy is for — prediction, explanation, or intervention. Tell me where I am wrong. If you conclude that some portion of the dispute is only about words, say exactly which portion and why; if you think a crux is genuine but currently untestable, say so rather than inventing a test. Please keep the whole thing to roughly eight hundred to a thousand words, and do not cite or recommend any reading: I want the argument, not a bibliography.
