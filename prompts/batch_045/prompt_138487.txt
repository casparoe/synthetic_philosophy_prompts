So I've ended up somewhere I don't love and I want someone to try to knock me off it. Background: I'm not an academic at all, I do procurement and logistics for a small medical-supply nonprofit, and over the last ten years I've done long stints in west Africa, the Gulf, and one stretch in eastern Europe, and I keep watching the same scene. Someone local hands a hospital administrator a goat, or slips a clerk money to move a file that has already been approved, or hires a cousin for the warehouse, and the visiting evaluator writes it up as corruption; the local colleague thinks the evaluator is the wierd one, and honestly I've come around to their side. My position now is roughly this: I've started calling it the two-ledger problem — the very same transfer gets entered in one moral ledger as a gift that builds an obligation between people, and in another ledger as a bribe that wrecks an impartial process, and both descriptions are right there available to everyone involved, so what an anti-corruption framework really does is announce which ledger is the real one. And the ledger it announces is a pretty recent, pretty local one: the state as an impersonal machine, offices as things you hold rather than relationships you are in, helping someone you know as a betrayal. On top of that I have my own home-made distinction between speed money and steering money. Speed money changes how fast an outcome arrives when the outcome was already the right one — the permit was going to be granted, the file was next in the pile. Steering money changes which outcome you get: whose bid wins, which road gets built, who walks. I think only steering money is really corruption, and I think the thing that actually guts countries is steering money, and I think steering money is mostly legal in the places that write the rules — campaign finance, the revolving door, a contractor's donation, the friendly regulator — which is why I've come to suspect that the big annual corruption index put out by that Berlin-based transparency NGO, and the US law against bribing foreign officials from the seventies, and the UN convention from the early 2000s, are mostly measuring distance from an ideal that the rich countries themselves don't follow and exporting the difference as a moral verdict on everyone else. My friend Marisol thinks I've talked myself into something convenient and self-serving, and I'm worried she's right.

So: play it straight and tell me what would count against that. Not the obvious cheerleading for the other side — I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want them sorted by what my own side would actually concede rather than only what a critic would say. Concretely: which observations would show that clientelism and gift-giving harm people on their own terms and not just on a borrowed yardstick? I'm thinking about things like whether patronage systematically starves public goods (you'd rather your patron get you one job than vote for a water system that serves everyone), whether predictable small payments are actually different in kind from arbitrary ones, whether there's a trap where once everyone expects a payment nobody has an interest in being the first honest one, and whether the guy at the bottom of the pile — the mother turned away from the clinic, the driver who gets stopped twice a day — is worse off under the relationship version than under the impersonal version. And what about my speed/steering line specifically: is there a real case where speed money isn't just a queue fee but quietly decides who even gets in the queue? Be honest about whether I've insulated myself: if every example of real harm gets re-described by me as "no, that's not clientelism, that's predation" or "that's capture, not gift-giving," then I've qualified my view into something that can't lose, and I want to know whether I've done that, and if so whether that's actually a fatal problem for a view of this kind or whether some views are legitimately allowed to be framework-level claims that you argue about rather than test. If you think some of my defeaters have arguably already happened, say which.

What I'd personally find most moving, so weight it if you think it deserves weight: evidence about whether people inside these systems experience them as unjust in their own vocabulary rather than in imported NGO vocabulary. I have this rough thing I call the complaint test — if the participants themselves complain, in their own words and their own concepts, then something is genuinely going wrong there and I should drop the cultural-relativist line; if they only complain once an evaluator shows up with a checklist, then I think I'm still right. Tell me whether that test is circular (do people just absorb whichever framework has power over them?) and whether it survives. Please write in American English, somewhere around 1200 to 1800 words, and one practical thing: I'm on a satellite connection out here for another month, so don't tell me to go read a book or look up a paper or send me a list of authors and studies — I can't get them and guessed-at citations are useless to me. Just argue it out with cases and reasoning, and if you have to invent a hypothetical to make a point, make it a concrete one I could imagine actually happening at a warehouse or a district hospital.
