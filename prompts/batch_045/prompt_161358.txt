For most of my working life the question of whether my plays are propaganda has been academic in the dismissive sense. I write for a small circuit of Black companies; the audiences arrive already sharing most of my premises; nobody has ever offered me money to change a stranger's mind. That changed last month.

I have been offered the largest commission of my career by a foundation whose charter commits it to racial justice, and the terms have forced the old question on me in a form I am not equipped to answer. The fee is substantial, and roughly a third of it is contingent on what the foundation calls measurable audience impact: the production is to tour to four partner houses in largely white suburbs, and subscribers there will complete short questionnaires before the show and after it, administered by an evaluator the foundation hires, measuring movement on a scale of racial attitudes. There is also to be a facilitated talkback curriculum after each performance. The play I want to write is a two-hander about two sisters settling their mother's estate in a Mississippi town; it runs ninety minutes; no white character appears in it, and none is discussed at any length. I did not design it to convert anyone.

Two people I respect have given me opposite advice. A director I have wanted to work with for years withdrew from the project when he read the contract, on the grounds that a play whose final third is paid for by a shift in a survey number is propaganda, and that whatever propaganda is good for, it is not what art is good for. A much younger dramaturg on the same project told me that this objection is naïve, that all art is propaganda and always must be, and that the only real question is whose propaganda it is. Both of them invoke W. E. B. Du Bois's 1926 essay on the criteria of Black art, where he argues that all art is propaganda despite what he called the purists, and where his supporting thought seems to be that a work which disclaims any message still does political work, because it quietly ratifies the arrangement of things that already obtains.

It seems to me that their disagreement is an equivocation, and I want the equivocation spelled out and then settled. As I have been turning this over, I have found it useful to distinguish three things that get called propaganda. In the first, aim sense, a work is made in order to bring about some specified change in an audience's attitudes or conduct, and the maker would count the work as having failed if the change did not occur. In the second, uptake sense, a work tends in fact to move its audiences in a politically relevant direction regardless of what the maker intended or disclaimed. In the third sense, which I have been calling the ratifying sense, a work's political character consists entirely in its being addressed to a community that already has the orientation in question: it confirms them, makes their experience legible to themselves, and sustains a shared life, rather than attempting to convert anyone outside it. Du Bois's claim looks to me to be true, if it is true at all, only in the second or third sense; and it is not obvious that a claim true in those senses either supports or condemns the arrangement in this contract.

Two further considerations keep reversing my judgment, and I want them addressed rather than gestured at. The first is about aesthetic cost. Even granting that every work does political work on its audience, it does not follow that making a work to a specified attitudinal specification is aesthetically free. Something has to give: the audience is treated as the site of a measurable outcome rather than as an end; scenes that would confuse the intended takeaway get trimmed; characters are not permitted to want what the message forbids. But there is a counter-consideration pointing the other way, which is that a work addressed only to the already-converted is exposed to its own characteristic vice, flattery, and that the missionary mode imposes a real discipline, namely that the work must become legible to someone who does not already agree, which may be why so much of the art I actually admire was written under hostile conditions.

The second is about time. Propaganda in the aim sense is self-consuming: if it succeeds, its occasion disappears, and the work is left referring to a fight nobody is having. Propaganda in the ratifying sense is self-renewing, because the community it addresses is what it is partly in virtue of being addressed. This makes me suspect that what my director really objects to is not persuasion but obsolescence, and that obsolescence is a defect only on some views of what art is for, and only for some modes of political art. It also raises a question I cannot settle about my own play: whether the deepest problem is not the surveys themselves but the mismatch between the audience the play implies and the audience the tour actually supplies, a ratifying work consumed as a missionary one, with a questionnaire to certify the conversion.

What I am asking for is an essay, not advice about my career, though the essay should end by applying itself to the case. Do the following: say in which of the three senses, if any, Du Bois's thesis is actually defensible, and say what its truth in that sense does and does not license; say whether the aesthetic objection to propaganda attaches to political content, to persuasive aim, to measurement, or to the mismatch between implied and actual audience, and give the mechanism by which it does its damage; say how much weight the self-consuming character of persuasive art should carry, given that plenty of undisputed art is occasional; and then, on the basis of that, tell me plainly which of taking the commission as offered, taking it while refusing the contingent tranche and the talkback curriculum, or refusing it outright is the defensible course, and why. Do not conclude that the considerations balance. Commit to the strongest version of the view you end up holding, and state what would have to be true for the opposite view to win.
