I'm 71, retired chemist — thirty-odd years of NMR, mostly proteins, not that it matters — and I've drifted into reading philosophy of mind since I stopped work. It started because I re-read Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach last winter, which I first read back in about 1981 and loved, and this time I kept stopping to ask whether the book's central analogy, the move from self-referential formulas to self-aware minds, is doing any argumentative work or whether I was just being swept along by it. I cant tell from the inside, so I want to stage a fight.

Please draft the joint statement that two people could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

Camp A: the analogy has teeth. Roughly the Hofstadter/Dennett-flavored line — that the machinery which lets a rich formal system encode claims about itself, and the tangled hierarchy or strange loop that results, is the right shape of explanation for how a physical system comes to have a first-person "I", and that the resistance people feel to this is a failure of imagination rather than an argument.

Camp B: the analogy is a pun with footnotes. Gödel's theorems are results about provability and consistency in formal systems extending arithmetic; self-reference in a machine is trivially cheap (quines, programs that print their own source, systems that answer questions about their own code); nothing in the first incompleteness theorem bears on consciousness; and the stronger Lucas-Penrose style moves that try to extract an anti-mechanist conclusion from it are just invalid.

Now the part I actually want you to be careful about. Everyone who talks about this lumps together three quite different pieces of the Gödelian machinery, and I want the document to keep them apart:

1. Fixed points / diagonalization: you can build a sentence, or a program, that refers to itself.
2. Unprovability: that sentence comes out undecided — and this needs the system to be consistent, or sound, and to be able to reason about its own proofs.
3. Unselfcertifiability: the system can't see from the inside that everything it proves is true — second incompleteness, Löb-style limits, the fact that a system cannot certify its own soundness.

For each of the three, I want both camps to say, in wording they'd accept for their own side, whether that ingredient does any work in the argument about self-aware minds, and if so which. If part of the dispute dissolves once you separate them — say, if camp A's real claim only ever needed ingredient 1, while camp B's objection only ever lands on ingredient 3 — I want the document to say so explicitly and to mark that part of the disagreement as verbal rather than substantive.

Then: cruxes, and for each crux a case or probe both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down before the fact. Here's a candidate set of probes to start from — improve it or replace parts of it if you think I've loaded it toward one camp:

Build two systems matched on third-person task performance. System S has a dedicated subsystem that maintains and updates an internal model of the whole system — a self-model in the plain control-theory sense, the thing a controller actually consults — and can be queried for reports about itself. System N has no such subsystem; its "self-reports" come out of the same general machinery that answers third-person questions. Probes: (a) can either be made to predict its own future outputs better than an outside predictor with the same training data and the same compute? (b) if you lesion S's self-model so it becomes subtly inaccurate, do you get a dissociation — first-person reports degrade while third-person performance stays intact — and does N show that dissociation already, at baseline? (c) can either be driven into something structurally like the Gödel sentence, by being asked a question about its own answer to that question in a way that forces a fixed point it can't settle consistently, and if so does the failure look like anything we'd want to call the system's being puzzled about itself, or just a crash?

Format: a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table with each side's own wording for its own position, then the cruxes with predictions.

At the end, a short section you write as yourself rather than in the joint voice — call it whatever you like — covering: which agreed claim is most likely a polite fiction that both camps signed because it was vague enough to be meaningless; which single crux, coming out the other way, would do the most damage to the framing of the whole document; and where your reconstruction of one camp is weakest, meaning which premise you attributed to a side that a real person holding it might disown. Also flag anything the document quietly assumes that I didn't specify — for instance, whether "self-model" means something causally efficacious in the system's behavior or something that merely mirrors it, and whether the target phenomenon is consciousness or something thinner like self-knowledge or reportability. And if you think my three-way split of the Gödelian machinery is itself the weak point, say that too.

No reading lists, no citations, no suggestions for what to read next — I'm not going to look anything up. I'd rather have 1200 words that take a position than 3000 that survey the literature.
