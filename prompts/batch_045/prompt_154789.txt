Looking for a concept here, not just a label — I want to know what this thing is, what it isnt, and where its edges are.

Pattern I keep running into: an utterance whose literal wording commits the speaker to nothing, where the *kind of act* it turns out to be gets settled by what the hearer does with it afterwards, and where the speaker seems to be aiming for exactly that.

Two cases. My boss says, very pleasantly: "it'd be a real shame if your name didnt end up on this filing." No order, no promise, nothing about his own future conduct. If I take it as pressure and comply, it was pressure; if I laugh it off, it apparently was only ever a remark, and he can always retreat to the sentence he actually said. Same shape: a neighbour says "nice bike, be a shame if anything happened to it."

The thing I want to seperate this from is the speaker implying extra content. I half-remember the word "implicature" from a class, but that seems to be about extra content, and here nothing extra is being communicated — the content is fully out in the open, it's the force that's up in the air. What looks distinctive is that the force gets deliberately handed over: he intends, roughly, that whatever you take him to be doing is what he's doing. The intention is about the uptake rather than prior to it, which seems to break both of the obvious stories — force fixed by what the speaker meant, and force fixed by the rules or conventions attached to the sentence.

So: does this configuration have an established name, and how does it sit with the nearest ones — is "indirect speech act" this, or something else? If nothing fits, say so and coin a term with a definition, plus one case that barely qualifies and one that looks similar but doesnt. Dont just hand me a list of names and papers, I want the carving.
