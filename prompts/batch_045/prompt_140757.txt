A friend of mine and I spent two hours last night arguing about whether a change we are considering is really a Pareto improvement, and we never settled it, so I now have to teach the idea properly rather than gesture at it. The setting: we can alter the default configuration of a system many users interact with. Call an intervention a *safe* Pareto improvement over the status quo when, for every user, expected utility is weakly higher under the intervention than under the status quo under every prior in some set of priors we are willing to countenance; call it *doubly safe* when, in addition, no user's expectation falls once she learns the intervention has been adopted and is offered a genuine opt-out. Our dispute was whether the first condition already settles the ethics: I said yes, she said the second is the whole point.

Next week I have eighty minutes with a group of our engineers to explain the concept. They are fluent in expected utility and in A/B testing, have no welfare economics, and will arrive assuming that "Pareto improvement" just means "nobody gets hurt" and that "safe" means "riskless."

Do not explain the concept to me; tell me how to teach it. What has to be in place before the "safe" qualifier can so much as be stated — the ordering of ex-post, ex-ante and interim comparisons, the role the set of priors plays, whether behavioural response to the intervention belongs inside the definition at all — and why in that order rather than another. Give me the single example that should carry the whole session, and say what it must contain and what it must deliberately not contain (no money, or no more than two agents, or no lotteries: your call, with the reason). Then the misconceptions, in the order in which they will actually form in the room, each with the point in the sequence at which I head it off: I expect the slide into compensation-tests, "safe" read as riskless, ex-ante gains taken to license imposition without consent, and the thought that an opt-out is just redundancy. Give me one closing question that tells me whether they have the distinction rather than the vocabulary, and say what I should openly refuse to cover in eighty minutes.

Under 450 words for the whole plan.
