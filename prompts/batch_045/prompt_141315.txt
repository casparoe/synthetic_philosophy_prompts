I'm 71, retired from a career in fisheries stock assessment — thirty-five years of staring at multiplicative dynamics in fish populations, which is probably why all of this grabbed me — and in the last couple of years I've got interested in decision theory, mostly from the ergodicity / geometric rationality end of it. I now run my own retirement portfolio Kelly-style, and I sit on the board of a small conservation trust where I keep trying to get the others to think in the same terms. I've written down what I think I believe and I'd like to know whether it actually hangs together, because I have a feeling there's a contradiction in there somewhere and I cant locate it. My son-in-law, who works in something adjacent to mathematical finance, says at least two of these can't both be true but won't say which.

Here's the set.

1. The rule is maximize expected log wealth. What convinces me is the long-run argument: over a long sequence of multiplicative gambles, the log-optimal rule ends up ahead of any rival with probability going to one, and that strikes me as a fact about how compounding works rather than a fact about my tastes. I also like — and this may be where I'm double counting — that the same rule drops out of ordinary expected-utility theory if you just plug in log as your utility function, so it isn't an ad hoc thing I invented. Because of that second bit I tend to tell my board colleagues that anyone in a repeated multiplicative situation ought to size this way, whatever their attitude to risk.

2. I like that ruin gets handled without me stipulating anything. Log of zero is minus infinity, so the rule never risks everything and never takes a bet at fair or worse odds, and there is no parameter for me to tune about how bad going bust is. The refusal of a small chance of total loss in exchange for a large gain falls out of the maths rather than out of my temperament, which is why I trust it.

3. Except I have in fact modified the rule for myself, and I think of it as a refinement rather than a fudge. Zero isn't really zero for me: if I lost everything my kids would not let me starve. So I score outcomes by log(w + c), where c is what I'd be bailed out to — I put it at roughly two years of my current spending. I like that this lets me accept small chances of total loss when the upside is big, which plain Kelly won't. I've been assuming it stays myopic and that it's a small correction that changes nothing structural. Maybe both of those assumptions are wrong.

4. I use half-Kelly in practice because I don't trust my own probability estimates. I've seen it said that this costs only about a quarter of the asymptotic growth rate while roughly halving the swings, and I stopped worrying about whether that's inconsistent with thinking full Kelly is optimal.

5. I think the rule is myopic — size each opportunity on its own, when it arrives, without planning the sequence — and I also size the several opportunities that turn up in the same month each separately, which I've been treating as the same claim.

6. I want to apply the geometric lens well beyond money. The trust grants every year out of an endowment, and I've been arguing that when we commit to something risky and multi-year we should size it to maximize expected log of the endowment rather than expected dollars moved, on the grounds that we'll be doing this again for fifty years and the dynamics are the same. I don't deny that the good done by any particular grant is near enough linear in the dollars at these sizes, and I don't deny that what we care about is the sum of the good done by each grant rather than the product. I also think the argument doesn't depend on any of us personally gaining, which is part of why I think it's a principle and not just prudence about my own money.

7. But I also think that for a decision that genuinely won't recur, where nothing compounds, maximizing expected value is simply correct. And yet I notice I want to apply the cautious geometric lens to one-off personal decisions as well — a surgery with a small chance of a bad outcome and a good chance of fixing something, say — where as far as I can see nothing is compounding at all. I don't know whether that's the same principle or just cowardice.

So: please audit this rather than explain it to me. I know what Kelly betting is. Which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it? Rank the real ones by severity. For each, say precisely what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me — I especially want to know whether the modification in (3) is a harmless tweak or whether it quietly wrecks something I said I valued in (1) and (2), and whether (6) follows from (1) at all or whether I'm smuggling in an extra premise about what the trust is for. Then tell me the single cheapest revision that cleans up the most. If some of it is honestly fine as it stands, say which.
