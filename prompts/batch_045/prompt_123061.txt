I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy background, and my team is building a memorial product I've started to feel uneasy about: we ingest a dead person's entire digital residue — mail, chat logs, drafts, photos, calendar, search history, location and health data — and generate a book-length life written in the first person, which the family can also query later and have it answer in the subject's voice. Two tiers are on the table. "Complete" suppresses nothing: the only editorial act left is which thread to follow first, since nothing may be cut or reordered. "Curated" lets the family, or the person while still alive, flag material to be dropped, and reads in practice as a tribute.

I've been reading Plutarch's parallel lives of Greeks and Romans alongside this, and I'd like to know where he would come down. Concretely: which tier, if either, would he count as a life rather than a chronicle, and could either one do the improving work he seems to have wanted the genre to do? I mean answers that argue from what he actually did and said — that he opens the Alexander by saying he writes lives, not histories; that a stray remark or a joke can show character better than a celebrated battle; that he sets each Greek beside a Roman and weighs them; that there is a preface somewhere about holding these men in front of you like a mirror — rather than from the assumption that he would simply be appalled by the machinery. Two follow-ups I especially care about: would he see the curated tier as the kind of flattery he warns about, or is his own practice closer to it than we like to admit; and would he think a life assembled by a machine, with judgement removed from selection, can teach anyone anything at all? For what it's worth, most of our customers are completely ordinary people rather than generals and statesmen, which may matter to him too.
