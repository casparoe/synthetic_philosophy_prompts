I chair the exceptional-funding panel of a regional hospital system. Twice a month we sit for three hours and work through twelve to twenty applications for treatments that are not on our formulary: roughly twenty-five minutes per case, including discussion and the written statement of reasons we owe each applicant. We fund about half. Our decisions cannot be appealed on the merits, only on procedure. The panel is a rotating clinician, a lay member, a health economist from our finance office, and me; anything above fifteen thousand dollars also needs the finance officer's signature, and we are audited annually. Our regulator requires us to assign every application a single integer score on a zero-to-one-hundred need-benefit scale and to publish the scores, so applicants can and do read two files side by side and ask why theirs came out lower.

The part of the work I have become convinced we are bad at is not clinical and not economic. It is that every one of these decisions requires comparing one person's gain in welfare against another person's, and I do not think we have any defensible way of doing that. We have drifted into two home-made devices. The first we call the standard-case ladder: three fixed written vignettes describing a mild, a moderate and a severe version of the relevant condition, and an applicant is asked to place themselves against them rather than give a bare zero-to-ten number, on the theory that people calibrate a bare number differently. The second we call the swap question: when two applications compete directly, someone asks how many applicants of the second kind would justify turning down one of the first kind, and we treat the answer as the exchange rate between them. I suspect the second device is simply the original problem restated in a form that feels answerable, and I suspect the first helps less than we assume, but I cannot say precisely where either one fails.

What I want from you is a decision procedure my panel could actually run, in the time we have, without training in welfare economics. Concretely: the questions to ask in order, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the point at which the procedure stops and returns a no; the point at which it hands the decision back to a human being because the question is one of values rather than facts; and an honest statement of which steps are judgment calls wearing the costume of a procedure. Please also tell me what to do when a number we need is simply unavailable, because that happens in most cases, and please do not give me citations, a reading list or a bibliography — I want the operational content only.

Three things seem to me to be at the centre of it, and I would like the procedure to take a position on each rather than describe them. First, the different comparison tasks are not equally demanding: ranking two people's levels of well-being, comparing the size of the improvement each would get, and comparing the ratio of one gain to another are three different jobs, and it may be that many of our decisions only require the second and so should not be turned into the first or the third. Second, the three currencies we could use — a money-metric one based on what someone would pay or accept, a subjective-well-being one based on self-report, and an objective-list or capability one based on shortfall from some reference level of functioning — fail in different and partly opposite ways: the first is contaminated by exactly the differences in means our charter forbids us to consider, the second is corrupted by the fact that people who have lived with a condition for years recalibrate their own scale upward while the recently diagnosed have not yet done so, and the third quietly imports a contested weighting of what matters and overrides what people say about themselves. Third, there is the question of whether a reported preference or a reported satisfaction is one we should correct because it was formed under conditions we would not endorse, and I want the procedure to say clearly when it refuses to make that correction and hands it to us as a values decision.

Please then run the finished procedure on three cases from our recent docket. The first is a clean one: nine thousand dollars buys either a device that would give a seven-year-old with a painful chronic condition something close to a normal childhood, or an intervention that would buy an eighty-four-year-old with metastatic disease about three months of moderate comfort, with a real chance of complications. The second is the one I expect to be uncomfortable, and I would rather you let the procedure go where it goes than spare my panel: fourteen thousand dollars buys the same tremor-suppressing surgery for either a thirty-four-year-old professional violinist, diagnosed eighteen months ago, who rates her quality of life at three out of ten and says her life is effectively over, or a fifty-eight-year-old warehouse worker, diagnosed eleven years ago, who has retrained into lighter work, is now losing even that, has no savings, and rates himself seven out of ten on the grounds that he has got used to it. The panel's instinct was to fund the violinist. The third is a case I suspect the procedure cannot settle at all: a cochlear implant for a two-year-old who cannot report anything and whose benefit everyone agrees is enormous and speculative, competing against a year of home care for six elderly patients whose benefits are smaller, better evidenced, and reportable. For that third case, tell me what the procedure's output should be, what fallback rule you recommend when it returns not determinable, and how we should record it so that we do not launder a guess into a published score.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which of the disagreements that actually divide my panel it will leave exactly as it found them.
