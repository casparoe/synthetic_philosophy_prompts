Hey — I'm taking this seminar on 18th-century moral philosophy right now (we did Hume a few weeks ago and we're about halfway through Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments), and I also sit on my university's undergraduate honor council, which is maybe the weirdest combination of things to be doing in the same semester. How it works: we hear somewhere between five and ten cases a term, hearings are capped at 45 minutes because the room is booked straight after us, the four or five of us on the panel are students with no philosophy or law training (one engineer, two econ majors, a nursing major), and we write a short recommendation — anything from a written warning up to recommending a one-term suspension — that goes to the dean's office, and she signs off on it maybe ninety percent of the time. Most of what we get is plagiarism or unauthorised collaboration, but in the last year we've started getting cases where the accusation is basically an output from online proctoring software flagging suspicious behaviour (repeated eye movement off screen, a second voice in the room, someone leaving the camera frame) with no actual evidence of copying, and a growing number of cases where what got broken is a rule nobody seems personally bothered by, like a shared answer key for a problem set worth two percent, reported by a TA rather than by anyone who was actually harmed. My roommate is on the same board and thinks all this ethics stuff is just a fancy way of saying use your gut, and I'd like to be able to show him it isn't.

So what I want is the operational version of the impartial spectator: a numbered set of steps I could actually read out at the start of a hearing and then walk through, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a no-sanction, and where it stops and hands the question to a human being because it's a values call rather than a factual one. Not a summary of what the spectator is — I've read the book, I don't need that. The pieces of Smith's machinery I think have to survive the translation, but tell me if any of them shouldn't: the business of projecting yourself into the other person's situation and bringing your feeling and theirs into some kind of mutual concord, rather than just asking how you'd feel in their place; the gap between the actual people in the room and the internal judge you're meant to consult instead of them; the requirement that the spectator see everything that produced the action before its verdict counts for anything; and the way he ties punishment to resentment, so that something merits sanction only if it's the sort of thing an impartial spectator would resent — which I think forces a decision about whether we're punishing what the person was aiming at or what actually happened. If one of those just collapses when you try to make it a checklist, say which one and why, because I'd rather drop it deliberately than have it quietly disappear.

I also need you to be blunt about where this misfires. One: our panel's gut reactions are visibly skewed — athletes and people in the big fraternities get way more benefit of the doubt than international students or people who are quiet and awkward at the hearing — and Smith himself says somewhere that we're disposed to sympathise much more readily with the rich and the great than with the poor and the obscure, which sounds like it would be a feature of his procedure rather than a bug in ours, so I want a step built specifically to catch that, and I want you to tell me honestly if you don't think there is one. Two: when the rule that got violated belongs to the university rather than to a person, so that nobody is resentful or the person who would be resentful doesn't exist because the professor genuinely doesn't care, the resentment half of the theory seems to run out — does the procedure stop there with a no, or does his own fallback to general rules (the kind of rule you'd want everyone to keep, including you) give us something to go on? Three: with the proctoring flags we usually cannot reconstruct what the person intended at all, and I don't want a procedure that pretends otherwise. Then run the finished thing on three cases from our queue: (1) a student who copied the analysis section of a lab report from a friend the night before it was due, during a family emergency, who admitted it immediately when asked and whose friend's own grade was dragged down by it; (2) a proctoring flag where the student's eyes go off screen about forty times in a midterm, no notes were found, the student says they were having a panic attack and were looking at a stuffed animal on their shelf, and the professor says the student's grade is unremarkable for them; (3) the shared answer key, used by roughly the whole class, worth two percent, professor thinks it's a joke that we're hearing it, TA who reported it is furious and wants a sanction. I have a definite feeling that on (3) the honest Smithian answer is one our board will hate, and I'd rather find that out now than forty minutes into a hearing where we end up doing whatever the chair wanted anyway. Finally, mark which steps are really judgment calls dressed up as procedure, because I have to be able to say that out loud when the dean asks why we decided what we decided.
