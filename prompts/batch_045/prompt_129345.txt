I serve on the integrity panel of a nonpartisan watchdog that keeps a public register of official falsehoods. For each contested statement by a public official we record one of three verdicts — censure, caution, or no finding — together with a one-sentence rationale, and we try to keep the register consistent with itself rather than merely intuitive. The project's founding statement says that we are not in the business of routine fact-checking but of protecting the shared factual world on which political judgment depends; the distinctions our rationales appeal to are broadly Arendt's about lying in politics, though we have never written them down as rules. Our seven entries so far:

1. A governor, after a beating by police that was filmed by several bystanders and seen by millions, says the footage was staged by a foreign intelligence service. — Censure. Rationale: denies what the public saw with its own eyes; would require citizens to distrust the evidence of their senses.
2. A mayor's office releases altered photographs to overstate turnout at a rally. — Censure. Rationale: substitutes a fabricated record for one that anyone present could have checked.
3. A defence minister tells parliament that a covert programme, whose existence is known to a handful of officials, does not exist. — Caution. Rationale: a lie about a genuine secret; it conceals a fact without rewriting anything the public can check.
4. A finance minister, holding internal forecasts of one per cent growth, publicly predicts four per cent. — No finding. Rationale: a claim about the future; a matter of judgement and persuasion rather than of fact.
5. A party chair, with evident and apparently sincere conviction, repeats that the last election was stolen, offering no evidence, and continues to do so after the courts and the recounts have rejected the claim. — Censure. Rationale: sincerity is no mitigation; what is at issue is not the speaker's intention but the destruction of the shared standard by which fact is told from fiction.
6. A foreign ministry publishes a long report with invented sources, presenting a complete alternative account of a massacre that international observers documented at the time; almost nobody outside the government believes a word of it. — Censure. Rationale: that it persuaded no one is irrelevant; the offense is the construction of a self-consistent rival world and the habituation of an audience to prefer coherence to fact.
7. A national statistical agency, under instruction, quietly stops publishing a long-running regional series while continuing to publish the national figure; nothing false is asserted. — Caution. Rationale: no falsehood is asserted and the absence is visible to anyone who looks; a real loss, but not a replacement of the record.

Reading back over these, three distinctions seem to be doing the work: whether the fact lied about is publicly checkable, whether the lie builds a self-consistent rival world or merely conceals something, and whether the speaker believes the falsehood. I cannot tell which is load-bearing, and there may be a fourth I am missing.

The new case: ten days before a municipal election, a city official with no prior entry in the register releases a short audio recording, produced with commercial voice-cloning software, in which a local investigative journalist — a real person, well known in the city — appears to accept a payment at a meeting that never took place. There is no accompanying apparatus: no forged documents, no corroborating witness, no narrative built around the clip, only the recording and an invitation to listen to it. Within a day three independent laboratories and the city's own forensics unit confirm that the audio is synthetic, and the journalist's editors point out that the cadence is wrong. The official continues to insist the recording is genuine and, by all appearances, believes it. No one directed him to release it, and he has no history of similar conduct. Most voters see or hear about the clip. Most say they do not believe it; a measurable minority say they no longer know what to think. He loses the election.

What verdict should we record, and with what rationale? Please do the following: set out the rules — plural — that fit all seven precedents, including any I have not listed, and say what each predicts about this case; identify the closest analog among our precedents and say where the analogy breaks; give the verdict you recommend and the reason for preferring the rule that yields it; if our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, state exactly what principle we would be adopting with each of the available choices; and name one further case the panel could rule on that would discriminate between the surviving rules, with each rule's prediction about it recorded in advance.
