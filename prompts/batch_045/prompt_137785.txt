Hi — bit of an odd one. I'm a retired materials scientist (most of my career was on phase transformations in alloys, so I spent a lot of my life being relaxed about the same macroscopic state sitting on top of completely different microstructures), and for the last couple of years I've been sitting in on a second-year "Mind and Machine" module at the local university as a sort of unofficial auditor. The lecturer, who's a friend of a friend, found out I used to set exams in my old department and half-jokingly asked me to write one essay question for the final. I was arguing about it with my walking group on Sunday and they convinced me I shouldnt just hand in my half-baked idea unedited, so here I am.

What I want is a question on functionalism and multiple realizability that you cant pass by reciting the summary. Every answer I've heard in the seminar so far is basically: mental states are functional states defined by their causal relations to inputs, other states and outputs; the same mental state could be realized in all sorts of different hardware, therefore the type-identity theory is false; then a paragraph about the "whole population arranged to act like a brain" style worry and a paragraph about the exclusion problem and done. I want a question where writing all of that gets you a C. The crux, as I see it, is that multiple realizability cuts both ways: it's the standard argument *for* functionalism, but it also looks like a threat to it, because the more abstract you make the role in order to cover every possible realizer, the more you seem to be describing things that plainly have no minds, and the more you thicken the role so it only catches the real cases, the closer you creep back to something close to an identity claim and you've lost the argument you started with. What I'd like the students to have to do is say, precisely, what a functionalist is committed to about what makes two systems' states the *same* mental state, and then say what the abstractness worry would have to establish to be an objection to functionalism rather than to a straw version of it.

I also want to throw in two cases of my own that they wont have seen, and you can sharpen them or replace them if theyre duds. The first is my annealing case: in my old lab we'd hold a specimen at a fixed point in a phase diagram while the microstructure underneath kept drifting, and nobody had any trouble calling it "the same state" at two times — so imagine a creature whose functional organization at every instant matches ours, but whose realizers drift continuously, so that no realizer type ever appears twice and the mapping from our states to its states isn't stable from one minute to the next. Same mental states as us, or not, and what does that show? The second is a kind of reverse case: one and the same physical state of one and the same system realizing two different mental states depending on what's going on around it. I've got a vague memory of something like this in the literature but I dont want the question to depend on it — if it's just a re-statement of the usual holism point, tell me, and give me a better pair. Anyway, what I need from you is: the question as it would actually appear on the paper (with whatever sub-questions and guidance you think is right — 45 minutes, 25 marks, closed book, second-years, about half of whom have only done one philosophy course); a model answer of about 700 to 800 words that takes a definite line rather than surveying options; and a marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves separating an A from a B and a B from a C, with a short list of the recitable bits that earn no marks and a one-line note on the mistake students will actually make for each. No reading list needed — they've done the standard stuff, I just want to know whether they've understood it.
