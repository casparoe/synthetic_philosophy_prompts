My friend and I got into a huge fight last night about the 2008 bailouts and we ended up talking past each other about whether the government was literally being money pumped. Her version: Bear Stearns gets rescued in March 2008, Lehman gets allowed to fail in September, then within days AIG is backstopped and the big banks get TARP money — the line every time was never again, and every time the next failure came along they caved and paid. Same agent, cycling plan, poorer each round, back where it started: that's a diachronic money pump. My version: nothing about the preferences changed, the evidence did. Nobody really knew in 2007 what an unmanaged Lehman failure would do to the payment system, and once you learn that, of course you act differently. Belief change isn't preference reversal, and I dont think the money pump argument has anything to say about it.

I want you to actually do the mapping instead of picking a side, and don't just tell me that intransitive preferences are exploitable — I know the synchronic argument, the diachronic one is a different animal. So: say what the diachronic money pump story would have to claim in order to apply here — who the agent is, what the nodes are, what the offers are, who the operator is, and whether the agent finishes where it started minus cash or somewhere genuinely different. Then the two bits I actually care about. First, the escape hatch: a diachronic pump only works on an agent who can't refuse the later trades, i.e. resoluteness or precommitment or handing the keys to someone else. Is let them all fail what resoluteness prescribes here, and is the fact that doing it once made things much worse an objection to the resolute prescription or just an objection to applying it at that node? Second, whether the theory even fits the case: is the US government one temporally extended agent whose preferences flip, or a series of different principals (Congress before, Treasury during, Congress after) where the diachronic framing is some kind of category error? And if the polity always wanted both stability and no moral hazard and just kept revising the weights as evidence came in, is there any sense in which it was diachronically irrational at all — or is the whole apparatus silent once you can't seperate preference change from belief change? Where the theory does apply, say what it would have told them to do differently; where it goes quiet, say that rather than forcing it to speak.
