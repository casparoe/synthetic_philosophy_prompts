I just joined the board of the tiny rural water district where I live — my own kid drinks this water — and we're completely stuck. We have to decide whether to keep our current chlorination, which produces a disinfection byproduct that's inside the legal limit but that some newer animal studies hint is a developmental risk well below that limit, or switch to a different treatment that handles much better the parasite that gave us two boil-water advisories, but which has thin human data and one suggestive endocrine signal. One member invoked the precautionary principle to argue for switching, another invoked the exact same phrase to argue for staying put, and we've had three meetings going in circles.

My working theory is that this isn't a scientific disagreement — we all agree on the facts — but a disagreement about who gets stuck with the burden of proof when nobody can run the study that would settle it, and that the phrase is just a flag people plant on whatever default they already liked. Is that right, or is there something substantive underneath: is precaution a procedural rule about who has to go get evidence, or a substantive decision rule about minimizing the worst case, and can it even apply to doing nothing? Diagnose what our board is actually fighting about, tell me what would count as progress here — a distinction, a rule, a different question — and be honest if the concept is doing no work in a case where precaution points both directions.
