I want you to analyze the Budget Control Act of 2011 and the sequester it triggered as a case of institutional weakness of will — a Ulysses contract that a legislature wrote for itself.

The facts as I remember them (treat them as given even if I'm slightly off on a detail — I don't want a fact-check, I want the analysis): in August 2011, at the end of the debt-ceiling fight, Congress passed a law that set up a twelve-member "supercommittee" with instructions to find $1.2 trillion in deficit reduction by late November. If the committee failed, across-the-board automatic cuts would kick in starting January 2013, split roughly evenly between defense and domestic discretionary spending, with things like Social Security and Medicaid largely protected. The idea, said out loud at the time, was that the cuts were meant to be so ugly that Congress would never let them happen — the pain was the point. The committee failed. The cuts were delayed by about two months at the end of 2012, then mostly took effect in 2013. Then, in the Ryan-Murray deal at the end of 2013, Congress lifted the caps for 2014 and 2015 (undoing something like $45 billion of that year's cuts) in exchange for extending the caps further into the future.

Two distinctions I want you to actually use. First, call a device "hard" if the agent genuinely cannot get out of it at the moment of temptation, and "soft" if a route back remains open — the mast is still there but the rope is tied with a slipknot anyone can undo. The BCA was obviously soft: a future Congress could always pass a new law. Second, I want you to separate what I'll call "first-order" weakness (taking the reward now against your own better judgment) from "second-order" weakness (knowing full well that you will renegotiate your own device, and building it soft anyway).

Here's what I'm after:

1. Do the mapping honestly. Who or what is the agent here — the 112th Congress, the federal government as a continuing entity, each party, each individual member? What plays the role of the immediately available reward and what plays the role of the delayed good? What is the discount function supposed to be over — money, elections, pain to constituents? And notice that the penalty was deliberately aimed at *both* parties' priorities at once, which makes it a self-binding device and a hostage-taking threat in the same package; can one mechanism really do both jobs, or do the two functions pull against each other?

2. Was the design naive or sophisticated in the technical sense? A naive discounter thinks future self will do the right thing; a sophisticated one knows it won't and builds accordingly. Building a penalty you can still vote away looks naive, but building one so painful that reneging requires an actual coalition and an actual crisis looks sophisticated. Which is it, and does "soft but expensive to renegotiate" behave like a real commitment or not?

3. The hard question: was the 2013 delay and the 2015-ish easing of the caps weakness of will at all? The usual story requires the agent to act against its own all-things-considered judgment *at the time of action*. But the 113th Congress wasn't the same people as the 112th, and it had new information about what the cuts actually felt like. So tell me what would discriminate between genuine akrasia, plain preference change, reasonable re-planning on new evidence, and strategic reneging that was baked into the plan from the start — and say which one you think this actually is.

4. Run it back the other way: does the episode confirm the hyperbolic-discounting picture, is it an anomaly, or does it fall outside what the theory was ever about? I suspect the interesting result is that the theory is silent somewhere — maybe on collective agents with changing membership, maybe on the fact that escaping a soft device here required *agreement* rather than willpower, maybe on how much of the work is being done by deadlines and transaction costs rather than by anything you'd call self-control. Say where it goes quiet instead of forcing it to speak.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give me the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate the question back at me.
