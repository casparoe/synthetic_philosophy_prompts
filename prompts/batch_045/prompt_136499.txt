I run a small reading group that grew out of the rationalist-blog meetups — most of us have been reading those blogs and forums since the Sequences — and we close each block of sessions by reading one poem aloud. We are finishing four weeks on philosophy and psychiatric classification, and the last meeting is on Hacking's looping effects. I want a villanelle.

The structural reason: a villanelle's two refrains return in exactly the same words while everything around them has moved on, and that is very close to the phenomenon itself. In a looping effect the label persists — the same criteria, the same words in the manual — but what it is to be an instance of the kind has shifted, because the people classified respond to being classified, and the kind is then revised in light of how they turned out. So let one refrain be a third-person clinical line of the sort that could sit in a diagnostic manual, and the other a first-person line in which someone takes that description up as a way to be. (The second is the part people usually leave out: new classifications open up new ways to be a person, including new ways to be ill, to act, to explain oneself, and to be held responsible.) Both refrains must come back verbatim; the tercets between them have to do the work of making each return land differently, so that by the closing quatrain the reader feels the words have been remade without having been altered.

Three constraints matter to me more than polish:

First, the final quatrain, where the two refrains at last appear together, must not resolve priority. Not the category produced the condition, and not the condition was always there and the category merely came along and found it: the poem should make it visible that neither claim has a stable answer when the thing being asked about has already been changed by the asking.

Second, please avoid the version of this idea that circulates in my corner of the internet, where naming looping effects has become shorthand for saying that psychiatric disorders are merely socially constructed and so not really real. Hacking's own position is harder to place than that: the kinds are historically contingent and interactive with the people they sort, and yet the suffering is real, the people are real, and a classification can be genuinely useful and genuinely true of them. Hold both. It should still work as a poem for someone who has never heard of Hacking.

Third, I want a second-order loop in it, something I have not seen discussed. The people in the poem's world know about looping: patients read about it, clinicians know they are watched for it, communities trade stories about how diagnoses travel and what a diagnosis does to a person. That knowledge is itself something people respond to, and it changes what the first-order loop does. Stage that without ever naming it, and without jargon anywhere in the poem.

Nineteen lines if you can manage it; if the form fights the material, break it deliberately and say where in the note. After the poem, a short prose note (150–200 words, no citations) on which of the poem's commitments are uncontroversial among people who actually work on this, which are live disagreements — how much of any given category's history is looping rather than something else, and whether looping threatens the reality of the kinds at all, including how to read Hacking's own metaphysical stance — and where the poem simply goes past the evidence, as I expect the second-order loop does.
