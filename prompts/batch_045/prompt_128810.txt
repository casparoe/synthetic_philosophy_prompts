I sit on the oversight commitee for a little experimental currency project on our island — we're calling it the Harbour Scrip — and we have to vote on a pilot issue at Thrusday's meeting. The assembly would print paper notes with no gold or silver behind them. Half the money would pay workers to finish a pier and drain some marshland; the other half would make small loans to tenant farmers and linen weavers. The notes could be used to pay taxes, and maybe swapped after the harvest for barley or a lease on some of the drained marsh plots.

Our board has been told to use a Berkeleyan framework for the decision — from George Berkeley, the philosopher-bishop who also wrote about money, industry and Irish poverty — and the principles we adopted (paraphrased) are these. One: money is not real wealth, only a token or tally for recording and transferring claims on what people actually produce. Two: the real wealth of a community is its land, its natural resources, and the industry and labour of its inhabitants. Three: the point of a currency is to set industry in motion and circulate the fruits of labour, not to be hoarded as treasure. Four, the uncomfortable one: deliberately creating new wants in poor people — shoes, better food, maybe tea or sugar — is a legitimate, even necessary, way to make them industrious, because idle contentment is the real enemy.

Here's the draft term sheet we keep going around in circles on, reduced to four dials:
1. How much scrip: 2,000 pounds, 5,000 pounds, or 10,000 pounds face value.
2. What it's redeemable in: (a) nothing, just a tax credit the assembly accepts; (b) a fixed quantity of barley or a lease on some drained marshland after harvest; or (c) half grain/land and half tax credit.
3. Whether we ear-mark about 10% of the issue to stock a small shop selling shoes, tea, sugar and wool on credit to labourers and weavers, with the explicit aim of giving them new tastes so they'll work for more than bare subsistence.
4. How long it runs: a six-month trial, a twelve-month pilot with a mandatory review, or open-ended.

The pro-scrip faction, call them the Industry side, wants 10,000 pounds, pure tax-credit scrip, the tea-and-shoes shop funded, and a twelve-month pilot. Their pitch is that metal-backed money is a superstition and the only real question is whether the paper sets hands and looms to work. The anti-scrip faction, call them the Anchor side, wants 2,000 pounds at most, strict grain-and-land redemption, no shop, and a six-month trial with a hard stop. Their pitch is that without a link to real, usable wealth the scrip is a collective delusion, and that engineering desires in the peasantry is a cynical trick that will leave people dependent on the board and the shop. A few of us think the middle option on redemption plus a very small shop might be the only politically viable thing, but we can't agree on the numbers.

Please draft the short decision memo I'll table at Thrusday's meeting. It should give a clear recommendation — approve the Industry version, approve the Anchor version, or a specific middle path with exact numbers — say exactly how we should set all four dials, and give a time frame for any pilot and its review. It should be written so the board could act on it, with reasons the rest of the board could actually respond to. It also needs to engage the strongest argument on the losing side rather than just restating the framework: if you come down for issuing the scrip, deal honestly with the fear that unbacked paper plus manufactured needs means dependency and a run on the shop; if you come down against it, or only for a tiny pilot, deal honestly with the fear that treating grain or land as the only real anchor just recreates the gold-fetish in another form and leaves real industry unfunded. No citations, footnotes, or reading list — I just need a memo the commitee could vote on. Aim for under 800 words if you can, but don't pad to fill space.
