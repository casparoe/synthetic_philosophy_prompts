I'm in a small reading group — half economic history, half philosophy of social science — and we've spent two sessions circling the question of whether history is ever really "locked in," or whether, given enough time and enough pressure to solve the same problems, the outcomes we care about would have arrived anyway. I keep rebuilding the same argument in my head, and every version of it lands somewhere I don't want to be, so I'd rather have it taken apart than keep patching it. Here it is, as cleanly as I can state it:

(1) We only ever explain historical outcomes under a functional description. Nobody tries to explain why this exact arrangement of matter obtained; we explain why the railways of a continent ended up sharing a gauge, why a writing system looks the way it does, why one programming language or video format or legal form became standard — that is, why some coordination or design problem got some solution.
(2) Functional descriptions are multiply realizable: at the moment the competing candidates were actually on the table, plenty of concretely different arrangements would have served the function about as well.
(3) If the candidates were functionally equivalent to within the noise, then the winner did not win by serving the function better. Something local did the selecting — an accident of timing, a marketing decision, a war, a demonstration race nobody should have read as evidence.
(4) So a path-dependence narrative never explains the functionally described outcome. What it explains is the residue: which key sits where, which side of the road, whose standard.
(5) By (1), the functionally described outcome is the one we care about. So the outcome we care about was going to arrive anyway — some writing system, some road rule, some standard, sooner or later.
(6) The residue cannot accumulate into anything that matters, because later agents face their own functional problems and will re-solve them; an arbitrary inherited detail is precisely the kind of thing that gets re-optimized away once it starts to bite. Lock-ins are delays, not destinies.
(7) Therefore: for every outcome that matters, the same outcome would have arrived anyway, and path dependence is at best a theory of trivia.

The conclusion is stronger than anything I actually believe, so I'd like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you? I'd most like to keep (2) and (3), and I'd rather give up (6) than (1) or (4), but I know that ranking premises by how much I like them isn't an argument, and I suspect (6) is doing more work than I've admitted. I also can't tell whether the argument is invalid or valid-with-a-false-premise, and I'd like you to say which it is before you start pricing the alternatives.

Two things I'd want folded in. First, a worry about my own framing, which may be a symptom or may be a red herring: I've been trying to make (5) precise by saying an outcome is R-inevitable at a baseline date if, in most re-runs of history from that baseline, some outcome falling under R occurs — but then R seems to do all the work, since at coarse granularity everything is inevitable and at fine granularity nothing is, and I don't know what fixes the right level. Second, my group distinguishes two claims I may have conflated: that a settled arrangement is now very costly to reverse, and that it would have arrived anyway under re-runs of history. If the argument were sound, would it establish both, or only one? Please also say how it bears on the cases where what matters is the order in which events happened versus merely which events happened at all — the argument as I've written it runs identically on both, and that uniformity makes me suspicious of it.
