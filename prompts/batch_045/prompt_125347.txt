I write literary fiction, and for about two years now I have kept a working notebook of my characters' pleasures — not of the events that produce them, but of the experiences themselves, set down as precisely as I can manage the way I would set down a room or a gesture. I began it because I had a working assumption I have since lost: that pleasure is a feeling with a texture of its own, something like warmth or nausea, which a novelist ought to be able to render directly. I cannot find it. Whenever I write the pleasure of cold water after a long swim, or of a passage finally locking into place in a rehearsal room, or of a man realizing he has been forgiven, what lands on the page is the description of the situation plus the fact that the character is absorbed by it. What the episodes seem to share is not a quality but a direction: attention caught and held, and held willingly. If that is right, then the thing I was looking for was never a sensation at all, and I would like to know what philosophers call the argument that led me there — but that is not really my question, because something else turned up in the notebook that I cannot place.

The something else is this. Take two experiences that are, by the character's own report and by mine, equally pleasant, involving the same activity, the same person, the same week. A woman who has trained for eight months swims a mile in a cold lake at dawn: it is mostly unpleasant while it lasts, it is not an experience she would describe as enjoyable, and she does it again the next morning. A month later she swims the same water on a warm afternoon, effortlessly, and it is unmistakably, sensuously delightful — more pleasant, she says, than the first. But there is a property the first swim has and the second lacks, and it is not intensity, not duration, not how much she likes it, and not how worthwhile she takes it to be: she thinks the afternoon swim is worth just as much as the dawn one and resents neither. The property is something like the experience's having been paid for. It shows up in the memory too, in an oddly specific way — the afternoon swim has almost no afterlife, while the dawn one is still available to her years later, and not as a memory of achievement, as a memory that still feels a certain way. I have the same note about a translator who solves a sentence she has been stuck on for a week, and about a man who spends an entire morning cleaning a window he did not dirty; in each case there is a version of the same episode, shorn of the effort or the risk, that would be pleasanter and that the character would not accept in exchange.

I have a name for it in my own notes — I call it the wage — and I have a deflationary hypothesis about it, which is that the "wage" is not a feature of the experience at all but a feature of what the experience licenses afterwards: you may remember it, you may feel entitled to it, you may tell a story in which it figures, and all of that is pleasant later. But I do not think this works, because the wage seems present while the episode is still going on, in cases where nothing later follows: a man finishing a task he will never mention to anyone, alone, at four in the morning. There is also a test I have been using on my drafts, which I think of as the swap test — offer the character the same experience with the effort and the risk removed, free, and see whether she refuses — but I recognize that refusal might show only that she wants the achievement and not the experience, which is a different, duller thing, and I cannot get the two apart from the inside.

So my question is whether there is an established concept, in philosophy or in psychology, for this specific axis: the felt earnedness or deservedness of a pleasure, as something distinguishable from how pleasant the experience is, from how much the subject enjoys or likes it, from whether the subject judges the activity valuable, and from the value of having achieved something. I am not looking for a reading list, and I would rather you flagged a term as uncertain than produced one you are not confident is real. If nothing established fits, I want the thing carved rather than labelled: what exactly it is, which nearby notions it is liable to be confused with and how it differs from each, and where its edges are — in particular whether my second family of cases, where the experience is grim and barely pleasant at all and still has the property, belongs to it or is something else, and whether an experience can have the property without the subject ever having done anything, which I suspect it can.
