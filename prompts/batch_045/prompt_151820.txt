I sit on a small advisory panel that a government has asked for recommendations on whether, and in what form, it should issue a formal apology for a dispossession carried out under one of its predecessor authorities in the nineteenth century: land taken, a population removed, and consequences still legible in the material conditions of the descendants today. We have been reading the texts of a good many official apologies issued elsewhere — by former colonial powers for killings committed under colonial rule, by national governments for children removed from their families under assimilation policies, by churches and cities for the parts they played in the same — and reading them side by side, I keep noticing a configuration I don't have a vocabulary for.

The statement is addressed to the descendants of the wronged group, and on the usual checklist it is impeccable: it names what was done, it says plainly that it was wrong, it avoids the hedged constructions people call non-apologies. But the apology is also heard by a second audience — the apologizing state's own public, and the wider community of states — and for that audience what seems to matter is only that the declaration was made: it displays humility, modernity, moral seriousness, a certain kind of national adulthood. The response of the wronged group appears to be largely irrelevant to that second hearing, and here is the part I cannot get straight: when the descendants refuse the apology, or accept it while insisting that it be followed by restitution, the domestic audience tends to read the refusal as bad faith or ingratitude, and the standing of the apologizing state appears to rise rather than fall. The apology seems to work best for the audience it was not addressed to precisely when it fails with the audience it was. A colleague called this virtue signalling, but that strikes me as wrong, or at least as missing the structure: what I am describing obtains even when the officials are entirely sincere and the statement is in every respect a full admission, so it is not a claim about motives. It is not a non-apology either, since nothing is hedged, and I don't think the notion of a costless signal gets it, because here the signal is fully credited — by everyone, including people with every reason to know better.

So: is there an established concept for this, and if not, would you name and define it, giving its boundary cases? I am after the carving more than the label. In particular, I would like to know whether the phenomenon is one or two, because I also noticed something adjacent that may or may not be the same thing: many of these statements locate the wrong wholly in the past, in a way that lets the present relationship begin fresh, with the result that continuing claims by the wronged group are treated as reopening a matter that has been settled — and I can't tell whether that is the same mechanism operating at a different point in the sequence or a genuinely different failure. Tell me which existing concepts are nearest, exactly how what I have described differs from each, and which fits best or why none does. I would also value a diagnostic we could actually use: what to look for in the wording, the timing, or the sequencing of a statement to tell whether it is doing this, and whether there is any way for a government to apologize at all without doing it. Please answer in continuous prose, as you would in an email or a letter — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
