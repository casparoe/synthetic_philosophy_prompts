I am an undergraduate in a philosophy of biology seminar, and for the last three weeks we have been working through quantitative genetics: the decomposition of phenotypic variance into additive genetic, dominance, environmental, and covariance components, the definition of narrow-sense heritability as the ratio of additive genetic variance to total phenotypic variance, and the designs (twin, family, adoption) used to estimate it. I can state the definition and do the exercises, but I cannot tell what I actually believe about what the coefficient means, because my reactions to particular cases pull in different directions. I have written down my verdicts below in the hope that you can extract the principle behind them, tell me which verdicts a defensible principle would force me to give up, and predict my verdicts on cases I have not listed.

(1) Number of kidneys, or number of fingers on a hand. Verdict: these are about as genetic and as innate as anything gets, and I want to say they are not heritable at all, or barely, because in an ordinary population almost none of the variation in them is genetic. So heritability is not a measure of how genetic a trait is.

(2) The classic metabolic disorder in which a missing enzyme causes severe cognitive impairment unless the child is placed on a restricted diet. Verdict: the enzyme difference is almost entirely genetic, the impairment used to run in families and would have shown up in family studies, and yet the impairment is entirely avoidable by an environmental change. So heritability is not a measure of how fixed or inevitable a trait is.

(3) Height among well-nourished people in a wealthy country: heritability around 0.8, and yet average adult height in those same countries rose by several centimetres across the twentieth century. Verdict: both claims are true and there is no conflict. But I also have a persistent sense that if the coefficient is that high, further improvements in nutrition cannot do much, and I know that sense must be wrong in some way. I would like to know whether there is any reading of it that survives, or whether it has to go entirely.

(4) A genetically uniform crop variety sown across soils of very different quality. Verdict: heritability of yield within that population is near zero, and yet yield plainly depends on the plant's genes. Mirror image: genetically varied seed in a uniform greenhouse gives a high heritability. So the number is relative to the actual spread of environments in the population studied, and a zero does not license any conclusion about genetic causation.

(5) The pair I keep going back and forth on. Suppose the heritability of some psychological trait — a cognitive test score, or years of schooling — is estimated at around 0.4 in a contemporary population. (a) I think this is genuine evidence that genes are among the causes of that trait in people. (b) I also think it is no evidence at all, or essentially none, that a difference in the trait between two groups — two birth cohorts fifty years apart, or children from different social classes in the same country — is genetic. These sit badly together: if genes are among the causes of the trait, why are they not candidates for explaining a difference in the trait? Our instructor's line was that heritability is a within-population statistic and says nothing about differences between groups, but I want to know exactly what licenses that refusal, and whether the honest version of (b) is weaker than I have stated it.

(6) A trait of the child's environment rather than of the child: growing up in a house with many books, or being read to regularly. Verdict: I want to say this can be heritable, because the parents who provide it also transmit whatever makes them provide it, and I want to say simultaneously that it is obviously not innate and that an intervention which puts books in houses could change it. Is heritable but thoroughly environmental a coherent verdict for me to hold, or does the principle have to say that in such cases the heritability is only apparently about the child's genes?

(7) Measurement. If a test is noisy, the heritability computed from it drops. Verdict: so the coefficient is partly a fact about our instruments. I do not want to conclude that heritability is not a property of the trait at all, and I want the principle to explain why I do not have to.

Here is my tentative statement, which I would like tested and repaired rather than replaced. Call it the variance-bookkeeping view: heritability is a ratio of variance components in a specified population at a specified time under the conditions that actually obtained; it answers the question how much of the differences among these individuals are statistically associated with genetic differences among these individuals; and it answers no other question. In particular it is (i) contrast-blind, saying nothing about the cause of any specified difference between groups or of any change over time, (ii) intervention-blind, saying nothing about what would happen under a change in conditions, and (iii) not a measure of the strength of genetic causation.

Please tell me whether that statement survives my seven verdicts, which clause does the most work, and which verdict it overturns. Then I would like predictions on cases I have not settled. What should I say about a trait whose variation in the population traces almost entirely to one rare mutation segregating in a few families, against a background of substantial environmental noise? What should I say will happen to the coefficient after a universal intervention, such as removing a widespread nutritional deficiency or correcting everyone's eyesight, and is the principle silent here or does it have a direction to predict? What should I say about a population in which mates are strongly sorted by the trait, across generations? What should I say about a heritability estimate that comes out larger in better-off families than in worse-off ones, a finding my instructor mentioned in passing — does that break the population-relativity clause, or does it just mean the coefficient is a family of statistics indexed by subgroup? And is there any well-defined notion of the heritability of a difference, or of the heritability of a change in a population mean, or is asking for that a category error that my clause (i) should explicitly forbid?

I am going to use whatever comes out of this as the analytic frame for my final paper, so I would rather have a precise principle with an honest statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate than a broad survey. Please do not give me a bibliography or point me to further reading; I will supply citations myself from the course syllabus.
