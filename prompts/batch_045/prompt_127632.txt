My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the old torture-versus-dust-specks question — whether there is some number of barely noticeable harms whose total is worse than a single instance of severe suffering — and we got nowhere, largely because we both kept appealing to what the law and the regulators actually do, and reading it in opposite directions. I want to go back to her with something better than an intuition, so I should like the practice itself set out clearly first.

Here is the pattern as I understand it. Safety regulators work in bands of risk: below some very low annual chance of death to a member of the public — I think something of the order of one in a million — a risk is called broadly acceptable and no further money need be spent on it; in the middle band the risk must be reduced so far as is reasonably practicable; above the top band it is not tolerated at any cost. And in working out what is reasonably practicable they multiply a tiny individual risk by a very large exposed population, arrive at an expected number of deaths, and put a cash value on that number, so that a hundred million people each bearing a one-in-a-million chance is treated, for costing purposes, as a hundred deaths. The courts are far less generous with small harms. A claimant whose only loss is trivial recovers nothing worth having, or is pushed into a regime of fixed tariffs and the small claims track that makes suing on one's own not worth the trouble; and a symptomless exposure that merely raises the chance of a future illness is not, I am told, actionable damage at all. Yet when a firm has imposed precisely such a trivial loss on a million people — misused personal data, an overcharge of a few pence on each of a million bills — the response is a group litigation order or a collective action, or a regulator's fine calculated as a percentage of worldwide turnover, or a statute fixing a set sum per affected person, and the specks have abruptly been added up after all. Then there is the top end: the absolute ban on torture and on inhuman or degrading treatment admits of no balancing whatsoever, not even in a public emergency, and no quantity of aggregated smaller benefit will justify it. And in between, the criminal courts are oddly inconsistent in their arithmetic: a fraudster's sentence is driven by the total sum taken, however many small victims it was taken from, whereas a man who assaults three people serves sentences concurrently, so that his third victim costs him almost nothing at all.

My friend made two moves I could not answer. First, she said the regulator's fine and the statutory per-person sum are not aggregations at all but tariffs: what is priced is the wrongdoer's conduct and the profit it yielded, not the sum of a million specks, and the practice is equally well explained by a deterrence theory which denies that there is any aggregate harm there to add up. Second, she offered what she called the postbag case: suppose an organisation opens and reads the letters of a million households and does nothing further with what it learns, so that each householder is wronged, each would rather it had not happened, and none would cross the street or pay more than a few pence to prevent it. If specks aggregate, she said, that is among the very worst things that has ever been done; if they do not, it is close to nothing; and the law's actual response — a fine nobody individual claims and nobody individual receives — is exactly what a system does when it cannot make up its mind.

So: what view of harm, and of the aggregation of harm, does this whole pattern of practice presuppose? I should like it reconstructed as a theory — where the thresholds are supposed to come from and whether they track anything in the harms themselves, whether the adding-up is meant to hold within a single life as well as across different lives, whether a risk nobody ever actually experiences counts as a harm in the first place, whether adding across persons is really the same act as adding within one person's history, and how the lexical ban at the top is meant to coexist with the arithmetic in the middle. Then tell me which parts of the pattern that theory strictly requires and which it merely sits comfortably with, such that several rival theories would license them equally well; which views of aggregation the practice quietly rules out; and what, concretely, would have to change in regulation, in civil litigation and in sentencing if we went over wholeheartedly to the view that specks really do aggregate — and again if we went over to the view that some few harms are lexically prior to any number of smaller ones.
