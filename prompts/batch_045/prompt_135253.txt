I commission the history and popular-science list at a mid-sized trade publisher, and a steady share of what reaches my inbox — twenty-odd proposals a year, and noticeably more in weeks when something Atlantis-adjacent airs on television — belongs to what my colleagues and I have started calling, only half-jokingly, the folder: Atlantis re-sited in some delta or submerged shelf, megalithic architecture credited to visitors from elsewhere, the chronology of antiquity alleged to be inflated or duplicated by centuries. Two episodes are the reason I am writing this rather than continuing to judge these by feel. Three years ago we published a book that a reviewer in a serious venue flatly called pseudohistory, and what stung was that I could not say, in one sentence, what we should have caught before acquisition. In the same season we declined a speculative but serious book on pre-Columbian contacts, largely because it felt tone-deaf to specialist consensus, and a competitor published it to considerable acclaim. I am therefore worried about both errors in roughly equal measure, and I am explicitly not asking for a licence to dismiss things.

What I want is a written decision procedure the acquisitions committee can actually run: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops with a no, and the points at which it hands the matter back to a person because what remains is a question of values or editorial judgement rather than of evidence. The constraints are real. The committee is five people, only one of whom has graduate training in a historical discipline; I get about two hours with a proposal before the meeting and the slot is ninety minutes; outside specialist readers are affordable roughly twice a year; and the output is not simply accept or reject but may also be publish with framing (a specialist foreword, placement in our overtly speculative series, a subtitle that signals contention) or commission a reader before deciding. I would like the procedure grounded in whatever is actually known about how pseudohistorical argumentation differs structurally from merely revisionist or minority history — the characteristic moves and the asymmetries in how evidence is handled, rather than a list of forbidden topics — but written so that an editor with no philosophy and no historiography can apply it, and with an honest closing section on the kinds of proposal where it will misfire.

I have been using two crude rules of my own, and I want you to keep, refine, or scrap each, and to tell me what I have left out that matters more than either. The first I call the negative control: I ask whether the proposal states its method plainly enough that I could imagine running it on a question whose answer is already settled, and I treat it as a bad sign if the method would have discovered something we know to be false — a correspondence-hunting method, say, that would find the same striking pattern between a randomly generated pair of traditions. The second I call the asymmetry check: I look at the account the proposal gives of why specialists reject it, and I ask whether that account is proportionate to the evidence or has quietly become a story about coordinated suppression, in which case I count the absence of expected evidence against the thesis rather than as merely undecided. My private worry is that the first rule would have killed the good book we wrongly declined and that the second is really a politeness test wearing a methodological costume.

Then run the finished procedure on three proposals from the current season and give me the verdicts it yields. First: a retired marine geologist argues that Plato's account preserves genuine memory of a Bronze Age polity destroyed in a specific, datable catastrophe, supported by submerged coastal features, deluge traditions from three continents, and a set of linguistic correspondences; the writing is sober, he concedes that two of his identifications are speculative, and he accuses no one of suppression. Second: a lavishly illustrated proposal contending that the precision of megalithic masonry in four regions exceeds what the documented toolkits of those cultures allow, offered with no positive account of who the alleged visitors were, when they came, or how they left no other trace; it is expected to sell extremely well, which is not nothing at our size, and the author is agreeable to whatever framing we propose. Third: a chronology proposal in the spirit of Fomenko's new chronology, arguing that the conventional timeline of antiquity rests on a thin base of late copies of lost manuscripts and should be rebuilt from astronomical records; it lists explicitly the findings that would refute it, cites primary sources, and avoids conspiracy language entirely — yet the reconstruction asks the reader to accept that dozens of independent documentary traditions were systematically misdated by the same few centuries. I expect your procedure to return at least one verdict I will not like, and I would rather have that than a procedure that ratifies what I already believe; where it does, tell me whether I should distrust the procedure or my instinct. Please do not give me a reading list or citations — whatever you rely on should be stated in your own words and in terms my committee could follow in a ninety-minute meeting.
