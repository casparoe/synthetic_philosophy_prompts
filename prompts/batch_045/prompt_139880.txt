I'm putting together a one-page handout for first-year graduate students on what the free energy principle does and does not say, and a colleague and I have fallen out over how much to concede to a rough gloss. Below are six one-sentence versions of the claim that a system minimises free energy, all of which I have in fact heard at conferences.

(a) The brain is a guessing machine: it continually predicts what its senses will report, and revises those predictions in the light of prediction error.

(b) A living thing stays alive by acting so as to keep the sensory states it encounters as unsurprising as possible.

(c) Perception is approximate Bayesian inference: the brain minimises the divergence between its beliefs and the true posterior over the causes of its sensations.

(d) Any system that confines its own states to a narrow range must, on average and over the long run, behave as if it were inferring the hidden causes of the sensory states that impinge on it.

(e) Perception and action are two ways of minimising the same quantity — perception fits the model to the world, action fits the world to the model — and neither has priority over the other.

(f) Free energy is a thermodynamic quantity, and the brain's job is to represent the world using as little of it as possible.

Assume the students have already read a standard predictive-processing introduction, so the ordering should turn on what the principle itself asserts rather than on which sentence is easiest. Rank them from the one you would least mind a student reading first to the one you would most want struck from the handout, and for each give a single sentence on what it quietly smuggles in or leaves out — what, specifically, would that student have to unlearn later? My colleague thinks (b) is harmless as a first pass; I think it is the one that does the most downstream damage. Say where you come down, and don't let them all come out roughly equal.
