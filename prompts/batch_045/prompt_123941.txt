I'm a journalist — I cover science and ideas for a weekend magazine — and I'm working on a long read about whether neuroscience is about to delete the self. A neuroscientist I interviewed walked me into the following argument over lunch and I've been turning it over ever since. Heres my reconstruction of it, more or less as she gave it:

(1) 'Belief' and 'desire' work like theoretical terms: they name whatever plays a certain causal role in producing behaviour, and if nothing plays that role they name nothing.
(2) A term picks out a real kind only if the things it covers share the causal-nomological role the theory assigns them. Coarse similarity at the level of whole-person behaviour isn't enough to make a kind.
(3) As a matter of fact, what we lump together as believing that the train leaves at nine is done by wildly different neural machinery in different people and different contexts, and the generalisations that cite beliefs hold only ceteris paribus, with a long tail of exceptions.
(4) Say also that the best current predictive models of behaviour use states that aren't remotely belief-shaped: call them policy vectors — they don't compose, they aren't true or false, they carry no reasons, and they beat any belief-ascription at predicting what someone will do.
(5) So there is no shared role left for 'belief' to name, hence no such state, and 'belief' goes the way of 'phlogiston'. Eliminativism is true.
(6) But run (1) and (2) on any other special science. The molecular gene is a tangle of overlapping roles — coding, inheritance, regulation, splicing — that don't line up with each other. 'Memory' covers consolidation, working memory and procedural learning, which look like different systems. 'Sleep' may be several unrelated processes. 'Money' picks out different stuff depending on whether you mean M0, M1 or M2. All of them have messy realisers and ceteris paribus laws, and most have been through long stretches of looking stagnant.
(7) So genes, memories, sleep and money don't exist either.
(8) That can't be the interesting claim the eliminativist wants. If it's true of everything, eliminativism about belief isn't a discovery about the mind, it's a general rule that no science's posits exist — and then we can't even say what the neuroscientists found out about memory.

Something has gone wrong, but I can't name the step. I'd rather keep the bit about realisers being messy — that seems to be just what the imaging and the decoding work actually show — than keep the conclusion. But tell me if I'm wrong about where to cut.

So: which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what would I be paying if I rejected each of the other likely candidates instead? Please don't just tell me that multiple realisability is compatible with non-reductive physicalism; I've read that sentence a hundred times and I want to know what it actually buys me here. My readers are smart but they're not philosophers, so plain language, and answer in British English — my editor is fussy about realise and colour.
