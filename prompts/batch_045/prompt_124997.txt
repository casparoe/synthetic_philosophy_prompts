You are a philosopher of social science who also reads applied microeconomics and has sat on hiring committees in interdisciplinary social science departments. Before you write anything, spend a few paragraphs of rough notes distinguishing the objections to a culture-and-values causal claim that are genuinely damaging from the ones that are only rhetorically satisfying, and then select and order the ones this particular room would actually raise.

The setting: next month I am giving a job talk in a public policy school's Institutions and Development seminar. The room is mostly applied microeconomists who think in terms of quasi-random variation, exclusion restrictions, pre-trends and effect heterogeneity; one historical-comparative sociologist of state formation, who regards culture talk as what people invoke when they have no mechanism; and one analytically trained philosopher of social science, who cares about the line between constitutive and causal claims and about whether a cause must be manipulable to be a cause.

The thesis I am defending is a revisionist Weberian one. The Reformation, I argue, did not cause capitalism by installing new preferences — more industriousness, more thrift, a weaker taste for leisure — but by lowering the cost at which an agent could keep an account of itself. Confessional self-examination, visitation registers, the personal diary, double-entry bookkeeping: these made long-horizon conduct describable and auditable to the agent and to others, and conduct that can be audited can be deferred, accumulated, and pledged to third parties. Call this the auditability channel. Empirically I lean on a border design across sixteenth-century villages assigned to Reformed rather than Lutheran church orders, with the adoption of personal accounting practices as an intermediate outcome that is supposed to carry the effect; along the way I try to show that the familiar nineteenth-century Prussia literacy and human-capital story does not absorb what I am pointing at. I do not want a bibliography or citations — I want the argumentative structure of the question period.

Deliverable: a prep sheet of eight objections, ordered by how much damage they would do rather than by how likely they are to be asked. For each: name which member of the audience raises it and phrase it in that person's idiom, give me a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, and add one line on where that reply is soft. Tell me which single concession I should volunteer in the first five minutes and which claim I should treat as non-negotiable even under pressure. Flag any objection that a defender of my view should simply grant rather than answer. Close with the question I am hoping nobody asks, and why it would be the hardest one to handle.
