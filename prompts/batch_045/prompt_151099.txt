I write novels, and I sit on the three-person acquisitions committee of a small archive that collects the working papers of twentieth-century novelists. Next month we vote on an offer I expect to split us, and I would like to know which way to argue before the meeting rather than after it.

Our record so far, unanimous in every case: we declined at any price a forged holograph letter by a well-known novelist that had deceived two experts for a decade, but paid well for a genuine shopping list in the same hand; we paid a great deal for a heavily revised working draft of an unfinished novel and declined a perfect facsimile of it; we bought an author's own annotated typescript, with her pencilled corrections in the margins, but not a clean retype containing exactly the same corrections; we bought, cheaply, an imitation page from the same period that had been made as a school exercise with no intent to deceive and that has since acquired a documented history of its own; we bought a book out of an author's library for her marginalia and declined the same book without them; and we declined a transcript of an interview in which an author described in detail the ending she had planned for a novel she never wrote.

The new offer: the estate of a novelist who died recently has had a language model trained on her complete published work, her letters and her surviving drafts; her literary executor chose one text from among several that were generated; and the manuscript itself was written out over eleven months by a machine holding her own fountain pen, on her own paper, at her own desk, in an imitation of her hand. The estate is entirely open about the process and claims nothing on her behalf. The object is offered to us as exactly what it is.

Please tell me the following. Which rules, each consistent with everything we have voted for so far, come apart on this case — something about her hand having causally touched the object, something about the object being a record of creative decisions she actually made, something about deception being the thing that makes a fake bad, or something I have not thought of — what each of them predicts here, and what your best guess is about how the vote goes. Then: which single further case would separate the surviving rules cleanly? I have two candidates, a novel dictated word for word by an author who could no longer hold a pen, and a novel written by another writer from the author's own detailed chapter-by-chapter instructions; tell me whether either of them does the discriminating work, and what would do it if neither does. Finally, if I want the archive to be consistent with itself, which way should I push, and what would we be committing ourselves to by pushing that way? My interest is not scholarly: my own papers will go to an archive one day, and I would like to know what it is we think we are keeping.
