I've more or less talked myself into a position about expected value fanaticism and I want to know what would knock me out of it. The short version: fanaticism isn't a problem with expected value reasoning at all, it's an artifact of pretending you have one sharp probability when what you really have is a muddy range. So I represent my uncertainty with a convex set of credence functions instead of a single one, and I say an act is *required* only if it maximizes expected value according to every function in the set, and merely *permitted* if it maximizes according to at least one. That already defuses the practical sting of Pascal's mugging: handing over your wallet is allowed but not mandatory, so you're free to just buy the bednets.

The part that's mine rather than the standard package is a tie-breaker, because permissiveness alone doesn't give me what I want. Call it the spread rule: among the permitted options, prefer the one whose expected value is least sensitive to which credence function in the set you happen to pick, measured as the ratio between its best and worst expected value across the set. The idea is that astronomical-payoff options have expected values that swing across many orders of magnitude as you move through the set, so they lose on robustness even when they win on admissibility. Call the whole thing the imprecision escape.

Tell me what would count against it, and be straight with me rather than diplomatic. Concretely, here's what I want covered:

Two cases I keep turning over. In the first, a token costs $1000 and buys a 1-in-10^12 chance of a chain of events creating 10^30 decent lives, where the mechanism is one you can inspect and the probability falls out of a well-confirmed physical model. In the second, a stranger in a bar says they're from outside the simulation and will create 10^30 lives if you hand over your wallet, and your credence is genuinely somewhere between 10^-20 and 10^-4 with no mechanism at all. Does my view treat these differently, and is the difference principled or is it ad hoc? Note that in the first case the probability is fairly sharp, which seems to push my own rule toward endorsing the fanatical act — I think that's the deepest worry and I want you to press on it.

Relatedly: imprecision shrinks as evidence accumulates. So my view seems committed to saying that a very well-informed agent in my situation would be *required* to be a fanatic, and that the only reason I'm off the hook is that I'm ignorant. Is that a reductio, a bullet I should bite, or does the view have resources? And if I try to patch it by making the spread rule sensitive to uncertainty about the size of the payoff as well as its probability, haven't I just become a stakes skeptic or a bounded-utility theorist with extra steps? I'd count that as a serious defeater — collapsing into a rival I didn't want — so tell me honestly whether that's what's happening.

Also: the standard complaint that imprecise credences get money-pumped, or dilate under learning, or are unstable under conditionalization, or that any tidy rule for selecting from a set is secretly just a weighted average of the set and so a sharp prior in disguise. Which of those actually bite on my version, and which does a defender of my view get to dismiss as question-begging?

Rank whatever you find from fatal to merely awkward, and mark which ones a committed fan of the imprecision escape would concede as genuine and which they'd shrug off. Say where the view sits between the two failure modes everyone worries about, paralysis and blanket permissiveness. Tell me what kind of thing would actually move me — I'm most moved by arguments about what an idealized agent with lots of evidence would do, and least moved by money-pump and sure-loss style arguments — and include whether anything empirical could in principle count against it. Finally, give me an honest assessment of whether I've insulated the thing into unfalsifiability, since I can always widen my set of credence functions to dodge any verdict I don't like, and whether for a view like this that's actually a problem or just fine.
