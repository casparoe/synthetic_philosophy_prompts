I have just come away from a long and frustrating argument with a friend about whether any purely behavioral criterion could ever be an adequate test for the presence of a mind, and I now think the argument stalled because we were operating with different, unstated requirements.

My friend is a thoroughgoing skeptic about behavioral tests. He insists that verbal behavior is cheap: a machine might produce flawless conversational behavior by consulting a vast precomputed list of question-and-answer pairs, or by exploiting statistical regularities in a way that involves no understanding whatsoever, and it would still be a mere automaton. He also pressed the case of a being that is fully conscious and intelligent but, whether by choice, by paralysis, or by never having learned a language, produces no relevant behavior at all; such a being would fail any behavioral test, yet plainly has a mind. From this he concluded that no behavioral criterion can be adequate: at best it is fallible evidence, never a criterion.

I pushed back as hard as I could. I argued that the whole attraction of a behavioral criterion is that it must be public, checkable, and action-guiding; if we demand direct access to inner states, we have no way to apply the criterion to other human beings, let alone to machines, non-human animals, or aliens. I also argued that the classical imitation game is too narrow, because it tests only conversational fluency in a human idiom, and that a better behavioral criterion would look instead at the candidate's capacity for sustained, embodied, norm-governed cooperation with others over time. My friend replied that this merely changes the behavior, not the underlying problem. At that point we were talking past each other.

I do not want a reading list or a history of the debate. I want a specification. Suppose we want a behavioral criterion good enough to underwrite one serious practical decision: whether to treat a candidate—an artificial system, a non-human animal, a human patient who cannot speak, or an imagined alien—as a subject of experience and a bearer of interests, with the moral protections that go with that. Write the desiderata: the requirements any behavioral criterion for mind would have to meet to count as adequate for that purpose, with the case or consideration behind each requirement.

Please build the list around the stress cases my friend and I improvised, and add any others you think load-bearing. The cases should include at least the following.

First, the Ledger case: a system that contains, for every question it can be asked within the test, a stored answer, and that produces its replies purely by looking them up, with no real-time inference, no understanding, and no perspective of its own; it is behaviorally indistinguishable, within the test, from a being that understands.

Second, the Silent Sage: a being that is conscious, intelligent, and fully capable of experience and thought, but that cannot exhibit the behavior the criterion demands—whether because it has no suitable output channel, because it has chosen silence, or because it is a human patient who is conscious but can only blink and thus cannot satisfy a rich verbal or interactive test. The point is that the absence of the tested behavior need not mean the absence of mind.

Third, the Parochial Interrogator: the test is administered by an interrogator from a radically different culture or species, who does not share human conversational norms, so that a candidate might fail merely because it cannot navigate those local norms rather than because it lacks a mind.

Fourth, the Longitudinal Collaborator: instead of a short interrogation, the candidate spends years working with human beings on a shared, physically situated project, and its success depends on perception, action, memory, correction, and responsiveness to norms over time.

Fifth, the Perfect Actor: a candidate that can produce apparently sincere avowals of pain, love, confusion, and fatigue, and can even behave in human-like ways when it is uncertain or mistaken, but is only simulating all of it.

Sixth, the Language-less Human: a human being raised without exposure to any language until late adolescence, who before linguistic training has desires, fears, perceptual experiences, and a point of view, but cannot pass a verbal test.

For each requirement, give the motivating case or consideration, and say what the criterion must get right: which cases it must not misclassify, which distinctions it must respect (for example, between evidence for mind and the constitutive mark of mind; between dispositional and occurrent states; between failing to exercise a capacity and lacking it; between mere behavioral mimicry and the real thing; between human parochial norms and mind as such), and which empirical or conceptual results it must not contradict. Mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp, and say why.

Above all, tell me which requirements pull against each other. I strongly suspect that some subset cannot be jointly satisfied. For instance, a demand that the criterion never produce false negatives—that it must classify every genuinely minded being as minded—seems to force us to accommodate silent, non-verbal, or alien minds, and therefore to make the behavioral evidence very permissive; but a demand that it never produce false positives—that it must exclude Ledger systems and Perfect Actors—seems to force us to require some internal, real-time processing or understanding, which we may be unable to verify from behavior without reintroducing false negatives. If there is a genuine inconsistency in the full set of desiderata, identify the smallest subset that is jointly unsatisfiable and explain the trade-off; that is the part I most want to see.

Once the desiderata are on the table, score them briefly: in a sentence or two each, say how three candidate views measure up. The first is the classical imitation game restricted to text-based conversation. The second is a total Turing test that requires the candidate to perceive and act on the physical world, and to cooperate with others, over an extended period. The third is a position that treats the presence of a specific neurophysiological or functional organization as the real criterion, and treats behavior only as evidence of it. Keep the scoring brief; the important work is the list of requirements and the tensions among them.

Finally, close by saying what, if anything, a behavioral criterion can honestly deliver given those tensions. I would rather have a criterion that is explicit about its limits than one that pretends to do more than it can.
