I'm a couple of years into a philosophy PhD, mostly hanging around the logic side of things, and I'm finally writing the part of my prospectus where I have to say what I actually want out of an account of mathematical objects. I keep rereading Benacerraf's two classic papers — the 1965 one about how the Zermelo and von Neumann reductions of the naturals are both perfectly adequate, and the 1973 one about how you can have a semantics for mathematical language or a causal-ish epistemology for mathematical knowledge but apparently not both — and everyone treats the upshot as a dilemma you just pick a horn of. I'd rather have a specification first. So: write me the desiderata. What does a theory of mathematical objects, reference, and truth have to deliver to count as adequate for the purpose I care about, namely underwriting the claim that proofs give us knowledge of necessary truths and not merely a spectacularly reliable formalism? I want the case or consideration behind each requirement, not just the label. Put the obvious ones on the list if you have to — that it explain how arithmetic can constrain what we say about electrons, that it not let an arbitrary object like Caesar or my coffee cup turn out to be the number two on some reduction, that it make the compellingness of a proof a matter of obligation rather than of causation or social pressure, that it respect the fact that mathematicians freely identify a structure with any isomorphic copy of itself, that it cover analysis and functional analysis and not just the naturals by special pleading — but I especially want you to deal with three constraints I've been going around with for a month and can't tell if their real desiderata or just something I invented because I was already sympathetic to one camp: (i) the finite-world test, that if we found out tomorrow the physical universe contains finitely many concreta and is discrete all the way down, nothing whatsoever about arithmetic should change, and I want to know whether a theory owes us that or whether demanding it is circular; (ii) the settledness asymmetry, that mathematicians treat the claim about there being no largest prime very differently from the claim about whether there's a set of intermediate size between the naturals and the reals, and a theory should say what does that without also making the prime case look open; (iii) extrinsic justification, that people adopt new axioms partly because of the theorems they buy you, and I want to know whether a theory has to make that look like evidence or is allowed to file it under research policy. Then the part I care about most: mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just what one camp happens to want, and if some subset of them can't be jointly satisfied, say so as loudly as you can, because that's the actual result. My guess going in is that the list will reproduce Benacerraf's incompatibility as a clash between, say, the semantic-uniformity requirement and the epistemology requirement — tell me whether it does, and whether it's the same clash or a different one that the extra three constraints expose. One or two lines each on Platonism, structuralism, and nominalism is plenty, I'll do the scoring myself. And please flag where you're least confident: which single requirement knocks out the most candidates, which one you'd drop under pressure, and what argument or case would make you drop it — plus where your reasoning is most likely to be failing, e.g. if you've smuggled a conclusion about objects into a requirement that was supposed to be about language. I'd much rather have a list I can defend in front of my committee than a confident one that falls apart when someone presses on a single entry.
