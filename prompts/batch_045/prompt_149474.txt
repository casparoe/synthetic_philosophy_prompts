I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on population ethics and extinction risk, and I'm preparing a graduate seminar session — and, I hope, a paper — on the hinge of history hypothesis: roughly, the view that we live at an unusually consequential point in human history because the number of people who could exist in the future dwarfs everyone who has lived so far, so that the expected moral weight of what we do now is dominated by its effects on a remote and vastly larger posterity. I want to teach Alfonso Cuarón's Children of Men (2006) alongside that argument rather than as decoration for it, and I'd like a reading that takes both the film and the hypothesis seriously: what the film shows, dramatizes, complicates, or gets wrong about hinge reasoning; where the hypothesis illuminates particular scenes and choices; and where the film resists the reading and an honest critic has to say so. Please don't give me plot summary or scene transcription — assume I know the film.

Let me say what I most want pressed. First, my own worry about the hypothesis is what I've been calling the transmission assumption: hinge reasoning seems to presuppose that what we do now propagates into the far future rather than being undone, re-decided, or washed out by later choices, by technological convergence, or by institutional decay. If influence decays, the astronomical numbers stop generating astronomical duties, even though they remain astronomical numbers. On the face of it the film is about failed transmission at every level — biological, since there are no children; institutional, since the British state has become a machine for detention, propaganda and rationing; cultural, since the art of the past survives in the enclave of the Minister of Arts while the society that produced it does not. But by the end the film may be dramatizing the opposite: that transmission has collapsed into a single person's refusal, Theo's decision to walk Kee and her newborn daughter to the boat, and that the argument's machinery has nothing to say about a case like that. If so, is the film a counterexample to hinge reasoning, or just a restatement of it in individual form?

Second, hinge reasoning looks committed to something like the claim that the badness of extinction is a function of forgone future quantity, which implies that a world ending in a century and a world ending in five centuries differ astronomically in value while differing hardly at all in what it is like to live in them. The film's world is populated by people living well past the reach of that distinction: Jasper in his secluded hideaway, the detainees and the Romani woman at Bexhill, the soldiers and insurgents who stop fighting when they see a newborn. Its ending seems to me to place weight on how a world ends, and not only on how much future it retains. Does the film actually take a position here — something like a closure value, or a value in the decency of the last generations — and if it does, is that position an objection to the quantity-driven framing or merely a separate value that the framing can absorb?

Third, both the Fishes and the Human Project act in the name of a future they will not see, and both lie and kill in order to do it, while the state has made itself the custodian of a future it cannot have. Hinge reasoning is often accused of licensing precisely this: concentrating power in whoever credibly claims to speak for posterity. Does the film support that criticism, or does its contrast between the Human Project's secrecy and the Fishes' instrumentalization of the child amount to a distinction between better and worse custodianship that answers it?

I also want the limits stated plainly. The film is not an argument; global infertility is not a plausible extinction pathway of the sort that animates the contemporary debate; the Human Project is close to a deus ex machina; the religious register and the long-take realism may be doing affective work that makes no claim at all; and the film's entire emotional architecture depends on fixing our attention on one identifiable child, which is exactly the bias hinge reasoning exists to correct. So where should I refrain from using it as evidence, and what would a sympathetic defender of the hypothesis say the film simply fails to address — the possibility that the future is net negative, for instance, or the question whether extinction is bad in a way that has nothing to do with numbers?

Close by telling me which two or three scenes you would build the session around, and by saying what, if anything, the film adds to this debate that the philosophical literature alone doesn't. If you know P. D. James's 1992 novel and think its ending shifts the reading, say so, but I don't want the session to depend on it.
