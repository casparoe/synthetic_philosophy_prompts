Last night I argued myself hoarse with a friend — an economist, and a stubborn one — about Aumann's agreement theorem. His position was that the result is vacuous: people disagree about everything, all the time, in the face of each other's stated opinions, so the assumptions must simply be false, and nothing of interest follows. My position was that the vacuity charge mistakes what the theorem is about. It is not a prediction that reasonable people will in fact converge; it is a constraint on what can coexist — common priors, shared knowledge of each other's updating, and standing, mutually recognised difference. The interesting object is the *process* of exchanging numbers, and the surprising thing is not that people do not agree but how little has to be exchanged, and how finely, before agreement is forced. We went in circles for two hours and I am now supposed to run a session on this, so I would like the argument settled in the only way I know how: by making fourteen people walk into it.

The session is the third meeting of a reading group on disagreement — advanced undergraduates and a few first-year graduate students, fourteen people, seventy-five minutes, a room with moveable chairs, a whiteboard, and a projector I would rather not use. Almost all of them have had a course in probability; none has had formal epistemology. No real money may change hands, and I do not want to lecture before the activity or name the theorem, or say the phrase common knowledge, until the debrief.

What I want them to feel, in their bones and not from my mouth, is threefold. First, that the theorem concerns a dynamic: two people can trade statements back and forth and be *driven* to the same number, and the driving is done by the structure of the exchange, not by anyone being persuasive. Second — and this is the part my friend never engaged with — that the conclusion is sensitive to the *grain* of what is said. If I am permitted to announce only that I think something is likely, or that I put it somewhere between a half and three-quarters, then the exchange can reach a fixed point where we keep repeating ourselves forever and our actual degrees of belief never meet; the forcing disappears. I would like the session to stage exactly this: an early phase in which announcements must be drawn from a deliberately coarse fixed vocabulary and disagreement visibly locks in, then a later phase in which the vocabulary is fine or exact and the same pair of people, still holding the same evidence, is pushed together. Third, I want them to catch themselves wanting to say fine, then let us bet at the midpoint — and then to see that this instinct is the very same phenomenon, because being mutually willing to accept a bet at shared odds is just agreeing to disagree about a probability in another guise.

Please design the whole thing. Give me a concrete state space small enough to be enumerated on a card and simple information partitions over it, with the actual numbers, so that a participant can in principle do the updating by hand; a card, envelope, or role scheme that assigns private evidence; a public record sheet; a payoff scheme in points that does not require money and that rewards accuracy rather than confidence (I would like you to choose the scoring rule and to say in one sentence why it is the right one); and rules for who may announce what, when, and in what vocabulary. Say how many rounds of each kind, how participants are paired and re-paired, and how the betting phase is sequenced so that a willingness to bet is recorded before the number is announced rather than after. Then give me a debrief that maps specific moments of the session onto specific parts of the idea, including one paragraph that states precisely what my friend's objection gets right and where it stops being an objection — I intend to send him that paragraph. Tell me honestly what will go wrong the first time I run this and how I should handle it when it does. And be frank about which feature of the theorem the activity cannot reproduce at all, so that I stop pretending the game does that work and simply say it myself.

Structure your reply in exactly these sections, in this order:

1. Setup and materials — about 150 words, including the state space, the partitions, and what is on the cards.
2. Rules, vocabulary, and payoffs — about 450 words; give the coarse announcement vocabulary with your reasons for its grain, the rules for the exchange, the betting phase, and the scoring.
3. Timeline — about 200 words, minute by minute for the seventy-five minutes.
4. Debrief — about 450 words, mapped onto moments in the session, ending with the paragraph for my friend.
5. Failure modes — about 200 words; what breaks on the first run and what I do about it when it breaks.
6. What the activity cannot show — about 200 words; the part I must supply by hand.
