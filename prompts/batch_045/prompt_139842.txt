hey — I'm on the benefits committee for my employer's self-insured health plan (we're the people who actually decide what gets covered), and we keep having the same fight: half of us think the plan exists to fix what's broken and nothing more, the other half think paying to make someone better-than-fine is obviously what insurance is for. Before we argue about specific drugs I want the spec. So: write me the desiderata for a therapy/enhancement distinction that could actually be used as a coverage rule — what a line would have to get right for me to trust it, with the case behind each requirement — and tell me which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just one camp wanting the line to deliver its own conclusion.

Cases it has to handle (add your own; these are the ones that broke us):

- insulin: fixes no underlying mechanism, restores nothing permanently, and function is normal only for as long as you keep taking it. Any line on which "durable," or "repairs the mechanism rather than managing it," is part of therapy has already lost.
- the noise town: a town where nearly every adult over 40 has measurable hearing loss from the plant that employs them. Is hearing care treatment there? Does the answer change if the reference population is the species, the country, or the town? And if the real fix is earplugs at the plant or shutting the plant, is that the medical answer or the political one?
- stimulants for a kid with a diagnosis, for a resident on nights, for a long-haul pilot — same drug, three verdicts, and I want to know whether the difference is really in the intervention.
- orthodontics after an accident vs. for a teenager with an entirely average overbite in a culture where straight teeth affect getting hired.
- something that gives a child a capacity no human has had, which is needed for an ordinary life in the environment they'll actually grow up in (make up the environment, I don't mind if it's speculative).
- the person who doesn't want the fix: Deaf parents declining implants for their kid, someone whose condition is bound up with how they identify. Does "departs from typical" have to mean "worse for this person," and what do we do when the person says it isn't worse — or when they can't say, or when they've adjusted their expectations down to what they think they can get?
- the ratchet: if we cover something and nearly everyone takes it, does normal move? If normal moves, have we manufactured a new deficiency for everyone who declines, so the line eats its own tail?
- repurposing: a drug developed for a disease and later used cosmetically, and a cosmetic drug later found to treat a disease. The verdict shouldn't depend on which indication came first.

Distinctions I keep watching the line collapse: therapy vs. what we owe someone; "not normal" vs. "bad for you"; restoring this person's own baseline vs. raising the floor for everybody; intervention vs. environment; "medically necessary" vs. "works." And it has to be administrable — decided with thin evidence, by non-philosophers, in a way that doesn't secretly track cost or how new the technology is.

Where I most want you to push: which pair of requirements is jointly unsatisfiable (my guess is that "the baseline has to be value-free and biological" and "the line has to do the moral work — entitlement on one side, suspicion on the other" aren't both available, but say it better than that), and which single requirement eliminates the most popular accounts. Also flag anything on my list that's really just a prejudice of one side of the argument rather than a real desideratum.

A table is fine, but the requirements are the part I'll use, so keep the weight there. Short scorecard at the end for the two or three standard lines people reach for — the species-typical-function one, the subjective-welfare one, the "what a reasonable person would want" one — if you have room. American English please, and keep it readable enough to be read out loud to a room of managers who do not want a philosophy lecture.
