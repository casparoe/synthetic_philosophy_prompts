I run a fortnightly reading group at work — roughly a dozen backend and data engineers, nobody with any philosophy training, ninety minutes per session — and I have volunteered to lead the next one. The topic is something I keep running into in my own work and have never been able to get straight, so what I want from you is not an explanation but a plan for presenting it.

The situation I want to build the session around: we own a legacy pricing service. No source, no documentation, original authors long gone. We have three years of complete call and response logs, we can submit any input we like, and we can read every metric it emits. The service is being rewritten, and two teams produced replacements that are indistinguishable from the original by every test we can run and every intervention we can perform at its interface: identical outputs for every input, identical latency profiles, identical behaviour when we throttle requests, backdate the system clock, corrupt an input field, or swap the database underneath. Internally, though, the two replacements model the domain quite differently — one reifies each customer contract as a persisting entity with a lifecycle of its own, while the other has no such entity and derives everything from an ordered stream of events. Both pass everything. Someone then asked which replacement is the same system as the original — not merely in behaviour but in what it takes the world to contain — and we could not say what kind of question that is, whether it has an answer we are simply failing to reach, or whether there is nothing there to be right or wrong about.

Please plan the session for me. In particular: the order in which the component ideas have to arrive and, for each, what it borrows from the ones before it; the single example that should carry the whole thing, what that example must have and must lack to do its job, and why it should be that example rather than the obvious alternatives (I am inclined to use the rewrite story itself, but I worry it is too friendly to the deflationary answer, and I cannot tell whether the argument is better served by a case where the difference in what the systems posit is one we would care about for its own sake); the misconceptions this particular audience will arrive with or form, in the order in which they will form them, and the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off — my guesses are that they will say it is only a matter of where you draw the boundaries between components, that the answer is fixed by whatever the original source code said, that we simply need more exhaustive testing, and that if nothing observable could ever turn on it then there is no fact of the matter; one closing question that would let me tell whether they have understood why the question is hard rather than merely agreed that it is; and what I should openly decline to settle at this level, together with how to say so in a way that does not make the topic look empty.

One wrinkle I would like you either to place deliberately in the sequence or to exclude deliberately, with your reason: a third candidate that matches the original under every intervention available to us but would diverge under one we cannot perform — we cannot replace its clock, or run it on hardware with a different word size. Does that case show the answer is merely hidden, or is it the same kind of absence as before? I genuinely cannot tell whether it is the heart of the matter or a distraction, and I would rather it be decided than left in by accident.

Two constraints: no reading list, please — we have no library access and no one would follow it up — and please let the plan not depend on terminology I would need to look up, since I will be the one standing at the front of the room.
