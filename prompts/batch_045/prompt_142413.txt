I keep getting offered the same kind of deal, and my reactions to it are not consistent in a way I can articulate. The deal has this shape: another party offers to do something I value on condition that I do something they value, where neither of us would have done the other's thing for its own sake — the whole point is that our values differ. In the circles I move in this goes by the name of moral trade, and the argument for it is the ordinary argument for trade: differences in valuation create gains, so each of us can buy a lot of what we care about using a currency that is cheap for us and expensive for them. I find that argument persuasive in the abstract. But my verdicts on actual proposals are all over the place, and I want to know what principle I am implicitly applying, or which principle I should be applying instead, together with an honest account of which of my verdicts I would have to give up.

The cases, with my verdicts.

1. A colleague who cares about preserving historic church buildings offers $2,000 to a malaria bednet charity I support if I spend a Saturday helping to repair a church roof. I think the roof is a poor use of resources by comparison, but harmless. Verdict: accept, without hesitation.

2. Same colleague, same $2,000, but the condition is that I anonymously give $200 to the roof fund. Verdict: accept.

3. Same colleague, same $2,000, but the condition is that I sign, under my own name, a public letter supporting the preservation campaign. Verdict: refuse. Not "refuse unless the sum is large enough" — I do not think there is a sum.

4. The same letter, but she will accept my signature under a pen name that cannot be traced to me. Verdict: I believe I would accept, and I am fairly confident that I should not be able to, given 3. This is the pair I find hardest.

5. A neighbour whose values I think are bad offers $5,000 to the bednet charity if I spend a weekend canvassing against a proposed affordable-housing development nearby. Verdict: refuse.

6. The same neighbour, same $5,000, but the condition is an anonymous $200 to the litigation fund fighting the development. Verdict: refuse, though clearly less bad than 5. What I cannot explain is how this differs from 2, where I also anonymously fund something I think is a mistake. The obvious answer is that the litigation is harmful and the roof is merely wasteful, but that commits me to trading on a continuous scale of expected harm, and if that is what I am doing I should be able to name the exchange rate at which 6 becomes acceptable. I cannot, which makes me think the real work is being done by something else.

7. I sit on a hiring committee. A donor offers $50,000 to the bednet charity if I vote for his nephew, whom I believe to be the weaker candidate. Verdict: refuse — and this feels like a different kind of refusal from 5 and 6, not because the outcome is worse but because of what I would be doing with a judgment that is not mine to sell.

8. A donor offers $2,000 to the bednet charity if I make a small lifestyle change I barely minded making anyway, and then lets slip that he was going to give the $2,000 regardless. Verdict: pointless, walk away. Had he concealed it deliberately I would also think he had wronged me.

9. Two variants that look alike to me and that I judge differently. (a) A stranger says he will post abuse in my blog's comment section every day unless I subscribe to his newsletter. (b) A protester who lawfully pickets an organisation I work with says he will skip a Saturday of picketing and work at a food bank instead, if I donate $200 to his legal defence fund. Verdicts: (a) is not a trade at all and I refuse flatly; (b) I think I would accept. Both are cases of someone refraining from something I dislike in exchange for something from me.

10. A man whose politics I find contemptible offers $1,000 to the bednet charity if I mow his lawn, and I know he will spend the time he saves writing a piece I think does real harm. Verdict: accept, and I am faintly surprised that it is fine. Since money and time are both fungible, I do not see how this differs in kind from 6.

The principle I have been using is roughly this: a trade is acceptable for me if and only if I prefer the whole bundle — both sides' actions — to the status quo, and my counterpart's contribution is genuinely conditional on mine. Against the list this handles 1, 2, 8 and 10, is silent on 3 and 9, and, as far as I can tell, wrongly permits 5, 6 and 7, since in each of those I may well prefer the bundle to the status quo. So I need at least one further clause, and I do not know what it is or how many clauses it takes.

I also want a verdict on a case I have actually been offered and cannot settle. Suppose a broker existed that did three things: matched parties with opposed values, held both sides in escrow so that neither could renege, and concealed from each party both the identity of the other and the specific cause their money reached. The broker would also allow diluted contributions: my $200 would enter a pool with several thousand dollars from other people, and the pool would be capped so that no individual contribution changed what the funded cause could do. Through the broker I could take the deal in 6, and my contribution would make no marginal difference to anything at all. My two reactions are that this dissolves the objection to 6 completely, and that it is a machine for manufacturing clean hands while the harm still happens. Is there a principle that sorts this, and does it sort it by ruling on the broker or by ruling on 6?

What I would like from you: a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it without coming back to you; each clause tied to the case that motivates it; the clauses that would have to be added to my gains-from-trade test to repair it; verdicts on the two cases above (3 versus 4, and the broker); verdicts on two cases I have not listed — a trade whose counterparty is someone I take to be probably delusional about what they value (he maintains a shrine because he believes a supernatural being will otherwise punish the village, and I assign that a low but non-zero probability), and a trade that binds my own future judgment over years rather than requiring one action now; and a frank statement of which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and which of them you think I should abandon. Where the principle has free parameters — a threshold, an exchange rate between harm prevented and harm funded, a list of things that are inalienable — please say so explicitly rather than picking a value on my behalf.
