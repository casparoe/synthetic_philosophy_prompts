Hey — I've been chewing on the meta-problem of consciousness for ages and I think I've talked myself into a stronger claim than anyone in the literature actually holds, and I want to know how exposed it is.

The claim: the hard problem isn't a separate problem from the meta-problem (the problem of explaining why we make the phenomenal judgments and reports we do — why we say experience has a subjective qualitative character, why zombies seem conceivable, why there seems to be an explanatory gap). If someone handed us a complete, mechanistically detailed account of the psychology that produces those judgments, there'd be nothing left over. The hard problem would be dissolved rather than solved. Call it the dissolution claim. I know Chalmers himself is more careful — he presents the meta-problem as easier, and as something whose solution would put us in a much better position on the hard problem, not as identical to it — so I'm defending more than the official version.

What I want is a map of my exposure, in this order:

Lead with one sentence: your bottom line on the dissolution claim. Then rank what would count against it, from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which defeaters a sympathetic meta-problem person would concede rather than just what critics shout from outside.

Two wrinkles of my own that I want you to take on directly, because I genuinely don't know how much weight they bear:

(a) The acquaintance constraint. I don't think the data to be explained is just the occurrence of phenomenal judgments; it's also the demonstrative, recognitional use of phenomenal concepts — the capacity to re-identify a qualitative character as the same again across arbitrary re-encounters without any independent way of picking it out. If a meta-solution explains the judgments but not that capacity, has it explained everything? Or is that capacity just more judgment, in which case my constraint is circular? Say which, and why.

(b) The mirror twin. Suppose we build a system that produces thoroughly human-like phenomenal discourse — including the hesitations, the I-can't-put-it-into-words behaviour, the resistance to functionalist re-description — via mechanisms we fully understand, and the best available meta-solution applies to it exactly as well as it applies to us. Does that show the meta-solution is hard-problem-neutral, and so can't be doing any dissolution work? And is the obvious reply (the twin merely simulates introspection, whereas we actually do it) available without smuggling in the very thing at issue?

Then, honestly: has the dissolution claim been qualified into unfalsifiability? If I'm prepared to say that whatever residue is left after a complete meta-solution was never a genuine explanatory target, am I just insulating it — and is that insulation a problem for a claim of this kind, or fine?

Last thing: my intution is that I'd be moved most by an empirical result — say, that the mechanisms generating phenomenal reports turn out to be domain-specific and dissociable from the mechanisms behind other self-directed reports (about intentions, beliefs, or about emotions as opposed to feelings), in a way that tracks our own sense that experience is special. Tell me whether that's the right kind of thing to be moved by here, or whether I'm looking for the wrong kind of evidence entirely.
