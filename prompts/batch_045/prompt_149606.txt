I write and edit for a readership spread across several countries, and I have noticed that I assert and reject claims about national character with a confidence I cannot justify. I would like to know what I actually think, so here are my verdicts as honestly as I can give them, and I would like the principle behind them — or to be told which of them I have to abandon.

1. "The Dutch are direct; they will tell you the meeting was a waste of their time." I say this constantly and think it is fine.
2. "The Dutch are stingy." Not fine — though it is the same kind of claim, about the same people, resting on the same kind of anecdote.
3. "The Swiss are neutral." Fine, and I mean it as a fact about a state's institutions and history. "The Swiss are cold." Not fine.
4. "In the Nordic countries, people trust strangers and public institutions to a degree that turns up in every cross-national survey." Fine; I would print it. "Northerners are emotionally frozen." Not fine, and I cannot say what separates it from the previous one beyond tone.
5. A Dubliner says, at a table of Dubliners, "We drink far too much, the lot of us." Fine, funny even. The same sentence from a visiting English colleague: not fine, and no account of the content can explain the difference.
6. "The German office will want the agenda a week in advance." Fine as a working assumption. "Don't put her on the client team — she's German, they can't do small talk." Not fine, and worse than the first by more than the difference in stakes.
7. "Denmark's welfare state works because Danes trust one another, so it cannot simply be copied elsewhere." I find this serious and worth discussing. "The French are witty." I find this embarrassing, though both are explanations that cite a people's psychology.
8. "Bavarians are more conservative than Berliners." Fine. "People whose surnames begin with a letter in the first half of the alphabet are more conscientious." Absurd, even if some survey could be made to show it.

I have tried to rescue myself with two devices of my own, and they will not both do the work. Call the first the slow-swap test: imagine the population of a country gradually replaced over fifty years by people from elsewhere — same language, same institutions, different grandparents. "The French are bureaucratic" survives that swap; "the French are witty" does not; and I want to say the survivors are the respectable claims. Call the second the partition problem: for almost any trait there is some way of drawing boundaries that makes "the X are T" true, and I do not want to be committed to the gerrymandered ones. But the partition problem seems to sink case 8 as well, since I have no principled reason to treat Bavaria as a real unit and surname-initial groups as fake ones, and I am not willing to give up case 8.

So: is there a principle that captures these verdicts, or a small set of candidates if no single one does? For each clause, tell me which case forces it. Then tell me what the account says about cases I have not listed — generalizing about a profession ("litigators are combative"), about a generation, about a country's electorate, about a people defined by a state that no longer exists — and state plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and whether on that account "national character" is a coherent notion at all or a phrase I should retire and replace with something narrower.
