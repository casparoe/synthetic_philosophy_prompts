I have no formal training in philosophy, but I've been thinking on my own about what filmmakers owe the real people they dramatize — the biopic, the "based on a true story" legal drama, the miniseries in which a named historical figure says and does things a writer made up. I recently read a long magazine essay on exactly this, and I want to know how much of it to trust before I build a view of my own on top of it. Its argument, as I took it and condensed:

"Dead people cannot be harmed, because there is nobody left to be harmed: harm requires a subject who undergoes it, and a corpse or a memory is not a subject. So nothing we owe concerning a real dead person is owed to them; it is owed to the living — their descendants, their community, and the rest of us, insofar as we need a true account of the past to understand the present. That is why the law lets you say things about the dead that you could not say about the living, and why a defamation suit dies with the person defamed: not a legal technicality, but the moral fact that no subject remains to be wronged. It follows that a dramatization is answerable to the record, not to the person. Invented scenes are unobjectionable provided they do not falsify what actually happened, and the filmmaker's duty runs to the audience, whose understanding of history is the only thing at stake. One qualification: we should be more careful with people who were themselves mistreated in life, since a distorted portrait adds a further injury to the living people who share their identity."

The opening move strikes me as obviously right and the conclusion as not obviously following, but I can't locate the break, and I suspect my suspicion is too quick somewhere in the middle. Would you go through it more or less sentence by sentence and say what someone who works on this professionally would grant, what is a fair compression of a disagreement that is still live, and what is a genuine conflation or overstatement — and in particular whether the strong claim about invented scenes survives the parts of the argument that do?

For what it's worth, here is the idea of my own I am testing against it: that dramatizing a real person is less like writing about them and more like being let into their house and using their things — not something you owe to an occupant who is still present, but not merely something concerning them either, since you are using what is theirs with their name still on the door. Does that hold up once the essay is corrected, or does it just collapse into one of the positions the essay waves away?
