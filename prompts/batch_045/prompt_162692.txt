We read Melville's Bartleby the Scrivener in my reading group last week and I'm writing up a short response piece, and I want to try reading it against all the philosophy of political obligation and civil disobedience. Here's the framing I've been going in circles on.

As I understand the standard picture, civil disobedience gets defined by a cluster of conditions — the act is public and nonviolent, the agent accepts the legal penalty, and crucially it's *addressed* to the community as an appeal to a shared sense of justice, aimed at getting a law or policy changed. That address is what supposedly seperates civil disobedience from ordinary lawbreaking, and also from conscientious objection, where the objector only wants an exemption carved out for herself rather than a change in the rule for everyone.

Bartleby seems to break the whole apparatus. He refuses, repeatedly, but refuses without address: he asserts nothing, demands nothing, explains nothing, doesn't seem to want an exemption, and isn't trying to get the law of copying changed — he doesn't object to the work as such, he just stops. So I've started using "claiming refusal" for the kind that says something to someone, and "bare refusal" for the kind that asserts nothing, and my suspicion is that most theories quietly build in claiming refusal and then have no category left for the other thing.

What I want: (1) take the story seriously — the office, the narrator's failure to get rid of him, the fact that the narrator ends up moving premises rather than turning him out, the narrator's offers of money and a home and different work, Bartleby's death in the prison, that dead-letter-office rumour at the end. Which of those actually carries the political weight, and which is just the narrator managing his own conscience? (2) Take the theory seriously — where exactly does Bartleby fail the conditions, and is that a strike against him or against the conditions? (3) Be honest where the reading strains: I don't want him glam-ed up into a heroic resister, he might just be passive or unwell or dying, and the story may not be about his politics at all.

And what does the story add that the arguments don't? My hunch is something like: authority needs the compliance of the governed, and a subject who simply stops answering is a problem that consent theories and natural-duty theories both lack the vocabulary for — with the narrator's charity being the tell, since private benevolence is exactly *not* a political response to a refusal. But maybe the ending is a verdict against bare refusal instead: politically null and self-destructive.

I know the usual names in this literature, so please don't give me a reading list or a citations section. What I do want is a hinge: tell me which single scene or interpretive choice your reading turns on, where you're least confident, and what would change your mind — i.e. what reading of a particular moment would flip your verdict from "the story vindicates bare refusal" to "it buries it". I'd much rather have that than a confident take.
