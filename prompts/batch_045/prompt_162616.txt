I've been sitting on the internal review board for ML stuff at my company for a couple of years — I'm an engineer, not a philosopher, though I read a bit of this on the side — and I keep noticing a shape in the work that I don't have a word for, and I'd like to know whether it's a thing that has one.

Here's the shape. We put an enormous amount of effort into making sure a system doesn't fail in the ways that show up in a screenshot, or in a headline, or on a form a regulator already has. And we put very little into failures that are slow, or boring, or that happen to people who don't write in. Nobody decides this. There's no meeting where someone says let's protect the loud cases. It just happens, because that's where the review time goes, because that's what's testable, because that's what we have words for. Two instances. A friend at a hospital system described a triage model with a dozen checks for disparity across a handful of named categories and nothing at all for whether it quietly routes people who write in a non-native register of English into worse pathways — even though, as he put it, everyone knows it does and it isn't hard to measure. And in my own work: we have a rich vocabulary for some harms — bias, privacy, toxicity — and basically none for others, for being condescending to a user, for making someone's day administrtively harder, for a kind of faint contempt that no one could ever quote back. The named harms get audited for. The nameless ones never make it into anyone's spreadsheet, so as far as the process is concerned they don't occur.

So the shape is: the ethical profile of the finished system ends up being a map of where the attention was, not of where the harm was. What I can't get straight is whether that's a defect, because every individual choice inside it was reasonable, and nobody gamed anything. It isn't Goodhart's law or teaching to the test: there's no target being exploited, no one trying to look good on a metric, and the metrics we do have are honest — it's just that some metrics exist and others don't. Someone said ethics washing, but that seems to need a PR motive and a bit of bad faith, and here everyone is being conscientious and thorough; it's the distribution of the conscientiousness that's crooked. The streetlight thing is closer, but as I've seen it used that's about a person failing to notice something that was there to be noticed, whereas this is about building something that takes the shape of the light. There's also that crumple-zone phrase I've come across for how blame lands on the human operator instead of the system, but that's about blame afterwards, not care beforehand.

Does this have a name, and if not, can you carve it rather than just label it? I want what exactly the phenomenon is, what has to be true for it to be present, what it isn't, and where its edges are — I won't be satisfied with a bare term, and I don't want a reading list either. Some edges I keep chewing on: if the scrutiny happened to line up perfectly with the stakes the shape would be fine, so the badness must live in the mismatch, which makes me wonder whether the system's wrongness is only borrowed from the wrongness of where attention goes, and whether a second-hand wrong is still a wrong of the system. If a team deliberately allocated effort by stakes and ignored the loudest complaints, is that obligatory or just paternalistic? And is failing to mitigate a harm nobody has a word for yet a different failing from failing to mitigate one you could name?
