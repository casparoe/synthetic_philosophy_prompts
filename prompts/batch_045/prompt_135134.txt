I've somehow become the department's updateless-decision-theory apologist, and I'd like someone to take a serious run at knocking me off it. Here's the position in the compressed form I keep giving people: a rational agent shouldn't condition on what it has observed. It should score entire policies — functions from observation histories to actions — against a prior over the ways the world, including the mathematical facts, might be, and then execute whichever policy scores best. Newcomb, the twin prisoner's dilemma, counterfactual mugging with a logical coin: all handled, and handled by the same move, which is most of why I like it.

What I want from you is a map of how exposed this actually is. Not a list of objections — defeaters, ranked from fatal down to merely awkward, and for each one I want to know whether *I* should concede it, not whether a causal decision theorist would enjoy it. Some material to work with, roughly in the order I'd attack myself:

First, a case a student threw at me that I don't have a clean answer to. A predictor wants to know whether you'd pay in a counterfactual mugging, but it can't or won't simulate you exactly: it simulates a variant. Your decision procedure with one line changed. Then with a different memory. Then the version of you that went to a different grad school. Then a rough model fit to your behaviour by someone who met you once. Setup: if the variant pays when it's told the coin came up tails, you get $1,000,000; if the coin did come up tails, you're asked for $1,000. So: how far down the perturbation chain do you keep paying? The natural thing to say is that you pay just in case the variant is strongly enough "logically correlated" with you that its paying and yours aren't independent. But nothing in the set-up tells you what counts as the same computation, and the obvious similarity measures give you a sorites — for any cutoff there's a variant just below and just above it with no relevant difference between them. If you say pay for all of them, then anyone who builds a crude model of you can extract $1,000 from you at will. If you say pay only for exact copies, you can't even handle a predictor who runs your code on different hardware with a different memory layout. Is this a real fork, or is there a response I'm missing — a graded notion of correlation where the expected-utility sums degrade smoothly instead of flipping?

Second, a case I think might be worse: an observation your own policy rules out. You're executing the policy that scored best, and then you see something that policy assigned probability ~0 — the predictor's simulation crashes and it asks you anyway, or you end up in a branch your own prior said couldn't happen. The expected-utility computation conditions on the observation, and the conditional is undefined or degenerate; every policy ties. What does an updateless agent do at that point? I want to know whether this is a measure-zero technicality that any competent formulation handles in its sleep, or whether it's evidence that updatelessness can't be the whole story and some conditionalizing machinery has to be bolted back on, or whether it bites hard in practice for any agent with a long observation history — since nearly everything such an agent sees is, presumably, unprecendented in the relevant sense.

Third, the thing that worries me most about my own dialectical position. UDT reads to me like a schema rather than a theory: the load-bearing part is whatever handles logical uncertainty, and the original formulations more or less leave that as a black box. When people have tried to fill it in with proof-based machinery, you get the famous pattern where the agent takes the five dollars because it can't prove that taking the ten pays, and the related pattern where a predictor who punishes provable recklessness gets to blackmail the agent out of crossing a bridge. Fill it in with something like a logical-induction-flavoured prior instead and the answers start depending on details of the prior. So, honestly: at what point does "supply the right account of logical uncertainty and this all works" stop being a research program and start being insulation? And if it has been insulated, is that a problem *for a view of this kind*? Is it different in kind from the way "rationality is expected-utility maximization" is insulated, and if so how?

Fourth, the collapse worry. Is updateless decision theory just evidential decision theory with a fancier prior — one that ranges over computations and logical facts rather than centered worlds? If that's right, what exactly is left of the distinctiveness, and is the novelty in the updatelessness or entirely in the prior? What would settle the question — a theorem, a case where the two come apart, a case where they come apart and updatelessness gives the answer I don't want? And what should I say about the possibility that the Newcomb-style intuitions I keep leaning on are just intuitions about perfect predictors in one-shot cases, i.e. about situations that never occur? Do they carry any weight as evidence, or is the whole case for the view a priori?

For calibration, the thing that would move me most is roughly this: if it turned out that there is no principled way to draw the "same computation" line and verdicts flip under arbitrarily small perturbations, I'd treat that as fatal, because the entire selling point was that updatelessness swaps murky causal notions for something cleaner. But tell me if that's asking too much — if the line-drawing problem is one that every decision theory has in some form (causal decision theory has to say what counts as an intervention, after all), then my fatal is really just a shared discomfort and should be ranked accordingly.

Give me the ranked map, tell me which items a sympathetic updatelessness defender would concede as counting against them versus which they'd rightly dismiss, and give me your honest read on whether the view still risks anything at all. I'd rather be told it's well insulated than be handed fake defeaters — but I do want to know which of the two it is.
