hey — I'm a metaphysics PhD student and I've ended up defending Lewisian modal realism, but with an amendment I call Isolation-as-Causal-Separation (ICS): the isolation of worlds isn't a brute modal primitive, it's just absence of causal and spatiotemporal connection. So the thesis makes a contingent, broadly empirical claim — no cross-world causation — and I like that, because it answers the people who say the view is unfalsifiable and therefore cheap. I'm thinking of things like the finite-N many-interacting-worlds approaches to QM, where a large number of quasiclassical worlds exert weak forces on each other; I'd count that kind of physics as evidence against me.

What I want is a map of my exposure: what would count against the core thesis, and what against ICS specifically, ranked fatal to merely awkward, and which of these a Lewisian would concede rather than dismiss? Two worries of my own: whether ICS lets the actualist say my worlds are just distant regions of one big world, so the view collapses into the rival it was meant to beat, and whether making isolation contingent loses the reductive payoff (counterfactuals, de re modality) that was the whole reason to buy the plurality. I'm much more moved by defeaters of the "it can't do the work" sort than by ontological queasiness. If the package is honestly well insulated, say so, and say whether that's a problem for a view of this kind.

Give me exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no intro and no conclusion.
