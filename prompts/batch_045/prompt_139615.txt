hey — I need a piece of satire for a newsletter I put out, and it shoud be in British English please.

Write an internal guidance memo from a fictional government body — call it the Office of Revealed Welfare or something equally grimly cheerful and acronym-heavy — instructing its caseworkers on how to decide whether a benefits claimant has been made better off. The premise is that the Office has taken revealed preference theory completely seriously: welfare just is whatever gets chosen from the feasible set, and there is nothing more to it.

So in numbered clauses and a deadpan civil-service voice, walk caseworkers through things like: removing options from a claimant's menu is an efficiency saving with no welfare cost, since the item they end up with is still revealed-preferred to everything on offer; a claimant who takes the smaller portion so there's enough left for her children is recorded as having revealed a preference for smaller portions; a claimant whose choices cycle when the menu changes has failed the weak axiom and can therefore be logged as having no coherent preferences and so no assessable needs; and because utility is only defined up to a positive affine transformation, every improvement is logged as non-negative and can never be compared between two claimants.

About 600 words. No winking, no nudging, nothing that admits the absurdity — keep the voice straight throughout, and let each horrible clause follow from an actual commitment of the view rather than from something you invented. The laughs shoud come from the theory being got right: menu-dependence, the entanglement of belief and motive with choice, the fact that a utility function is only a representation of choice behaviour and not a feeling anybody has. If you can work in an appeal form that the claimant fills in which, by the Office's own doctrine, changes their choice set and so can't speak to the original decision, that woud be lovely. Don't name, quote or cite any real economist — invent the Office, its forms and its acronyms.
