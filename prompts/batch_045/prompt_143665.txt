Right, context first: I help run a fortnightly reading group that sits awkwardly between the maths department, atmospheric physics and philosophy, and this term we've been grinding through determinism, chaos and the limits of prediction — Lyapunov exponents, the stretching-and-folding picture, error doubling times, and the bit that has actually divided us: whether chaos really gives you a *bounded* predictability horizon or whether better instruments just buy you more time, logarithmically, forever. I'm in the camp that says the horizon is hard, partly because of that result of Lorenz's about error growth in a flow with many scales of motion (late sixties, some meteorology journal — I can't remember the exact reference), where the small-scale error saturates and contaminates the larger scales faster than any single exponential rate would suggest, so shrinking your initial error only buys you a few extra hours and then stops buying you anything. A friend of mine doing a PhD sponsored by a forecasting centre says none of this matters operationally anyway, because in real forecasts it's model error that dominates, not initial-condition error — I think shes half right and half changing the subject. Anyway, we want to close the last session with a poem instead of another summary slide, and I'd rather it wasn't the usual thing where chaos gets used as a vague metaphor for how unpredictable life is.

So: write me a sestina — six stanzas of six lines plus a three-line envoi, no rhyme needed, iambic-ish but don't let the metre bully the sense. Pretend you're a poet who's also read a decent chunk of dynamical systems theory (or a philosopher of science who can actually write verse rather than doggerel), and before you draft anything, spend a few minutes brainstorming how the form itself carries the argument, because that's the whole point. Six end-words I'd like you to use, in whatever order you think best: LAW, GRAIN, SHADOW, HORIZON, FOLD, RETURN — I'm imagining law for the deterministic equations that never stopped holding, grain for the finest resolved scale where the unresolved model error lives, shadow for the shadowing result (the computed orbit that diverges from the one you meant is still trailed by a real trajectory with slightly different initial conditions, so the model isn't exactly lying), horizon for the predictability limit, fold for the stretching and folding that mixes the phase space, and return for recurrence. The permutation is the trick I care about: it's a perfectly fixed deterministic rule, and a reader who hasn't spotted it experiences the drifting end-words as close to arbitrary, while one who has can predict every stanza in advance — and the rule cycles back to the starting order at the envoi, which is where I want the recurrence to land, but also where all six words get crammed into three lines at once, which I'm hoping can read as every scale of error saturating simultaneously rather than tidily. Things to get right: the distinction between sensitive dependence (halve the error, buy a fixed slice of extra time) and the harder multi-scale claim that the horizon doesn't dissolve as your instruments improve; the fact that determinism survives intact while predictability dies early, without turning that into a free-will sermon in either direction — leave it open, we genuinely ended the term unresolved.

A few constraints. Please, no butterflies, no Brazil, no flapping, no order-disguised-as-randomness line, and nothing that rhymes chaos with anything; don't resolve the tension into reassurance, and don't lecture the audience either. Someone in that room who knows what a Lyapunov time is should find the physics right, and my partner, who coming along and won't know one from a lyrebird, should still just hear a good poem. After the poem, give me five or six lines of notes on which stanza does which bit of the work and where you'd defend the science if challenged. Oh — and British English throughout please: organise, metres, colour. Take your time with it.
