Here's my position: "better than" is not transitive. I don't mean just that our judgments are noisy — I mean the relation itself can cycle, and the spectrum arguments in population ethics (mere-addition style, and the "essentially comparative" line generally) are best read as genuine intransitivity rather than as paradoxes to be dissolved. The mechanism I lean on is something I'll call salience-threshold comparisonism: a respect only enters a pairwise comparison when the gap along it clears some threshhold, and which respects clear it varies with what's on the table — so you get A better than B (big gap on d1), B better than C (big gap on d2), C better than A (big gap on d3), with no inconsistent weighing anywhere.

So: what would count against this? Give me a ranked map of its exposure, fatal down to merely awkward. I already take the cyclic-choice / money-pump stuff seriously, but tell me whether an intransitive betterness that isn't action-guiding is really pumpable, or whether that objection only bites a choice rule I never signed up for. Then: could a single-scale-plus-noise model fit the same pairwise data better, making my cycles measurement error rather than structure? Does salience-threshold comparisonism really deliver intransitivity, or does it quietly collapse into incomparability/parity (Chang-style) or into a hidden scalar — and if it collapses, which direction hurts me more? Please separate what a committed defender like me should concede as counting against the view from what I'd be right to dismiss as changing the subject.
