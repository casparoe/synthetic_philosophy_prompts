I sit on the allocation committee of a small longtermist fund, and I have been arguing internally that the two levers our field normally argues about — keeping humanity alive through the hinge, and steering what the long-run future ends up caring about — do not exhaust the space. I want a third one costed properly before we decide whether to fund it. Call it trajectory diversification: spending money to increase the number of substantially independent, self-sufficient civilizational lineages (separate off-world settlements, jurisdictionally and infrastructurally separate archives and institutions, deliberately pluralistic value communities), on the theory that if you cannot reliably steer the future you can at least stop a single lineage from monopolising it.

The argument pushing me there is about expansionary selection. Suppose the hard-steps picture is roughly right: if we get through the current period of concentrated risk, we begin to settle the accessible universe, and how much of it we eventually fill is set mostly by how early and how fast the expanding process starts, not by what anyone later decides. And suppose that under open-ended expansion the value systems that persist are the ones best at generating expansionary power — competitive, growth-favouring, largely indifferent to whatever the people alive in the hinge century happened to care about. On those two suppositions the case for steering looks considerably weaker than it usually does in our circles, and the case for not letting one attractor swallow the long run looks correspondingly stronger.

Please do the following.

Rate each of these six levers 0–10 for expected cost-effectiveness per marginal million dollars, under the suppositions above, and then give an allocation across them summing to 1.0 for a $200M fund: reducing existential risk concentrated in the next century; reducing outcomes that destroy the quality of a large future without ending it, such as stable totalitarian lock-in or astronomically large suffering; differential technological development, i.e. delaying dangerous capabilities while accelerating protective ones; deliberate early value lock-in via long-lived constitutions, institutions, or treaty regimes designed to hold their original aims; trajectory diversification as defined above; and general civilizational capacity-building, including epistemic and institutional meta work.

Then rank the three framings — survival, steering, diversification — by the share of total attainable expected value each plausibly accounts for under the same suppositions, giving the shares as numbers summing to 1.0.

Give one sentence of justification per lever, plus a closing paragraph on two things: which of my suppositions is the real crux, and how the ranking would move if that supposition were false. Also flag explicitly which single lever you think is most at risk of being a Pascal's-mugging-flavoured trap rather than a genuine expected-value play. Assume I have been reading this material since the early blog days, so skip the standard preamble on why the future matters, and don't cite or name-drop anyone — I want your own numbers and your own reasoning.
