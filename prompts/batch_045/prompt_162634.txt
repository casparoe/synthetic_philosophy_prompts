I'm giving a talk next month to our department's biology-and-philosophy reading group on where I've landed about the units of selection, and I'd like an audit of my position before I commit it to slides. My commitments are below, stated in my own terms; parenthetical labels are only pointers.

The case I keep returning to is a stylized model of my own, not a real organism, so please don't try to place it in the literature: a colonial marine invertebrate that grows as a branching stalk and reproduces by releasing clonal propagules. Two colonies occasionally fuse, yielding a chimeric colony whose propagules contain cells of both genotypes. In that system: (i) a cell-lineage allele D biases its own lineage's contribution to propagule germ tissue; colonies carrying D yield about a third fewer viable propagules, yet D spreads; (ii) chimeric colonies build taller feeding crowns and out-reproduce either pure genotype, so a within-colony conflict and a colony-level productivity effect push in opposite directions; (iii) an allele P suppresses D's over-contribution, at a cost to its bearer within the colony and a benefit to colony output; (iv) colonies vary in how early the germ line is sequestered, and daughter colonies resemble parent colonies in this respect.

My commitments:

1. Causal criterion. "X is a unit of selection" is a causal claim: heritable variation in a trait at X's level must be doing causal work in explaining differential reproductive output at that level. Elegance or convenience of description is irrelevant to it.
2. Reproducer constraint. Following the Griesemer/Godfrey-Smith line, being a unit of selection requires being a reproducer — a Darwinian individual with an integrated life cycle, something like a bottleneck, and reproduction of wholes rather than parts. Genes in a sexual population are replicators, not reproducers, so strictly they are not units of selection, and the gene's-eye view is a bookkeeping device.
3. Yet I also think the gene's-eye view carries explanatory weight no colony-level description can recover: D's spread is selection for the allele's own transmission and against the colony's output, and I want to say that is a fact about where selection acts, not a decomposition I happen to like.
4. Equivalence. I accept that the multilevel Price partition and the gene-level contextual (neighbor-modulated) partition always agree on total change, so "genes or groups" is, at first pass, a choice of bookkeeping.
5. Verdicts on the model. I count (ii) as genuine selection among colonies, (iv) as a colony-level adaptation produced by it, and P's spread as a case in which the colony-level force wins.
6. Strictness about the label. I don't want to hand out "unit of selection" to every statistical partition; I'm inclined to reserve it for levels at which selection has actually accumulated complex adaptation.

One further thing I have been told is incoherent: I want to say both that D is being selected even though it lowers colony output and that colonies are being selected for higher output. That seems to me obviously fine, and I'd like to know whether it is.

Please go through the set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, ordered by severity rather than listed as equal; for each real tension, say what would have to give and what that revision costs; and then say which single revision would clean up the most. If you think the set is already consistent, tell me which distinction I am missing instead of just reassuring me.
