Not sure this is the right kind of thing to ask here, but here goes. I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training beyond a couple of podcasts, and I keep running into something in my own work that I dont have a word for. Every time I try to describe it to colleagues it turns into an argument that goes nowhere.

Background first, so you know what I'm talking about. When I'm trying to predict what some system will do, there seem to be three gears I can be in. Gear one: I simulate the actual thing — step the instructions, watch the memory, no shortcuts. Gear two: I read it as built — this thing is a cache, it's supposed to evict least-recently-used, so it'll do that unless it's broken. Gear three: I treat it like it has a mind — it 'wants' to keep the hit rate up, it 'thinks' key K is still hot, so it'll keep K around. I only found out recently that philosophers apparently have names for these three, something about stances, so I may be reinventing the wheel. Fine. But the bit I actually can't name is this:

Sometimes gear two and gear three both predict the system's behaviour perfectly. I never make a wrong call with either. But they disagree about what the events *are*. Under gear two, what I'm watching is one event: eviction of key K. Under gear three it's two: the system stops believing K is hot, and then the system drops K. Or the other way round — under gear two it's two seperate components each doing its own thing, under gear three it's one agent doing one thing. Predictions identical. The only difference is where the joints go.

This matters to me for a boringly concrete reason: logging. I want to write down 'what happened' in a way that isn't just an artifact of which gear I happened to be in that day. So I've started informally applying this test: a description is load-bearing if it survives translation into all three gears — I can restate it under gear one, gear two and gear three, get the same predictions, and get the same answer to 'did that happen once or twice?'. If it dissolves when I switch gears, I call it notation and stop arguing about it. If it survives all three, I treat it as a real fact about the run.

Questions:

1. Does that test have a name, or is it a garbled version of something that already exists? I have a vague sense it's near some idea about patterns being real when they're compressible or predictive, but that seems to only get me gear-one-vs-gear-three agreement about *outcomes*, not the counting problem (once or twice?), which is the part that actually bites me.

2. Is 'survives redescription' even the right test? Counterexample I keep hitting: a bug in a garbage collector that only shows up under gear two. The design says 'this object is unreachable'; at the instruction level there's a window where it isn't; and there's no gear-three story at all. It doesn't survive translation, but it's obviously real — it's the bug. So my test files a genuinely real thing under notation. Is there a fix, or is the test just wrong?

3. And the reverse: cases where a description survives all three gears and I still want to call it fake. I can spin you a story about what the system 'wants' that is perfectly consistent with the instruction trace and with the design and predicts nothing I couldn't have predicted more cheaply some other way. So does 'survives' need an extra clause — it has to buy you prediction or work you couldn't get otherwise — and if so, does adding that clause collapse the whole test back into the weaker compressible/predictive thing I said wasn't enough?

I'm not after a reading list. I want to know whether I've independently stumbled onto a genuine distinction, what its actual edges are, and what to call it in a design doc. If there's no established name, coin one, define it, and tell me where it breaks.
