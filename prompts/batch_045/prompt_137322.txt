Hey — I'm on the technical staff for a frontier-AI regulator's advisory panel (can't say which one, sorry) and we have the same fight in every meeting and it never goes anywhere, so I want to force it into an adversarial collaboration document. Two camps: the "assurance" people, who think a safety case should be an argument whose force doesn't come from sampling a distribution you then generalise over, and the "measurement" people, who think the only thing that has ever actually bought safety is empirical eval and iteration, and that worst-case talk in ML is either vacuous or quietly resting on the same empirical premises it claims to rise above. I want the joint statement they could both sign.

The concrete case they have to write it around, because otherwise it stays abstract: a mid-size lab has submitted an agentic coding-and-ops system for pre-deployment sign-off. Two wrinkles. First, the system will have write access to part of its own finetuning pipeline and to the cluster scheduler, so whatever guarantee you offer has to cover a distribution the system itself partly shapes. Second, the eval suite is 4,000-ish held-out sabotage and misalignment scenarios, but earlier models in the same family helped generate and filter a lot of it. I've started calling that "lineage leakage" in my notes: the benchmark isn't independent of the system under test, because the system's own ancestors helped build the yardstick. Our statute doesn't help: it says sign-off requires "no more than a 1-in-1,000 chance of a catastrophic outcome per deployment-year," which shoves the whole disagreement into a number one camp says is unmeasurable in the regime that matters and the other says is the only honest form a standard can take.

What I want:

1. A numbered section of claims both camps can sign. Please don't make these vacuous — every one should be something a smart outsider would actually deny. Include at least something about what lineage leakage does and doesn't show, something about what non-catastrophic deployment data can and can't tell you, and something about what happens to the disagreement when the thing you're certifying can act on the certification process.
2. The disagreement itself, stated twice: once in the wording the assurance camp would accept for its own view, once in the wording the measurement camp would accept for its own. Both versions, neither one a strawman.
3. A cruxes table. For each crux: why it's load-bearing, one concrete test or case or argument both sides agree would bear on it (I don't care if nobody can run it this year — I care that both would accept the result as evidence), what each side predicts it would show, and a one-line "what would change my mind" from each. Write the predictions down as if we're preregistering, because I want to grade us in two years.
4. A short section on which parts of this fight are verbal. I suspect some of it is — probably something about what "guarantee" and "acceptable risk" are doing — and if so say exactly which part and why, and which parts are then genuinely left.
5. Where each side's reasoning is most likely to be wrong, said plainly, in their own voice.

Two house rules. Define any term you coin in one line — three of the people who'll read this are lawyers. And don't pad it with references or suggested reading; everyone in the room knows the literature and I'll circulate that separately. If you need an empirical fact you don't reliably have, say so in one line instead of inventing a number, and if a claim genuinely can't be signed by both, put it in section 2 rather than softening it until it's meaningless.
