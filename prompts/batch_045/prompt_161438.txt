I've more or less talked myself into Galileo's rule for collisions between a text you take as authoritative and natural science: where something has genuinely been demonstrated, and a plain reading of the text contradicts it, the plain reading gives — because the text was written to be intelligible to ordinary people and is in the business of salvation, not of describing the furniture of the cosmos. I don't only use it on scripture; I reach for it whenever a revered text (a founding document, a founder's letters, a movement's founding statement) makes an incidental empirical claim that's now known to be false.

What I want from you is the honest map of what would count against it. Roughly ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and please be clear about which items a defender of the rule would himself concede as counting against it, versus which he'd be right to shrug off as attacking a straw version.

I've got the two obvious objections rehersed already — the one about who decides what counts as demonstrated, and the one where the rule just lets each generation rewrite the text to match whatever the lab currently says — so either go deeper on those or tell me straight if one of them is more damaging than I'm treating it. The things I actually don't have a grip on:

1. The rule's own author seems to have flubbed the test case. The argument Galileo was proudest of, the one from the tides that he originally wanted in the title of the book, doesn't work; the mechanism he proposed can't produce what he claimed it produced. Does that count against the rule itself, or only against one application of it? Is there a version of the rule that survives the fact that the person who gave it to us couldn't tell a demonstration from a hunch in the very case he cared most about?

2. What I've started calling the reverse-Galileo test. Accommodation always seems to run one way: science moves, readings get revised to follow. Suppose a future science ended up supporting a reading that an earlier round of accommodation had abandoned. Would the rule let the reading come back? If not, is it really a rule about truth, or just a rule about which authority wins? And if a defender bites the bullet and says it can come back, what does that commit him to?

3. I've been trying to protect the rule with a distinction between rent-free claims (the sun goes round the earth, and nothing else in the text leans on that) and load-bearing ones (a story where God stops the sun, where the cosmology is doing real work). I like the distinction but I'm suspicious of it: whether a claim is load-bearing is itself exactly the kind of contested interpretive judgement the rule was supposed to settle. Is the distinction doing work, or just relocating the problem one step back?

4. Whether it has been qualified into unfalsifiability. Demonstrated has a way of quietly turning into what the best current science says, and once it has, nothing could ever count against the rule. Is that fatal for a rule of this kind, or is it fine because a procedural rule for reading was never in the truth-tracking business? I want the honest version here, not the reassuring one.

5. What the rule predicts that its rivals don't. A strict-literal reading, or a reading on which the text simply stays silent about cosmology, should leave a different observable pattern — in how often readings get revised, in which direction they get revised, in what happens to the text's authority over a couple of centuries. What pattern would actually count against the rule, and has anything much like it happened?

The thing that would move me personally, since you're asking: I'm not bothered much by the possibility that the science is wrong later. What bothers me is the possibility that the rule only sounds principled to me because I already know which readings I want to keep, and I'd like to know whether that worry can be made precise rather than just gestured at as bias.

One last bit I can't place. The rule rests on a claim about what the text is for, arrived at in a particular century by a particular person with particular pressures on him. Does it have any resources for saying why that account of the text's purpose isn't itself just an accomodation to its own time? Or is that question malformed — and if it is, why?
