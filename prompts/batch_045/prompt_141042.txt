For the last couple of years I've been arguing with people online about genetics, schooling, and inequality, and in the process I've tried to assemble what I take to be the sane, non-crank position on heritability — the one that avoids both the "genes are destiny" crowd and the people who think heritability estimates are meaningless. I'm not an academic; I've worked this out on my own from popular books and arguments with friends. Before I write it up as a long post for the amateur reading group I'm in, I'd like someone to check whether the pieces actually fit together, because something in the set feels like it's doing more work than it can bear and I genuinely can't tell which part it is. Here is where I've landed:

(1) Heritability is a ratio of variances in a particular population at a particular time: it says nothing about how much of any one person's trait is due to genes, and a high heritability within a population gives you no license to explain differences between populations. It is a description of how variance happens to be distributed, not an explanation of why it is distributed that way.
(2) Heritability is not fate: a trait can be highly heritable and still be moved a long way by changing the environment. So I'm comfortable saying both that some trait is, say, 0.6 heritable and that it is worth spending public money on a universal environmental intervention aimed at it.
(3) What I've started calling the audit reading: within a population, a high heritability for something like educational attainment is evidence that environmental opportunity has been roughly equalized, and a low heritability is a red flag that the environment is doing the sorting. My illustration is two greenhouses — in one, every plant gets identical and excellent light, water, and soil, so all remaining variation in height is genetic and heritability approaches 1; in the other, light and water are handed out unequally and heritability drops. So, I want to conclude, rising heritability can be good news about a society.
(4) Genes and environment are not separate channels: much of what gets measured as environment — parenting style, peer group, neighborhood, how often a child gets read to — is itself partly an output of people's heritable traits, so the environmental side of the ledger isn't simply something that happens to people from outside.
(5) Literacy: reading ability is substantially heritable in countries with universal schooling, and I read that as evidence that instruction is now roughly comparable from place to place — while also wanting to say that reading ability depends entirely on instruction existing in the first place, since without any instruction there is no reading at all.
(6) Livestock breeders apparently use heritability to predict how much a herd's average yield will shift after a generation of selective breeding, which sounds like exactly the kind of individual-level, forward-looking use that (1) forbids.

Please audit this package as a whole rather than lecturing me on definitions. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, and how badly? Which pairs only look like they are, and why do they dissolve on inspection? For each real tension, tell me precisely what I would have to give up and what that would cost me elsewhere in the set; rank the tensions by severity instead of listing them as equally bad; and if there is a single revision that cleans up the most, say what it is. I'd rather not just drop whichever claim a critic happens to shout about loudest. Please answer in American English.
