I sit on the programme committee of a private foundation whose charter directs it to fund work that reduces intense suffering in nonhuman animals, and which explicitly instructs us to give priority to the worst suffering over the greatest number of lesser harms. We have one application left in this cycle and money for one grant: a county animal-services agency asks for $740,000 over three years for trap–neuter–return across roughly 4,000 feral cats, together with veterinary treatment for the sickest animals and euthanasia for those that cannot be rehomed. Their own estimates: annual kitten births in the colonies fall from about 6,000 to about 1,500; some 70 per cent of kittens currently die before weaning, over two or three days, from starvation, cold or infection; each sterilised cat is expected to go on killing 30 to 40 birds and small mammals a year; and colony size is not projected to fall much within five years, because abandoned pets keep arriving.

One committee member has circulated a note arguing that on our own charter this is the wrong buy: keeping 4,000 predators in place for years imposes tens of thousands of violent deaths on wild animals, and if intensity of suffering really is what matters we should fund removal of the cats instead, or decline and redirect the money to an intervention aimed at the prey species. Another replies that those wild lives may well be worth living, that the programme's own costs are thousands of moderate pains — capture, transport, surgery, confinement — incurred to avert a smaller number of far worse ones, and that we have no evidence on whether the deaths we would prevent are, on balance, bad for the animals that die.

Draft the committee's decision memo — fund as proposed, fund with conditions, or decline — with a clear recommendation, a time frame, and monitoring conditions the agency could realistically meet. It should say how much weight to give to suffering we merely expect rather than measure; whether the painless deaths of the cats themselves count as a cost at all on a view that treats the absence of a happy life as no loss; and whether we should aggregate the many moderate pains against the fewer intense ones or give the worst suffering something close to lexical priority. Give the strongest version of the case for whichever option you do not recommend. Close by stating what evidence or argument would lead you to change the recommendation, and where you think the reasoning is most likely to fail. The memo goes to the full board and, in redacted form, to the applicant.
