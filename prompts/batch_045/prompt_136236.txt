I've somehow let myself be talked onto my country's advisory panel on technological risk — six meetings a year, no staff of our own, a mandate that basically just says we're supposed to weigh uncertain risks before they land. I'm the token philosopher. I've been reading back through our decisions since 2019 because we've got a genuinely nasty one coming up and I'd like to say something non-embarrassing, and the thing that's bugging me is that I can read our own track record two or three different ways and I can't tell which one is actually ours.

What we've done so far, compressed:

- Insect-resistant GM maize: ok'd contained field trials, told them to hold off commercial planting for five years pending gene-flow and soil studies.
- A flame retardant used in children's mattresses, thin evidence of developmental harm, a cheap substitute already on the market: recommended phasing it out.
- Radio-frequency exposure from mobile masts, weak and contested epidemiology, nothing really to substitute for it, and a mast can be turned down or moved: no restriction, just disclosure and voluntary siting guidance.
- A degreasing solvent with forty years of use behind it, small and uncertain carcinogenic risk, a ban would gut a domestic industry: no restriction, monitoring only.
- A few years back, during the epidemic, a vaccine with incomplete safety data: we recommended rolling it out immediately, on the grounds that delay had a body count of its own.
- A consumer credit scoring model using postcode-level proxies, no demonstrated disparate impact: we told them to drop the proxies, because the people on the receiving end couldn't see the decision, couldn't contest it, and couldn't get out of its way.
- A proposal to sell mild cognitive enhancers from vending machines on campuses: we came out against it even though the safety data was decent, mainly because once everyone's taking them, not taking one stops being a free choice.

The live case. A ministry wants to require that every smart speaker and every microphone-equipped TV sold here ship with an always-on ambient audio classifier, running locally on the device, listening for three things: falls and speech-pattern collapse in the over-70s, and sounds of violence between adults in the home. On a hit it alerts a human-staffed centre that can send an ambulance or the police. No raw audio leaves the device — only a flag and a twenty-second clip from around the moment of the hit. Mandatory on all new devices, so roughly four years to near-universal household coverage; no opt-out for the purchaser, though of course anyone can unplug the thing. The vendor says the false-positive rate is low but won't publish the numbers, and the proposed evaluation is a twelve-month pilot in two cities with the metrics chosen by the vendor. The ministry considered and rejected the alternative of subsidising personal alarm pendants plus an awareness campaign: about five times the cost per life-year and far lower coverage.

Here's what I'd like from you. What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves — wave it through, wave it through with conditions, or recommend against — and which of our seven is the closest analogue, and why that one rather than the others? The three rules I keep coming back to for our record are something about how bad the worst case is, something about whether the people bearing the risk opted in and can get out, and something about whether we can unwind the thing later; I suspect at least two of those come apart on this case, and I want to know which, what each predicts, and which one you'd bet we're really running. If our precedents genuinely underdetermine it, tell me what principle we'd be committing ourselves to with each of the three options, because that's what I'll end up saying out loud. And if you could design the variant of this case that would best tell the competing rules apart — change one feature at a time — that's the bit I'd find most useful, since I might actually get to ask for it.

One more thing I'm unclear on and you can take or leave it: everything we've ever ruled on has been a substance or a machine, and this is arguably a change in what people do to each other in their own kitchens. Does that matter for whether our whole way of talking applies, or is that a distinction without a difference? Sorry, this got long — hope it's not too much.
