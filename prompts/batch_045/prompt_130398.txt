I've been playing one of those endless online Nomic games with some friends — the Peter Suber thing that Hofstadter wrote up in Scientific American back in 1982, where proposing a rule change just *is* a move — and it got me into an argument about self-amendment that I now can't let go of. Here's where I've landed. I think the paradoxes of self-amendment aren't paradoxes at all: they're an artifact of thinking of a legal system as a set of sentence-types rather than as a series of time-indexed rule-tokens. On my view (a friend calls it the "ratchet view") there is no such thing as a rule amending itself. There's system S at time t with some amendment-procedure token A@t; exercising A@t produces a successor system S' at t+1; and everything that looks like vicious self-reference is really just cross-temporal reference to a distinct, earlier token. Two consequences I'm committed to and happy about. First, the famous two-step manoeuvre around an entrenched clause — you first amend the amendment rule, then repeal the thing it was protecting — isn't a loophole or a drafting defect, it's just what an unrestricted amendment power means, and the proviso in Article V that "no State, without its Consent, shall be deprived of its equal Suffrage in the Senate" is a speed bump, not a wall. Second, real entrenchment, where there is any (the eternity clause in the German Basic Law, the basic structure doctrine in Indian constitutional law, a court that strikes down amendments), has to come from outside the rule-system: constituent power, institutional fact, whatever. Nothing can bind a successor system from inside.

What I want is the map of my exposure. Which arguments, cases, or possible developments would be fatal to this, which would merely be awkward, and which a defender like me would shrug off — and be explicit about which is which, including where you think I'm being defensive. A few things I've been chewing on, and I want you to take a stand on them rather than lay them out even-handedly. (1) Retroactive amendment: a rule saying it "shall be deemed to have been in force since" some earlier date seems to let a later token rewrite which token was in force earlier, and so maybe folds my index back into a circle. Is that the real defeater, or only a reason to make the index a partial order instead of a line? (2) The continuity problem: if S and S' are distinct systems, what makes anything carry over — old obligations, statutes, the authority of whichever body says what counts as an amendment — given that the transition rules are themselves amendable and so can't do that job without circularity? (3) The one I think would move me most if it works: my view seems to predict that there's no principled difference between an amendment and a coup, or between a valid amendment and a revolution, since either way you just get a new system with its own criteria. If I have to qualify my way out of that by smuggling in an external continuity condition, I suspect I'll have insulated the view into unfalsifiability — say whether that's fair, and whether it would actually be a problem for a view of this kind. Also tell me whether the ratchet view predicts anything its rivals don't (the Tarskian level-hierarchy fix, Suber's own line, Hart- or Kelsen-style rule-of-recognition stories), because if it's only a redescription of the same commitments I'd rather know that now.

Context on why I'm asking: I'm giving a short talk to a jurisprudence reading group next month and I'd rather walk in having already lost the argument in private. So please argue rather than summarise who has said what — I've read enough of the secondary stuff to recognise a guided tour of the literature, and one thing I definately don't need is to be told that this is a hard and much-disputed question. If you think a distinction I'm leaning on is doing no work, say so; if you think the whole framing is a category error, say that instead.
