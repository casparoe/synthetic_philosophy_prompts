I've been reading on my own about this idea that there are three ways to predict what something will do: track every last particle in it, assume it was built to do a job, or assume it wants things and believes things. The view I got from the book is that all three pick out patterns that are equally real, and that a pattern is real just when its the sort of thing you can bet on. I tried to write that out carefully and now I think I've backed myslef into a corner.

(1) A pattern is real just in case noticing it lets you predict the data better than you could without it — real patterns are the ones worth betting on.
(2) If you knew the exact state of every particle in a system and all the laws, the particle-tracking way of predicting would get every future detail right. There is nothing left over for the other two ways to predict.
(3) So the "it was built to do this" and "he wants X and believes Y" ways don't add any prediction the particle-tracking way hadn't already given. All they add is that they're cheaper — same results, less work.
(4) But how much work something takes is a fact about whoever is doing the predicting. What is one obvious compressed pattern to a creature with a small memory and a slow brain can be noise to a machine with a huge one, and vice versa.
(5) So whether a pattern is real depends on what kind of predictor you are, and there's no non-arbitrary predictor to settle it. If a real pattern is just whatever some predictor or other can compress, then anything can be a real pattern for some predictor: the "system" the gambler sees in the roulette numbers, the influence of the stars on personality, the face in the clouds. The test stops ruling anything out.
(6) The only predictor that isn't arbitrary is a perfect one, and by (2) a perfect predictor finds only the particle-level patterns. So wanting, believing, being-built-for-a-purpose, even the shape of a chess opening, aren't real patterns at all — just conveniences for the slow.
(7) Both ends are unacceptable. I want to say my dog's wanting to go outside is a real pattern and the roulette system is not, and nothing above lets me say both.

If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (2) and (3) than (7). Which step do you think I should give up, why did it look fine when I wrote it, and what does giving it up cost me? Please actually take a side rather than laying out the options for me to pick from — name the step, defend it, and then tell me how confident you are and what would most weaken your answer.
