I'm putting on a film-and-philosophy night in a couple of weeks — small group, mostly undergrads and a few locals who just turn up — and we're doing Nolan's Inception alongside the strange-loop stuff: Escher's Drawing Hands, tangled hierarchies, the idea that a hierarchy is only safe as long as some level is inviolate, a ground floor that isn't itself drawn by anything further down. I'd like a reading of the film through that lens, and I want it to actually argue something rather than just spot the parallels.

Here's where I keep getting stuck, and what I'd like you to think through. I've been calling it the authority regress: any test that tells you which level you're on has to be administered from somewhere, and if it's administered from inside the level in question it's already compromised. The film seems to know this — nobody else is allowed to touch your totem — but the totem is still the thing I can't make up my mind about. It's supposed to be level-invariant, but it's private, it can't be checked by anyone else, and worse, the top was Mal's: its authority derives from a level whose reality-test famously failed. Does the totem solve the regress, or is it just the regress with better production design?

Then there's the question of whether the film is actually tangled at all. Most of the machinery is a plain stack: a countable number of levels, an architect, a chemist, a kick, time dilation as a fixed ratio. A stack with a bottom isn't a strange loop, it's just an onion. But some things don't behave like that: Mal, who is a projection and therefore sub-personal furniture of Cobb's mind, goes around shooting people and steering the whole mission; inception itself works by hiding an idea's origin from the level it operates on; and limbo is a place with no structure and no clock where you can't tell if you've been there before. So: genuine heterarchy, or layered model with a couple of loop-y bits bolted on for pathos?

I also want you to take the ending seriously rather than as a tease. Cobb stops looking at the top. He turns away before it falls. Is that the film dodging the question, or is it saying something the concept-talk can't — that something caught in a loop can't settle its own level from the inside, and the way out isn't epistemic at all? And be fair to the film's resistance here: Nolan needs us to believe there's a real world with actual kids in it, or Cobb's arc is worth nothing, so maybe the loop is just set dressing.

Give me the reading, say which scenes I should stop the DVD on, and give me a verdict on whether the film adds anything to the strange-loop discussion that the diagrams don't.
