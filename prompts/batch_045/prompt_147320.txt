I work with a small team that rewrites legal and medical documents — consent forms, witness statements, tenancy notices — into plain language and into other languages. Nearly all of our job is deciding which rewrites preserve what the original conveys, and we keep running into a layer of conveyed content that I cannot get a handle on, and for which I have no word.

Certain words and constructions seem to carry, alongside the main claim the sentence makes, a second thing that the sentence also conveys; and this second thing behaves unlike anything else I know how to handle. Three features, as I have noted them over the past year or so.

First, it is not what the sentence is about. If a reader wants to push back, they push back against the main claim, not against this. And yet it is clearly conveyed: a writer who uses one of these words and then adds a clause saying "not that I mean to suggest such-and-such" produces something that reads as self-contradictory, not merely as cautious.

Second, it does not appear to depend on the situation or on what the writer was trying to achieve. I get it from the sentence on its own, in isolation, from any writer, in any context. This is what separates it from the ordinary suggestions that readers draw out of what people say — the suggestions that evaporate when the circumstances change and that the speaker can always withdraw afterwards.

Third, it survives being wrapped inside other constructions. Negate the sentence, turn it into a question, put it after "if", and the second thing is still there, still apparently being conveyed.

I have begun sorting the words into two groups, and I am not confident the sorting is real.

Group A: "regret", "realize", "stop", "again", "too", "still" (as in "still waiting"). Here the second thing looks like a fact that is being treated as already settled between writer and reader. If the reader does not know it, the sentence feels as though it is smuggling something in, and the natural response is "wait — when did that happen?", a challenge about the facts. And when I report such a sentence about someone else ("she said that..."), the second thing can be heard as hers.

Group B: "but", "although", "nevertheless", "moreover", "even", "unfortunately", "frankly", "allegedly", "so-called", "damn". Here the second thing is not backgrounded at all — it is new. The reader's natural response is not "I didn't know that" but "why are you putting it that way?", a challenge to the framing rather than to the facts. And when I report such a sentence about someone else, the second thing seems to stay with me, the reporter, rather than transferring to her.

A friend who once took a pragmatics course tells me the whole class is just "conventional implicature"; from what little I have read, that label seems to be used for a narrower set of cases and to be itself contested. Someone else said "presupposition", which strikes me as right for my Group A and wrong for my Group B. A third said it is all simply part of the literal meaning of the words, which cannot be right either, since it is plainly not part of what the sentence states or can be called true or false of.

What I need is not a label. I need, first, a characterisation: what feature or cluster of features makes these words special, stated so that I could apply it to a word I have not considered. Second, a verdict on my two groups — whether the split is real or whether I have gerrymandered the data, where exactly the boundary runs, which items fall on which side, and whether there are items that fall in between. Third, a set of contrasts: how this differs from the ordinary context-dependent kind of suggested meaning, from the backgrounded kind, and from plain literal content, and how those three neighbours differ from one another. Fourth, if nothing established fits my class as a whole, a name and a definition with boundary cases, phrased so that my team could use it to decide whether a given word belongs. And, since our problem is practical: if the distinction holds up, does it tell us anything about which paraphrases are safe — is there a rule for when a sentence can be rewritten without losing or gaining this second layer?

Please also tell me plainly if you think I have the data wrong: part of what I am after is which of my three features is the load-bearing one, and which of them I have over-generalised from a few favourite examples.
