I'm writing a historical novel set in Soho during the late summer of 1854, and I want the central investigative chapter to turn on John Snow's reasoning about the Broad Street pump. I need more than period atmosphere: I need the chapter's argument to be philosophically disciplined. Accordingly, I'd like you to analyze Snow's handling of the Broad Street cholera outbreak as a case study in inference to the best explanation.

The broad facts I am working with are these; please take them as given and do not look anything up. In 1854 a severe outbreak of cholera occurred near Broad Street and Golden Square in Soho, London. The prevailing medical view held that cholera was caused by miasma, poisonous particles in the air arising from filth and decaying matter. Snow defended a rival hypothesis: that cholera was spread by a germ or morbid matter ingested in contaminated water, in this case water from the Broad Street pump. He mapped the cases, observed their concentration around the pump, and persuaded the St James parish authorities to disable the pump by removing its handle. His chemical and microscopic examination of a sample of the pump water did not conclusively prove its danger; what persuaded the authorities was the pattern of illness and death. I am also stipulating several details that matter for my chapter: a nearby workhouse that had its own well was largely spared; a nearby brewery whose workers drank beer rather than the pump water suffered few cases; a widow living outside the neighborhood, in Hampstead, who liked the taste of the Broad Street water and had it delivered to her, died of cholera; and the epidemic may already have been in rapid decline by the time the handle was removed, so the removal itself was not the dramatic confirmation later legend made it. I am also stipulating that Snow had in hand a broader comparison of mortality among households served by different water companies, with districts served by companies drawing water from a more contaminated stretch of the river suffering higher cholera mortality; my narrator may use this as corroborating evidence. Finally, the cholera bacterium was isolated in 1854 but became well known and accepted only decades later, long after Snow's death in 1858.

Treat inference to the best explanation as the thesis that, when faced with competing hypotheses, one is justified in accepting the hypothesis that would, if true, provide the best explanation of the evidence, where best is measured by explanatory virtues such as scope, precision, mechanism, and unification, and not necessarily by the hypothesis's prior probability. I want the analysis to engage three theoretical wrinkles. First, the distinction between the explanation that would be most probable, or likeliest, and the one that would be most explanatory if true, or loveliest. Second, the bad-lot objection: the worry that if none of the hypotheses under consideration is close to the truth, then selecting the best of a bad lot gives us little reason to think the winner is true. Third, the relation between inference to the best explanation and later probabilistic or Bayesian confirmation.

Your analysis should proceed in both directions. First, use the theory to illuminate the case. Reconstruct Snow's evidential situation as an inference to the best explanation. What were the candidate explanations available to him, at minimum the miasma account and Snow's waterborne-germ account? What did each take the evidence to be, and what did each predict or explain? Give an explicit mapping of the salient evidence onto those hypotheses: the spatial clustering of cases around the pump, the comparative mortality of households supplied by different water companies, the apparent exemptions of the workhouse and the brewery, the case of the Hampstead widow, and the inconclusive water sample. On the theory's own terms, what should Snow have inferred at each stage, and was the removal of the pump handle a genuine confirming test or an anticlimax given the declining epidemic?

Second, use the case to test the theory. Which features of the episode does inference to the best explanation explain well, and which does it misdescribe or remain silent about? In particular, say what the delayed isolation and slow acceptance of the cholera bacterium does to the analysis. The full true mechanism, a specific waterborne bacterium, was not among Snow's explicit candidates in the form later accepted; his positive hypothesis involved a vaguer germ or morbid matter. Does the episode therefore confirm inference to the best explanation, refute it, or reveal a boundary condition, for example that it is truth-conducive only when a roughly true hypothesis is already in the pool, or that it tracks loveliness rather than likelihood and can be vindicated only retrospectively? What should my narrator, defending Snow before the parish and a miasma proponent, be able to claim, and what should the narrator concede?

Please be disciplined about the mapping: specify who or what plays the role of the inquirer, what counts as the evidence, what the option set of hypotheses is, and where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak. I do not need a bibliography or citations, and I do not want the answer to rest on contested or obscure details beyond the facts I have supplied. What I want is a rigorous two-directional case analysis I can put in the mouth of a novelist's narrator without embarrassing myself.
