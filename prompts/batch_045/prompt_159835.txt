Last night I got into about a four-hour argument with my friend Jonas about moral luck, and I went to bed annoyed because I still can't locate the broken step. Please act as a professional philosopher who works on moral responsibility and agency — take it slowly, brainstorm two or three different diagnoses before you commit to one, and don't just tell me Nagel and Williams fought about this in the 70s, I know that much. Here's his argument (cleaned up, but its basically what he said):

(1) Desert-Control (DC): how much blame someone deserves can be raised only by something that was within her control.
(2) Outcomes are never within our control. The drunk driver controls the driving; whether a kid steps into the road at exactly that second is settled by the world after she's already chosen. Same for the jammed gun, the faulty detonator, the cup that gets knocked out of her hand.
(3) So the drunk driver who kills and the one who makes it home on the same road with the same blood alcohol deserve exactly the same blame.
(4) Uniformity: DC has to be applied the same way in every case. If "outside your control" disqualifies a factor in the outcome case, it disqualifies it everywhere — otherwise you're just stopping the machine wherever it suits you.
(5) Now run the same move one level down. What the agent even tries — what she wants, how well she can resist, what she notices — is fixed by her genes, her upbringing and her situation, and she chose none of those. So the trying is outside her control in exactly the way the outcome was: in neither case did she determine the causal inputs that produced it.
(6) So by DC the trying can't raise desert either. Nobody ever deserves blame or praise for anything, and the whole practice of holding people responsible rests on a mistake. Jonas happily went further and said we should scrap blame for something like quarantine-plus-deterrence, which I found chilling rather than convincing.

He also kept using this little device he calls the Severance Test: hold the agent's will fixed and vary everything else; whatever wobbles when you do that is severed, and severed stuff can't count toward desert. He says the test is what gets (3) cleanly, and that refusing to re-run it at the deeper level in (5) is just level-shopping.

Two things I can't shake. I want to keep (3) — that's the bit that made moral luck feel like a real problem to me, and I'm even fine with the corollaries, that attempted murder deserves what successful murder deserves and that the guy who reaches for his keys instead of calling a cab is just as blameworthy when he gets lucky. I definately don't want (6). And there's something else: if (6) is true then (3) is true but worthless, because both drivers deserve zero, so the thing I liked doesn't survive the argument, it just gets swallowed.

But I can't point at the break either. (1) sounds like the definition of the topic. (2) looks like plain causal fact. (4) looks like a consistency requirement you can't deny without cheating. (5) looks like (2) applied honestly. So: which step do you reject, why did that step look fine, and what does rejecting it cost me — including the cost of keeping (4) and biting (6), and the cost of dropping (4) and letting people run the Severance Test at whatever depth they feel like? If there's a standard move in the literature that answers this, name it in half a sentence. I don't want citations, papers or a reading list, just the shape of the answer. Also please tell me plainly if the argument is actually valid and the only real question is which premise is cheapest to give up — I'd rather hear that than an invented flaw.
