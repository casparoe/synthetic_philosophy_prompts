I am in the final year of my linguistics degree and I am taking a seminar on speech act theory, and at the same time I work as one of four paid research assistants on a department project that is building a coded corpus of utterances from university conduct-board hearings and the messages that led up to them. We code about four hundred utterances a week. The coding team is undergraduates with roughly one week of training, and the principal investigator adjudicates disputed items in a weekly meeting. We have a working code set — commissive, directive, assertive, expressive, declaration — but no decision procedure, so our disagreements are currently settled by intuition and the PI's authority, which is exactly what a coding manual is supposed to prevent.

I would like you to write the procedure itself: the questions a coder asks, in order, when confronted with one utterance in a transcript; what counts as a sufficient answer to each question given only the transcript and a fixed context window; the point at which the procedure stops and returns a definite code; and the point at which it hands the item to the PI because the question is genuinely underdetermined by the record rather than merely hard. Please ground it in the actual apparatus of speech act theory — felicity conditions and the different ways an act can go wrong — but write it so that a coder who has never read the literature can apply it in about ninety seconds per utterance. If a theoretical distinction cannot survive being turned into a question a trained undergraduate can answer, say so and say what is lost by dropping it; if a distinction must survive, say exactly where in the sequence it does its work.

Several features of our corpus are the reason I cannot just adapt a textbook summary, and I need the procedure to have an answer for each of them rather than a footnote. Many utterances are indirect: no performative verb, or the wrong one. Some contain an explicit performative verb that does not seem to do what it names — an email from the student-government president opening with a formula of authorization he has no standing to issue, a resignation "accepted" by someone who cannot accept it, a "promise" made as part of a joke that both parties laugh about. Some were never received: voice notes and direct messages sent to accounts that had been deactivated the day before, or read only by an automatic forwarding service. Some are reported rather than performed — a witness during a hearing quoting what someone texted her, or describing what she would have said. Some are addressed to a group, or issued by a panel speaking with one voice. Some fall inside a stretch the transcription service marked inaudible, so that the clause carrying the commitment or the threat is missing or guessed at. And some are conditional, or issued in the middle of a negotiation, where it is unclear whether the speaker is committing herself, predicting what she will do, or offering to commit herself if the other side does something first.

Please do three further things. First, separate the two things we currently conflate: the type of act attempted, and whether the attempt succeeded. A void or defective act and an insincere but fully executed one are different items for us, and I want the procedure to say which failures make the act null and which leave the act in place while making it hollow, with a test for telling them apart that does not require access to the speaker's intentions. Second, specify what is to count as uptake in a corpus of asynchronous messages, and be explicit about whether a message addressed to nobody, or to a machine, or to an account that no longer exists, can carry the act it purports to carry. Third, set a rate you expect the cannot-settle bucket to run at, and say what sorts of utterances should be dumped into it rather than forced into a code.

Then run the procedure on three items from last week's batch, showing the questions and the answers at each step rather than just the verdict:

1. A respondent leaves a voice note for the complainant the evening after a hearing: "You'll regret talking to them." Her account had been deactivated the previous afternoon, and the message was never retrieved.
2. The panel chair opens every interview with the same sentence: "We take every report seriously and someone will follow up with you about next steps." My PI's view is that this is boilerplate and should not be coded as a commissive. I suspect the procedure will disagree with her, and I would like to see the argument laid out either way, since whatever we decide will be applied to about sixty hearings.
3. A housing officer says to a tenant, "I can't approve that on my own, but if the panel signs off I'll put the transfer through on Monday."

Finally, tell me honestly what the procedure cannot do: which of our questions it will leave as guesses, where its output will be mistaken for a finding about blame or about what the board should do when the code is only a code, and which parts of the九十-second version are judgment presented as if it were rule.
