I am finalizing the research agenda for a small, three-year, six-person project — two philosophers, two evolutionary or dynamic modelers, two empirically oriented social scientists — with a deliberately narrow remit: to explain how norms arise, persist, and break down in large, decentralized, mostly anonymous online communities that have no central enforcer and no shared history. Think open-source projects with thousands of transient contributors, federated servers with incompatible clients, or governance fora attached to digital assets. The theoretical starting point is Lewis's account of convention: a regularity of behavior in a recurring coordination problem, sustained by people's expectations about what others will do together with their preference to conform given those expectations. Beyond that starting point, the group is free to move toward the dynamic and evolutionary treatments that ask how such regularities can get going and spread without anyone designing them.

There is one phenomenon I want the agenda to confront head-on, because I do not think Lewis's apparatus was built for it, and because I suspect it is doing most of the explanatory work in the cases we care about. Call it sedimentation. In these communities, a great many stable regularities are not held in place by anyone's expectations at all: they are baked into defaults, templates, boilerplate, permission structures, moderation bots, and merge-gate scripts. Agents conform without forming any belief about what others will do, and frequently could not defect even if they tried. Such a regularity can persist while literally nobody has either an empirical expectation (a belief that others conform) or a normative expectation (a belief that others think one ought to conform) concerning it. The pattern lives in the artifact, not in the participants' heads. Say that a regularity is sedimented when it would survive the wholesale deletion of everyone's expectations about other people, because the choice architecture alone is sufficient to regenerate it.

Sedimentation seems to me to generate a cluster of questions that this project has to decide how to rank, and I would like your own argued ordering rather than a menu. Some candidates, offered only to be criticized, replaced, or reorganized:

Whether a sedimented regularity is a counterexample to the expectations condition in Lewis's definition, or a limiting case that the definition should be revised to accommodate — for instance by moving to dispositions that would produce conformity under counterfactual removal of the scaffolding, or by treating the artifact as a kind of proxy participant whose behavior can be expected even when no agent's can.

What the de-sedimentation test reveals. When the infrastructure changes — a client update, a fork, a change of defaults — the scaffolding is partially removed and we see what remains. Does what survives that event tell us whether a convention was ever there, or only how well the artifact was designed?

How sedimentation interacts with the standard mechanisms: salience, precedent, chains of precedent, the generation of common knowledge, and, in the dynamic models, the size and shape of the basins of attraction around competing equilibria. Do defaults artificially enlarge a basin, producing a kind of counterfeit path-dependence that would evaporate under perturbation?

What the distinction is worth, extensionally: in the communities we study, how much of apparent norm stability is really artifact stability, and is the interesting class of cases the sedimented ones or the residual ones that survive de-sedimentation?

The weaning problem, which is where the funder's practical interest lands. If a designer wants a regularity that outlives the scaffolding — that becomes a genuine norm people carry with them into new communities and new tools — can defaults be used to bootstrap real expectations and then be withdrawn? Is there a principled account of when scaffolding converts expectations and when it merely suppresses them, possibly atrophying the very dispositions it was meant to seed?

And the measurement question: what empirical procedure would actually separate a sedimented regularity from an expectation-sustained one in a live community, given that you cannot simply ask participants, and given that many of the obvious interventions are ethically or politically unavailable to a research team?

Here is what I want from you. Give me a single ranked list of the two or three lines of work this group should spend its time on, drawn from the above or, better, from questions I have not thought of, and defend the ordering explicitly: which criteria are doing the work — how many other questions turn on the answer, whether it is tractable in three years with this particular mix of people, whether it is genuinely neglected as opposed to merely unfashionable, and how much it would pay off for the practical aim of designing communities that repair themselves after a shock — and where those criteria conflict, say which one you let win and why. For each item, tell me concretely what the group would have in hand at the end that it does not have now: a definition, a theorem, a model, a falsifiable prediction, a measurement instrument. Then tell me which candidate on my list is overrated or a trap, and why.

Finally, be opinionated and be accountable about it. Commit to one ordering with reasons rather than laying out considerations and leaving the choice to me. Say which two of your judgments you hold most confidently, name the place where your ranking is most likely to be wrong, and state what the group could discover in its first year that would move an item up or down the list. I do not need a literature review, citations, or a map of who has said what; I need the reasoning. Where you are relying on a contested reading of what a convention is, or on a stipulation about the cases that you think I should not grant, flag that rather than smoothing it over.
