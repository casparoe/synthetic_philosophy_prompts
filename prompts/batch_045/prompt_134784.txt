Write a dialogue between two epistemologists, Ravi and Sam, arguing about whether knowing-that-you-know comes for free when you know something.

Ravi defends something like the KK principle: if you know p, you're in a position to know that you know p. His underlying thought is that knowledge which floats completely free of your own point of view can't be the thing that guides your reasoning, so a conception of knowledge on which KK fails isn't a conception of knowledge at all. He should also be careful to distinguish KK from full-blown luminosity (roughly, that whenever you're in some mental condition you can tell that you are) — his call whether to concede the second and still want the first, or argue they stand or fall together. Sam thinks KK fails exactly in the thin cases, and wants to run a margin-for-error argument: lay out a long series of cases so finely graded that no subject could tell neighbouring ones apart (someone slowly warming up, a bank balance draining cent by cent), with p comfortably true at one end and clearly false at the other, and then ask where knowledge of p gives out.

I want the argument to actually get quantitative, and its fine if you tidy up or replace my set-up where its sloppy. Stipulate for the sake of discussion that (a) S knows p at a case only if p holds there and at both adjacent cases, and that this margin is also sufficient, and (b) S's total doxastic state — including any belief about whether she knows — is the same at adjacent cases, since she can't discriminate them. Have them work out what that yields: it looks like each extra iteration of "knows that" eats one more step of margin, so nth-order knowledge needs p to hold across 2n+1 cases. Get them to check that, and then to fight about what it shows.

The bit I most want them to grapple with is a move I'm making up, which Sam offers as a conciliation: call it One-Step KK — if you know p, you're always in a position to know that you're either in the know-p zone or at most one step from its edge. That's strictly weaker than KK, but it arguably still gives you everything KK was supposed to be for: deciding whether to rely on p, whether to assert it, whether to stop double-checking. Ravi's job is to push back hard: is a principle that holds only "up to one step" any kind of luminosity thesis, does it really deliver what KK was for, and does the fact that the margin cost is uniform across iterations show that knowledge is graded rather than that KK fails? Sam should answer those rather than just restate the set-up. Both of them should be good at this — no strawmen — and I'd like them to end up having located at least one thing they genuinely disagree about rather than dissolving the dispute.
