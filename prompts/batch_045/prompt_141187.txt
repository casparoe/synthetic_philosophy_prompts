I write fortnightly on language and cognition for a national newspaper, and I have a follow-up to file (900 words for Sunday, plus an eight-minute radio slot on Thursday) to a column I published last month on reclamation. The column argued that the question readers actually care about — may an outsider use a reclaimed slur? — is badly served by the membership test everyone reaches for, and that the interesting cases are the ones where the speaker's group membership and the point of the utterance come apart: a member of the targeted group using the word as an insult against another member; a non-member singing it along with a stadium crowd that is mostly in-group; a comedian quoting it in order to make the bigot the butt; a court transcript read aloud in which the word appears as evidence. The letters since then have mostly converged on one image, and I want to know how much weight it carries. A reader in Leeds wrote that a slur is a loaded gun: dangerous in anyone's hands, doing its damage whether or not you meant to fire it, not made safe by insisting you were only playing with it — so the only responsible policy is universal prohibition, reclamation is at best a private comfort and at worst a way of pretending the safety is on, and there is no interesting difference between the cases I listed. A second correspondent, a musician, offered a different picture: a slur is a regimental badge, which wounds only when worn by someone with no right to it, and which is a mark of honour pinned to the right chest — so membership really is the whole story, and the hard cases dissolve once you ask who is wearing it.

I don't want a literature survey or footnotes; I want the audit. Please set out for each image what maps onto what — the speaker, the word-token, the hearer, the intention, the group membership, the history of the word, the offence — and then take it apart: where the correspondence fails, which failures are irrelevant to the work the image is being asked to do and which are load-bearing, and what each picture quietly imports that the target lacks. The gun seems to me to import that a slur's capacity to wound is a stable causal power of the token, fixed independently of what any audience believes and of what the word has come to be used for; if that were true, the mishearing case — a word that merely sounds like the slur, with an unrelated etymology, hearers wounded all the same — would count as a firing rather than as a malfunction, and the slow death of an obsolete insult would be impossible to describe at all. The badge seems to import that the right to the word is something a speaker can possess and carry around, rather than something a practice confers and can withdraw, and that the relevant relation runs between speaker and word rather than between speaker, hearer, and whatever institution the word belongs to. Say whether the reader's conclusion (prohibit, and stop treating reclamation as a real phenomenon) and the musician's (membership settles everything) survive once the disanalogies are on the table; say whether either image can be repaired rather than replaced; and if there is a better analogy for what a slur is, give it to me — while if the honest answer is that this part of language has no good mechanical analogue, say that instead, because that is itself something I can print.

One constraint on the piece itself. Our style guide does not print the words in full, so the column has to do all of this with placeholders — 'the N-word', 'the Q-word' — and readers have started writing in to say that the placeholders have acquired a charge of their own, that the dash is now doing the work the word used to do, and that they are as startled by the dashes as they would have been by the syllables. I would like the audit to close on that: whether a convention of non-use can end up reproducing the very thing it was introduced to avoid, what the existence of such a convention tells us about where a slur's offence actually lives, and what I can claim about it in a newspaper without overclaiming.
