I'm a software engineer — no philosophy training, and only undergraduate physics — and I recently read a chapter in a popular book on the physics of computing that I half believe and can't check. I'd like an accuracy audit rather than a reading list: I have no library access at the moment, so please argue from the physics itself instead of referring me to Landauer, Bennett, or Szilard.

Paraphrasing the chapter closely: Maxwell's demon shows that information is physical. A demon that can see which molecules are moving fast opens a trapdoor at the right moments and sorts a gas into a hot half and a cold half without doing any work, which would let you run an engine off a single heat reservoir and break the second law. The escape hatch is the demon's memory: it must write down what it sees, its notebook fills up, and the only way to reuse it is to erase — and erasing one bit necessarily dumps at least kT ln 2 of heat into the surroundings. That payment more than repays the work the demon extracted, so the second law survives. The same accounting governs real machines: every logically irreversible step in a computation destroys a bit of information and so costs at least kT ln 2 — an assignment that overwrites a register, a branch that discards a flag, a garbage collector reclaiming an object. Today's processors are within a factor of ten or so of that floor, which is why the only route left to faster computation is reversible logic that never erases.

My own suspicion is about the last part: tying a Landauer tax to statements in a high-level language looks to me like confusing a variable going out of scope with a physical degree of freedom being reset, and if it were true I'd expect my laptop's thermal budget to look very different. But I can't tell whether that's a real objection or a category mistake of mine, and I have no view on the earlier claims.
