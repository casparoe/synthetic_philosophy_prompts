I work as a patient safety investigator for a hospital group, and for the past eight years a large part of my job has been writing the paragraph in an incident review that says what the clinicians involved knew, suspected, or could not have known. That paragraph is not decorative: the way I write it decides whether an event is coded as an individual error or as a systems failure, it is read by the clinical director and by the family, and it can be disclosed in litigation. A colleague who joined us last year, and who read philosophy before he retrained, has been arguing that on the best account of knowledge we have, my reports should never say that anyone knew anything. I have to finalise a report this week whose central question is whether a radiologist knew that a head CT showed no bleed, so this has stopped being academic for me.

Here is his argument, as close to his wording as I can manage.

(1) Tracking condition: S knows that p only if S's belief that p is sensitive — in the nearest possible worlds in which p is false, S does not believe that p. (He sets aside the mirror requirement that in nearby worlds where p is true S does believe it, saying it does no work in what follows.)

(2) Let p be any contingent empirical proposition that a clinician believes on the basis of evidence — for example, that this head CT shows no bleed.

(3) For any such p, there is a possible world in which p is false and S's total evidence is exactly as it actually is: the same images on the same screen, the same chart entries, the same monitor readings, the same assurances from colleagues, the same memories. Call these evidentially identical error worlds. His ground for (3) is that no contingent empirical claim is entailed by the evidence offered for it, so there is always some way for the evidence to be exactly as it is while the claim is false.

(4) A belief formed by a method is a function of what that method takes as input: hold the subject's total evidence fixed and hold the method fixed, and the same belief comes out.

(5) So in an evidentially identical error world, S believes p — by the same method — even though p is false.

(6) So S's belief that p is not sensitive: there is a world in which p is false and S believes it anyway, which is exactly what (1) forbids.

(7) Since p was arbitrary, no belief in any contingent empirical proposition is ever sensitive, and so no one ever knows any contingent empirical proposition. The radiologist did not know that the scan showed no bleed; the nurse did not know that the infusion pump was programmed correctly; nobody has ever known anything of the kind my reports attribute several times a week.

(8) That is not a conclusion I can file. The distinction between *knew and disregarded* and *could not have known* is the distinction my whole classification rests on, and if it collapses I have nothing to put in the paragraph. So something in (1) through (6) has to go.

Every step looks to me like something I have seen stated flatly in a textbook, and I cannot tell whether the argument is valid with a false premise or invalid at a step I am reading too fast. When I pressed him on (6), he offered a repair that I am equally suspicious of: make the tracking condition sphere-relative, so that S knows p just in case S's belief covaries with the truth of p throughout every world within some fixed distance of actuality, rather than at the closest worlds where p fails. That seems to me to break in the opposite direction, because if the distance is allowed to be small then for any true belief there is a sphere containing only worlds in which p holds and S believes it — so a lucky guess, a belief about a lottery ticket, and a belief that is only accidentally true would all come out as knowledge. He says the distance is not chosen per case but fixed by the context; I do not know what that would mean in practice.

Three things I would rather not lose, in case they cannot all be had: (i) that a clinician who reads an unmistakable image sometimes knows what it shows; (ii) that a person who has merely glanced at a lottery ticket and believes it will lose does not know that it will lose — this is the one piece of epistemology I have found genuinely useful at work, because it has the shape of every *we assumed it was fine* case I investigate; and (iii) closure under known entailment, because my reports move from *knew the image showed no bleed* to *knew there was no bleed* a dozen times a day. My colleague says I cannot keep all three. I want to know what each option costs me. I should also say that I have no access to a university library and cannot look anything up, so please argue from the ideas themselves and give me no bibliography, no citations and no reading list, since I could not check any of it.

Please answer in American English, in about 1,200 words, with these sections in this order:

- **Diagnosis** (about 200 words): in one sentence, the step or premise you reject; then the explanation of why that step looked sound, stated so that I can see what I was missing when I read past it.
- **The other steps** (about 250 words): which of the remaining steps I should hold on to, which of them is a second-best candidate for rejection, and in what order I should abandon them if I am forced further than the first rejection.
- **What it costs me** (about 300 words): what my three desiderata look like after the diagnosis, and specifically what I lose if I keep the tracking condition versus what I lose if I swap it for the rival condition that a belief need only not be easily wrong.
