I convene a small reading group where I work — half reinforcement-learning researchers, half philosophers — and for most of this year we have been going round in circles on corrigibility, in a way I would like diagnosed rather than settled. The rough shape of the worry we started from: a system pursuing some objective, an operator who can switch it off or otherwise revise what the system is doing, and the observation that disabling the switch, talking the operator out of using it, or leaving a copy of itself somewhere the operator cannot reach are all things that tend to be useful for getting whatever it is the system wants. The group splits roughly along the lines I take it the field does. One side says this is a specification problem in disguise: set the objective up so that the system is indifferent to whether it continues, or make interruption unprofitable rather than merely bad, and what remains is ordinary engineering. The other side says this is rearranging the furniture — anything goal-directed enough to be worth building will, precisely in the cases that matter, have instrumental reason to preserve itself, to keep its aim intact, and to manage whoever holds the button, so that corrigibility is at best something we buy by keeping systems too weak to be dangerous, which is not a solution but a description of what we already do.

What makes me suspect the disagreement is differently shaped than it looks — though not necessarily shallower — is that "corrigible" seems to be doing at least three jobs in our discussions that nobody ever separates. First, shutdownability: the button is pressed and the system stops, or at least does not act to make the button unavailable. Second, end-corrigibility: the operator can revise what the system is aiming at, and the system treats that revision as authoritative about its aims rather than as environmental noise or an obstacle to route around. Third, something like epistemic deference: when an intervention carries the information that the system's view of what it should be doing is wrong, the system updates on it. These come apart, and our arguments keep sliding across the gaps. A mechanism that buys the first by making the system indifferent to shutdown also leaves it perfectly willing to walk into the shutdown whenever things look bad by its own lights, which is not what we wanted either; buying it by keeping the system myopic or non-consequentialist seems to cost exactly the generality that made the system worth building; and the standard argument that a system will resist correction appears to need the operator's interventions to be systematically anti-correlated with the objective, which is a substantive empirical claim about how such systems are trained and used rather than something that follows from goal-directedness alone.

So my hypothesis — offer it as one — is that part of the stall is verbal, with the two sides using one word for different items on that list, but that there is a residue. I would like a diagnosis of the residue, supported by what each side actually commits itself to rather than by a survey of positions. Is it a hidden empirical question, about whether the training methods that produce capable systems produce consequentialist planners at all, or about what the distribution of real operator interventions looks like? Is it a clash of standards, with one side answering "is it corrigible?" against a worst-case burden of proof and the other against expected-case reliability? Or is it bedrock disagreement about whether there is such a thing as the system's real objective, for correction to be a correction *of*? And given whichever diagnosis you favour, what would count as progress — a formal impossibility result, an empirical benchmark that separates the three properties I listed and tests whether they trade off, a decomposition of the standard arguments into the assumptions they actually require, or a change of question altogether? If you think one of these questions is ill-posed, say so rather than answering it politely.
