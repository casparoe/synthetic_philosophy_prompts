I've got a departmental colloquium next thursday in front of an analytic metaphysics group — most of them work on persistence, composition and vagueness, and the two who've actually read the personal identity literature are conventionalists about it (roughly: just pick the relation that does the work and stop arguing about "identity"). No ethicists, and nobody who cares about prudential rationality for its own sake.

Thesis: Parfit's fission argument shows at most that identity isn't necessary for what matters in survival, not that identity is irrelevant. What matters is psychological continuity/connectedness (call it R) holding non-branchingly — I get what matters from a future person only if that person is R-related to me without a comparably strong rival claimant. Sketch: (1) in asymmetric branch cases, where the scan leaves the original alive but damaged and the replica intact (or the other way round), the no-rival clause picks the stronger branch, which I claim is what people actually judge, and it does that without Parfit's talk of R coming in degrees; (2) in symmetric fission I concede there's no survival and try to locate the badness of fission in the loss of non-branchingness itself rather than in the loss of identity; (3) against degree-of-R views I use the case where a duplicate flickers into existence for one second on the far side of the galaxy, which I argue forces them to credit scattered partial survival everywhere.
