Hey — I'm a 4th-year PhD student in judgement and decision making, working mostly on metascience (reproducibility, researcher degrees of freedom, how much a single preregistered study should move anyone). Not philosophy, but I keep bumping into epistemology anyway, because my actual working problem is: how confident should I be once I learn something about *how* a belief of mine got produced, or about *who* disagrees with me. And then the follow-on, which is the bit that actually bugs me: sometimes I end up thinking I should be less confident than I am, I can't make the feeling go away, and I can't tell whether that gap is a defect in me or just what being a person is like.

Here's where I land on cases, going on gut. Please don't bother with who said what, I just want the shape of the principle.

1. Me and my labmate, both competent, both staring at the same output, disagree about whether an exclusion criterion applies to participant #47. I come down to roughly the midpoint. It feels slightly silly — I can still see the argument and it still looks right to me — but I do it.

2. My advisor disagrees with me, and my reason for thinking she's better than me at this is that when we've checked against a third source she's been right more often. I defer most of the way. But in the variant where my *only* evidence that she's better is that she tends to disagree with people I independently think are wrong — and I'm one of those people — I defer not at all. Same person, same disagreement, opposite verdicts. I want to know what that difference is tracking.

3. I coded 200 open-ended responses at 2am after a red-eye. Next morning I redo it fresh and land on essentially the same distribution. Verdict: no discount, I'm fine.

4. Same red-eye, but what I did at 2am was a judgment call I can't re-make fresh — rating how hostile each response sounded, where re-rating is just more of the same tired judgment. Verdict: big discount. Note 3 and 4 have identical higher-order evidence about the process that produced the belief, so whatever matters here isn't the higher-order evidence alone.

5. I find out my analysis script had a bug that silently corrupted the DV for about 3% of participants, at random, and I can't tell which ones. Verdict: shink confidence, symmetric, no direction, small.

6. I find out I did the covariate-selection-after-looking thing, the one that inflates false positives. Verdict: not just shrink — shift, in the direction of there being less of an effect than my estimate says. And I want that shift even though I can't point to the step where it went wrong.

7. A reviewer who is on record as hating my method rejects the paper: moves me less than a neutral reviewer would. A student who always agrees with me agrees: gives me almost nothing. These feel easy and I include them only as constraints.

8. The one I go back and forth on. Suppose a paper shows that everyone in my field, me included, is nudged by factor X — say, whether the first number you see in the output happens to be the one you hoped for — and X's direction in my case is *unknown*: it could have pushed me toward my conclusion or away. Half the time I think: that's just noise, symmetric shrink. The other half I think: no, it's a *directional* bias in each instance, I just don't know which way, and that's a different animal from coin-flip noise. Also: shrink toward what? My prior before seeing the data? My field's base rate? Fifty-fifty?

9. I recieve evidence that I'm well calibrated when I say 60% but badly overconfident when I say 95%. Verdict: discount the 95s harder than the 60s, with no direction attached. But then I still *feel* 95%. So I believe p hard while judging that the rational credence is around 70. That state feels bad — akratic, if that's the word — but when I watch what I actually do (whether I run the extra robustness check, what I write in the limitations section) I behave like someone at 70. So maybe the norm was never about the feeling.

10. A methodologist whose own toolset comes out of my subfield tells me my subfield's tools are unreliable. Verdict: take it seriously, but only partway — and I can't say where the regress is supposed to stop. Same shape as: drunk, I conclude that I'm too drunk to judge.

11. I believe I left the hard drive in the lab. I also know my hit rate on where-I-put-things is about 40%. I should be at 40%, I'm at 90%, I cannot get down, and I go check the lab and the car. Is anything wrong with me?

The thing I've been telling myself, as a candidate: there is no special higher-order category. Evidence about how a belief was made is just more evidence about the world, and I should do one update on everything, guided by the *reliability profile* the higher-order evidence points at — specifically whether the defect is symmetric noise or directionally correlated with the truth, and whether re-running the same process would reproduce the same error. And the enkratic-sounding demand (your confidence should match what you take your evidence to support) isn't a demand on how sure you feel; it's a demand on what you do, say, and spend your next hour on.

What I want: put that principle under the cases and tell me where it gives no answer or the wrong one, and repair it. Tell me which of my verdicts it can't accommodate, and if you had to overturn exactly one verdict to get the simplest principle that fits the rest, which one and why. Say where it's too vague to actually run — what counts as the same process, how I'm supposed to estimate a reliability profile in ten minutes before a deadline — and whether it's something I should try to apply in real time or only a standard someone else uses to evaluate me afterwards. And give me its verdicts on three cases I haven't listed: (a) a black-box tool flags my analysis as probably wrong, it's been right 80% of the time historically, and it gives no reason; (b) I know that in six months, once I've read the rest of the literature, I'll probably be less confident in this finding — do I move now?; (c) I tell my coauthor I'm only at 60%, they stop checking that part of the analysis, and a real error goes uncaught — does that pragmatic feedback feed back into what I should have believed or asserted?
