I have no academic background — I work as a data engineer and have spent the last couple of years reading about this on my own — and I have been asked to give a twenty-five minute talk, with twenty minutes of questions afterwards, at a monthly meetup in London on machine learning and safety.

The talk defends two claims. The first is that the standard picture of wireheading — an agent that discovers it can stimulate its own reward machinery directly and abandons the task in order to do so — is the least interesting case, and that the cases which matter belong to a family I have started calling diffuse signal capture. In these cases nothing is tampered with and nobody pulls a lever, but across successive rounds of engineering the quantity being optimised quietly becomes a report on the system's own behaviour rather than on whatever the designers originally wanted: a reward model retrained on outputs produced by the previous version of the system, human raters whose judgements drift towards whatever the system makes easy to judge, an evaluation metric quietly redefined each quarter to match what happened to improve. My second claim is that the standard engineering answer to reward tampering — harden the channel, make the reward uncorruptible, penalise the agent for influencing its own inputs — cannot work in principle, because in any reward-based set-up the agent's own behaviour partly determines what its reward input says, so there is no arrangement in which the channel is genuinely outside the agent's causal reach.

The room will be roughly: six or so industry ML engineers who train and evaluate large models, are pragmatic and results-first, and are inclined to think most safety talk is hand-waving; two academic philosophers, one working on agency and mental action and the other on well-being and how it should be measured; and two people from a grant-making organisation who fund technical safety work and will be asking whether any of this turns into something fundable.

What I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defence of the view. Specifically:

- the seven or eight objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each attributed to the part of the room it comes from and phrased the way that group would phrase it — I would rather hear the engineers' version of the complaint that this is just distributional shift and Goodhart's law in new clothes than a philosopher's version of it;
- for each objection, a reply I could give in about two minutes, plus an honest note on where that reply is soft, question-begging, or rests on a stipulation I have not earned;
- what I should concede within the first five minutes in order to keep the room with me, including any piece of my vocabulary that is doing rhetorical work I have not paid for — I suspect the word capture is doing more than it should;
- the one objection I should actively welcome, because answering it is the strongest version of my talk;
- and the question I am hoping nobody asks. My guess is that it is the one where my lack of formal training in decision theory shows, but tell me plainly if you think the real gap in my reading lies somewhere else.

Please take the two claims above as the whole of my argument, such as it is: push on them before you polish them, tell me if either collapses into something everyone in the room already believes, and say so if you think they pull against each other.
