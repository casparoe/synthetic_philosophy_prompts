Question 3. In Reasons and Persons Parfit asks us to compare four populations. In A, ten billion people all enjoy the same very high level of well-being: call it 100 units. In A+, those same ten billion people enjoy exactly the same level, and a further ten billion people also exist, each at a much lower but still positive level: call it 50 units. In B−, the same twenty billion people exist, but the first group is slightly below its level in A+ (92 units) and the second group is far above its level in A+ (83 units), so that total and average well-being are both higher than in A+. In B, the same twenty billion people all enjoy the average level found in B− (87.5 units). Parfit then makes four suggestions: that A+ is no worse than A; that B− is better than A+; that B is exactly as good as B−; and that B may nevertheless be worse than A.

(a) Show that these four suggestions cannot all be true if 'better than' and 'at least as good as' are transitive and if any two populations can always be compared. (b) Explain why some philosophers have concluded that the relation 'better than' is not transitive. (c) Assess that conclusion: what ways are there of resolving the difficulty, and what, if anything, would follow for practical reasoning if 'better than' were intransitive? You may illustrate your answer with these numbers or with a spectrum of cases of your own.

The script reads as follows:

(a) The four populations can be set out in a table. A: ten billion people at 100 units, total 1,000, average 100. A+: the same ten billion at 100 plus ten billion more at 50, total 1,500, average 75. B−: ten billion at 92 and ten billion at 83, total 1,750, average 87.5. B: twenty billion at 87.5, total 1,750, average 87.5.

The four suggestions then generate a contradiction. By the fourth, A is better than B. By the third, B is exactly as good as B−, and equal value can be substituted in any comparison, so A is better than B−. By the second, B− is better than A+, so by transitivity A is better than A+. But the first suggestion says only that A+ is no worse than A; since Parfit does not also say that A+ is better than A, and since an outcome which is neither better nor worse than another is equally as good as it, A and A+ are exactly equally good. So A is better than A+ and A+ is equally as good as A, which is impossible. The four suggestions therefore cannot all be true.

(b) The contradiction depends entirely on chaining the comparisons together. If 'better than' is not transitive, the chain does not close: as Temkin and Rachels have argued, it can be true both that A+ is no worse than A and that B− is better than A+, without its following that B− is better than A. Each comparison then holds only locally, between populations that lie close together on the spectrum, and the sequence as a whole admits of no single ordering. This seems to me the best response. Rejecting the first suggestion commits one to saying that adding people whose lives are worth living can make an outcome worse. Rejecting the second commits one to saying that a large gain in total and average well-being, distributed more equally, is no improvement at all. Rejecting the third makes the value of a population depend on how we carve it up into groups. Rejecting the fourth commits one to saying that twenty billion people at a modest level of well-being is a better outcome than ten billion at a very high level. Each of these is very costly, whereas denying that 'better than' is transitive costs us only a formal property of the relation, and one which we have no independent reason to insist upon. The paradox is therefore best read as a proof that 'better than' is intransitive. The same point disposes of the Repugnant Conclusion: if comparisons do not chain, then repeating the move from A to B over and over cannot establish that an enormous population whose members have lives barely worth living is better than A, and so the Repugnant Conclusion is false.

(c) There is, however, a serious objection to this. If 'better than' is intransitive, then for any outcome there is some other outcome that is better than it, and a person who always chose the better of two outcomes would be led in a circle: from A+ to B−, from B− to B, from B to A, and then round again, paying a small sum at each step for the improvement, and finishing where she began and poorer than she started. That is the money pump, and choice which cycles in that fashion is straightforwardly irrational, so a relation which required it cannot be the right one. Intransitivity is not, after all, a genuine option, and on reflection the cheapest escape from the paradox is to reject the fourth suggestion and accept that B is better than A.

In conclusion, the paradox shows how unstable our comparative judgements about populations are. Once we see that the four suggestions cannot all be true, we must either give up transitivity or give up one of the suggestions; and since giving up transitivity would make rational choice impossible, we should give up one of the suggestions instead, most plausibly the fourth.
