Write a sestina (six stanzas of six lines, then a three-line envoi) for the closing page of a seminar handout on the symbol grounding problem, spoken in the voice of a system that has every definition right; somewhere in it I want the image of a learner working through a dictionary written entirely in the language it is trying to learn.

The six end-words are word, world, hand, ground, token, use, and they must rotate through the sestina's prescribed order rather than recur wherever they happen to scan, because the form is meant to carry the argument: a closed set of terms handing sense to one another forever without ever stepping outside itself is exactly the failure mode at issue.

Two things should be right enough that a philosopher of mind would not wince. First, the difficulty is not that the system lacks symbols or that its symbols are disordered — a purely formal system can have flawless inferential role and still be ungrounded — but that nothing in its operation touches anything non-symbolic. Second, the word grounding is itself ungrounded, a spatial metaphor doing the work of a definition, and the poem should notice this rather than lean on it innocently.

End with an envoi in which all six words crowd into three lines and the sense of ground shifts: the speaker tries to trade the metaphor for something with weight in it, a hand closing on a thing, a body that can fail or be hurt — and leave it open whether that trade is a solution or only a seventh symbol. Keep it to a single page, and make it work as a poem for a reader who has never opened the literature.
