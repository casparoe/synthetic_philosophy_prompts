I took over my family's small furniture workshop about two years ago, and since then I have kept running into one particular shape of thing that I have no word for. It has stopped being an idle curiosity: I am trying to decide how to run a business, and every piece of advice I get seems aimed at a different shape.

Three instances, all from the trade I am now in.

(a) Nobody in our regional association of workshops decided to start building case goods out of veneered particleboard instead of solid hardwood. I have asked the older members directly. Every one of them thinks the change made the furniture worse, and every one of them made it. But when I ask when they decided, they describe something else: a retailer moved a contract to a competitor quoting thirty percent lower, they had to match or lose the account, and by the time the account came back they no longer had the staff or the tooling for the old way. It reads less like a decision than like the workshops that did not change quietly stopped being available to be asked.

(b) Six of us tried last year to agree on a minimum standard — nothing enforceable, just a signed understanding among people who have known each other for decades. It did not fall apart because anyone cheated. It fell apart because two of the six lost their main accounts within eight months, in each case because a buyer found a cheaper supplier, and the understanding had no way of holding anyone who was still solvent to a standard that was costing them contracts. The members who complied were the members who were eliminated.

(c) The same shape shows up inside our own workshop at smaller scale. We have drifted into a practice everyone here dislikes: booking jobs we cannot finish properly and finishing them late rather than refusing them. Nobody chose it. Whoever is on the phone in a slow week takes the job because the alternative is an empty bench, and the version of the month in which we said no is a version none of us ever got to live through and compare against.

What I am after is the carving, not a label. The candidates I have been offered do not seem to fit. A race to the bottom, the game-theory version my brother-in-law calls a multipolar trap, and Moloch, which is what people say online — all of these, as far as I can tell, describe situations where each participant chooses the thing that is bad for everyone, knowing it is bad, because the alternatives are worse for them individually, and where the natural remedy is therefore some way of binding everybody at once. My cases do not have that structure. There is no moment at which anyone picks the worse thing over a better one that was on offer; the worse thing spreads because the firms and practices that did not adopt it stopped being around, or stopped being tried. And a binding agreement does not obviously help, because nobody is in a position to defect from one. They are in a position to go out of business, which is not defection.

So, in the order in which I care about them:

1. Is there an established concept for the attrition-shaped version as opposed to the choice-shaped version — degradation that runs through the differential survival or differential copying of the firms, practices or people involved, rather than through anybody's decision? I have had two near-misses suggested to me, one about bad money driving out good and one from a friend in insurance about the good risks dropping out of a pool; if those are the nearest established things, I want to know which of them my case actually is, how each works, and where each one stops.
2. If there is no name, give me one and define it tightly enough that it does not simply swallow the ordinary prisoners'-dilemma-shaped race to the bottom. I need the boundary cases either way: what would have to be true for my case to collapse back into the ordinary kind, and what would have to be true for it to be something else entirely?
3. Is the distinction real, or is it cosmetic? I can imagine being told that adding entry and exit to a game is a change of dynamics rather than of kind, and that any selection story can be rewritten as a choice story if you pick the right chooser. If that is right, I would rather be told now, before I organize anything around the difference.
4. Assuming the distinction does hold: what follows from it? Every intervention I can think of — collective agreements, certification schemes, the just-don't-do-it approach — seems designed for the choice-shaped version and seems to fail for the reason given in (b). If the two really are different, they should call for different responses, and I would like to know what that difference is and why it follows from the distinction rather than from anything else.
