hey — you're a professional political philosopher who does conceptual analysis and a bit of ideology critique, and you're good at untangling slogans that people use to shut arguments down. take it seriously: before you write anything, brainstorm rough notes on every reading you can think of, then prune and order them, then write the final answer. i'd rather have something careful and slightly pedantic than something smooth.

situation: I'm on the board of my student union and we had a genuinely stuck debate last week about whether to suspend our exchange programme with a partner university abroad, given how that university (and the country's laws) treat women and LGBT students. The moment the meeting froze was when someone said, very calmly, that gender equality is a Western value and who are we to impose it on them. Someone else fired back that human rights aren't Western, they're universal. First guy said that reply was exactly the imperialism he was talking about. We ran out of time and are picking it up next week, and I don't want us to just repeat ourselves.

So: the one-liner I want taken apart is the shape "X is a Western value" (in this case X = gender equality). Not "who was right about the exchange" — I want the menu of precise claims that sentence could be making, and verdicts.

Some distinctions I've been half-forming, please use them but don't feel bound by them:

- origin vs content vs custody: does "Western" say where the idea historically came from, what the idea actually says, or who currently endorses and institutionalises it? Those seem like totally different claims with different evidence.
- checklist vs cluster vs prototype: is "Western values" a list you have to satisfy all of, a family-resemblance cluster, or a prototype with fuzzy edges and central cases? And if it's the checklist version, whose list, and what happens when a country satisfies nine of ten items?
- descriptive vs honourific vs coalition banner: maybe the phrase isn't really describing a property at all but doing work — praising, recruiting allies, marking an out-group. Someone in my seminar once called it an "essentially contested concept", and "that's not a Western value" seems to function a lot like the no-true-Scotsman move, so I suspect a big chunk of its meaning is rhetorical rather than descriptive.
- the contrastive thing: I have a feeling the phrase is mostly defined negatively, by whatever it's currently being contrasted with, which would mean its content drifts depending on who's in the room.

What I want for each reading: state it as a precise thesis someone could actually assert; say whether it's trivial (true but says nothing), substantive and defensible, false, or empty/merely rhetorical; say roughly what kind of evidence would settle it (conceptual argument? history? survey data? institutional facts?); and — the part I care most about — say whether that reading would actually do the work the guy needed it to do. For quite a few readings I suspect the premise comes out true but the conclusion just doesn't follow: "it originated in Europe" doesn't get you to "don't pressure anyone about it" without something extra, which smells like a genetic fallacy or an is-ought gap to me. Be explicit about which readings fail there and what the missing premise would have to be.
