I'm a rising senior at a large public high school, and I sit on the student advisory board that meets with our district's data office. This spring the district started using an "early alert" score: every ninth grader gets a number from 0 to 100 estimating the chance that they won't graduate on time, and anyone above 40 gets placed in a mandatory after-school support block. A number of families and teachers have objected that the flags don't fall evenly across groups of students; the data office's answer has been that the score is calibrated, and that this settles the fairness question. I don't think it does, but when I tried to write down why, I ended up with an argument that seems to show that nothing could settle it. I'd be grateful for help finding where it goes wrong.

(1) Same meaning. A number that is used to sort students into a program has to mean the same thing for everyone. Among all the students assigned, say, a 60, the share who actually fail to graduate on time should be the same whichever group you look at. If a 60 means "about half of these students won't finish" for one group and "almost none of these students will" for another, then the score is doing something other than measuring risk, whatever the data office says.

(2) Equal miss rates. Fairness requires that the share of students who need the support and don't get it be the same across groups. A student who is struggling and never gets flagged is the plainest failure the system can have.

(3) Equal wasted-flag rates. Fairness also requires that the share of students who would have graduated anyway but get pulled into the block be the same across groups, because the block takes several hours a week, carries some stigma, and can push a student out of an elective or a sport.

(4) The groups' actual on-time graduation rates are not equal. That is the whole reason anyone is worried in the first place.

(5) The result I was shown — I worked through it with my statistics teacher, and I'll describe it rather than try to cite it — says that if (4) holds, then no imperfect score can satisfy (1), (2), and (3) at the same time. The only ways out are a score that is already perfect or base rates that are already equal.

(6) Our score is not perfect; nobody's is, and ours is predicting something four years away from noisy records.

(7) So our score is unfair. But nothing in (1) through (6) mentions software, or machine learning, or even numbers: the same reasoning applies to a counselor's ranked list, to a cutoff on GPA, to anything that ranks students and cuts the ranking somewhere. So no sorting procedure at all is fair.

(8) It gets worse. Take the two procedures that are normally the gold standard of equal treatment: flag everybody, or flag nobody. If you flag everybody, everyone sits in one bucket, so (1) asks whether the on-time graduation rate is the same for every group — which is just (4) again — so (1) is violated, even though (2) and (3) hold trivially. Flag nobody and the same thing happens in reverse. So (1), the premise I like best, condemns treating everyone identically, which is supposed to be the safest thing a system can do.

(9) So either "fair" in (7) has quietly stopped meaning what I meant at the start, or at least one of (1), (2), and (3) is not really a requirement of fairness, or I should bite the bullet and say that no procedure for handing out a scarce, supposedly beneficial resource can be fair, and that we should stop sorting and give it to everyone or draw names from a hat. I can't accept that last option. A word that applies to every possible arrangement, including the ones everyone agrees are fine, has stopped doing any work.

Two more things that make me suspicious of my own argument. First, I'm not sure (3) is the right kind of condition here at all: the block is supposed to help the people in it, so being flagged carries a cost but possibly also a benefit, while being missed is only a loss. If I have to give up one of (2) and (3), I'd give up (3) — but I can't say why, and I suspect that instinct is exactly where the argument is cheating. Second, I noticed you can always satisfy (2) and (3) by making somebody worse off: flag more students in whichever group currently has the lower rate until the rates match. That doesn't look like what fairness asks for, and I don't know where it leaves (2) and (3).

One idea I want checked rather than assumed. A parent on the board suggested getting rid of the cutoff entirely and allocating the limited seats in the support block by lottery, with each student's chance of a seat rising with their score. My thought was that (2) and (3) are both defined relative to a cutoff, so with no cutoff there is nothing to equalize and the conflict in (5) dissolves. Is that right, or does the same conflict come back as soon as you look at the expected rates under the lottery? Separately: is it true that making the score more accurate can push it further from satisfying (2) and (3) together? If so, that seems like another reason to suspect the criteria rather than the score.

Please tell me which step you'd reject and what rejecting it costs, and if more than one step is load-bearing, price them against each other. I'd rather keep (1) than accept the district's line that calibration settles it, and I'd rather keep (2) than (3), but I want to know what I'm paying to keep them. Please also be blunt about confidence: mark which of your claims you'd actually defend and which are contested among people who work on this, and if one of my premises misstates the result I was shown — or if the framing is confused and "is the score fair?" isn't the question I should be asking — say that plainly instead of answering the question I asked politely. Please write in American English, with American spelling and punctuation.
