I sit in on a decision-theory reading group in our department, and after last week's session two of the regulars — Nadia, who works on formal epistemology, and Rhys, who does normative ethics with a taste for infinite values — got into an argument about the St. Petersburg game that the rest of us couldn't settle, and I've been volunteered to referee. As I remember it the exchange ran as follows. (1) Nadia: the "paradox" is only a paradox if you think expected monetary value is the quantity a rational agent maximises. Once you maximise expected utility and your utility in money is at all concave — diminishing marginal value, the ordinary thing — the expected utility of the game converges, there is a definite and modest sum worth paying, and the puzzle evaporates; people's unwillingness to pay much was sound intuition applied to the wrong quantity. (2) Rhys: that just relocates the bump. For any unbounded utility function you can redesign the payoff schedule so that each successive run of tails adds a fixed increment of utility rather than a fixed increment of money, and then no amount of concavity saves you; if instead you impose an upper bound on utility to secure finiteness, you pay for it elsewhere, because the gap between a very great good and an arbitrarily greater one shrinks towards nothing and you end up nearly indifferent between a million years of bliss and a googolplex of them — and in the unbounded case you don't reliably get "infinite" either, you sometimes get no determinate expectation at all, a sum whose value depends on the order in which you add the terms. (3) Nadia: I'll concede part of that, but I think we're both over-analysing the contract rather than the decision. No actual house can write an unbounded cheque; any real bookmaker has finite reserves, which truncates the game after roughly the base-2 logarithm of those reserves tails and puts the fair price in the tens of pounds whatever your utility function looks like. The infinite-expectation game is not something anyone could ever be offered, so a decision rule that chokes on it hasn't been shown to be wrong about anything that could happen. (4) Rhys: relocating the puzzle isn't answering it, and your reply has a hidden parameter. First, it assumes a single play: across a long run of independent plays the sum worth paying per play isn't constant but grows without bound, roughly logarithmically in the number of plays, so "the fair price" isn't a number at all and any finite figure you produce is an artefact of how often you'll be allowed to play. Second, the finiteness you're buying can come from completely unrelated sources: if the house pays out the 2-to-the-n stake but only n-to-the-n years later, then any positive rate of pure time preference makes the discounted expectation finite — so whether the game has a finite value gets settled by a parameter that has nothing to do with risk attitudes, and if you're happy to lean on discounting here you owe us an argument for why that isn't a dodge.

I also had a scribble of my own that I couldn't fit to either side, so treat it as raw material rather than evidence for anyone: suppose the house's cap is unknown to the player and drawn so that it equals 2-to-the-k with probability 2-to-the-minus-k, and the house pays 2-to-the-n if the first head falls on toss n, capped at that limit. I make the expected payoff finite and small — around three times the minimum payoff — while the variance diverges; but I can't tell whether that is a point for Nadia, a point for Rhys, or a point about nothing, so check the arithmetic and use it only if it genuinely bears on something.

What I want is an adjudication, not an essay: go through the four moves in order, grade each on a scale from clearly right through more right than wrong, half right and more wrong than right to clearly wrong, and justify each grade in a clause by saying what the move actually establishes, concedes or silently assumes — including flagging any move that rests on a mathematical claim that is false or only half-true, since I'd rather know that than have it politely passed over. Please don't give me citations, a reading list or any named positions from the literature; I know the shape of it, and I want the arguments rather than the footnotes. Answer as exactly five numbered items with no introduction and no summing-up beyond the last one: items one to four for the four moves in order, item five for who is more right overall, what if anything both of them are missing, how confident you are in the verdict as a whole, and which single claim in your reply you are least sure of. One or two sentences per item, in British English.
