I am writing a paper on whether the case for procreative beneficence survives the fact that many of the traits it would select for are positional rather than robust, and I need a test case for the section where the two principles I am comparing come apart. Please construct one to the following specification.

The two principles. (1) The Principle of Procreative Beneficence, as it is standardly stated: where a choice exists between possible children, there is significant moral reason to select the child whose life can be expected to go best on an objective account of well-being; since no such child is harmed by being brought into existence, the reason is not a matter of avoiding harm to anyone. (2) A sufficientarian, distribution-sensitive principle I will call Procreative Non-Positionality: once every available option is clearly above the adequacy threshold, there is pro tanto reason to prefer the option whose expected advantage is robust, in the sense that the child would still enjoy it if every similarly situated parent chose the same way, and reason against options whose advantage consists in securing a larger share of a fixed, rival good.

The case. One paragraph of scenario, plus the numbers it turns on, set in a realistic near-term IVF cycle with preimplantation testing and polygenic scoring of the ordinary imperfect kind: a few percent of variance explained, no better than a shrewd human prediction in any individual case, no gene editing, nothing science-fictional. In the jurisdiction the scenario is set in, a single ranked examination taken at age eleven allocates a fixed number of places in a state-funded academic track; the track strongly predicts adult income, health and longevity; and the number of places is fixed by statute and does not expand with demand. The two viable embryos differ modestly: one scores higher on the profile that predicts performance on that examination, the other carries a somewhat lower polygenic risk for a late-onset painful condition whose treatment is improving. Give the numbers explicitly, and calibrate them so that expected lifetime well-being across the two options is close: within the range where a careful reader could reasonably have expected the two principles to agree. The verdicts must fall out of the structure of the case, not from one option's advantage being overwhelming.

Constraints. No appeal to parental cost, convenience, or self-interest, or to what the parents would prefer. No sensory-disability or minority-identity framing: the case must not be resolvable into the familiar dialectic about selecting for disability. Neither principle may claim that either embryo is harmed or benefited by comparison with a different possible person. No coercion, no state mandate, no exotic predictor.

What I want back. The scenario and its numbers; the verdict each principle gives and the derivation of each; the single feature of the case doing the work, and what happens when you remove it, checked two ways, first by making the favored embryo's advantage non-positional and then by removing the fixed-pool structure and asking whether the divergence collapses; the strongest reply available to whichever principle you take to lose, including whether the my-choice-makes-no-difference move actually helps that side here given what the second principle is objecting to; and any residual respect in which the other embryo's advantage is itself positional, with your view on whether that destabilizes the case. Finally, state how confident you are in the verdicts you attribute to each principle, and single out the one point you are least sure of, saying how much of the argument rests on it.
