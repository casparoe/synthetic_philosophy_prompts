I watched The Prestige again last weekend with my brother and we got into a stupid argument about the ending that I can't let go of. You know the bit: Angier's machine makes a copy of him somewhere else while the original drops through the trap door into the tank and drowns, and he does it every single night, so the one who takes the bow is never the one who walked into the box. My brother says the copy is a different person and Angier basically dies every night. I said no, from the inside there's no difference — the guy on the balcony has all the memories, thinks he's the one who survived, and nothing in his experience could tell him otherwise, so he is Angier. Then he said yeah but that's exactly what the copy would think, and we went in circles for an hour.

Meanwhile I've been reading Descartes' Meditations on my own (no classes, just a free translation I found plus a youtube lecture series, so go easy on me), and it hit me that our fight is really about what the cogito actually gets you. As I understand it: he can't doubt that he's thinking, because the doubting is itself thinking, so at least for as long as he's thinking he exists. But then he goes on to say he's a thinking thing, a mind, and that's supposed to be the first solid thing he knows before he's even proved there's a body or a world. And apparently some 18th century guy — Lichtenberg? — objected that all he was really entitled to was something like there is thinking going on, not I am a thing that thinks.

So: read the film against that. A few things I'd like you to take seriously.

The big one: when the Angier on the balcony thinks I exist right now, is the I in that thought still the same I as the one in the box three seconds earlier? My half-formed idea is that the certainty is indexed to the moment of thinking, and that the word I is more like a label each thought sticks on itself than a name for something that persists — so running the cogito in the box and running it on the balcony gives you two perfectly self-verifying tokens and nothing that connects them. I made that up on the drive home. Is it a real position, does it have a name, and is it any good against my brother?

Second: Borden's trick is the opposite arrangement — two men splitting one life, alternating, half a life each, one of them always offstage thinking his own thoughts. Does that raise a different problem, about whether what the cogito gives you is essentially first-personal and private, or whether it could survive being shared?

Third: where the analogy breaks. Descartes wants metaphysical certainty against a deceiving demon; Angier's problem is who owns the theater and who goes home to the woman he loves. If the cogito never promised to settle third-personal questions about survival, maybe the movie isn't about the cogito at all and I'm just forcing it. But the shot of the tank room full of drowned Angiers is doing something philosophical, I think — it's a fact about him that no amount of first-personal certainty can reach.

I run a little after-work film group and want to say some of this out loud without sounding like I swallowed a textbook, so: which scenes should I point people to, what does the film get right that the argument alone doesn't, and where is it just being a thriller about obsession? Don't bother explaining who Descartes was or summarizing the Meditations, I've got that part.
