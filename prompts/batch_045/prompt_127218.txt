I run a small graduate reading group that does one session a term on population axiology, and I'm putting together the handout for this term's session. What I want is a decision tree — one of those "so which population axiology are you actually committed to?" flowcharts — that sorts people by what they say about concrete pairwise comparisons rather than by which label they're willing to put on themselves. Assume the group knows the basic Mere Addition set-up and why the repugnant conclusion is supposed to be embarrassing, but has basically none of the literature at hand. Please don't give me a bibliography or name-dropped reading list; I want the object itself, not pointers.

Some constraints, sorry if these are fussy:

- Build it from the logical space, not from a list of textbook theories. Start from the questions and the answer-combinations they allow, then locate the actual named views (totalism, averagism, critical-level views, person-affecting views narrow and wide, asymmetric/negative-leaning views, sufficientarian or critical-range views, the incomparabilist escape, the intransitive escape) in that space. Mark "unnamed" where nobody defends a combination.
- 10–12 branch nodes, 8–12 leaves. Every node should be a concrete comparison of two populations with stipulated welfare numbers, phrased so someone can answer it without knowing where it leads.
- Nodes I definately want included: a few very happy people vs a huge number of lives barely above neutrality; the reverse direction (fewer people, each better off); adding one barely-worth-living life to a population of thriving people; adding a life that is *below* the level at which life is worth living for the person living it, and a version where the same quantity of suffering is instead loaded onto someone who already exists; a same-number case where one person is swapped for a different, slightly less happy person; the create-a-happy-person / prevent-a-miserable-person asymmetry; one mildly happy person vs nobody existing at all; a levelling-down case inside a fixed population (I want to catch people who are secretly answering distributive questions when I asked them a population question); and at least one node under uncertainty, where the options are gambles over populations with nobody in common — because I want the tree to force people to say what their ordering is *for*, i.e. whether it's meant to be plugged into expected-value reasoning when who exists is itself unknown.
- Please include a "chain" node: a long sequence of steps each of which people are inclined to accept, ending somewhere they hate. I want that node to be where transitivity (or the willingness to give it up) does real work.
- Also include at least one node where saying "neither better nor worse" is genuinely different from saying "exactly equally good", and make the tree handle the difference.

The bit I've been chewing on and want the tree to make room for: I've started calling them split-threshold views, where the level at which a life is worth living for the person living it comes apart from the level at which adding a life leaves the world no better. Most of the standard theories bolt those two thresholds together, and I want people who dont get forced onto a leaf that collapses them. If that combination is unoccupied in the literature, say so and tell me whether you think that's because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

For each leaf give me the name (or "unnamed"), one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and one sentence on which of the unwelcome conclusions it's stuck with. Then flag at least three pairs of leaves your tree can't really separate, with the extra question that would separate them; I suspect incomparabilist and narrow person-affecting views both just return "no verdict" on the different-number cases, and that critical-level views collapse into totalism or into something negative-ish depending on where the parameter lands, but check whether that's actually where the trouble is.

One warning: I don't want the tree to be a single dial measuring how repugnant someone is willing to be. I want at least two nodes where two people who both accept the repugnant conclusion come apart, and at least one node where someone who accepts it ends up somewhere more comfortable than someone who rejects it.

Aim for about 1300 words, formatted so I can turn the nodes into a one-page printable flowchart with a short key underneath. Casual register is fine, it's a reading-group handout.
