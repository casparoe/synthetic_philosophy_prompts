ok so I'm TAing an intro ethics course this term and we did well-being last week, and since I have to run a discussion section on it I tried to write down my actual views instead of just teaching the four standard theories off the slides. Here's roughly where I landed:

(1) I take the experience-machine intuition seriously as evidence — not as a knockdown argument, but as something a theory of well-being has to either accommodate or explain away.
(2) I think what it's tracking is that we want contact with reality: to actually do the things, actually be the friend, actually matter to someone, not just to have experiences as of those things.
(3) I'm a desire-satisfaction theorist, but the plain actual-desire version, not the informed or idealized version. The whole appeal for me is that "good for you" shouldn't come apart from what you actually want, and idealized-desire theories always seem to be smuggling the philosopher's own values back in under cover of "correcting" your desires.
(4) I also think it's bad for you to be deceived about the things you deeply care about even if you never find out — the unfaithful-spouse, fake-friends kind of setup. That clearly makes a life go worse in my view.
(5) But I think a machine that only smooths over the stretches you'd dread — chronic pain, a year of grief, the humiliating bits — while leaving the rest of your life untouched is totally fine, and I'd take it tomorow. which on reflection sits oddly with (2).
(6) I don't think anyone should be prevented from plugging in, or unplugged once they're in and happy.
(7) I hold that nothing can be good or bad for a person that couldn't in principle show up in their experience or in what they care about — that's my reason for rejecting objective-list theories — but I don't think that collapses into hedonism.

Two variants came up in section that I have firm verdicts on and can't reconcile with the rest: a "lifecast" machine, where what you get is a first-person feed of a real person who really exists and is really doing those things (so reality is maxed out but you aren't the agent at all) — I want that one even less than the original. And a "duet" machine, where two real people go in together, their experiences are coordinated, and both know exactly what's going on — that one gives me almost no pause, and if my partner were up for it I think I'd do it.

My own guess is that the trouble is (2) against (7), since those feel like flat contradictions, but I'm not confident and I'd rather not give up any of them, so tell me if I'm looking in the wrong place.
