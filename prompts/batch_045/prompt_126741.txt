I'm in eleventh grade and I debate for my school's team. Last weekend's resolution was "Patriotism is a virtue," and the negative bench kept coming back to a single line: "My country, right or wrong." Their use of it was something like this: patriotism is at bottom a loyalty that does not track whether its object deserves it, a disposition that is insensitive to reasons cannot be a virtue, so patriotism is at best a sentiment and at worst a vice. My partner answered by reciting a longer version I have also seen floating around online — "My country, right or wrong; if right, to be kept right; and if wrong, to be set right" — and the negative replied that this simply changed the subject, since a person busy setting the country right is being loyal to something else, not to the country. I could not tell who was right, and I suspect neither of them was being precise about what the sentence actually says.

So I would like that sentence disambiguated. Please give me a menu of the precise claims it could be making, ordered from the ones that are nearly trivial to the ones that are genuinely strong, with a verdict attached to each: which is a platitude nearly everyone already accepts in some form, which is substantive and might survive scrutiny, which is false or incoherent, and which one people who repeat the line are usually reaching for. For every reading, say whether it genuinely supports the negative's argument, or instead supports my partner's reply, or does neither.

Two complications of my own, since they are where I got stuck. First, I have been trying to sort patriotisms into kinds, and I cannot tell which kind the slogan is even about: (a) the team-support kind, where you want your country to come out ahead and feel pride when it does; (b) the gratitude kind, where you owe the country something for what it has given you; (c) the trustee kind, where you have been handed an institution along with its stated ideals and are answerable for holding it to them; (d) the kin kind, where you simply give compatriots more weight than strangers. Second, my test case: a friend who loves this country's language, music, and countryside, thinks its founding involved serious injustice, and works against several of its present policies — yet says she does all of that because she loves the country and wants it to become worthy of the promises it made. Under which readings is she a patriot, and under which is she something else under a borrowed name?

One constraint on the answer, please. Do not lay out the menu and leave me to choose at the tournament: commit. Tell me which single reading I should concede to the negative, which single reading I should force them to defend, and give me the one sentence of rebuttal you would actually stand behind, together with your reason for preferring it. Then flag the verdict you are least confident about and say what would change your mind on it.
