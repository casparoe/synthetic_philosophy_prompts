I'm the philosopher on a four-person committee drafting the deception section of the communication code for a large hospital system — the document will govern what clinicians, interpreters, and chaplains may say to patients and families, and it will be applied retrospectively by a review committee that was not in the room (I'm on the committee mostly so that I can be told that my interesting cases are unrealistic). Before we write a word of it, I want the specification: what an account of the moral difference between lying and misleading would have to deliver in order to be fit for that purpose, with the cases rather than the slogans. Give me the full desiderata list — which cases any such account has to get right, which distinctions it must refuse to collapse, and which constraints from neighbouring areas it cannot contradict — each with the case or consideration behind it, and each marked as non-negotiable for our purpose or as the private preference of one camp.

The cases that keep arising in our drafts, and that I want sorted into genuine desiderata versus noise, are these: the deferred-disclosure case, where a clinician says something literally true, expects the family to act on it now, and expects to correct the impression weeks later once the window for acting has closed; the double-audience case, where a true remark addressed to a family conference is heard by a relative who lacks the context the others share; the interpreter case, where what is said passes through a third party; the competence-gradient case, where hearers differ in their capacity to draw the corrective inference; the hollow-option case, where the speaker leaves the hearer nominally free to ask the follow-up that a steep status asymmetry makes unaskable; the borrowed-credibility case, where a true statement is permitted to be relayed by someone whose relay will be read as endorsing more; and the hostile-uptake case, where a fully true and cooperative statement reaches a hearer already determined to extract a false conclusion from it. I also want your view on the structural requirements, since these may be where the list breaks: must the account yield verdicts determinate enough to be audited by an absent third party, teachable in ninety minutes to staff with no philosophy, and stable across thick religious and secular moral commitments — and can those three be had together with real sensitivity to context? My own working candidate is an option-space view: the currency is not asserted content but whether the hearer retains the planning-relevant options they would have had under full disclosure, with a follow-up question counted as an option only when it is live rather than nominal. Say plainly whether the hollow-option and deferred-disclosure cases sink it. If some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, identify the subset and demonstrate it with the pair or triple of cases that forces the choice — that is the result I most want. Which single requirement excludes the most candidates, and which of the demands commonly made in this literature are really only camp prejudice?

Finally, and briefly, as an appendix rather than the main event: score the main families of view against the list — accounts that locate the difference in asserting as opposed to merely conveying, accounts that locate it in invited or warranted inference, accounts that locate it in intention to deceive, trust- or relationship-based accounts, and flat expected-harm consequentialism — a table is fine, and I will do the detailed scoring myself.
