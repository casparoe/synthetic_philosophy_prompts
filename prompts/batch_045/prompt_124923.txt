I'm a novelist and I've backed myself into a corner in chapter nine. One character — an ex-philosopher who now runs a glassblowing shop, obviously — is explaining to another why she'll never manage to blow two *perfectly* identical paperweights. Her line is: if there were genuinely no difference between them in any respect whatsoever, there wouldn't be two of them, there'd be one, so two exactly similar things is a contradiction and there's always a difference somewhere if you look hard enough. I need to decide whether that stays a flat assertion or whether the other character gets to push back on it, and how hard.

So: what's your credence that the identity of indiscernibles is true, in the version where it isn't trivially true — necessarily, if x and y share every qualitative property, monadic and relational, excluding haecceity-style properties like being-this-very-thing-in-particular, then x = y?

Don't just tell me it turns on what counts as a property. That's one of the terms and I want it priced like one. What I actually want is a number and then the decomposition underneath it. My own guess is about 0.35 and my first reader thinks its more like 0.8, so I also want to know which sub-question that gap probably lives in. Here are the terms I can see — merge them, split them, add ones I've missed:

- Is Black's two-spheres setup — two exactly similar spheres in an otherwise empty universe — really a metaphysical possibility, or is the description subtly incoherent, or merely picturable rather than possible?
- Is the unrestricted principle (all properties, including being-identical-to-a) trivially true, and is there any non-question-begging way to cut it down to the qualitative ones? How much of the whole dispute is really this term?
- Can there be a bare numerical difference, a difference with no qualitative difference doing the work — substrata, bundles, haecceitism, whichever way you'd frame it?
- Does the modal epistemology hold: is the fact that we can apparently conceive and describe the setup any evidence at all that it's possible?
- Does physics get a vote? Identical bosons in the same state, permutation invariance, whether that's a case where the principle holds or a case where the question never gets going because the particles aren't individuals in the first place.
- Leibniz's sufficient-reason motivation — God would have no reason to put this one here and that one there. Any independent force left in that, or is it dead weight now?

Then how they combine, which term your final number is most sensitive to, and what single argument or finding would move it the most.

There's also a case I invented for the book and honestly I care more about this than about the general question. Say the two indiscernible spheres sit there for an hour and then, at midnight, one of them acquires a scratch. After midnight there are plainly two objects: the scratched one and the unscratched one. Before midnight, how many objects were there? If the principle is true it looks like there was one — which means the number of objects in that empty universe went from one to two, and that the single pre-midnight sphere is the same thing as both of the post-midnight ones, which by transitivity makes the scratched sphere identical to the unscratched one. So a defender either has to say a story we can tell perfectly straightforwardly was never possible, or give some account of counting and persistence through time that I can't see. Does that bite, or does it just relocate a cost that was already on the books? And what does it do to your number — I'd like a second figure for the principle conditional on the scratch case being a genuine problem for it.
