hey — I've been roped into running one session of a cultural-evolution reading group and I want to use the Ice Bucket Challenge as the case study, but I definately don't want it to turn into ninety minutes of "lol, memes are like genes." Can you do the analysis properly and then tell me where the theory runs out?

Facts as I remember them (correct me if I've got something badly wrong, but please don't go and look anything up — I want to see what the framework does with the case, not a fact-check): summer of 2014. You film yourself tipping a bucket of ice water over your head, post the video, nominate three other people who have 24 hours to do the same or give money to a motor-neuron-disease charity (in the US, the ALS Association). Within a few weeks it was everywhere — celebrities, athletes, politicians, entire offices. The association took in something over a hundred million dollars that summer, apparently mostly from people who'd never donated to it before. Then within about a year it was dead as a practice, and a decent number of the people who did it couldn't have told you what ALS standed for.

So: do the meme's-eye analysis of that. I mean the real version, with fidelity / fecundity / longevity as the three things a replicator needs — not just pointing at things and calling them memes. Specifically:

- Map it. What exactly is the replicator here and what's the vehicle? What's the environment, and what were the actual selection pressures (the 24-hour clock, the nomination mechanic, the fact that it's summer, the fact that the output is a video)? Which variants were fitter and why — the plain bucket vs. the escalation variants with the elaborate setups, the ones that went wrong, the celebrity versions.
- Then break it. I've got four places where I think it buckles; tell me which of them are real problems for the theory and which I'm overrating.

1. The cost thing. The standard line is that memes spread by being easy and cheap to copy. But the ice water is aversive — it's unpleasant, it's mildly risky, people did get hurt. Costs to the host are supposed to *reduce* fitness. So why does the costliness look like it helped? My pet idea is that the cost is doing signaling work: it buys the host deniability (I'm not showing off, I'm being dunked) and makes the participation legible as a small sacrifice, which lowers the social price of posting a video of yourself. Say whether memetics can generate that on its own or whether it has to go borrow from costly-signaling theory, the handicap-principle stuff — and if it has to borrow, what work is the Darwinian framing still doing afterwards?

2. The cargo problem. The donation element is optional in the meme's own instructions, and it seems to be the part that dropped out most often. So is the cause part of the meme or just a passenger? Give me a principled way of deciding, and be honest that we can't run the counterfactual where the challenge has no cause attached to it. Then: if the payload decays while the form persists, is it the same meme? That smells like the species problem to me — so state what individuation criterion you're using for memes and what it commits you to downstream.

3. Why did it stop? Selection explains differential spread, not extinction, and longevity is one of the three criteria but I can't see anything in the framework that retrodicts a burn-out in weeks rather than years. Was it novelty exhaustion, a saturated niche, platform mechanics? If the answer lives outside the theory, how much of the *spread* was the theory explaining either?

4. The one I actually care about: does the meme's-eye view explain anything here, or does it redescribe the same events in Darwinian vocabulary? Be strict with yourself — name one or two things a person could have predicted in advance, in mid-2014, using the framework, and mark clearly which of your claims are post-hoc reconstruction dressed up as explanation. If you can't find any real predictions, say so and say what that does to the case for memetics generally.

Two extra things if you have room. (a) Someone in the group always argues that this was really a story about the platform — autoplaying video in a feed, plus a nomination structure that recruits you by name — and that "memetics" is decoration on top. Give that objection its strongest version and then the best reply you can manage. (b) I want to close the session by asking whether the same analysis can be run on something that isn't a joke — a protest slogan, a piece of disinformation — without it becoming either vacuous (everything that spreads is a meme, so what) or morally obscene (the meme's-eye view has no room for the harm). Give me maybe a paragraph on that, and leave it open rather than resolving it.

800–1000 words would be ideal, and please spare me the "viruses of the mind" routine.
