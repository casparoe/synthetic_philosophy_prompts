I'm in a final-year seminar on the philosophy of historiography, and one thing has been bothering me all term. Our professor has a habit of ruling, case by case, on whether some traditional story "is history" or "is only legend," and he does it with total confidence but never states the test he's applying. He uses three verdicts: history (the events really happened, more or less as a critical source tells them), legend (nothing behind it), and his favourite intermediate category, which he calls a kernel — something roughly of that kind happened at roughly that place and period, but the narrative itself can't be trusted as reportage. Because I want to use his method in my term paper rather than just admire it, I've been logging his rulings after each session, along with the one-line reasons he gave.

Here is the track record as I have it: Alexander's campaigns, history (near-contemporary accounts, plus coins, inscriptions and foundations on the ground). The Peloponnesian War, history. The Trojan War, kernel but not narrative — he cites a real city destroyed by fire at Hisarlik in the Late Bronze Age and diplomatic archives from Anatolia that mention a polity in that corner of the region under a name resembling the Greek one, but he flatly refuses to call anything in the epic reportage, and he says the horse is just a story. King Arthur, legend: nothing contemporary, the earliest sources are centuries late, and the proposed "historical Arthurs" are names in later texts doing no evidential work. The Exodus as narrated, legend: no Egyptian record of plagues or of a huge enslaved population walking out, no trace of a generation-long camp in the Sinai — though he did allow, when pressed, that some Semitic-speaking people very probably left Egypt at some point, which he declined to count as a verdict. Romulus, legend. Robin Hood, legend, with the aside that the "several originals" theory doesn't convert ballads into evidence. Gilgamesh, split: a king of Uruk probably existed, the story is not history. And, oddly, the traditional date for the first Olympic Games: he said the games are history but the founding date is a later retrojection, which he treated as a different kind of mistake altogether. He also conceded that the Catalogue of Ships in the Iliad may preserve genuine Bronze Age geography, but only because it can be checked against the archaeological map — if it didn't match, he said, it would tell us nothing at all.

Three cases come up next week and I'd like to predict him rather than wait. First, the Theseus story: a large Late Bronze Age palace really was excavated at Knossos on Crete, with a maze-like plan, but the tale it is attached to involves a bull-headed monster, a human tribute and a ball of thread. Second, the Xia dynasty: traditional Chinese history places a dynasty before the Shang, and archaeologists have found a substantial Bronze Age site in the Yellow River valley with palatial structures and the earliest bronze-casting workshops known from that region, but no contemporary writing survives that names any such dynasty, and the story is first told in sources written a millennium or more later. Third, the Norse voyage to Vinland: a saga describes a landing in a land west of Greenland around the year 1000, the sagas were written down much later and contain obvious fairy-tale material, but a Norse-style site has been excavated at the northern tip of Newfoundland and dated to around that time, and a German chronicle written within a few decades of the voyages mentions a land west of Greenland. Please do three things. (1) Set out the different rules that are each consistent with his whole track record — something about independent corroboration by non-narrative evidence, something about how close the sources stand to the events, something about genre and whether the story could physically happen, something about whether an event of the right rough kind occurred at the right place and period — and say what each predicts for each of the three cases, with a rough probability attached. (2) Give me your best guess for each case and say which rule you think he is really running, and why it beats the alternatives that also fit the record. (3) Tell me which of the three cases best separates the surviving rules, and describe the hypothetical case — the one he hasn't ruled on — that would settle the question decisively if he were willing to commit to a verdict in advance.
