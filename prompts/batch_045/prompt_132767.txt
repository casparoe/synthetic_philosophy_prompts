I'm taking a seminar this term on Darwin's and Turing's inversions of reasoning — the move of treating competence without comprehension as the starting point, and comprehension as the thing to be explained out of it, rather than as the source that competence has to be derived from. I've been asked to lead one 75-minute peer-facilitated section for about fourteen undergraduates, and I want them to run into the inversion rather than be told about it: no framing lecture up front, and the thesis shouldn't be named until after the activity has done its work. Materials are limited to index cards, printouts, and a whiteboard.

Here is my sketch, which I suspect is flawed. Students sit in a chain. Each holds a few labelled examples — say invented organisms with a handful of visible features, marked viable or not viable — and a card carrying a list of numbers, one weight per feature. Nobody is ever told the rule. On each round a student sees one example, nudges the weights by a fixed arithmetic update, and passes the card on. After enough rounds the card classifies fresh organisms almost perfectly, though nobody in the room can say what it is tracking. Then a safety-manual stage: the group must write down, in sentences, the rule the card appears to be using, and we score their sentences against the card on new cases — the sentences typically lose. My idea for the debrief is that this gap inverts the usual picture: comprehension begins to look less like the source of the competence than like a lossy compression of a competence that was already there, and that compression is precisely what makes the competence portable and discussable at the cost of some of its accuracy.

Two things I can't get right. First, I worry the exercise only teaches that dumb statistics beats explicit rules — a point about learning methods, not about the direction of explanation, and not about comprehension. Second, I can't stop people from deciding that the real understanding sat with whoever designed the update rule, so the comprehending designer is just pushed back one step. Could you redesign the activity, or replace it, so that what is at issue is the direction of explanation, and so that this designer-regress is felt as exactly the move the inversion is meant to dissolve? I'd like the rules and roles, a timeline, a debrief mapping each stage onto both inversions, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and a frank note on which part of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to say in so many words myself.
