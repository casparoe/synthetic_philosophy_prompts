Last night a friend and I argued for hours about a case we couldn't settle, and I want you to reconstruct how she is most likely to rule on it. She reads political lying almost entirely through an Arendtian lens, and over the past year she has handed down the following verdicts, which I think I can report accurately:

- A government deceiving an enemy about troop movements in a defensive war: not condemned at all. She calls that a tool of action, not an attack on the factual world people share.
- A government that builds a single official story to justify a war, where the officials who built it repeat it to one another until they believe it: the worst item on her scale, worse than ordinary lying, because the deceivers lose their own grip on reality and no one is left in a position to correct the record.
- A health ministry that lies to the public about a measure's effectiveness to secure compliance: condemned, but as a lesser wrong, because the claims concern checkable facts, records survive, and someone can set them straight later.
- A forger making papers for people escaping a secret police, and a dissident lying to interrogators: not condemned. The liar here isn't remodelling a shared world; he is surviving in one that has already been wrecked.
- A whistleblower leaking true classified documents: not lying at all, praiseworthy even, though she insists the truth-teller has no political power and shouldn't expect the leak to change policy.
- A satirical magazine printing a claim every reader recognises as invented: not condemned, since nothing is falsified for anybody.
- A campaign ad stating a flatly false claim about an opponent's voting record: condemned, but she treats it as the ordinary case, because a claim is asserted and is therefore answerable to evidence.

The case we fought about: forty-eight hours before an election, a campaign releases a synthetic video of the opposing candidate saying something he never said. Nobody in the campaign asserts it is genuine; the line is that they haven't verified it and people can draw their own conclusions. Strategists later say belief was never the point: the aim was to lodge a certain trait in voters' minds, and it works whether or not they believe the clip, since even voters certain it is fake now spend the day thinking about that trait. There is also a second payoff — once synthetic clips exist, the campaign's own supporters can dismiss the genuine recordings that surface later as probably synthetic too. Her own guess, and mine, is that the clip moved few votes; the mechanism was salience rather than belief.

My hypothesis is that she is running a self-deception criterion rather than a criterion about the destruction of the shared factual world, and that on that basis she should treat this as less serious than the health-ministry lie, which she detested. She says that gets her exactly backwards.

I don't want your own ethics of synthetic media, or a survey of what has been written about post-truth politics, and I don't want citations. I want her track record read back to me: the rule, or rules, her verdicts are compatible with, including the ones that fit them all equally well and differ only here.

Please answer in four titled sections: "Candidate rules" (about 150 words), "What each predicts about the 48-hour clip" (about 120 words), "Best guess and why" (about 80 words), "The variation that would settle it" (about 80 words) — by which I mean the single further case you would put to her to tell the surviving rules apart.
