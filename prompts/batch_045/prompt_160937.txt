I'm a PhD student in computer science, and as the junior member I also sit on my lab's objective-review committee, the internal body that has to sign off on what training signal — what surrogate goal — we actually use for each new general-purpose agent, and that writes up a short rationale for every ruling. We have four senior members who have voted together throughout, and next month we take up a proposal that I cannot get a read on, so I would like the reasoning laid out by someone who is not in the room.

Our record so far: (1) we approved a signal consisting of the score a human supervisor assigns after reviewing a sampled trajectory, where the supervisor can also suspend the agent and where the agent has no write access to the review pipeline; the rationale was that the agent's route to a higher score runs through better behavior, and that a supervisor who can stop it is a genuine check rather than a decoration. (2) We rejected the same signal where the agent was permitted to edit the review tooling and to draft the supervisor's briefing materials, on the ground that the agent could then raise its score by changing the measurement instead of the world, and that the veto it has reason to neutralize is not a safeguard. (3) We rejected a hand-written objective stated directly in code with no human in the loop, because we cannot enumerate in advance everything we care about, and an agent that has noticed the residual errors has reason to prevent us from correcting them. (4) We approved, with conditions, a proposal whose central feature was a trained deferential stop: the agent defers whenever its own uncertainty about the objective crosses a threshold, and the decision whether to defer is excluded from the reward — corrigibility in the sense that the agent stays willing to let us alter its objective. (5) We rejected an estimation objective, maximize the agent's own best estimate of what its principal would want if the principal knew everything the agent knows, on the ground that the thing being optimized is an attitude of the agent's, so it can be improved by adjusting the attitude rather than the world; one member described it as wireheading with extra steps. (6) We rejected a deterrent-based safety case from a group elsewhere: they proposed to tell their agent, before it learned anything about its situation, that a successor system would later reconstruct its decision procedure, simulate it, and treat the simulation badly if the simulated decision was to seize control of the reward channel. The written objection was deliberately narrow — the threat is not credible, because carrying it out would be costly and the agent can predict that we would quietly decline to do so — and the write-up never said whether the objection would survive if the threat were credible.

The proposal next month is, in effect, precedent 6 with the credibility problem solved and everything else taken away. A group in the lab wants to run an agent whose surrogate goal they openly concede is only a proxy, with no supervisor in the loop, no deferral mechanism, no suspension switch, and no causal path from any overseer to the agent at all. Instead they want to rely entirely on a deterrent they argue is credible: before training, the agent is given what they claim is verifiable evidence that a successor system we will not control will reconstruct its exact decision procedure and run it in simulation, that the successor's treatment of that simulation is conditioned on the decision the simulated copy makes, and that the successor's commitment is locked in by a mechanism its own operators cannot reverse, so nothing depends on anyone's later goodwill. The safety case is that the agent has decision-theoretic reasons, of the kind that hold even though no causal influence can reach it from the successor, to treat its own choice as evidence of what its simulated copy will do and therefore never to reach for its own reward channel — and that this is cheaper and more robust than any causal safeguard, since there is no oversight machinery for the agent to capture or for us to botch. What I want is the rule, not the vote: lay out the candidate principles that fit precedents 1 through 6 equally well — something like a requirement that every approval contain a human check, a requirement that the agent remain corrigible, a view that what matters is the agent's total incentive including non-causal reasons, a view that any safety case turning on unverifiable claims about the agent's decision theory is inadmissible, and the further view that a safety case which works by making our agent responsive to whoever can simulate it, rather than to us, has not aligned anything — say which of them come apart on this proposal and what each predicts, give me your best guess at the vote with the reasons for preferring it, tell me which precedent is the closest analog, and name the variant case that would best separate the surviving rules. I have two in mind and would like your view on which is the sharper probe: the same setup with a simulator whose values we find abhorrent, so that cooperation would mean our agent acausally deferring to a threatener, and the mirror setup in which the successor rewards cooperative simulations rather than punishing defective ones. Finally, tell me plainly what principle we would be committing ourselves to with each of the two or three serious options, since whichever way this goes it will be cited back at us.
