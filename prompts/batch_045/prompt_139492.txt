I am preparing the sunk-cost session for an upper-level seminar on practical rationality, and I would be grateful for an audit of two analogies that are doing a great deal of unexamined work in it.

A colleague in the economics department has been asked to advise our provost on whether to continue a large capital project that is over budget, behind schedule, and politically unpopular. His one-page memo rests entirely on two pictures: that continuing is like throwing good money after bad, and that grieving what has already been spent is like crying over spilt milk. The conclusion he draws is unqualified — no rational agent permits a bygone to influence a forward-looking choice — so that every consideration the committee is tempted to raise (the money already sunk, the promises already made, the embarrassment of abandoning a project the university publicly championed) is strictly irrelevant, and the sole remaining question is the ratio of future costs to future benefits.

My own counter-analogy pulls the other way: the commander who burns his boats on landing, so that retreat ceases to be one of his options and both his own future self and his adversaries may rely on his staying. Here the point of the past outlay is precisely that it constrains the future, and this looks to me not merely rational but prudent. The two pictures cannot both be carrying the weight their users place on them.

What I want is an audit of each. For both analogies: an explicit mapping of what corresponds to what in my colleague's case (a table of correspondences is welcome); the features of the source domain that the analogy quietly imports and that the target lacks; the points at which the mapping breaks; and — this is the part I care about — which of those breaks matter for the use to which the analogy is being put and which are merely decorative. Then tell me whether the unqualified conclusion survives, and if it survives only in restricted form, state the restriction.

Three constraints I want the verdict answerable to, because these are the cases on which I keep losing my grip:

(i) Ledger-sunk versus residue-bearing outlay. The money is gone either way, but spending typically leaves something durable behind: a half-built structure, a trained team, forty annual waves of a longitudinal dataset whose next wave is worth more than its first precisely because the earlier ones exist, a reputation for finishing what one begins. These strike me as forward-looking considerations that merely happen to be caused by past outlay. Is there a formulation of the bygones principle that condemns the first while permitting the second, or do the analogies conceal a distinction the principle itself needs?

(ii) Outlay as evidence rather than as cause. I suspect much apparent sensitivity to past investment is better explained as sensitivity to information: that I, or a better-informed earlier self, or a committee that screened the project, invested at all is evidence about its prospects; that others are abandoning is evidence the other way. If so, the milk-and-money picture imports the assumption that the past outlay is known to be pure loss and carries no news. Tell me how much of the phenomenon that explains and where it runs out. Note too that the aircraft which gave this fallacy its second name was continued for reasons of prestige, industrial capability and employment that were not bygones at all, which makes me suspect the label begs the question it names.

(iii) The strategic and diachronic cases: commitments credible only because retreat is expensive; promises, vows and public declarations that alter what is permissible rather than what is advantageous; the fact that quitting is itself not free; and the possibility that a standing policy of never abandoning what one has begun beats case-by-case optimization when returns are back-loaded and one's own judgment about when to quit is unreliable. Relatedly, I have the impression that people escalate more readily when they made the original decision than when it was thrust upon them — is that a mark of irrationality, or a mark of exactly the information-and-commitment structure I have been describing?

Finally, say what a better analogy would be, if there is one, and close with a single machine-readable line: \answer{...} containing exactly one of survives, survives-in-restricted-form, or does-not-survive, followed, if you choose the middle option, by the restriction in no more than ten words.
