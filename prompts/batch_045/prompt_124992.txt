Hey — I'm a novelist, about 200 pages into a draft and stuck on something I clearly dont understand well enough to write about, so bear with me.

The book: my narrator is an outsider living with a small religious community whose language has mandatory evidentiality. You literally cannot say "it rained" — the verb forces you to mark whether you saw it rain, were told, or inferred it, and the same slot shows up in every statement about the past or the future, no declining it, no hedging out of it. The obvious thing to write is "so they're incapable of believing anything on authority, they can't hold a thought they can't source." I don't believe that, and I also don't believe the opposite (that it changes nothing and the grammar is just decoration). I keep hearing that the strong Sapir–Whorf hypothesis is false and the weak one is trivially true, which is useless to someone trying to write a scene.

So here's what I actually want: read 1984 back to me through this question. Not as lit-crit, as a test case. Syme's pitch for Newspeak in the canteen is the strong thesis stated flat out — shrink the vocabulary and the thoughts become unthinkable — but the same novel runs on doublethink, which requires that thought constantly outrun the words available for it, and on Winston's diary, where he writes that freedom is being able to say that two plus two make four, which makes language the guardian of a thought rather than its cage. And then O'Brien torturing him toward two plus two make five is a third thing again. I want to know whether the book is committed to the determinist claim, quietly undercuts it, or is running two incompatible pictures at once because it needs both for the plot.

I think the useful move is to split the big claim into three and ask which ones the novel gives any support to: (1) the lexical ceiling — no word, no concept, no thought; (2) obligatory grammar as a tax — the categories you're forced to encode don't remove thoughts but make some of them expensive, effortful, or hard to hold for long; (3) the "sayable" claim — what language really controls isn't what you can think but what you can say out loud, and therefore share, and therefore think *together*. My guess is the novel quietly supports (3) and needs (2), and only ever asserts (1). Tell me where that goes wrong, and where the reading breaks down and you'd have to admit Orwell just isn't doing linguistics and I should stop leaning on him.

Then bring it back to my draft. If I want the honest version, is it that mandatory evidentiality makes my characters worse liars or that it makes them much better ones, because once you always have to mark your source you can start marking it strategically? Which two or three scenes should I reread with that in mind, and what's the version of this book where the language does almost nothing to the way people think but still wrecks the narrator's life?

Please don't quote the novel at me from memory and don't send me to studies or real-language examples — I can't check them and I don't want to fake anthropology. I just need the conceptual scaffolding straight enough to write from.
