Last night over dinner I got pulled into a three-hour argument between two friends — a relativist and a philosopher of physics — about what the hole argument in general relativity actually establishes. I contributed a suggestion that both of them waved away, and by the end I found myself agreeing with whoever had spoken last. I would be grateful for a careful, point-by-point adjudication of the exchange as it actually went, since I suspect several of the moves were better than the other side credited, and also that both of them were circling around something neither said out loud.

The setup they agreed on: take a globally hyperbolic solution of Einstein's field equations, a Cauchy surface, and a matter-free region — the "hole" — lying inside that surface's domain of dependence. Let a diffeomorphism act on the model that is the identity everywhere outside the hole but non-trivial inside it. Because the equations are diffeomorphism-covariant, the result is a second solution; because the hole is matter-free, the matter distribution is unchanged; and because the map is the identity outside the hole, the two models agree on all the initial data and on everything outside, while disagreeing inside about which point carries which metrical properties.

(1) Nadia opened by saying that this refutes manifold substantivalism outright. If spacetime points are real individuals whose identity is Primitive — not inherited from the fields defined on them — then the field equations plus the total matter distribution plus the initial data fail to determine what metrical properties each point has, and they fail in a way no possible observation could ever resolve. Since general relativity is not indeterministic in any ordinary sense, we should conclude that the points lack the primitive identity the substantivalist needs; spacetime is, at most, the structure the metric field has.

(2) Owen replied that the argument shows only that diffeomorphism-related models are gauge-related: two descriptions of one physical possibility, the way two choices of electromagnetic gauge describe one field. On that reading there is no indeterminism, because there is no second possibility. What the argument really targets is the conjunction of substantivalism with haecceitism about points — the view that there is a further fact, over and above all qualitative and relational facts, about which point is which. Drop the haecceitism and keep the points: spacetime exists independently of matter, but there is no fact of the matter as to which point plays which role. He called this the "sophisticated" version and noted that if the argument worked against the existence of points as such, it would work equally against the reality of any field whose formulation carries a gauge freedom.

(3) Nadia then said the gauge analogy helps her, not him: in electromagnetism the moral everyone draws is that the potential is not the physical ontology and only the gauge-invariant structure is, so by the same reasoning the manifold drops out of the physical ontology of general relativity. She added that the "sophisticated" position is the relationalist conclusion in a new vocabulary — if there is no fact about which point has which metrical properties, then metrical properties are not had by points at all. She also pressed a generalization: run the construction with a diffeomorphism that is the identity on a neighborhood of the whole Cauchy surface but non-trivial everywhere to its future. The resulting model shares all the initial data yet differs at every later point, and there is no longer any "outside" region where the models agree to anchor the disagreement. The hole, she said, is a heuristic; the phenomenon is just diffeomorphism invariance, and that makes it harder, not easier, to see the difference as a physical one.

(4) Owen's rebuttal had two parts. First, a dialectical complaint: to say that the models "agree outside the hole" and "disagree inside" is already to compare properties across distinct models, and that comparison needs a standard of cross-model identity for points. Under the "drag-along" standard you get agreement; under a haecceitistic standard you get the difference. If you help yourself to the haecceitistic standard in stating the argument, the conclusion is close to circular. Second, a substantive challenge: the "indeterminism" here is not the kind physicists worry about — it is invisible to every possible measurement, at every time. Either that makes it a cost a substantivalist can simply absorb, or Nadia is relying on the thought that unobservable differences are unreal, a premise that would discredit unobservable structure throughout science.

(5) Nadia answered that the verificationism charge is misplaced: the point is not that unobservable differences are unreal, but that structure the dynamics is entirely indifferent to is doing no explanatory work and can be dropped without loss. She also argued that absorbing the cost is not free: if which point has which properties is genuinely unsettled by the whole past, then the persistence of a point's identity through time no longer underwrites anything, and the substantivalist owes an account of why the dynamics should respect those identities at all. Finally she turned the gauge point back on him: his own position either concedes everything she claimed or is a version of the view she holds.

(6) Owen then raised what I thought was his strongest point: consider a spacetime with a great deal of symmetry — a perfectly homogeneous vacuum cosmology, say — where all the invariant, qualitative structure is the same everywhere. The relationalist or structuralist who tries to recover spacetime from patterns of relation has nothing here to distinguish one location from another, and either gets a spacetime with a single point or quietly helps herself to bare positions again. His claim was that Nadia's replacement ontology, not his, is the one that runs into trouble in the cases general relativity actually makes available.

(7) Nadia replied that this confuses the failure of local qualitative differences to individuate points with the absence of distinct positions, and that the fix is to identify spacetime not with a bare manifold or with relations among material events but with the metric field itself, taken as a physical particular with its own points. Owen shot back that this is just substantivalism again, and that it faces the hole argument in exactly the same form unless it too goes anti-haecceitist — at which point it is his view.

My own contribution, which both dismissed in about a sentence, was this: suppose we put a marker inside the hole — a single test particle, or a tiny patch of matter — so that there is something there to pin down which point is which. Nadia said a marker gets dragged along by the diffeomorphism as well, so nothing changes; Owen said the classic formulation requires the hole to be empty, so the case is out of bounds. I could not tell whether they were right to set it aside.
