My dad is filling out a living will / advance directive and I've been reading the paperwork with him, and the whole thing seems to presuppose something I can't pin down. The proxy section says the person he picks should decide based on what he would have wanted, not on what's best for him now — so his earlier preferences are supposed to beat his current welfare. But clinicians also quietly set directives aside when a demented patient seems pretty content and is resisting what's being done to him, and he can revoke the thing at any moment while he's still competent, for any dumb reason or none. And it's much easier to get a refusal of treatment enforced than a demand for treatment he'd plausibly want.

So what does the practice actually presuppose? That a person is a temporally extended agent who owns their whole life and so can bind later stages of it? That identity is psychological continuity, so the directive loses grip as continuity frays? Or something less metaphysical — the earlier self is just a better judge, because it isn't currently demented, manic, or in pain? Which rival views does it strictly rule out (say a straight time-slice view on which only whoever is in the chair right now counts), and where is it just underdetermined and compatible with several stories? Also: is this thing more like a will, which takes effect when its author has no stake left, or more like a contract, which binds a party who is still around and has to live with it?

Where I get stuck: it seems like the form ought to have a second-order box where you decide in advance how much your later objections get to count, plus some lapse rule where a directive stops binding if the later you keeps objecting for a month. Would adding those make the practice more coherent, or do they collapse it?
