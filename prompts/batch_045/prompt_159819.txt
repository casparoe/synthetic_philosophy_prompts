I'm retired now — 35 years in experimental physics, mostly condensed matter and instrumentation — and for the last little while I've been sitting in on a philosophy of mind reading group at the local university. They've asked each of us to write down where we've landed so the others can have a go at it. Here's mine, and I'd like an audit before I hand it in. I'm not after a summary of the positions — I've read enough to know the standard names — and I definately don't want a lecture. I want to know whether my particular package holds together.

1. Mental states are functional states. What makes something the belief that it's raining is the causal role it plays, not what it's made of. So no type-identity claims about the brain.

2. Multiple realizability, and I mean it without limits — silicon, aliens, an upload, nothing about carbon or wet chemistry is essential. I also want to cash MR out in a particular way: as a claim about which descriptions are stable, not about how many realizers happen to exist. The functional description is the one that survives perturbation of the substrate, and that's the only sense of "multiple realizability" I actually care about.

3. Mental states do real causal work. Psychological explanations cite genuine causes; I'm not an epiphenomenalist about anything.

4. Physics is causally complete. Every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause, and nothing mental is a spooky extra ingredient added on top.

5. Psychology has its own real generalizations and doesn't need rewriting in neural vocabulary to be respectable science. This was the thing that put me off the identity theory in the first place.

6. My instincts are still reductionist. I think a good explanation gets cashed out in a lower-level mechanism eventually, and I expect neuroscience to explain memory and perception properly one day. I keep worrying this fights with 1, 2 and 5, but I can't quite locate the fight.

7. Two case verdicts. (a) A gradual replacement — one neuron at a time swapped for a functionally equivalent silicon unit — would preserve the person's consciousness the whole way through, and I lean on this as my main reason for substrate independence. (b) A giant lookup table: no interesting internal states, just an enormous stored list of inputs and outputs, that nevertheless behaves exactly like a conscious person in every conversation and every test we can devise. I want to say that thing is NOT conscious. But here's the part that bothers me: I also want to say that how fast the realizing hardware runs, or how much memory it takes up, makes no difference to what a creature thinks — speed and size aren't the kind of thing that could matter to mentality. Someone in the group says the lookup table is the textbook objection to my position 1, and someone else says any table can always be redescribed as a machine with internal states, so the objection is empty. Given the rest of what I believe, I can't tell which of them is right, or whether my own gloss in 2 makes it worse.

So: go through these and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other, ordered by how severely, and for each one what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me. Please flag separately any pairs that only look like they conflict — I suspect at least one of the things I'm uneasy about is a non-problem, and I'd rather know which. Don't tell me which commitment I'd find easiest to drop; I really don't know, and that's the point of asking. And for whichever diagnosis you're most confident about, say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would flip you, and where you think your own reasoning is most likely to have gone wrong.
