I convene a cross-disciplinary graduate seminar — stratigraphers, environmental historians, historians of science, and two political theorists — and for four years the same session has gone the same way. We argue about when the Anthropocene begins, and nobody moves. That would be unsurprising if we were short of evidence, but we are not: everyone at the table accepts the same measurements. Nobody disputes the fallout records from mid-century weapons testing, the plastics and concrete and the mid-twentieth-century inflection in every curve of the Great Acceleration, the Antarctic ice-core record of carbon dioxide and methane, or the slow forcing produced by early agriculture. The disagreement is entirely about which of those is the right thing to be looking at.

The positions are equally stable. The mid-twentieth-century camp eventually carried the day inside the working group: a proposed epoch beginning in 1950, the boundary marked in the annually layered sediments of Crawford Lake in Ontario and keyed largely to plutonium, formally proposed in 2023 and then in 2024 voted down by the stratigraphic subcommission and afterwards confirmed as rejected by its parent bodies — a rejection whose official statement conceded, in effect, that the term would go on being used by Earth scientists, social scientists, politicians and the public whether or not it was ever ratified. There is the Industrial Revolution camp, for whom the causal engine is coal and steam rather than bombs. There is the early-seventeenth-century camp, which reads the dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide around 1610 as the signature of catastrophic population collapse in the Americas and of forest regrowth on abandoned farmland, and which is candid that such a date assigns the onset to conquest and expropriation rather than to industry or to humanity in general. There is the deep-time camp, for whom rice, land clearance and deforestation put the onset thousands of years earlier. And there are those who think the framing itself is at fault, because the relevant agent is not the species but a form of economic organisation, and the unit should be renamed accordingly.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a dispute about a date at all, and not a verbal one either, but a case of people reading different clocks while using one word. Call them the clock of onset, the clock of correlation, and the clock of authorship: when human activity first became a detectable forcing at planetary scale; when a signal becomes globally synchronous and durable enough to be picked out of rock or ice by somebody with no written records, which is close to what a formal boundary marker has to do; and when the causal story begins assigning agency, responsibility and the right sort of historical protagonist. On this reading the stratigraphers are reading the second clock, the historians the third, the Earth system scientists something like the first, and each hears the others as simply having got the date wrong.

But I can also see a second diagnosis, and I cannot tell which is doing more work: that the stalemate is jurisdictional. There is a designated umpire for a question of this kind, the body that maintains the Geological Time Scale. It has ruled. The ruling settled nothing, because the term escaped the discipline that coined it and now belongs to people who never granted that umpire's authority. If that is right, then verbal, substantive, and different-questions-under-one-name are all the wrong categories, and the interesting question is whether a jurisdictional stalemate has any characteristic resolution.

So: give me the diagnosis, and support it by working through the positions themselves — what each camp needs a periodisation to do, and why the rival dates fail by that standard rather than by the evidence. Tell me whether the entanglement of these dates with blame is a defect: does it make the question ill-posed that 1610 indicts conquest, 1950 indicts mid-century consumption, and the deep-time date spreads responsibility across the species, or is that simply what periodising one's own recent past looks like? Tell me how much of the disagreement is downstream of which archive one trusts — ice cores, lake varves, corals, speleothems — given that each resolves time differently and makes a different date look sharp; and if that is a hidden empirical question, say what measurement would close it, and if it is a question about criteria, say that instead. Then, given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress — a distinction, a division of terminological labour, a different question altogether — or argue that no progress should be expected and say why.

Please answer in continuous prose: no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, the way you would write a long email to a colleague. Work from what you already know; I do not want citations, references or a reading list, and I do not want a survey of candidate start dates, since I have just given you the ones that matter. Where my three-clocks framing is wrong, say so plainly.
