My mother died in the spring and my brother and I have spent the summer emptying her house. We keep disagreeing about what her belongings tell us about her, and I have come to realize that neither of us has any idea what we are doing when we do this. I would like help with the underlying question, not mediation advice about my brother.

The disputes all have the same shape. In a locked desk drawer she kept, for something like thirty years, a bundle of letters from her sister, unopened and still tied with string, next to a birthday card from the same sister with a pressed flower in it, dated 1996. My brother reads this as showing that she never forgave her sister and thought about the rift constantly. I read it as showing that she could not bear either to open them or to throw them out, or possibly as showing only that the locked drawer was where things went when she did not know what to do with them. Separately: her kitchen knives are immaculate and recently sharpened, but by every other sign in the house she had not cooked a real meal in ten years. Was cooking still central to how she understood herself, so that the knives are the last surviving piece of that, or are the knives simply the object nobody had a reason to remove? I process insurance claims for a living, where everyone repeats that a missing record is not a record of the thing's absence, and I keep feeling that the two of us are doing something even worse than that: each of us takes an object, invents a story that would produce it, and then treats the object as confirming the story. But I do not want to overcorrect into saying her things cannot tell us anything, because plainly some of them can: sealed, unopened letters are not what you would expect from someone with a warm and ongoing relationship with her sister, whatever else is true.

So here is what I am after. First, a way to sort good inferences of this kind from bad ones: what has to be true of an object before it can support a claim about what someone believed, valued, or was in the habit of doing, and which kinds of claim objects can carry at all — what a thing was used for versus what it meant to her, habit versus deliberate statement, one-off versus routine. Then tell me honestly which of our two disputes the objects can settle and what we should conclude in each, including if the right conclusion is that neither of us can know. Second, I have three half-formed ideas and I want to know which of them, if any, is on the right track and what I am missing. One: the collection in front of us is not a random sample of her life, because she curated it, and she curated it partly with the two of us in mind, so the record is closer to a self-portrait with an unknown audience than to a rubbish heap — which I think means the things she threw away are missing in a systematically biased way we cannot see. Two: the absences, such as no photographs from a certain decade and nothing religious anywhere in the house, feel like evidence, but I cannot tell whether they are evidence against her caring, evidence about what she discarded, or evidence only about the two rooms we have not reached yet; I suspect absence is weaker evidence than presence, in a way I cannot articulate. Three: we have no baseline. How often do people keep unopened letters for thirty years without thinking about them? Without knowing that, I do not see how either of us can say the object is more likely on my brother's story than on mine — so what would a comparison class even be here: other people's houses, the letters of hers she did throw out, our own memories? And last: we are not in the position of someone digging up a settlement whose inhabitants are all long dead and silent. We knew her, we have memories, and each of us has an interest in a particular picture of her. Is that an advantage such a person would kill for, or a contamination, and how should I treat my own memories as evidence given that they are also partly the thing being interpreted? I gather there is a whole profession that does this kind of reasoning about people who died thousands of years ago, and I would like to borrow whatever discipline they have developed — but please do not send me to any books, since I have no library access and I am after the reasoning rather than the reading.
