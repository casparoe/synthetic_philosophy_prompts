For the past eight months I have served as one of five commissioners on the Fairhaven Reparations Commission, established by city ordinance to make recommendations concerning the Cedar Flats taking of 1922, in which the city condemned a majority-Black neighborhood under eminent domain, paid assessments well below market value, demolished roughly 240 homes and 60 businesses, and built the Ash Street water works on the site; the enabling ordinance authorizes us to spend only on "remediation of harms traceable to the taking," and our city attorney reads that phrase as requiring an individualized causal showing for every recipient. We are now deadlocked between two funded proposals. Proposal A is the Cedar Flats Direct Descent Grant: a one-off payment to verifiable lineal descendants of the dispossessed owners and tenants, administered by the Cedar Flats Descendants Association. Proposal B, backed by the Flats East Neighborhood Council, is a community land trust and housing fund for the census tracts adjacent to the former site, which today are poor but demographically mixed and mostly populated by people with no ancestral connection to Cedar Flats. Three complications are eating our meetings alive. First, roughly sixty percent of current Fairhaven residents descend from people who arrived after 1970, and about a third of the water utility's ratepayers live in three suburban towns — Ellisburgh, North Rill, and Vinegar Hill — that incorporated in the 1950s; they will pay for any award through a surcharge, and their representatives argue they bear no responsibility for a wrong committed before their towns existed. Second, a genealogical audit has turned up several descendant families who are today measurably better off than the counterfactual best estimate of where they would be had the taking never occurred: the dispossession pushed one family to Chicago, and a great-grandchild is now a surgeon. One commissioner argues that if the point of redress is to restore victims to the position they would otherwise have occupied, these families' claims must be reduced or zeroed out; another argues that the wrong was the taking itself and that later windfalls are irrelevant to it. Third, a group calling itself the Ash Street Ratepayers' Legacy has filed a claim on the ground that their grandparents' small businesses on the adjacent commercial strip lost their custom when the neighborhood was razed, and that they are therefore also victims of the taking; we cannot see a principled place to stop once we accept them.

Please serve as a political philosopher with experience advising legislative redress programs — someone who has thought hard about how backward-looking and forward-looking justifications for repair come apart, what work an official act of acknowledgment does beyond the transfer of money, and what it even means to identify the bearer of a historical claim. Think the case through carefully rather than producing an even-handed survey: I need a decision, not a menu. Draft the memo I will table at our September meeting, roughly 1,100 to 1,400 words, addressed to my fellow commissioners and structured so we could vote on it. It should (a) give a clear bottom line — adopt, reject, or adopt with conditions, and if the last, state the conditions precisely enough that staff can implement them; (b) specify who counts as an eligible claimant and what standard of evidence and documentation you would require, given that the 1922 records are incomplete and a strict documentation rule will exclude precisely the families with the least archival trace; (c) take an explicit position, with reasons, on how to treat descendants who appear to be better off than they would otherwise have been, and on where the causal chain stops for claimants like the Ash Street Ratepayers' Legacy; (d) say how the program should be funded and why the suburban ratepayers' objection does or does not defeat that mechanism; (e) recommend whether the city should pair any payment with a formal public finding, and close with draft language for a two-sentence finding we could adopt verbatim; and (f) include a section headed "The strongest argument against this recommendation" that states the losing position as sympathetically as the winner — in particular, if you recommend individual payments, engage seriously with the case for neighborhood-level spending, and vice versa. Before you begin the memo proper, spend a short paragraph laying out the two or three decision rules you considered and rejected — for instance, a purely needs-based rule, or a rule keyed to present-day racial disparity alone, or a strict but-for test — and say what went wrong with each, since the discarded options are what my colleagues will ask about. Please do not cite cases, statutes, or scholarly literature: we have counsel and a law librarian for that, and I want the argument rather than the footnotes.
