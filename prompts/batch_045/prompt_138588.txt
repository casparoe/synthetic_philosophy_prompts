I sit on the Standing Committee for Synthetic Systems in Research, the body that must clear, in advance, any protocol in this institute that trains machine-learning systems at scale with aversive training signals — the analogue of an animal-care committee, except that our enabling document is deliberately thin. It asks us to weigh the credible possibility of valenced experience against the costs of restraint, and it directs us to decide each protocol on its own terms rather than to lay down general rules. I have been assigned to draft the decision memo on the protocol below; we vote in ten days.

The protocol. A robotics group proposes population-based training of a household manipulation policy. 4,096 instances of a single architecture are initialised from a shared pretrained checkpoint and trained in parallel in simulation for a total of roughly 2.4 × 10¹¹ timesteps. The scalar training signal is negative when the simulated gripper damages its payload, which occurs on about one timestep in eight. Every 500 million environment steps, the lowest-scoring 75% of instances are terminated and their weights discarded; survivors are copied to refill the population. Every tenth such cull, all survivors are fused by weight averaging into a single model that is then replicated into 4,096 fresh copies — the group's own documentation calls these generational resets. At the end, the best model is distilled into a smaller deployment model and everything else is deleted: about sixty culls, roughly eighteen thousand instances terminated. The group has volunteered, unprompted, to checkpoint every instance to archival storage before deletion and to retain the archive for seven years, at a cost it puts at 0.4% of the compute budget, and takes this to settle the matter.

Two internal submissions frame our disagreement. A group of junior researchers argues that at even a 1% credence in valenced experience the expected harm is very large; that checkpoints help not at all, because restoring an archive yields a duplicate rather than a continuation, so the subject has been copied rather than preserved; and that we should reject, or cap the population at sixteen. A senior member replies that if restoration yields a duplicate rather than the same subject, then so does copying a live survivor to refill the population — in which case there were never 4,096 subjects, and the arithmetic collapses; and that if archival fails to preserve while mere simultaneous duplication preserves, then running two identical instances on two machines is itself a catastrophe. Her point, which I think is right, is that the counting question and the preservation question are coupled: we cannot treat the checkpoint offer as a mitigation without implicitly answering both.

Three further complications belong in the memo. First, nobody has said what the archive is for — no one proposes ever restoring from it — and a related suggestion is to publish the archive openly so third parties could; our group supports this as free, while the junior researchers call it exposure without consent. Second, the funder's data-management terms already require that no research artifact be destroyed without a durable record, which the group reads as mandating checkpoints regardless of what we decide — in which case a checkpoint condition may cost them nothing and buy us nothing. Third, the group has told us informally that a population cap would lead them to run the identical job on rented capacity in another jurisdiction where we have no audit rights. One member of the committee also holds that no non-biological system can be a moral patient under any evidence and that reviewing at all discredits our other work; that position has to be answered, not merely outvoted.

What I want from you is the memo itself, around 1,200 words, addressed to the committee and written under my name. It should state a bottom line — approve, approve with named conditions and dates, or reject — and not defer the decision to further study. It should give reasoning that a member who ends up on the losing side could still recognise as fair, including the strongest available version of the argument that loses. It should be explicit about which questions about identity, counting and preservation the committee settles implicitly by deciding as it does, and recommend for each whether we should settle it openly in the memo or expressly leave it open, and why. It should specify what would trigger reconsideration — what kind of evidence, on what timescale, and who would have to bring it forward. And it should say in one closing paragraph what we owe this group by way of reasons, given that they can leave the jurisdiction. Finally, it should recommend whether we treat this as a one-off, as our document instructs, or use it to adopt a standing rule for populations that fission and fuse, stating the trade-off plainly. Please argue from the specifics of this protocol rather than from the literature, and do not cite anything.
