I should say at the outset that I came to philosophy late. I spent a little over thirty years as a physical chemist working on nonequilibrium systems — reaction–diffusion patterns, metastable states, and above all the way a formally symmetric situation resolves itself into one definite, asymmetric outcome once you stop idealising away the fluctuations. I have been sitting in on philosophy for about four years, and I have been invited to give a paper at a one-day workshop next month. What I want from you is a preparation sheet for the question period.

The paper is on Buridan's ass, and my thesis is that it is not a puzzle about rationality at all. It is a puzzle that appears only when one subtracts from the description of an agent the very mechanism that, in any real agent, resolves the situation. More carefully, I claim that three questions are routinely conflated and that keeping them apart dissolves the case: (i) the causal question, why the animal moves at all rather than standing still; (ii) the normative question, whether its moving one way rather than the other is rationally permissible; and (iii) the constitutive question, whether the movement expresses, creates, or reveals a preference. My argument runs roughly as follows. Buridan-style set-ups stipulate exactly balanced reasons and then quietly stipulate a noiseless, fluctuation-free agent, in which case there is nothing left to break the tie and paralysis follows by construction. In the systems I used to study, a symmetric unstable state plus an arbitrarily small fluctuation reliably yields a definite outcome, and the correct explanation of which outcome you get cites the fluctuation, not any asymmetry in the prior state. If an agent is that sort of system, then paralysis is an artefact of the idealisation, and the fact that nothing in the reasons selected the outcome is no objection to the outcome's rationality: rationality, I want to say, constrains the admissible set and not the selection within it. From this I draw a corollary I am rather fond of, namely that the completeness axiom is a convenience for representation theorems and not a requirement of rationality.

The audience is small, about twenty people, and as far as I can tell from the programme it splits three ways. Roughly half are philosophers of action with a decision-theoretic bent who work on reasons, on options that are "on a par" rather than equally good, and on whether an agent can have any authority over what she wills. Then there are two formal people: a microeconomic theorist who works on incomplete preferences and menu-dependence, and a decision theorist interested in what survives when the completeness axiom is dropped. And there is one experimental psychologist who fits sequential-sampling models — noisy evidence accumulated to a threshold — to response times and error rates in binary choice; I suspect she was invited precisely because someone guessed a noise-based story was coming.

Please give me the seven or eight objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they would do, each attributed to one of these three groups and phrased the way that group would phrase it rather than the way I have phrased things above: for each, a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, and a note on where that reply is soft. I would also like to know what I should concede in the first two minutes, before anyone asks anything, and which single reply has to land if the paper is to survive at all. Do tell me where my physics analogy is misleading — that is the part I most want to know — but please do not explain my own science back to me. If an objection turns on a term of art I will not have met, gloss it in a clause.

One constraint: I cannot chase references before the workshop and I will not cite work I have not read, so no bibliographies and no reading lists. If an objection I am likely to get is really just "this is so-and-so's view and so-and-so already answered it," say that plainly and tell me in a sentence what the answer was; I can decide afterwards whether to read it.

Finally, include the question I am hoping nobody asks. I have a sinking feeling it has something to do with whether what I am describing is something the agent does, as opposed to something that merely happens to her.
