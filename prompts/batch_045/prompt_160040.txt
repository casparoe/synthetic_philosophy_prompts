Context. I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am finishing a reported piece on the demographic-decline beat: fertility subsidies, pronatalist think tanks, and the small but increasingly articulate set of people who argue that having children is morally wrong. The asymmetry keeps surfacing as the intellectual engine on the anti-natalist side of that argument, and I want to describe it accurately and also to say honestly where it does and does not bite.

Here is how I have been summarizing it in draft, stripped of jargon. The presence of pain is bad. The presence of pleasure is good. The absence of pain is good, even though there is nobody there to be benefited by it. And the absence of pleasure is not bad, unless there is somebody who is thereby deprived of it. That is the version associated with David Benatar, or at least the version my interviewees keep rehearsing.

And here is the conclusion I have been drawing from it, which I would like you to check rather than confirm. As I read it, the asymmetry says that a world containing fewer people is better, or at least never worse, than a world containing more of them: each additional person adds the bad of their pains and only the merely-good of their pleasures, whereas their absence would have added the good of avoided pain and no deprivation at all. And since every actual human life contains at least some suffering — illness, grief, the ordinary friction of having a body and a mortality — it follows that every birth is a net harm. That in turn seems to give a clean policy conclusion, which is the sentence I most want to get right: if the asymmetry is correct, then the state's fertility incentives are not merely wasteful but injurious, and the harm-minimizing policy is to minimize births. I have been planning to put that to a pronatalist economist I interviewed, who argued that a child who grows up happy is a benefit to that child and that the state has an interest in there being more such children.

The complication that made me stop and write to you is what I will call the substitution case. A demographer I spoke to made the following objection, which I found hard to answer. Fertility decisions, she said, are almost never a choice between this child existing and nobody existing. If a couple is paid to delay by a year, the child they eventually have is a genetically different child; if a bonus induces a second birth, the child who results is a different child from the one who would have resulted from a different policy. So the alternatives on the table are: create candidate A now, create candidate B later, or create neither. Her claim is that the asymmetry compares A's existence with A's own non-existence, that this comparison is never the one any real policy puts on the table, and therefore that the asymmetry has no purchase on fertility policy at all — it is an argument about a choice nobody gets to make. The anti-natalist I interviewed bit the bullet differently: for each candidate, that candidate's existence is worse for that candidate than its own non-existence, so every available option is condemned, and the asymmetry tells us something is wrong with the situation rather than which lever to pull. I could not tell which of them, if either, has the asymmetry straight.

My editor raised a third worry that I want addressed rather than waved off: she says the whole thing cannot get started, because before anyone is created there is nobody for the harm to be done to, so a claim about what is bad for a person who does not exist is either empty or is really a disguised claim about what the world should look like. I suspect that worry and the substitution case are connected, but I cannot say how.
