You are a philosopher of social science who also reads applied econometrics for a living — the kind of person development economists ring up before they let a causal verb into an abstract. Before you write anything, spend some time brainstorming privately about what makes causal inference from history structurally different from causal inference with experiments: the small number of independent units, the fact that the treatment was not assigned by anyone, the way one country's institutions are another country's geography, and the way a mechanism can be true without the study in front of you being able to show it. Then set the brainstorming aside and give me something usable.

I write a column for a magazine that covers science and ideas, and about twice a month I am handed a new study about why some nations are rich and others are poor. They arrive as press releases and PDFs in three recurring flavours: institutionalist (rules that constrain expropriation, secure property, or bind the ruler), geographic (disease burden, crop potential, soils, navigable water, latitude), and cultural (trust, thrift, patience, work norms, religious inheritance). I have roughly fifteen minutes, no access to the authors' data, at most a regression table and a robustness section I can eyeball but not re-run, and an editor who wants a headline sentence. What I need from you is a decision procedure I can actually run in that window, on that evidence, repeatedly: the questions in the order I should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops and tells me to write the associational or conditional sentence instead of the causal one, and the point at which it stops and hands the question to me as a matter of judgment rather than fact. It must work for a reader who can interpret a coefficient but cannot replicate it, and it should be explicit about which steps are real tests and which are judgment calls dressed up as tests.

Two things I want you to engage with rather than merely accommodate. First, I have a private heuristic I have been using and half-trust, and I would like you to keep it, sharpen it, or replace it with something better and tell me which you did and why: I call it the swap test, and it says that if I can take the mechanism the authors propose and substitute a mechanism from a different family — read the institutionalist study as a geography study, read the culture study as an institutions study — and the design, data, and identifying variation they have could not tell the two apart, then the study has only earned a claim about some persistent cause and my sentence should say so. Second, please run the finished procedure on three cases I actually face. Case one is the familiar cross-country regression over a hundred-plus countries, institutions scored by some index of security from expropriation, income per capita today as the outcome, historical variation used to make the identification story go through, and a press release that says institutions cause prosperity. Case two is narrower and stranger, the kind of thing that has started appearing and that I never know how to write up: a within-one-country study of districts on either side of a river that was the boundary of a medieval monastic land-tenure system six centuries ago, where the districts that inherited the communal side of the boundary are about a fifth richer today, the gap appears right at the river, and it does not appear at a placebo boundary forty kilometres away. Case three is the one I will not enjoy: my own favourite pending piece, a culture explanation built on survey measures of patience and trust plus a historical religious-border design, which I have already pitched as a two-thousand-word feature and which I very much want to write. Please run the procedure on it honestly and bluntly, and tell me what the design can and cannot carry even if that kills or badly shrinks my story. Close by saying plainly what the procedure cannot do — the questions it defers rather than answers, including what to do when the truth is that several mechanisms are real and mutually reinforcing and the interesting question was never which one but how they composed. I do not need references, citations, or a reading list; I need the reasoning, and I need it in a form I can keep beside me and run under deadline.
