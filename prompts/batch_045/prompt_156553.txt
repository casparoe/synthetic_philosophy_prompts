Me and a friend ended up in a three-hour argument last night after a committee meeting and I've been going round in circles on it since. We're both on a small committee that has to split one pot of money between two rival projects — I'll keep it vague, say building out a bike lane network versus fixing up the old municipal pool. I think the pool is pretty much a waste of money, he thinks the bike lanes are a hobbyhorse of mine, and neither of us has shifted on that by so much as an inch. But here's the bit I couldn't get him to see: I'm genuinely confident I'm right about the bike lanes, and I still think an even split is better than tossing a coin to decide which project gets the whole pot. Not because I'm hedging — I really don't think there's some chance the pool is the better use of the cash — but because of the shape of what I want. The first chunk of spending on bike lanes does enormously more good than the last chunk: get a planner, paint the three routes people actually commute on, connect them up, and you've captured most of the value; money past that point is nice-to-have and not much more. So handing over the last 40% of my project in exchange for not risking getting zero looks like a clear win for me, and he says exactly the same thing about the pool from his side. We both prefer the split to the coin flip and neither of us has conceded anything about who is actually right.

So: is there a name for this configuration, and more importantly a clean account of what it is and what it isn't? A label on its own won't do me any good — I want the carving. My friend's first move was that this is just hedging, that if I were really as confident as I claim I'd want the coin flip, and I'm convinced that's wrong: the argument doesn't draw on me being unsure at all, it draws on the fact that my project gives me back a lot at the start and very little at the end. His second move was that it isn't compromise in any interesting sense, it's just deal-making — the same thing as two legislators trading votes, or someone buying off an opponent with an unrelated side payment — and I don't know what to say to that. It also seems different from what people usually mean by meeting in the middle, where both sides move along one shared scale toward each other; we aren't moving along a scale, each of us is giving up the part of our own thing we cared about least.

Here are the edges I noticed while arguing, which is really why I'm writing. First, sometimes it flips: if a project has a threshold, so that half of it delivers literally nothing (half a sea wall holds back no water, a bike network that doesn't connect to anything is just paint), then the split is clearly worse for me than the coin flip and we're back to a straight fight, so whatever this is, it depends on the shape of the returns and not just on the fact of disagreement. Second, it matters whether I think his project is merely worthless or actively harmful: if I think building the pool would make things positively worse and not just fail to help, then trading away my last 40% starts looking like a loss even given my falling returns, and I can't tell whether that's a difference in degree or a difference in kind. Third, I can't tell whether it matters that we're splitting one shared pot rather than each controlling our own budget and swapping across them. And the thing that bugs me most: if my own view says the good falls off that steeply toward the end of my project, does that just mean I never really thought the project was worth the full amount in the first place — i.e. is the whole manoeuvre secretly a concession I'm smuggling in while insisting I haven't made one? If there's an established concept here I'd like it and its nearest neighbours sorted out; if there isn't, tell me that plainly and coin something with a definition and the boundary cases pinned down.
