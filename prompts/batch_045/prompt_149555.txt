Trying to write up where I've landed on the internal/external reasons debate for a grad seminar and I've got a bad feeling that I'm smuggling something, but I honestly can't tell which item is the rotten one. Here's the package, roughly in the order I arrived at it:

1. I'm an internalist. Something counts as a reason for an agent only if there is some sound route of deliberation from what that agent already has in their motivational set, broadly construed — standing evaluative dispositions, commitments, projects, not just occurrent desires — to their doing the thing.

2. But I don't think that makes me a subjectivist. The items in the set aren't all desires, deliberation isn't only instrumental reasoning, and the bare fact that someone happens to care about X doesn't by itself make their caring a reason. So I resist the standard charge that internalism collapses reasons into desires.

3. The internalist story is, I think, the only one that keeps reasons tied to explanation: if I say that A's reason for doing something was that p, that had better be the kind of consideration that could actually move A. That connection is most of what I want out of a theory of reasons, and it's why I became an internalist in the first place.

4. I also want to say that the severely depressed person has a reason to take her medication, and that someone whose motivations were shaped by growing up in a very defferential household has a reason to refuse what she has been trained to want, even when neither of them can reach that by sound deliberation from their actual present motivational set. My usual move is that the relevant set is the one they would have absent the depression or the conditioning.

5. I think we can blame people for the motivational sets they have — for being callous, say — even in cases where internalism says they had no reason to act otherwise.

6. Which is why I want to keep the hard conclusion: the person who genuinely does not care about morality at all and cannot be reasoned into caring still has no reason to be moral. To me that is the payoff of internalism, not a reductio of it.

7. When I tell my friend she has a reason to quit her job and there is no route from her current concerns to quitting, I take myself to be saying something true, not just managing her. My gloss is that I am reporting a reason internal to my motivational set, and that this kind of statement is genuinely true while not being action-guiding for her.

8. Lately I've also been telling people in seminar that the internal/external dispute is at bottom a dispute about what the words 'has a reason' mean, so the two sides aren't really disagreeing about anything substantive.

Does this hang together, and where exactly does it not? Please rank whatever tensions you find by how bad they actually are, rather than giving me a list where everything is equally serious, and for each say what would have to give and what giving it costs me. Also flag any pair that only looks like it's in tension — I suspect at least one item on this list is innocent and I'm being paranoid about it. Finally: is there any version of 1 that keeps both 4 and 6, or is that the thing I can't have?
