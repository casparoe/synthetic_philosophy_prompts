I'm on the capital committee of the Cedar Basin Cooperative Federation — 34 worker-owned shops, mostly metal fab, food processing and print, plus a couple cargo-bike shops that joined last year. Every shop kicks 6% of revenue into a shared capital fund and every spring we fight about who gets it. This year the fight is structural, and I'm the one who has to draft the memo.

Two proposals on the table.

Proposal A, call it the price loop: the federation office sets an internal rental rate for each class of pooled equipment and an internal interest rate on capital itself. Shops bid for funds and rent the equipment out of what they earn, the office revises rates every quarter (rate goes up when subscriptions exceed what's in the fund, down when money goes unclaimed), and shops keep whatever is left over after paying, with a patronage refund at year end.

Proposal B, the allocation assembly: no internal prices at all. Shops submit proposals to an elected allocation committee, the spring assembly ranks them against published criteria — jobs retained, whether it moves us toward the federation's decarbonization commitments, whether the newer shops get a fair shot against the old ones — and capital goes out as a grant.

Here's why I think this is actually the old socialist-calculation argument wearing a flannel shirt, even though nobody here is an economist.

Something like two thirds of the demand on the fund is completely boring: replacing or duplicating equipment the shops have run for ten years, making things to stable specs for customers they've had for years. We have cost data coming out of our ears. We also ran a spreadsheet version of the price loop for two years on just the welding and press-brake equipment, and it kind of worked — except nobody actually had to pay during the pilot, so of course everybody over-subscribed, and I genuinely can't tell if the rate revisions were tracking real scarcities or being smoothed by hand.

The other third is definately the problem. Three shops want to start a line making heat-pump retrofit kits for old apartment buildings: a product that doesn't exist here yet, buyers we haven't identified, a supply chain none of us has. One shop wants a standing "try weird stuff" budget. My worry is that a rate-setting procedure has nothing to converge on there. You can revise a price up when there's a queue and down when there's a surplus, but that only works if there's already a queue or a surplus to look at.

So: I need a memo to the committee, due before the April 12 meeting, that recommends a concrete course of action with real dates and rough numbers, not "consider a hybrid." My instinct is two tracks — the boring two thirds onto the price loop with the revision rule published in advance so it can't be massaged, and the new lines handled by something bounded and explicitly unpriced. But I want the memo to also take seriously the strongest thing the other side says, which as far as I can tell is that in a co-op nobody's personal money is on the line, so a "bid" is cheap talk: market prices mean something because somebody eats the loss when they guess wrong, and if we fake the prices without faking the loss we've just built a machine for dressing up political decisions about which shops survive as technical ones. If that objection kills the price loop, say so — and say what we'd have to bolt onto it (real skin in the game? a hard cap? a sunset date?) for it to mean anything at all.

Two more asks. Please don't give me citations or a reading list; I'm never going to read them, I don't care who said what first, and I have no way to check anything you cite. And our old-timer thinks he's already won because "this was settled against the planners back in the 1930s" and keeps name-dropping Mises and Hayek, while another member says Lange basically answered them with trial-and-error pricing and that computers have only made that answer better. As you go, seperate the boring settled claims from the ones that are genuinely contested among people who actually work on this, because I do not want to hand that guy a memo that presents a live dispute as settled economics in either direction.
