hey, hope this is ok to ask here — I'm an engineer with no philosophy background, so this is its probably naive in places, but I keep going in circles on it.

Situation: I'm on a small team that owns the decision component of an automated bidding system. Right now it's a pile of hand-written rules — a predicted-value model, a variance penalty bolted on top, some hard floors and caps, and a patch added every time somebody found a case where it did something obviously stupid. A few of us want to rip all that out and replace it with one clean expected-utility maximizer: one utility function over outcomes, one probability model, argmax of expected utility, done.

The staff engineer who's skeptical of the rewrite actually explained the case *for* it to me, using an analogy I think he picked up from a decision-theory blog. His version: violating the axioms of expected utility is exactly like an exchange that posts prices admitting arbitrage. If your preferences are intransitive, or you violate independence, then there's a sequence of trades you'd accept one at a time that returns you to your original position minus some money — a money pump, Dutch book, call it what you like — and that is just arbitrage in a philosophy hat. On his mapping: your betting rates or willingness-to-pay are the prices, the bookmaker is the arbitrageur, no-arbitrage is coherence, and to be irrational just *is* to be exploitable. He also claims the theorem runs both directions, the way no-arbitrage is supposed to be equivalent to the existence of a pricing measure. And the conclusion he draws is the one we'd actually be acting on: any decision rule that isn't EU maximization is a bug in the same sense that crossed prices are a bug, and "our domain is special" doesn't get us out of it any more than it gets an exchange out of arbitrage.

I'm suspicious but I can't say exactly why. Two things bother me and I'd like them separated out.

First, I don't think the exploitability the argument talks about is the exploitability we have. Nothing is ever going to hand our system a crafted sequence of trades designed against a utility function it has read; what could happen is repeated interaction with counterparties that learn from its behaviour over many rounds. Those feel like different properties — one is "pumpable by someone who knows your preferences and can stage a single cycle," the other is more like "leaky in expectation under repeated play" — and I want to know whether the conclusion he wants is supported by the weaker one, or whether it only gets him as far as "don't have cycles" and "don't accept trades that are worse for you in every state." Related: someone on the team pointed out that even a genuinely intransitive agent can dodge a pump by anticipating its own future preferences and refusing the first trade now — which in the market picture would be like a price poster turning down profitable trades. I genuinely don't know what to make of that.

Second, I don't see what the analogy says about the variance penalty, or about the classic choice pattern where people take the sure payout over a gamble and then flip when the same chunk is removed from both options. If those are supposed to be arbitrage, who is the arbitrageur and what trade does he actually make against us, given we never post prices anyone can trade against?

So: can you audit this the way you'd audit any analogy? Give me the correspondence table explicitly (what plays the role of the price, the arbitrageur, the market, the capital, the transactions costs), then the places the mapping breaks — and please separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion we'd act on from the ones that are just decoration. In particular: what does it import from markets that our decision component doesn't have, does no-arbitrage-style reasoning actually deliver EU maximization rather than something weaker, and does the whole "therefore you must be an EU maximizer" survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it? And if there's a better analogy for what coherence arguments actually establish — especially one that would make sense to people who write software — I'd rather have that one.

Two more constraints on the answer, sorry: our probability model gets retrained periodically, so I care whether the argument assumes a fixed price schedule held still while the exploit happens; and small amounts of incoherence might just not be worth engineering away, so tell me if the analogy has anything to say about *how much* incoherence is tolerable or whether it's all-or-nothing.
