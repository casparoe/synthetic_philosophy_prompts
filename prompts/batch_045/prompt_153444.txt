I'm in a book club with two friends (we're all engineers, one of us did a history minor ages ago) and we just finished one of those popular big-history books that treats germs, weather and volcanoes as characters -- you know the type, chapters where the pathogen or the eruption gets the verbs and the people mostly react to it. I liked it, but I spend my days debugging distributed systems, so I kept translating the book into replay-a-trace-and-diff terms, and on the whiteboard after the last meeting we ended up with an argument I now find suspicious because it seems to prove way too much:

(1) Something counts as a historical actor just in case it makes a difference: there is some change to it, with everything else held fixed, that makes the downstream timeline diverge a lot. (This is literally "flip one input, replay, diff the trace.")
(2) Germs, climate and volcanoes obviously pass: replay without smallpox reaching the Americas, without the Black Death, without Tambora in 1815 and the year without a summer, without the potato blight -- each replay looks nothing like what actually happened.
(3) Anything that passes (1) earns the same narrative grammar we give people -- "the epidemic opened the interior to conquest," "the eruption starved the countryside into revolt" -- because it does the same job a human agent does in a story: it's contingent, it arrives at a particular moment, and it closes off options that were live before it.
(4) But (1) also passes things nobody wants to call actors: whether the dispatch was written in ink or pencil, whether one courier caught a cold, the depth of a particular river ford, background constants like gravity and where the magnetic pole happened to sit that decade. Flip any one, hold everything else fixed, replay, and the trace diverges.
(5) And it passes downward too: a general's decision passes (1) only in virtue of a million smaller facts -- that he slept badly, that some neuron fired -- and those pass as well, so the general, his insomnia and the neuron are all actors in exactly the same sense, or none of them are, and the book isn't even about people any more.
(6) Nothing inside (1) separates the cases in (2) from those in (4) except how much the outcome matters to us, which is a fact about the reader, not about history.
(7) So the "non-human actors" framing is either empty -- it just means "causes," and the verbs are decoration -- or it needs an extra condition, and every extra condition anyone in the group could come up with (having aims; being able to have done otherwise for reasons; being one persisting thing whose acts can be counted -- is the 1815 eruption one act or a thousand? do the ash veil and the failed harvest count as part of it or as its consequences?) is exactly the human-shaped test the book was trying to dethrone.

I can't take the first horn of (7) seriously -- the good chapters definately taught me something about *how* things fell apart, not just that they did -- but I also can't find the broken step, and (5) makes me think the argument is doing something sneaky rather than just being sound with a premise I dislike. Two fixes got proposed and I trust neither: my friend's "swap test," where you only count something as an actor if swapping it changes the outcome across most nearby variations of everything else rather than in one delicate arrangement; and the other friend's version, where the thing has to be a genuinely independent source of surprise, i.e. not already predictable from the human setup. One person also pointed out that the "hold everything else fixed" bit in (1) is itself how you get environmental determinism for free -- hold the settlement patterns, grain stores and quarantine practices still, vary the pathogen, and of course the pathogen looks like the whole story, even though the same germ reportedly killed wildly different fractions of neighbouring valleys. I'd rather keep (2) and (3) than (1) if I have to choose, but I want to know what I'm paying.

So: which step breaks, or is it valid and a premise has to go? If it's a scope or counterfactual-strength slide between (1) and (4), tell me exactly where it happens and why step looked fine on first read. And price the alternatives for me -- in particular whether either of my friends' fixes survives the objection that they also promote things like the price of grain or the printing press to full-blown "actor," which feels like it just relocates the problem from "what is an agent" to "what is a big cause." If the honest answer is that the framing earns its keep only as a heuristic for causal structure we can't model explicitly, I want to hear that too, and what I'd be giving up by settling for it.
