I'm in a small reading group working through wild animal welfare stuff — basically the question of whether shrinking a habitat can be good news for the animals who would otherwise have lived there, given how most of them die — and we keep hitting the same wall. Last week someone brought a concrete case: a company wants to drain about 20 square kilometres of coastal marsh to build a data-center campus, and one of our members argued that, grim as it sounds, this is probably a welfare improvement, because the marsh is largely a machine for producing huge numbers of juvenile fish, amphibians and invertebrates, the overwhelming majority of which starve, get eaten or die of disease within a few weeks, so the habitat's net welfare over time looks deeply negative. The person running the group's reply was basically "well, nature knows best," and that closed the discussion. I don't want to be the annoying person in the group, but it felt like that one sentence was doing four different jobs at once and none of them were spelled out.

So: lay out for me the distinct precise claims "nature knows best" could be making, as a menu from the trivial to the substantive to the straightforwardly false, with a verdict on each. The bits I keep going in circles on: what "nature" is even supposed to denote here — the evolutionary processes that shaped the system, the system's current state, some pre-human baseline, or the whole thing including us, in which case a data center is as natural as a beaver dam; whether a process with no foresight and no representational states can "know" anything in a sense that isn't just metaphor, or whether the slogan is really an empirical claim about track records; and above all best by which objective function — persistence, biomass throughput, gene copies, resilience, or the experiences of the animals themselves — given that what selection optimizes and what is good for the individual animal come apart pretty dramatically (mass starvation of juveniles, semelparous salmon, arms races that leave everyone worse off). I'd also like each reading pinned to the counterfactual it's comparing against: our intervening, our not intervening, or some earlier human interference we'd be "restoring" away from, since the marsh has been ditched and grazed for two centuries anyway.

What I most want is which of these readings would actually get the group leader his conclusion and which quietly backfire — my worry is that an "evolution has tuned this system" reading is a gift to the other side, since the thing being tuned is gene propagation and not welfare, and that the reading people usually mean when they say it to end an argument is the epistemic-humility one, which cuts against predicting the results of inaction just as hard as against predicting the results of action. Last test: run the same menu on a second case, a proposal to run a contraception-and-vaccination program on an overpopulated deer herd on some moor, where the intervention is meant to reduce suffering rather than to protect habitat. Does whatever reading survives the marsh case let you oppose both, only one, or neither? And flag any reading that's just question-begging — smuggling in the value of naturalness in order to conclude that human-caused change is bad.
