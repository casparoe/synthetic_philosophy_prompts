I sit on the data stewardship committee of a large academic health system, and I write up our decisions. Here are six rulings we have already made, together with the short reasons we recorded at the time.

1. Routine access to the full chart by everyone working the episode of care — residents, a consulting pharmacist, the billing office, the transcription vendor — with no itemized consent. Approved. Reason: these people are doing the work of the episode of care.
2. Remote scribes employed by a vendor, transcribing clinic visits into the record in real time. Approved with conditions (audit logging, 90-day retention, no secondary use, no offshore access). Reason: they act as the clinician's hands.
3. Transfer of identifiable psychiatric narrative notes to a research lab at a for-profit partner, contractually barred from re-identification and secondary use, with broad study consent from the patients. Rejected. Reason: a commercial partner in another sector should not be holding psychiatric narratives.
4. Training a diagnostic model on thirty years of stored, de-identified pathology specimens, no consent, no re-contact, after our own review put re-identification risk at vanishingly small. Approved. Reason: the tissue was removed in the course of care and the former patients have no continuing stake in it.
5. A mortality-prediction score computed by a model from the existing chart, displayed as a banner at the top of the record and visible to every staff member who opened it. Rejected. Reason: although every input was already in the chart and visible to the same staff, the score is a new thing to know about the patient, and it should not be the first thing anyone sees.
6. A sepsis flag computed from vitals already in the chart, shown only to the bedside nurse and cleared at the end of the shift. Approved. Reason: it tells one person to do one thing.

We have never required consent for clinical operations, so a simple no-consent-no-use rule does not describe us. Past that, I do not think we have ever said out loud what we think privacy is, and reading back over these six I cannot extract a single rule from them.

The new proposal concerns what I have started calling adjacent persons: people about whom we can derive reliable facts, who have never been in our records, have no relationship with us, and cannot be described as having shared anything with anyone here. Tumor-normal sequencing is now run on every advanced-cancer biopsy. A validated pipeline reads the resulting sequence and reports, with high confidence, that the patient's thirty-four-year-old daughter carries a pathogenic variant in a cancer-predisposition gene, and with moderate confidence that the patient's husband is not the biological father of her teenage son. Neither the daughter nor the son has ever been a patient here, has consented to anything, or appears anywhere in our data; the pipeline reads only the patient's own sequence, and no fact about either of them was observed — both were derived. The pipeline runs as part of clinical interpretation either way. The question before us is whether to copy both outputs, tagged as unconfirmed and inferred, into a research registry keyed to the patient, readable only by the molecular tumor board, with no disclosure to the patient, to the family, or to anyone else, and no clinical action taken on them.

Please do the following, in under 800 words. Lay out the two or three rules that fit all six precedents equally well, and for each say what it implies about the new proposal and which of the six rulings it fits least comfortably. Note explicitly how each rule treats the fact that nothing about the daughter or the son was observed or shared, only derived — whether the notion of a flow, or of a subject's stake, or of someone coming to know something, even applies to them. Identify which precedent is the closest analog, give your best guess at what we should decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves, and say honestly what we would be committing ourselves to either way if our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case. Close by specifying one further case, as close to this one as possible, that would discriminate between the rules you have identified — for instance a variant in which the output is written to a file that no human ever reads and is destroyed within a day, or one in which the daughter is told. A small table for the rules and their predictions is welcome; keep the prose tight.
