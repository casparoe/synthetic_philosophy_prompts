I've been reading about epistemic game theory — common knowledge of rationality, what iterated elimination of dominated strategies is supposed to represent as a chain of "I know that you know that I know you're rational", the Rubinstein email game result from 1989 where almost-common knowledge gives you a totally different equilibrium from actual common knowledge, and then the global-games literature (Carlsson and van Damme 1993, Morris and Shin on self-fulfilling currency attacks) that dissolves equilibrium multiplicity with a bit of private noise. I want to run all of that against a real case: the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank in March 2023.

Roughly as I remember the facts, so you don't have to guess: SVB had loaded up on long-dated Treasuries when deposits exploded in 2020-21, rates rose, so it was sitting on a big pile of unrealised losses on a held-to-maturity book. On Wednesday March 8 it announced it had sold over $21bn of securities, borrowed $15bn, and would do an emergency share sale to raise about $2.25bn. That announcement plus a bunch of VCs very publicly telling their portfolio companies to move their money triggered a run: about $42bn of withdrawal requests by Thursday, the bank seized on the Friday morning, and two days later the authorities announced that all depositors — including the roughly 89% of deposits that were above the insurance limit — would be made whole. Nobody queued on a street; it was wires and a website.

What I want is a disciplined mapping, not a vibe. Who exactly are the players in this game — depositors individually, or is the right player "the startup with payroll on Friday", or some aggregate? What are the strategies, and what are the payoffs, given that withdrawing early was nearly free and waiting was catastrophic if enough others withdrew? What is the *state* the theory is treating as uncertain: the bank's solvency, its liquidity, or whether the run happens at all — because I think those get slid between in casual tellings and they lead to very different models. And what does each of the two frameworks say the actors' higher-order beliefs were, and what they should have done by its own lights?

Then the questions I actually care about:

1. Does common knowledge of *rationality* do any work here, or is all the action in common knowledge of *fundamentals*? My sense is that in the Diamond-Dybvig style story the multiplicity is selected by a sunspot and rationality-as-common-knowledge is basically decorative, whereas in the global-games version the whole prediction falls out of iterated dominance. Say which one is doing the explaining, and whether the March 8 announcement is best modelled as new information about fundamentals or as the creation of a public signal that changed higher-order beliefs without changing much about the bank.

2. The email game makes me suspicious that these models over-predict runs: if "almost" common knowledge collapses to the safe action no matter how tiny the failure probability, then why wasn't SVB running in January, when the same unrealised losses were sitting there? What's the grain of doubt actually doing in the model, and is there a version where it does the work we need it to (i.e. explains timing) without predicting that every bank with a HTM loss runs constantly?

3. Here's the bit I've been chewing on and can't settle. I think a large fraction of SVB's depositors were not *playing* at all in the relevant sense — they were unaware the question was live. Their problem wasn't uncertainty about whether others were rational, it was that nobody had told them there was a game on, and the thing that changed on March 8 was that everyone got *told at the same time*. Call it the nobody-was-in-the-game problem if that helps. My question: can the standard epistemic machinery represent this, given that it wants everything to be uncertainty over a fixed state space, or does genuine unawareness break the framework? Relatedly: the run's own observable speed became public information as it went (screenshots, group chats, the portal slowing down), and I gather there are results — Angeletos and Werning, Hellwig and coauthors, in the mid-2000s — showing that when prices or other endogenous variables act as public signals, multiplicity comes back. If that's right, does it make the global-games prediction about SVB non-robust in exactly the way that matters?

4. Where is the theory structurally silent? Two candidates I'd like you to press on. First, startups genuinely needed cash for payroll, so "I withdrew because I need the money" and "I withdrew because I think you will" look identical to a payoff function — can the framework distinguish a correlated-liquidity-need story from a coordination story, or is that distinction simply not representable and therefore not something the theory gets an opinion about? Second, the whole thing took about 36 hours, and there's no clock, no settlement mechanics, and no notion of *which* depositors matter in the model — is the timing going to have to be imported from outside, and does that mean the theory explains the run's existence but not its date?

5. And then run it back the other way. Does the case confirm the epistemic story — runs are about what people believe about what people believe, fundamentals be damned — or does it embarrass it, by showing that a bank with 89% uninsured deposits and a $15bn hole was going to fail anyway and the coordination apparatus is decoration on a solvency story? Does the full backstop on March 12 work better as changing payoffs or as destroying the higher-order belief structure, and is that distinction the theory can even draw? I'd also like one paragraph on whether the *medium* matters: if you compare this to Northern Rock in 2007, where the run was visible as people physically queueing outside branches, does a theory built on public signals have anything to say about the difference between a queue you can see and a withdrawal request you can only read about?

Please be concrete about what the models predict and honest about where they go quiet rather than forcing them to speak.
