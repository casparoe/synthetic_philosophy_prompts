What's bugging me is how the whole field talks. Ribosomes "read" messenger RNA and "translate" it; the codon table is called "the genetic code"; the genome is a text in a four-letter alphabet; CRISPR "edits" it and "corrects a typo"; two different triplets that yield the same amino acid are said to carry "the same information," even though the letters differ. But the moment you press anyone, they say none of this is real meaning: nothing is about anything, the pairings got fixed by chemistry and then frozen by evolution, nobody is interpreting, and "information" here is either the technical Shannon thing or just a façon de parler. Then five minutes later the same person insists that a gene "codes for" a polypeptide and not for a trait, and then in the very next sentence writes a grant about "the gene for" alcoholism. And here's the bit I really can't square: I checked, and the triplet that means stop in the nucleus means tryptophan in our mitochondria — and biologists are perfectly happy to call both readings correct. On that picture the meaning seems to come from the decoding machinery, not from the string itself.

So: what does this whole way of talking presuppose? Is it quietly committed to DNA having genuine content — because of what it was selected for, or because of what normally consumes it, or something else — or does the practice get by fine on a purely causal story, with the code talk as a heuristic that happens to pay rent? Reconstruct the implicit theory for me, tell me which parts of the practice strictly require it and which parts several rival theories would license equally well, and say whether the parts fit together or pull in different directions.

Two more things. First, a case that barely exists yet but could: suppose we build a bacterium in which a codon that normally calls for one amino acid gets reassigned so it now picks up a synthetic one, and the bug does fine. Do we say its genome now says something different, or that the same genome is being misread? Whichever way the practice forces us, is that a coherent place to land? Second, if the code vocabulary were banned tomorrow — no "read," no "codes for," no "information" — what would actually change at the bench? I have a feeling the long fights over how much of the genome counts as "functional" would come out differently, but I cant say why, and I'm not sure whether that's a point for or against the vocabulary.
