Next term I'm convening a small and deliberately mixed workshop — myself and one other philosopher, two social psychologists who work on cross-cultural variation in norm strength, and a colleague from public health — on the question of how much rule-following a society should demand. The psychologists' literature frames this as variation in cultural tightness: how many domains of conduct are norm-governed, how costly deviation is made, how far norms are internalized rather than merely outwardly complied with. My job is to write the framing document, and what I want from it is not another tour of the empirical findings but a specification: what would a normative theory of optimal tightness have to deliver to be worth arguing about in a room like that? Let me fix the purpose, since adequacy is relative to it. The theory has to be usable by someone who must decide something concrete — whether a public-health authority should lean on shame and sanction rather than on information, whether a constitutional draftsman should entrench a sphere of deviance or leave it to ordinary politics — and it has to be usable in argument among people who disagree about the good, which rules out simply stipulating one. And it must not collapse into the observation that everything depends on the empirical facts: it has to say which facts carry the verdict and roughly how far they would have to move to change it. (One of my colleagues thinks the whole enterprise is incoherent on the grounds that a question of the form how much should a society demand has no proper unit; I'd like the specification either to answer her or to concede the point cleanly.)

I have a draft list of candidate requirements below, with the case behind each, and I strongly suspect that three or four of them are my own liberal scruples in a lab coat — so I want the list audited at least as much as extended. (1) The theory must separate breadth (how many domains of conduct are norm-governed), severity (how costly deviation is), and depth (whether compliance is internalized or merely performed); case: a polity with few laws and ferocious penalties against one with dense rules rarely enforced, and two populations with identical compliance rates but different counterfactual structures. (2) It must distinguish coordination norms from norms of conformity or purity; case: which side of the road to drive on versus what you may wear on your head or who you may be seen with. (3) It must handle multi-level nesting rather than taking the nation-state as the unit; case: a loose liberal state containing tight illiberal communities, or a draconian legal code loosely enforced above a dense private normative order. (4) It must handle feedback over time — tightening can erode the legitimacy it depends on, or habituate people into the trust that makes tightening unnecessary — and I want it to say which way the mechanism is expected to run in the pandemic case, where costly compliance now plausibly bought a faster return to looseness. (5) It must say whose preferences count when preferences come apart over time; case: seatbelt laws, constitutional precommitment, sin taxes — people may want to be made to follow rules they will later want to break. (6) It must give a verdict on the distribution of compliance costs, especially for those whose default conduct sits far from the norm — religious minorities, immigrants, neurodivergent people, speakers of minority languages — and must not treat cheap compliance and identity- or conscience-costing compliance as the same kind of burden. (7) It must say what to do with enforcement technologies that substitute for social sanction — speed cameras, algorithmic nudging, platform moderation, predictive surveillance — since it is genuinely unclear whether a society of ubiquitous gentle nudges and no informal disapproval is tight or loose. (8) It must be actionable under empirical uncertainty, naming the few parameters that carry the verdict. (9) It must not reduce tightness to punitiveness or to authoritarianism; case: a society with an intrusive state and unenforced laws, or one with almost no state and crushing informal sanction. (10) It must say whether tolerance of deviance is itself a value or merely an instrument, and on what basis, given that the theory is supposed to adjudicate between competing conceptions of the good. (11) It must have a stance on exit and sorting, since people migrate and self-select, and a verdict of too tight sits awkwardly with the fact that those who stayed may have chosen it.

What I'd like back is the specification itself, in roughly this form. Sort my eleven into genuine desiderata, desiderata in need of reformulation, and demands that are really just the preferences of one camp, with a sentence on why in each contested case; mark which are non-negotiable and which a theory may reject at some cost. Add what I've missed — I'd particularly welcome requirements that bite on cases I haven't thought of. Then flag any subset you take to be jointly unsatisfiable: my own suspicion is that the multi-level requirement, the diachronic-feedback requirement, and the actionability requirement can only be met together in some degenerate form, but I want that argued rather than asserted, and if the joint unsatisfiability of some subset is itself the interesting result, say so plainly. Tell me also which single requirement does the most work in excluding otherwise attractive candidate theories, and which requirement my draft most conspicuously fails by smuggling in a conclusion. Only at the end, and briefly — a few lines each, no more — score three families against the list: tightness-as-adaptation-to-threat consequentialism, harm-principle or neutrality liberalism, and a republican non-domination view. The criteria are where I need the thinking; the scorecard is just so I can see whether the list discriminates at all.
