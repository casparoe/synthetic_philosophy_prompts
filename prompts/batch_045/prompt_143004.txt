I write novels of the sort in which people are held to account, and I have been asked to give a short series of talks on what my fiction seems to assume about moral responsibility. Before I commit anything to print I should like to know whether the following set of commitments is one a person can actually hold at once.

(i) Galen Strawson's Basic Argument strikes me as sound. To be morally responsible in the demanding sense — the sense in which someone might truly deserve blame or credit — one would have to be responsible for how one is, mentally, at the moment of acting; but to be responsible for that one would have to have made oneself that way, and to have done that one must already have been a certain way, and so on without end. Nobody ever has been, or could be, responsible in that sense. I also accept the corollaries usually drawn: that the argument has nothing to do with determinism, and that compatibilism is therefore no reply to it.

(ii) I am a compatibilist about freedom all the same. An agent acts freely when the action issues from their own settled desires and judgement, without coercion or deception, and I think that is the only freedom there is or that anyone could sensibly want.

(iii) I hold that resentment, gratitude, blame and praise — the reactive attitudes, in the sense made familiar by P. F. Strawson — are often genuinely fitting, that a life without them would be much poorer, and that a novelist's business is in part to awaken them and to school them. When asked why, my answer is forward-looking: they regulate conduct, they damage or repair relationships, and they train a reader's moral perception.

(iv) I also hold that blame can be owed where nothing forward-looking is served. We owe condemnation to the authors of great harms who have long been dead; to withhold it seems to me a kind of betrayal of their victims; and I do not think this is merely a useful fiction I allow myself.

(v) I think responsibility admits of degrees. A self-fashioned person — someone who has spent decades examining, resisting and reworking their own motives — is more genuinely responsible than someone who acts on unexamined impulse, and the difference is not merely one of predictability or of social usefulness. My plots are built on it.

(vi) I feel morally answerable for what my characters do. The cruelties I write are mine in a way that is not simply a manner of speaking, even though the characters themselves, being what they are, could not have acted otherwise.

Go through these and tell me which pull against which. Please reply as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two long, with no introduction and no summing up: (1) the most severe tension in the set and the commitment that would have to give; (2) the next most severe, and likewise; (3) the next after that, if there is one worth the name; (4) the pair that looks most like a tension but is not one, and the reason it is not; (5) the single cheapest revision to the set as a whole, and what it would cost me in the thing I am least willing to lose.
