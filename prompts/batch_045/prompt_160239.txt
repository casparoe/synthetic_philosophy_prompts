I have to give a five-minute speech next month at the dedication of the new intake wing of the city animal shelter, which is named for my doctoral supervisor — forty years of teaching Kant's ethics, and a bequest that paid for much of the building. Please write it, and write it so that it is honest about her actual view rather than softening it into something the room will find comfortable.

Her view: we have no duties to animals; what look like duties to animals are duties to ourselves not to blunt our capacity for humane feeling, which is why cruelty to animals is genuinely wrong but painlessly killing an animal for food is not. Late in her career she added a refinement she called the institutional reading — that because what is at stake is the character of agents and not how anyone happens to feel at the moment, the duty is properly discharged by designing practices that do not require routine cruelty of the people inside them, so that a worker who kills healthy animals all day and feels nothing is not thereby exempt, and it is the practice rather than the sentiment that has to change. She took this to make the indirect-duty view more demanding of institutions than any view that has to weigh animal welfare against human benefit, since on her reading there is no animal welfare in the scale to weigh.

The room will be shelter staff and volunteers, two city councillors, her family, and three or four of my colleagues from the department; everyone except her family knows something of her work, and the shelter's own language is entirely in the register of rights and of what animals are owed. So the speech cannot say that animals are wronged, or that they are beneficiaries of anything, or that she loved them — she did not think so and said so — but it also cannot land as a joke about euthanasia, or as condescension to people who have devoted their working lives to this, and it does have to thank the donors. There is also a wrinkle I cannot resolve: the duty she cared about is a duty of self-cultivation, she is dead, so on her own terms the bequest cannot be her discharging that duty, and I do not know what the gift was for.

Take a definite line on what the bequest meant on her own terms and on what the wing's existence does or does not show about animals, and give me your reasons in a short paragraph after the speech. Then flag, briefly, any place where the speech depends on a reading of Kant you take to be contested, or on a view you think she could not consistently have held.
