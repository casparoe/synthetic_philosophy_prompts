I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of mind: 16 students, one 90-minute session. They have already done the Turing test, the Chinese Room, and the standard functionalist line that a mental state is defined by its causal relations to inputs, outputs, and other states. The next session is on mind uploading by gradual replacement: the procedure in which, one unit at a time, each part of a brain is swapped for a synthetic surrogate that does exactly what the original did, so that the overall functional organization is held fixed throughout, until nothing organic is left.

Design an in-class activity in which the students generate the central dilemma themselves rather than being handed it. No lecture up front, and I do not want the labels fading qualia, dancing qualia, or organization invariance used or named until the debrief.

What I have in mind, and what I want you to make workable:

The class is divided into three clinics, each running a different protocol that ends in the same fully synthetic system:

1. Drip — 5% of the units replaced per round over 20 compressed rounds.
2. Shadowing — for each unit, the surrogate is installed alongside the original and both are kept running, with a weighting that slides continuously from the organic to the synthetic while total contribution to downstream processing is held constant; at the midpoint of each slide, two complete implementations of that unit's function are live. The organics are switched off at the end.
3. Hemispheric — all units in one half replaced in a single step under anaesthesia, then a recalibration interval, then all units in the other half.

The point of the three protocols is that the endpoint and the stipulated behavioral evidence are the same while the routes differ, so anything the students say about the route is, on the functionalist view, strictly irrelevant.

Make the private first-person verdict the only data in the room. Each student is a patient. Before round one, they write down a sealed prediction of which round, if any, will be the first in which their experience differs. After each round they record, on an index card, a number from 0 to 10 for how much experience they take themselves to have now, and whether they would still consent to continuing. All verbal reports and all behavioral test results are stipulated in advance: whatever the number on the card, the patient says the same thing and passes the same tests. Halfway through, the facilitator collects the numbers anonymously and plots the three curves on the board, and only then are the sealed predictions opened.

The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto specific parts of the argument: why the divergence between the three curves is either noise or an admission that the judgments track something other than functional organization; why saying that nothing changed at any step seems to commit you, by accumulation, to saying nothing changed at the end; why saying that something did change at a step, while function was held fixed, costs you functionalism; and why the subject could not notice a gradual dimming even if it were happening, which is what makes the dimming claim so hard to argue against and so hard to believe.

Include rules, materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the exact wording of the instructions the students see, which must not tip the conclusion. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: I expect the three students who have already read the literature to try to name the argument early, someone to insist that we just ask the patient, someone to reject the stipulation that behavior is held fixed, and the 0-to-10 numbers to be noisy and partly performative; say how to handle each without shutting the discussion down. Finally, be frank about which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and which I therefore have to supply in the debrief myself.
