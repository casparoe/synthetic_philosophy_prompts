hey — I'm on the small committee that decides how a roughly $3M pot gets split across maybe five two-year projects, and it's the theory end of the org: decision theory, formal guarantees, epistemics of evaluation, the conceptual side of safety cases — not empirical ML. The argument we keep having and never resolving is worst-case vs average-case guarantees, and I want your view on which open questions here are actually worth money.

Where I'm stuck, so you know what I already believe and can tell me where I'm wrong. Worst case over all inputs looks useless: anything with real capability can be pushed into bad behavior on some input, so the claim is either vacuous or unachievable, and people mostly invoke it as a rhetorical standard rather than something they expect to meet. Pure average case looks gameable: you look great on the distribution you were evaluated on and awful on the one you get deployed on. And in our case I don't think the deployment distribution is even a fixed object — these systems write a lot of the code, draft a lot of the documents, and generally shape what tasks get handed to them next, so the "average" is partly their own output. I've started calling that the self-consuming average and people have started using it back at me, which worries me, because I can't tell whether it names a real problem or is just a way of making average-case sound deeper than it is.

Some candidate questions floating around, which you should feel free to reorder, merge, or say are junk:

- what the input set for a worst-case claim should even be. Worst case over "inputs a competent operator would plausibly produce"? Over everything a red team with compute budget B can find? Is B a principled knob or just a fudge factor we tune until the claim comes out true?
- whether there's a genuinely useful middle notion — guarantees over a set of distributions rather than over inputs — and when that collapses back into worst case versus when it's actually cheaper to satisfy.
- whether an average-case guarantee is even well-defined when the deployment distribution is partly a fixed point of the system's own behavior, and if not, what a fixed-point-relative guarantee would look like and whether anything about it is tractable.
- composition. Worst-case guarantees for components generally don't compose into a worst-case guarantee for the assembled system, and average-case ones only compose under independence assumptions that visibly fail here. Is there a notion that composes, or do we just accept that the system-level claim will always be weaker than the parts?
- whether "average-case guarantee plus a worst-case-triggered shutdown" is a real third thing or just two weak claims stapled together.
- the regulatory end: what a safety case can honestly assert. Is "no bad behavior on any input our red team found with budget B" the sort of sentence that should appear in a filing, and is work on making claims like that legible and non-misleading worth funding?
- whether the distinction is even tracking what we care about. Maybe what we want is a bound on the probability of catastrophe under deployment, which is neither worst case over all bad behavior nor average case frequency-weighted, and maybe that frame should just eat both.

What I want from you: given that pot, that timeframe, and that kind of researcher, rank the questions you'd fund and defend the ranking. I care much less about the list than about the reasoning — for each one, what it's upstream of, whether it's tractable for people who do math and philosophy rather than large-scale experiments, how neglected it is, and what you'd watch over the next two years that would move it up or down. Also tell me explicitly which of these you would NOT fund and why, including — if this is your view — that the worst-case/average-case framing is the wrong frame and the money should go somewhere else entirely. And if "self-consuming average" is a confused concept, say so and say what the right version of the point is; I'd rather find out from you than from a referee.

One format thing: lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the allocation you'd actually recommend, in order — then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restating my question back to me.
