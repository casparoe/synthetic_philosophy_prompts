I do a column on science and ideas for a newspaper, and I've noticed I reach for "the whole is more than the sum of its parts" about once a season — usually when I want to signal that whatever I'm writing about (a phase transition, a swarm, consciousness, the abilities that showed up in the latest model with noone quite planning them) is *emergent*, and so isn't something the "just reduce it to the physics" people can wave away. A physicist I interviewed last month used it the same way, and it's occured to me that neither of us may have been saying anything at all.

So: what are the actual distinct claims hiding in that sentence? I don't want a literature review or citations — I can't verify them and my editor cuts them anyway — I want a menu of precise readings, and for each one, whether it's a platitude, something substantive and defensible, or just false.

A few things I'd like the menu to be organised around, because these are the places I get stuck:

I think there's something fishy about the grammar. The "parts" and the "whole" are things, but the "more" is always a property or a behaviour — nobody means the whole contains extra matter. So the sentence looks like it's comparing across categories. Does that make it incoherent, or is there a natural way to cash it out that keeps it honest?

And "sum" can't be literal addition, but it's not clear what it is instead: composition, concatenation, some limit of a sequence of bigger and bigger systems, aggregation of the parts' separate descriptions. I suspect the reading people want is one where the "more" is a failure of some derivation — you have the complete story about the parts, you have the composition facts, and the thing about the whole still doesn't follow, or only follows after you've done work that no one could actually do. Please separate "can't be derived even in principle" from "can't be derived in practice because the computation is hopeless", since I think science writing trades on confusing those two.

Then the bit that actually matters for my usage: which of the readings would threaten a reductionist, and which are perfectly comfortable for one to accept? I have a feeling the interesting ones are the ones that are either trivially true or wildly implausible — the "new predicate applies to the whole" reading seems to cost a reductionist nothing, while the "the whole pushes its parts around" reading sounds like the only one with teeth but maybe doesn't make sense. Tell me if that's right.

Last thing: give me two or three sentences I could actually print that do the work I keep trying to make the slogan do, without being either empty or false. If there's a version of the claim that survives, I want to be able to use it in good conscience in a piece about, say, a model suddenly getting a capability, or about a flock.
