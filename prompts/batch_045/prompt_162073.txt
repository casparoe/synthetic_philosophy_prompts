hey — I'm writing up a chapter on how reparations bodies actually reason (political theory / public law, somewhere in between) and I've spent the last two years coding the published decisions of a statutory commission handling claims from a mid-century dispossession. I've anonymised and slightly changed the details. A new claim is coming up in a few weeks and I want to know whether their track record pins down an answer or whether it's genuinely open. Please don't give me a literature dump or citations — I've read the usual stuff, and what I need is a read on this body's own logic.

The setting, briefly: a highland community of roughly 4,000 was removed in 1957–58 to make way for a reservoir, with real violence, and resettled on marginal land. The commission's rulings so far:

1. Direct victims of a *separate* dispossession (land taken 1968), still alive, still poor: individual payouts at current market value of the land, no interest, nothing awarded for the decades in between.
2. Descendants of that same 1968 dispossession, now living abroad as affluent professionals: denied. The written reason was that the scheme addresses present disadvantage traceable to dispossession, not historic wrongs as such.
3. A descendant community still on marginal land, well below national averages, causal chain diffuse (school quality, then employment, then health): no individual cash, but community measures — a clinic and a scholarship fund.
4. A comparable dispossession carried out by a regime that no longer exists and has no successor: declined for want of a respondent.
5. A forced-assimilation claim — nothing taken, no measurable disadvantage today, claimants elderly: official apology, a memorial, and a small symbolic payment, publicly funded.
6. Descendants whose parents had refused compensation in the 1990s on principle: allowed anyway. The parents' refusal doesn't extinguish the descendants' claim.
7. A claim against a mining company still in operation: the company was ordered to pay, and the commission said explicitly that it will not use general tax revenue where the party that profited from the wrong is still around.

The new claim comes from descendants of the reservoir community. The twist is that they are, by any measure, doing fine: the resettlement put them next to a town, they got schooling, and their incomes are above the national average and very likely above what they would have had if they had never been moved. But the graves and sacred sites are under the water, the language has maybe two hundred speakers left, nothing was ever acknowledged, and the removal itself was brutal. They are asking for (a) an official acknowledgment plus a memorial, (b) ongoing funding for language revitalisation, (c) no individual payments. The current beneficiaries are a state power utility that is effectively insolvent and a bunch of downstream smallholder farmers, many of them poor; the state is in a fiscal crisis; and the claimants have said they do not want anything taken from the farmers.

What I want from you:

- Two or three rules the commission could be running that fit all seven rulings, stated sharply enough that they actually disagree somewhere — I can come up with one obvious candidate and I don't trust it.
- What each rule predicts on (a), (b) and (c) separately, because I suspect the three components come apart rather than standing or falling together.
- Your best guess on the overall decision, and what's doing the work in it.
- The single extra ruling, real or hypothetical, you'd most want to see in order to tell the leading rules apart — my access to the archive is decent, so if there's a document worth requesting, tell me what it would have to say.
- Where the precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, what principle the commission would be committing itself to under each option. That's the bit I actually have to write up.

Also: I can't decide whether a body can coherently grant the acknowledgment while denying anything material, or whether the symbolic award is doing all the real work and the cash/no-cash distinction is basically cosmetic. Say so if you think I'm drawing a fake distinction.
