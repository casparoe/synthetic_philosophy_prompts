I'm closing the last session of an advanced undergraduate seminar on aesthetics this Friday, and the topic is aesthetic testimony: whether a critic you have every reason to trust can, by telling you that a work is beautiful, put you in a position to know that it is — or to appreciate it — or whether first-hand acquaintance is the only thing that ever does that. I'd like to end by reading a villanelle instead of summing up, and I'd like the form to carry the argument rather than decorate it. I've tried drafting this twice myself and both attempts collapsed into a sermon about looking slowly, so I'm handing it over. Here is the setup, which you should use or rework as you like: the speaker has read a review by a critic whose every verdict she has checked has turned out right, and crosses town to a small museum to stand in front of one small untitled gray painting in a back room. The two refrains should be, on one side, the borrowed verdict in the critic's own flat, confident register — something like it is beautiful, and everyone who sees it says so — and on the other, the acquaintance demand — something like no one can stand here in your place. The five tercets should stage the failure: she does everything right, she waits, she comes back on a Tuesday, and still the verdict keeps arriving unbidden and not yet hers, which is exactly what a villanelle's returning lines are good for. In the closing quatrain the two refrains should reach the last two lines together and stay there unresolved — not reconciled, not one defeating the other, but audible at once as the two things the speaker now believes and cannot tell apart. Nineteen lines, ABA ABA ABA ABA ABA ABAA, in a loose iambic tetrameter I can actually read aloud without tripping.

Two content constraints, and these are the ones I care about. First, the poem may not cheat by transmitting the very thing the pessimistic side of this debate says cannot be transmitted. Describe the room, the bench, the guard's chair, the hum of the lights, the frame, the gray, the smudge of a fingerprint on the label — describe anything — but the reader must not end up admiring the painting through the speaker's words; if she does, the poem has quietly refuted the thesis it was meant to illustrate. It is fine, and in fact better, if the speaker's own vocabulary for the painting is visibly borrowed from the review. Second, I want the poem to work with a distinction my co-instructor and I keep circling and have never settled: the difference between being verdict-sensitive and being merit-sensitive. Someone who has only read the review can re-deploy the verdict perfectly reliably — she will tell you the painting is good, and she will be right — but her responses are not keyed to the features that make it good, so she cannot say what the verdict depends on, or whether it would survive a change in those features. The case I would most like to see in the poem is the one that has done the most work in our discussions: a room where nothing is labeled, and the possibility, never resolved, that the canvas in front of her is not the one the critic saw. Would she be able to tell? Would it matter? Get that in if you can.

One more thing, which is partly a constraint and partly a favor. My co-instructor holds that aesthetic testimony can yield genuine aesthetic knowledge, and he will be sitting in the front row, so the poem should be honest about where it leans: if it is a pessimist poem, it should feel the pull of the optimist case rather than caricaturing it. And after the poem I'd like a short prose note of about 150 to 250 words doing two jobs. The first is to separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, without presenting a contested view as the consensus: I would expect near-universal agreement that we rely on aesthetic testimony constantly and that the live question is what such reliance delivers, alongside real disagreement about whether what it delivers is knowledge, mere true belief, or something more like understanding, and about whether the acquaintance requirement is an epistemic one at all. The second is to tell me where the poem takes liberties — which reading of the debate it presupposes, where it simplifies, and which lines are doing philosophical work they couldn't strictly bear.
