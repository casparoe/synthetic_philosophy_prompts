hey — I need a piece for a tiny newsletter I send out (maybe 40 people: about half work on existential-risk-ish stuff, the other half are friends who got added years ago and never unsubscribed). I want something that works twice: the risk crowd should wince-recognise a mechanism, everyone else should just find it funny as bureaucracy. Can you write it?

The concept is the unilateralist's curse — the setup where lots of agents can each, on their own, bring about some big shared outcome (build it, release it, transmit it), and the outcome happens if *any one* of them decides to go. Since each agent is working off a noisy private read of a common unknown, the go decision gets made by whoever happened to draw the most optimistic signal. So conditional on the thing actually happening it's worse than each individual thought it was going to be when they were deciding, it gets worse the more agents there are, and the benefits land on the actor while the costs land on everybody. The usual remedy is some kind of conformity norm: don't act alone on your own judgement, defer to the group.

I don't want you to satirise the problem, I want you to satirise the *remedy*. So: an internal standard operating procedure — a handbook chapter, numbered sections, maybe with a footer and a version history — for a made-up body that exists to stop unilateral action, roughly 800 words, deadpan bureaucratic voice. Section 1 states the original problem correctly (that's where the specialists should nod); every section after that is a fix which quietly re-creates the problem or its mirror image. Things I definately want in there:

- a unanimity rule that hands a veto to whichever member is least well calibrated and creates a lively trade in manufactured pessimism — I've been calling this the blocker's curse: a proposal passes only if *everyone* is optimistic, so conditional on passing it's better than any member thought, and the more members you add the harder it is to ever act at all. Use a better name if you have one.
- a rotating lottery that designates one member as sole actor for a term, which just sets n back to 1.
- my favourite: an internal market in tradeable action credits, which is of course straight adverse selection — the most optimistic members value the credits most, so the market concentrates the right to act in exactly the hands it shouldn't.
- one generalisation step, where each member has to certify they'd be happy for every other member to act likewise, and the only people willing to sign are the ones you least want to.
- one tiny worked illustration with toy numbers (three agents, noisy signals) so someone who knows the formalism can check you didn't fudge the mechanism.

It should end with the committee constituting a subcommittee, and with a closing line that lands the real point without stating it: that the act/omission asymmetry is doing all the work and no procedure can fix it. Keep it deadpan — no winking, no "of course this is absurd" asides — and go after the pattern of reasoning rather than any named group, org or person, and no in-jokes that need twitter to read.
