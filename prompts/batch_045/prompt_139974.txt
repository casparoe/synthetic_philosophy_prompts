As a professor of philosophy who is designing a graduate seminar on the nature of creativity, I need a specification of what an adequate theory of artistic creativity would have to do in order to be usable in the age of AI-generated art, rather than another survey of the usual positions. Please write that specification. The theory’s practical purpose is fixed: it is to guide three kinds of decision-maker—(i) a prize jury that must decide whether an AI-assisted or AI-generated work is eligible for an award for creativity, and if so who or what should be named as creator; (ii) a copyright or policy office that must decide whether such a work is original enough to merit protection and who, if anyone, holds authorship; and (iii) an educator or critic who must teach or explain why a given output counts as a creative achievement rather than a mere novelty. Assume the users have no training in academic aesthetics but must apply the theory to concrete cases.

To keep the discussion from being a rehash of well-worn examples, I want the requirements motivated by cases, including a novel one I will call the latent-atelier case. In this case, a visual artist trains a generative model exclusively on a private archive of her own sketches, then destroys the archive and the exact model weights after producing a series of acclaimed paintings. It later emerges that, because of an untraceable quirk in the training seed, the series reproduces in minute detail a body of work by another painter who vanished before ever digitizing or exhibiting it; neither the living artist nor the model’s designers intended this, and the output is historically novel in the sense that no living person had seen the vanished painter’s work. A theory of creativity must say whether the series is creative, who if anyone is the creator, and how the verdict changes if the living artist had instead prompted the model with a single ordinary sentence rather than training it on her own sketches. I also want the requirements to respect, and not collapse, at least these distinctions: between a work’s being creative and its having artistic value; between historical originality and authorship; between causal contribution to an output and intentional or agential shaping of it; and between the creativity of a process and the creativity of a product.

Please organize your response in the following sections, in this order, with the approximate lengths given:

1. Purpose (about 5 percent of the response): restate very briefly what the theory is for and who is supposed to use it, and what would count as its failing at that purpose.
2. Desiderata (about 55 percent): give the requirements an adequate theory must meet. For each requirement, state the requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and explain why it is a genuine desideratum rather than a prejudice of one camp. Include at least six requirements, and make sure the latent-atelier case and the distinctions listed above do real work in motivating several of them.
3. Non-Negotiable versus Negotiable (about 10 percent): mark each requirement as one that any theory for this purpose must satisfy or as a preference of particular camps, and say what is lost if a negotiable requirement is dropped.
4. Conflicts and Trade-Offs (about 15 percent): identify any subset of the desiderata that appears to be jointly unsatisfiable, or that forces a hard trade-off; explain the trade-off and say which side you think a theory for this purpose should take and why. If you think no subset is strictly unsatisfiable, say which trade-off is most underappreciated and why.
5. The Most Exclusionary Requirement (about 5 percent): identify the single requirement that, in your judgment, knocks out the largest number of currently popular or natural theories of creativity, and explain why it has that effect.
6. Optional Brief Scorecard (about 10 percent): if you wish, score three broad families of theory—agential or intention-based theories, historical or contextual theories, and output-based or value-based theories—against the list in a short table or a few sentences each; if you omit the scorecard, say in one sentence why the criteria are more useful than the scoring.

Please write in a formal, polished register appropriate for a philosophy professor, use American English, and keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring existing theories. Do not give a bibliography, reading list, or citations: the seminar has no library access this term, and I want the argument to stand on the cases and requirements themselves.
