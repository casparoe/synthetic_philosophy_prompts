I go to a small ML safety reading group on thursdays — mostly grad students, one guy who works on evals, someone's advisor who shows up every third week — and last session two of them got stuck on debate as a scalable oversight method and I've been chewing on it since. The setup, in case it helps: a weak human judge listens to two AI systems argue opposite sides of some hard question (does this patch introduce a security hole, would this policy destabilize the region), the hope being that spotting a bad argument is easier than producing the right answer yourself. They were fighting over the obfuscation objection — that a dishonest debater can bury one wrong step in a long argument, and a judge who can't check every step can't locate it. Maya's opening move was that obfuscation isn't a trick a liar happens to reach for, it's a property of the question: if the honest justification takes L steps and the judge's attention budget is B, with B less than L, the liar wins by construction, so debate only helps where answers are already locally checkable — and where they're locally checkable you never needed the debate, you could have just had the judge check. Tobias said that treats the judge as a passive reader of monologues, that real protocols force narrowing — the objecting debater has to point at the exact step they deny, you recurse until you hit one claim the judge can settle, and every commitment the liar makes is another place to get caught, so obfuscation is an argument against the naive version rather than against debate. Maya came back with two things: that recursion bottoms out wherever it bottoms out, and for the questions we actually care about the leaves are still things like "this protein folds this way" or "this official would have responded thus," which the judge can't settle either — narrowing doesn't create verifiability, it just shrinks the thing that isn't verifiable — and that the protocol is symmetric anyway, since the liar can demand expansions too and drag the honest debater into a big tree; plus, forcing commitment doesn't favor truth, because the liar picks which false leaf to stand on and can pick one that sounds more plausible than the actual truth, so debate just systematically favors whatever is seductive to an unsophisticated judge, with obfuscation as the limiting case. Tobias conceded the seductiveness point but said it's a different failure mode from obfuscation and one that every oversight method has, including just asking a human, so it's a complaint about having a weak judge at all rather than a reason to prefer any alternative; his positive suggestion was "claim-tree lotteries" — don't let the debaters choose which node gets examined, have the protocol sample random nodes and force both sides to answer questions about them, so the liar needs a globally consistent false story while the honest side gets consistency for free, and the real asymmetry isn't that truth sounds nicer but that lies have to be maintained. Maya's last move was that sampling only pays off if the sub-answers are individually checkable, otherwise the liar answers each sampled node with something plausible or just hedges, and a weak judge can't hold the whole tree in mind to notice the answers are jointly inconsistent — detecting global inconsistency is precisely the expensive thing debate was supposed to offload — and that with superhuman debaters consistency is cheap anyway (perfect recall, cheap search) while verification is the expensive asymmetric cost, and it lands on the judge.

I'd also like your read on who's more right overall, and on whether both of them are missing something — some assumption they share, or a distinction that would dissolve part of the fight. Don't worry about being diplomatic, and please don't just restate each person's position back at me; I want to know where the argument actually breaks.
