I've got to write a short essay for my political philosophy reading group — we spent the last few weeks on the equality of what literature (Sen on functionings and capabilities, Dworkin on resources and the envy test, Cohen on expensive tastes and access to advantage, Arneson on opportunity for welfare, Nussbaum's list, the usual luck-egalitarian stuff and the harshness/disrespect objections to it) — and someone offhandedly said Gattaca makes a good teaching example, which I think is true, but everyone always reads it the same lazy way: genetic discrimination bad, meritocracy good, the human spirit beats DNA. I want to do something less obvious with it.

Here's the angle I keep circling around. Vincent doesn't seem to be demanding equality of resources — nobody is stopping him from buying things — and he doesn't seem to be demanding equality of welfare either; he's not saying he's miserable and wants to be compensated with good feelings. What he wants, as far as I can tell, is something like equality of conversion standing: he wants his effort and training to turn into the same functionings (being an astronaut, being considered a serious candidate) as anyone else's effort and training, and what actually blocks that isn't his heart condition or his genes as such but the fact that his genes are public — the whole apparatus of urine tests, hair samples, blood draws. So the film's complaint would be less endowments should be compensated and more your brute-luck deficits shouldn't be admissible evidence against you, which I think is a genuinely different position from the ones in the readings, and maybe a more interesting one. Relatedly: Vincent would be insulted by being handed compensation for his genome, but he has no problem buying Eugene's genetic material on the black market, and I think that presses right on the harshness objection, though I'm not sure in which direction. And here's a question I can't settle: for the child, is being born without the edits brute luck, or something closer to option luck, given that the parents chose?

But I also worry the reading flatters the film, so I'd like you to argue with me on a few things: whether the currency the film implicitly cares about is really capability or something more perfectionist and Nussbaum-flavoured (space travel, striving, excellence as central human goods), in which case its egalitarianism is much thinner than it looks; whether the ending, with the doctor quietly letting him through, is an argument that only a change in ethos and informal norms rather than institutional design can fix this sort of thing, or just a sentimental cop-out; and whether the film isn't quietly not egalitarian at all, since it's almost entirely uninterested in the Invalids who aren't Vincent — the cleaning staff, Eugene's despair — and really just wants one deserving guy to be permitted to compete. Which scenes would you actually build the essay around, if you were me? I was thinking the two swimming races against Anton, the business with Irene's own genetic profile and what she's told she's only fit for, and Eugene with his silver medal near the end. And where does the film push back on my reading — what does it see that I'm missing, or what does it get wrong about the philosophical debate that I should be careful not to inherit?
