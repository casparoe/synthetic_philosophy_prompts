Been lurking on rationalist blogs and forums since roughly forever, and somewhere along the way I absorbed the local line on acausal cooperation: in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma against a sufficiently exact copy of yourself you cooperate, and the reason generalizes past twins -- it isn't really about the copy, it's that there's one computation being run twice, so you should decide as if you're picking a policy for the whole class of computations that decide like you. I'm talking about that version, not the pop version where you cooperate with your twin because you're both rational so obviously you'll both see the same argument. I've even acted on a soft version of it twice in real life -- once splitting credit on a result with someone I was quietly racing, once paying into a thing where in the narrow sense I'd have done better to free ride -- and I don't regret either one. But I'd like to know how exposed the view actually is.

So: what would count against it?

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and please split them into outsider critiques -- the CDT/EDT-flavored "you're just magicking a correlation into existence" stuff -- versus internal ones that grant the framework and then show it gives no answer or the wrong one. The internal ones are what would actually move me, so I especially want those, and if you think a purported internal critique is secretly an outsider one in costume, say so.

Cases I specifically want run through it:

- The similarity cliff. I am never paired with an exact copy. I'm paired with something 97% similar: my own algorithm trained on slightly different data, or me after an extra hour of thinking, or me with a small patch applied. Is there a threshold where the reasoning stops applying? If similarity is graded, does my prescribed action flip discontinuously at some value, and is that a bug or a feature? If it's graded all the way down, what does the view say about the 4%-similar stranger, and does it then just collapse into ordinary evidential reasoning over my beliefs about how similar we are -- in which case what was the extra content?
- Slightly unequal twins. Exact copies, but the payoff matrix isn't quite symmetric: one of us gets an extra dollar, or one of us has a marginally stronger temptation to defect. Symmetry was doing real work in the original setups. Without exact symmetry does the view have a principled answer, or does it need a tie-break it can't supply from inside?
- The anti-coordination case. Two copies in a game where we both do better if we do *different* things (pick different meeting spots, only one of us sends the email, whatever). Correlated agents can't break a symmetry by correlating -- sharing a random number doesn't help. Does the view quietly require access to uncorrelated, indexical randomness, and is that a hidden empirical assumption about what agents are like? Is there an answer here that isn't just "mixed strategy" hand-waving?
- The population case. Not a twin at all: I'm drawn from a population where some small fraction reason roughly like me and most don't, and I don't know which pool I'm in or who I'm facing. Does the view degrade into self-locating uncertainty about which algorithm I am, and if so does it add anything over "have accurate beliefs about the correlation structure of the world"?
- Empirical check. The real-world analogues people reach for -- labs in a race, firms that could collude, states in an arms race -- are they evidence in either direction? My own guess is that these are all low-similarity cases where the theory is silent, which makes them useless as tests; tell me honestly if that's just special pleading.

Then the insulation question. Every time I've seen this attacked the reply is "that's not really the same computation" or "you changed the problem," and I want an honest read on whether the view has been qualified into something with no risky content left. And then whether that even matters: this is a claim about what rationality requires, not a prediction about what will happen, so what would its predictions even be, and what's the normative analogue of a defeater? For me the genuinely fatal category would be a case where the view tells you to do something you'd still reject on reflection after granting all its premises -- so I want those cases most of all, if there are any.

Give me the strongest internal critique you can, and tell me straight whether you think it lands. If you think the view is basically fine and all the live defeaters sit in the merely-awkward bucket, say that too -- but flag which awkward ones are unsolved rather than just hard. I can follow expected-utility notation and basic causal graphs, less so the deeper decision-theory literature, so keep it at that level. And skip the reading list and who-said-what; I dont need the citations, I need the arguments and the cases.
