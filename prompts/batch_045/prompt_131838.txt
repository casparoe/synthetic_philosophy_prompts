I'm building a small piece of tooling for our group that has to record, at each point in a run, what a bounded planning system can be said to know. The motivating case is whether the system "knew" that a plan it produced violated a constraint stated in its own task description, given that the violation was a fairly short derivation away from data the system already held but that the system never actually performed. My supervisor's view is that the standard possible-worlds treatment of knowledge is of no use here: any agent described that way is logically omniscient, and no real system is, so the semantics is, as they put it, straightforwardly false of the thing I am modelling. On that basis I abandoned it and now keep an explicit list of the formulas the system has actually derived, with no closure imposed.

A postdoc in the group, whom I only partly follow, says I have misidentified the target. Their claim is that "the logical-omniscience problem" names a bundle of separate properties that get run together — knowing every logically valid formula; closure under logical consequence in general; closure under known implication in particular; interchangeability of logically equivalent formulas; and the positive and negative introspection theses — and that these do not all come from the same place. I took them to be saying that the closure properties are the ones forced by possible-worlds semantics, while the introspection ones come from conditions on the accessibility relation, so that weakening the modal logic below S5 buys me nothing on closure; but I may have that backwards. They also said that the impossible-worlds or non-normal-worlds approach I was about to switch to next models something other than what I want — an agent that can fail to see a logical truth, or that holds p and p→q without holding q, which is logical ineptitude rather than an agent that has not yet got round to the inference — and that my explicit list does not solve anything so much as decline to represent what the system's state carries implicitly.

What I think I want is a semantics on which the system counts as knowing φ just in case φ is derivable from its inputs within k inference steps, k being roughly the budget it has actually spent. Two things worry me about this: for generous k it turns out to be closed under a single application of modus ponens anyway, and which formulas are derivable within k steps depends heavily on the choice of proof calculus, which seems like the wrong thing for knowledge to depend on. So my questions are these. Is a bounded-derivation semantics a solution to the logical-omniscience problem, or a different kind of object altogether? Is the point of this literature to produce a semantics in which agents are not logically omniscient, or is that simply the wrong description of what the proposed fixes are doing — and, relatedly, is my supervisor right that the idealisation is a defect to be eliminated rather than a modelling choice with costs and benefits? Should I also be weakening the modal logic, and what would that actually get me? And is there one correct choice here, or does it depend on what I need the knowledge operator for — in which case, depends on what, exactly? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give me the reasoning for it, with no preamble and without restating my question back to me.
