**What I want:** two cases, A and B, identical in every externally checkable respect — same roles, same amounts of distress and relief, same costs to the agent, same everything a hedonic act utilitarian gets to count — differing in exactly one *relational* variable. Care ethics has to come out with different verdicts across A and B (or, if you can only get this far, same action but a different reason and a different story about what would make it wrong — but I'd rather have the verdict flip). Meanwhile straight hedonic act utilitarianism has to give the *same* verdict in both. So the divergence has to be generated by the relational bit, not by welfare totals and not by mere proximity — it can't just be that one of them is her mate, her kid, or the patient she's had longest in some way that also moves welfare.

**The variable I had in mind** — call it an *interpretive need*: a need whose content isn't settled until somebody who has actually been paying attention to this particular person works out what it is. Whether Priya this afternoon needs to be pushed or needs to be left the hell alone isn't readable off her behaviour, but her keyworker, who has watched her for eight weeks, can tell — and can't fully cash it out in words to anyone else. Contrast a brute need (hypoglycaemia, a broken lift): any competent person can see what's needed and the need doesn't depend on who's asking. So A has an interpretive need plus a carer with the relevant history, B has a brute need plus the same carer with no relevant history, welfare pinned.

If you think that version can't actually be pinned — and I suspect it can't, because giving the carer the history also gives her better evidence, which moves expected welfare and drags the utilitarian along — say so and switch to the non-epistemic version: two staff members with *the same* information, only one of whom has actually been in the relation. Vary who has the history, not who knows what. Or propose a better variable. What I don't want is a case where you quietly rig the numbers.
