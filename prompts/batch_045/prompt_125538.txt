I'm a journalist — I write mostly about science and the history of ideas — and I'm several thousand words into a long feature on Kepler that I've started to worry rests on a misreading. The working title is "The Last Rational Astrologer," and the thesis runs like this: Kepler is usually filed as a transitional figure who did respectable astronomy out of the front door while quietly selling horoscopes out of the back, and I want to invert that. On my reading, he came closer than anyone to making astrology scientifically respectable. He stripped away nearly everything his contemporaries took for granted — the twelve signs, the houses, the subdivisions, the whole apparatus of dignities — and kept essentially one variable, the angular separation of the planets as seen from here; and he then underwrote that one variable with the harmonic mathematics he had arrived at independently in the motions themselves, from the nested polyhedra of 1596 through to the celestial harmonies and the third law in the 1619 *Harmonice Mundi*. Astrology, on this account, wasn't a superstition he couldn't shake; it was a hypothesis with a mechanism attached, and it was something close to an accident of intellectual history — the arrival of a mechanics with no room for it, rather than any decisive refutation — that finished it off. The fact that he was still revising a nativity for Wallenstein in the 1620s, while grumbling in his famous line about the foolish daughter having to feed her wise mother, is my evidence that he never let the thing go.

Two things are nagging at me, and I'd rather find out now than in the letters page. First: I can't get straight what the supposed causal chain actually is. I've been calling the surviving doctrine, in my notes, "configurational astrology" — one geometrical variable plus a receiving faculty down here — and I've been assuming the receiving faculty is physical, so that a given aspect angle is in effect a measurable nudge, crude but not crazy, and the only reason it looks crazy now is that we know the planets can't nudge us. But a historian friend says I'm reading Newton backwards into 1619, that what Kepler actually supplies is not a force but something closer to a sense — a capacity in the Earth to register a ratio, not to be pushed by one — and that this makes my "proto-science" framing a category mistake rather than a defensible interpretation. Second: which piece of his mathematics is genuinely load-bearing? I've been giving the third law the starring role, but when I look at what he actually does, the work seems to be done by divisions of the circle into consonant parts and by which of those are geometrically constructible, which feels like a rather different animal from a law about periods and distances. Relatedly, I've been telling readers that he shelved the mystical machinery of 1596 once he got Tycho's data, and I now have a horrible suspicion that he did no such thing.

So: is the thesis salvageable, or am I writing a Whiggish fable? Tell me plainly which link in the chain fails — the stripping-down, the mechanism, the harmonic grounding, the story about how it died — and if it's the mechanism, tell me what a correct account of it would say in one sentence I can use with a general reader. Don't give me a balanced survey of interpretations: give me a verdict, rank my errors from fatal to cosmetic, hand me the honest version of the piece if mine is beyond rescue, and be explicit about which of your claims about Kepler's texts you'd defend as settled and which you're reconstructing from what his astronomy commits him to. If my title is itself the mistake, say that too.
