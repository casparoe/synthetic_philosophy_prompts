I am writing this year's comprehensive examination in formal epistemology for our PhD cohort, and I need one question on van Fraassen's Reflection principle — the requirement that, for any proposition A and any number x in the unit interval, an agent's present credence in A, conditional on the proposition that her credence in A at some specified later time will be x, should itself be x. I want a question that no candidate can dispose of by writing down that formula, deriving it from conditionalization over a partition of possible future evidence, or rehearsing the diachronic Dutch-strategy argument.

The question should turn on a case you construct yourself, in which the agent will in fact receive perfectly reliable evidence bearing on a hypothesis H, so that conditionalizing is unproblematic, but where, independently of H and with a chance she knows, she will afterward be subjected to a belief-forming process whose effect on her credences is deterministic and exactly known to her now while being unavailable to her later: she will not know whether the process operated on her, and its specification will have been erased from her memory. A pharmacological intervention that compresses every credence toward certainty by a factor she can compute is one way to fill this in, but use whatever fills it in best. Close off the obvious escapes by stipulation: the evidence is genuinely relevant, the update on it is genuinely Bayesian, she cannot decline the intervention, and she has no later route to discovering whether it occurred.

The question should then force a decision, not a survey. Does the literal Reflection identity deliver the correct present credence in H? If not, what does — deference to the value she would get by inverting the known distortion, the martingale consequence of conditionalization taken on its own, or some third formulation? And what does the answer show about whether Reflection is best read as a coherence constraint on a present credal state or as a principle of deference to a later self treated as an expert, and about whether the Dutch-strategy argument supports the literal principle or something weaker than it? The candidate should also be pressed to say what structural condition on the relation between later credence and evidence is needed for the literal identity to follow from conditionalization at all, and why the case violates it.

Deliver the question exactly as it would appear on the paper, stipulations included, together with a model answer of roughly a thousand words and a marking scheme out of twenty marks built around five moves a strong answer must make. For each move give its mark value and one line naming the failure it is meant to catch — for instance conflating the martingale consequence with the literal principle, assuming invertibility of the distortion suffices to restore deference, treating the Dutch strategy as decisive without addressing what it means for a bookie to be able to exploit an impaired later self, or abandoning Reflection altogether without saying what governs the case instead. The scheme should make explicit what separates a first-class answer from a bare pass rather than only listing what is missing at the bottom.
