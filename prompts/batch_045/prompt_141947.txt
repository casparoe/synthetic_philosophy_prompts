I'm a magazine journalist — I cover science and ideas, which mostly means watching very smart people be extremely confident about things nobody can actually test. Right now I'm writing a piece about why historical analogies are so seductive and so unreliable: every standoff is Munich, every quagmire is Vietnam, and these days every rivalry is a power transition. My editor, hes a cynic by trade, wants the piece to say the whole 'Thucydides trap' framing is a rhetorical device that sold a book and doesn't track anything real. I think that's too glib, but I can't just assert it, so I want a number I can print in a chart.

Here's the proposition, as precisely as I can state it: before 1 January 2045, there is direct armed conflict between US and Chinese government forces in which at least 1,000 people die, and which at least one of the two governments, in official statements at the time, describes as being about the shift in relative power between the two countries. If there's a war but both sides frame it purely as being about a specific territory or a specific incident, count the proposition as false — I want the analogy's own framing to be doing the work, not a label slapped on afterwards. If nothing happens by then, it's false for my purposes.

Give me your credence on that. But the number is the easy part; what I actually want is the decomposition, because the piece is about where the number comes from. The terms I think matter:

1. Mechanism versus base rate. How much of the probability is really being carried by the structural story — a rapid shift in relative power generating fear, status threat, preemptive logic — as opposed to a flat base rate of great-power conflict that has nothing to do with transitions? My suspicion is that people citing the analogy are quietly borrowing credibility from a mundane base rate and attributing it to the mechanism.

2. The reference class spread. All rivalries? All power transitions? Transitions between nuclear-armed states? Pairs with deep trade interdependence? Give me a rough number under two or three different classes and tell me how wide the spread is. My hunch is the spread across defensible classes is bigger than the number itself, and if that's right it's the actual finding of my piece.

3. The nameless cases problem — that's what I've been calling it in my notes. Munich and Vietnam get names. The standoffs where someone backed down and nothing terrible followed don't get names, and neither do the crises that looked exactly like Munich for six weeks and then quietly didn't. So how much of the apparent track record of these analogies is just an artifact of which cases got to be famous? Related test I use: would the same person, writing in the same year, have cited the same case if it had resolved the other way? I went through maybe two hundred op-eds from the last decade that lean on a historical analogy: roughly two-thirds use one of three cases, and almost none name a case where restraint worked. Which makes me think the act of citing carries almost no information.

4. Fungibility. One case gets mined for opposite lessons — Vietnam was used to argue both that we should never intervene and that we must never loose. How much should that discount the evidential weight of any analogy, this one included?

Combine them into a single number and tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to. Then tell me which single term you're least confident in, and what would move it most: an actual coded dataset of transitions, some kind of historical case nobody has bothered to count, or something happening in the world in the next five years.

Where I'm standing: my own credence on the event is about 0.2, my editor's is about 0.35 — even though he thinks the framing is hokum and would put maybe 0.15 on it doing real explanatory work, where I'd put 0.6. So we disagree about the war and about the framing, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's two disagreements or one. Tell me where you think the real gap lives.

Please don't give me 'it depends' — I know it depends, I need a number and the machinery behind it. And don't recite the standard case list back at me; I've read the book.
