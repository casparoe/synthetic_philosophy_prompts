I have been reading LessWrong and the adjacent decision-theory blogs since the original sequences, and I help run a small reading group that works through decision-theory puzzles slowly, one per month. This month we got stuck on the absent-minded driver, and not on the part I expected. The setup we were using: a driver leaves START, passes a first intersection and then a second, and can exit at either; the two intersections look identical and he cannot remember whether he has already passed one. Exiting at the first is worth 0, exiting at the second is worth 4, and driving past the second is worth 1. If he continues with probability p at every intersection, his expected payoff is 4p − 3p², which peaks at p = 2/3 with a value of 4/3; the two pure plans available to him (exit whenever you see an intersection, continue whenever you see an intersection) return 0 and 1, so any mixture over plans is worth at most 1 and the 4/3 has to come from genuinely re-randomizing at each arrival. Under p = 2/3 the visit-frequency credences at an intersection are 3/5 on the first and 2/5 on the second, and at exactly those credences exiting and continuing come out equal at 8/5 each. One of us noticed that this is not a coincidence: the visit-frequency weighting is precisely what makes the marginal comparison at the node reproduce the derivative of 4p − 3p², so the at-node fixed point and the ex ante optimum have to agree. That convergence is what we are arguing about, because it is starting to look to some of us like a theorem rather than an accident.

The thing that actually split the group is a variant I made up, which is why I am asking for help rather than just working it out myself. Suppose the driver, before leaving, installs a randomization device, and suppose the only two devices on offer differ purely in the physical correlation of their outputs. The sticky device tosses a coin once at START, writes the result on a card on the dashboard, and the driver reads the card at each intersection; he cannot remember having read it before, so his information at the first and second intersections is exactly as in the original problem, but his action is the same at both, and his best achievable payoff is 1. The fresh device re-tosses at each arrival, so the two draws are independent even though the driver cannot tell them apart, and his payoff is 4/3. If he could instead install a counter that flipped its reading each time it was passed, the two intersections would stop being indistinguishable and he would simply continue at the first and exit at the second for a deterministic 4, which means his imperfect recall is costing him 8/3. My worry is that the achievable payoff in this problem seems to depend on something that is not information: two devices leave him with identical evidence at every intersection and identical memories, and yet one is worth a third of a unit more than the other, and the standard way of writing the problem down never says which device he has.

So I would like a dialogue of roughly 1200 to 1500 words between two people who have both thought about this a lot and neither of whom is a strawman, which I can hand out at the next session. Call them Dana and Mira. Dana holds that the driver's real choice is made once, before leaving, and is a choice of policy: she thinks updateless decision theory gets the case right for the reason that the visit-frequency identity suggests, that the fresh device is simply what a stochastic policy looks like when it is physically implemented, that the two tosses are two distinct events in the world rather than two agents, and that the sticky case is just a constrained version of the same problem in which the driver lacks the means to implement the policy he wants. Mira holds that the driver at the intersection has exactly one decision to make, that the sticky case shows a policy is not the kind of thing a single agent can choose without an implementation assumption, and that the 4/3 is only available if the driver treats himself as a population of two drivers sharing a bank account, which she thinks is a real and unargued step rather than a formality. I want them to actually answer each other rather than deliver speeches: Mira should have to say what she thinks the driver should do at the intersection under the fresh device and why it is not simply the best available option, and Dana should have to say what, in her picture, selects p = 2/3 given that at the node the driver is indifferent and could just as well exit. They should also work through two other things: whether the driver who insists on a credence of 1/2 at the node can be money-pumped by a bookie who posts even odds at every intersection and nudges him onto the side that loses a third of a unit in expectation, and whether Mira's view has anything to say at all about the pre-trip choice of device, given that both of them agree he should install the counter if it is free. Each should concede at least one substantive point and come out with a sharper statement of her own position rather than a repetition of it, and I would rather the argument stayed with the arithmetic than be settled by anyone appealing to published papers by name.
