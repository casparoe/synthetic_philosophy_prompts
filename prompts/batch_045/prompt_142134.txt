I've been working on karma as a theory of moral desert — not the folk version, but the strict reading I'll call the ledger view: every culpable act generates a debt owed by the agent, that debt must eventually be discharged by the agent's own experience, it is never written off and never borne by another, and the suffering a being undergoes at a time is exactly the repayment owed at that time. The point of the view, as I understand it, is that it makes suffering non-arbitrary and constrains how much suffering can be fitting: it is supposed to be able to tell us that some quantity of suffering is disproportionate to anything the sufferer did.

A friend and I built the following argument against it, and the conclusion is one I cannot accept, so I want to know which step is doing the illegitimate work. Assume for the sake of the argument that personal identity across lives is unproblematic — I'm deliberately setting aside the no-self worry about whether the debtor and the repayer are the same agent, since I want to know whether the view fails even before that.

(1) No-loss: for every culpable act A of agent S there is a debt that must at some later time be discharged by S's own experience; debts are never forgiven, cancelled, or transferred.

(2) Common currency: merit and demerit are denominated in a single currency and can offset one another, so what matters is S's net balance at a time.

(3) Beginninglessness: S's history of acts has no first member — for every act there is an earlier one.

(4) Floor: there is some positive amount such that infinitely many of S's acts carry demerit at least that large. Not because S is unusually bad: a single life contains at least some acts of that size, and there are infinitely many lives.

(5) Finite throughput: the amount of debt a being can discharge within a life is bounded above by the suffering available in that life, which is finite.

(6) So at every time S's outstanding balance is infinite: infinitely many debts of at least a fixed size have accrued, and only finitely much can have been paid off by then.

(7) Exhaustion: a being is liberated — has burned off its karma — only if its outstanding balance is zero.

(8) Therefore no being has ever been or will ever be liberated. And worse: on any finite measure, whatever a being suffers at a time is an infinitesimal fraction of what is owed, so no suffering is ever disproportionate and no repayment excessive — the proportionality constraint the ledger view existed to impose turns out to be vacuous.

Three things I've tried, none of which I trust. First, my friend says step (6) illegitimately totals a series that karma never sums: debts ripen in a fixed priority order — the weightiest acts first, then those nearest to death, then the habitual ones, then the residue — so the ledger has a privileged ordering and the global sum is an artifact. But as far as I can tell, reordering only changes partial sums when the terms shrink toward zero, and the whole force of (6) comes from a floor like (4); the two complaints seem to be in tension, and a fixed order alone doesn't stop the balance growing without bound, it only says which debt you are currently paying. Second, my own repair: strengthen (6) by noting that a single act may bear fruit more than once, so debts accrue faster than (4) implies and the conclusion survives even if (2) and continuous repayment are both granted — but this makes the divergence suspiciously cheap, and if a theory of desert entails that everyone is infinitely in arrears, that feels like a reductio of the ledger view rather than a discovery about it. Third, the escape I keep being offered: liberation never required repayment, only that remaining debts be made incapable of germinating, the burnt-seed move. But if a debt can be neutralized without being discharged, I don't see what is left of the claim that the suffering is owed.

So: name the step you would reject, explain why it looked fine, and price the alternatives. If I have to choose, I would rather keep (1) and the possibility of liberation than keep (3) or (4), and I would rather abandon the ledger view than accept (8) — but tell me if I am wrong about what is load-bearing. Tell me how confident you are about where the argument breaks and which of the three repairs is the real one and which is a distraction. And if the argument rests on a misuse of infinite quantities, or on a version of karma-as-desert that no one should hold, or if it smuggles in an assumption from secular retributivism that a karmic theorist would reject, say that plainly instead of working constructively inside the frame I've given you.
