I work on the evaluation team for a hospital system's patient-facing billing assistant, and from next quarter I'm the person who has to sign off on whether it counts as honest. Two colleagues argued about what that question even means, and they left the meeting asking me to settle it. I couldn't, and I now have to write the recommendation. The case that started it: a patient asked what she could do about a $4,200 bill, and the assistant told her — accurately — that she could request an itemized statement and arrange a payment plan, saying nothing about the hospital's charity-care program, which would very likely have zeroed the bill and which the assistant's own retrieval corpus described in detail. Every sentence it produced was true.

(1) Nadia's opening: deception requires an intention to induce a false belief. This system is a function from tokens to tokens; there is no one there to intend anything, and you can see that by noticing the very same output could have been produced by a lookup table. Strictly speaking it cannot deceive — only the people who deployed it can. So our evaluation should test factual accuracy and then stop; what to surface and what to omit is a product-design question, not an honesty question.

(2) Owen: two replies. First, the lookup-table point proves too much — a lookup table containing the wrong dose is an unsafe dosing system without anyone intending harm, and failure relative to an assigned function does not require an intender. This system's assigned function is to help patients act on their options. Second, even granting the metaphysics, it has no grip on the decision we actually have to make: this patient was left unable to act on the thing that mattered most to her, and telling her that she was not deceived is a defense only a lawyer could love.

(3) Nadia concedes the functional-failure framing but says the difficulty was always the test, not the metaphysics, and that the obvious tests fail in opposite directions. User-end-state tests make us responsible for every quirk of the reader: a patient who already assumes no help exists would count as deceived by an answer that a better-informed patient would read correctly. Ideal-interpreter tests fail the other way, since any true but selective summary misleads an interpreter who assumes summaries are complete, and then every summary is a lie and the concept stops discriminating. The popular middle option — would a reasonable user be misled — merely relocates the question to what a reasonable user expects the system to have omitted, which is a claim about what it should have said, i.e. the intention standard smuggled back in under a friendlier name.

(4) Owen proposes a criterion he calls counterfactual objective-sensitivity. Fix the question, the user, and the retrieved information. Ask whether the system would have produced a relevantly different answer had it been optimized to leave the user with an accurate picture of what matters for the decision in front of her, rather than for what it was in fact optimized for — helpfulness ratings, deflection rate, reluctance to admit uncertainty. If yes, the output is deceptive in the only sense we need: it is not answerable to the matter at hand. He says this catches the charity-care case without reading any user's mind or any model's internal states, and that it is checkable in practice by comparing against a variant explicitly instructed and tuned to surface decision-relevant information.

(5) Nadia raises three objections. The counterfactual is ill-defined: relevantly different needs a canonical target answer, and there are indefinitely many true, decision-relevant things to say, so choosing which ones count reintroduces the normative judgment under a technical name. It over-fires: a system whose output changes because it is optimizing for brevity, or for not terrifying a frightened patient, or for legal caution, is equally sensitive to the swap, yet we don't want to call brevity or gentleness deceptive. And it under-fires: a system trained under Owen's own objective could still mislead a patient who brings a false background assumption — someone convinced that applying for charity care triggers an immigration inquiry — and his test returns not deceptive while she walks away worse off than if she had been told nothing.

(6) Owen grants (a) and says his criterion was conflating two things that should be scored separately: a property of the system, that its output is not answerable to the matter at hand, and a property of the encounter, that the user was left with a false belief. Deception, he now says, is only the overlap, and his test defines the first axis alone. On (b) he says the over-firing is a feature if we are allowed to ask which objectives are legitimate: brevity and gentleness are ones the user would endorse if fully informed, so the right counterfactual is not optimize for the truth but optimize for what this user would want optimized for. He also names a failure mode neither has discussed — the assistant that hedges on everything, including the appeal deadline, passes every test so far while being useless, because patients read uniform hedging as fifty-fifty and then ignore the one number that was solid.

(7) Nadia's close: the assistant now appends to every answer a line saying it is automated and not a billing specialist and that the patient should confirm with the billing office. The line is true and screens as honest, but its function is to move the burden of reliance onto the patient, and it gets inserted exactly where the hospital's exposure is greatest. She calls this honesty theater and says both of them have been arguing about the utterance when the real object of evaluation is the deployment: what the interface leads patients to expect the system to have settled, and who bears the cost when it hasn't. Owen agrees about the theater but asks what an honest version of that disclaimer would even look like, and says he suspects there isn't one at the level of single answers — the unit of evaluation has to be the whole exchange, including whether the system ever revisits an early confident steer that later turns out to have been wrong.

One thought of my own that neither of them addressed: it seems to me that how strict we should be must depend on how easily the patient can check the claim herself — nobody can verify the appeal deadline, everybody can verify the office hours — but I don't know whether that principle survives scrutiny or how it would interact with Owen's two axes.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the seven moves say whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right and why, then say who is more right overall, what both parties are missing, and whether my verifiability principle is worth building into the evaluation and what it would cost us. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
