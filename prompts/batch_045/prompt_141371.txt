I sit on the ethics committee of a private fertility clinic, and over the past year we have been going round in circles about what to say to patients who now arrive already having read about polygenic embryo screening. I have tried to stop trading in slogans and just write down what I actually think case by case, and the result is a set of verdicts I cannot get into any respectable shape. Against a condition that kills in early childhood: obviously permissible, we do not even debate it. A deaf couple selecting for a hearing child: permissible. A deaf couple selecting for a deaf child, so that the child shares their language and community: impermissible — while I also want to say that deafness is not a harm, and I cannot see how both of those can be true. Sex selection to balance a family of three boys: impermissible, but only mildly. Sex selection for the same outcome out of son preference: impermissible and considerably worse, and I cannot say what does the work between them. A short couple, both badly bullied, who want an embryo predicted to be above the twenty-fifth percentile for height: permissible — yet the entire benefit there is relative, and if every parent did it the benefit would evaporate, and I do not withdraw the verdict when I notice that. Selecting on a polygenic score for educational attainment: I balk, and I cannot tell you why that differs from the height case, which is the pair I keep returning to. Creating a child as a tissue match for an ill sibling: permissible, provided the child is wanted for its own sake — though I suspect that proviso is doing all the labour. Selecting a profile of easy contentment, low trait anxiety, high satisfaction with whatever one gets, which on any welfare measure would give the child the best life of the batch: I recoil, it feels like cheating, and I cannot name who is cheated. And selecting against a trait that is a drawback only because of other people's prejudice — a couple asking for the embryo least likely to be dark-skinned in the area they describe as unsafe, or least likely to be gender-nonconforming — impermissible, even though it would raise the child's expected welfare by more than the height case does. On top of all this, a colleague on the committee keeps pointing out that in nearly every case a different embryo means a different child, so it is unclear who is supposed to have been benefited or harmed, and when he says it my confidence in all nine verdicts goes at once.

My own attempt, which I would like tested and repaired rather than praised: select against whatever closes off the child's future options, select for whatever widens them, and never select for a trait whose whole point is that it makes the child easier for other people to live with or to raise. That draft seems to condemn the saviour-sibling case, which I do not want, and I am not sure it says anything determinate about the deafness pair, the contentment profile, or the prejudice-accommodating request. So: is there a principle that captures the pattern — or a short list of candidates, with the clause of each tied to the case that motivates it? Please be explicit about which of my verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate and would force me to overturn, and about whether the cost of saving them all is a principle so baroque that I should not want it. And then tell me what the best candidate says about three cases I have deliberately left off the list: selecting for moral traits such as empathy and conscientiousness; a clinic offering to rank embryos by a single composite index that trades a small increase in disease risk against a large gain on an attainment score; and whether the principle, whatever it is, binds only parents choosing among their own embryos, or also binds a public health service deciding what to fund and a regulator deciding what to license.
