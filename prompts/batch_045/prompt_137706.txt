so I've got this half-baked way of thinking about referendums that I keep using and I want to see it actually fight a real case instead of just sounding clever in my head. British English throughout please, it's for a British outlet and I'll have to fix -ize endings otherwise.

The frame: a referendum result is an **incomplete contract**. It tells you which side won, but it leaves most of what has to happen next unspecified — so the interesting question isn't "what did the people say", it's who holds the *residual control rights*: who gets to decide what the result meant, at each point where it has to be made concrete, and how much room those actors have to reinterpret the mandate as they go. Two distinctions I want you to actually use, not just nod at:

- *mandate-preserving vs mandate-consuming* implementation. Some results are spent by being carried out once — a one-shot change in the law, nothing left to argue about. Others have to be re-derived at every subsequent decision, so each implementation step quietly reopens the very question the vote was supposed to have settled.
- the *counterfactual-stability test*. Take the outcome people actually ended up with, and ask whether the winning coalition, shown it in advance, would still have voted the same way. If not, the implementation was formally faithful and substantively not.

Main episode: Brexit, 2016 through to 2020. Do a proper mapping — who the parties to the notional contract were, what "leave" did and didn't commit to, where residual rights actually sat (government, Parliament, the courts, the Supreme Court case on whether ministers could trigger Article 50 alone, the 2017 election, the withdrawal agreement votes, the 2019 election), and how each actor's reading of "the will of the people" moved the outcome. Apply the counterfactual-stability test honestly: would the 2016 Leave coalition have taken the deal that was actually on offer? Then tell me where the contracting frame explains something I couldn't have got from "referendums collapse multidimensional questions into binary ones" — and where it's just a wanky relabelling of "the mandate was ambiguous".

Then a contrast case, same lens, different contract design: the Swiss federal popular initiative "against mass immigration", 9 February 2014, carried by roughly 50.3% against 49.7% and a majority of the cantons, adding an article (121a) plus a transitional clause to the federal constitution, which the Federal Assembly then had to implement without detonating the bilateral agreements on free movement. That's a much more *complete* contract on paper — actual binding text, not just a word — so: does completeness of initiative text reduce the residual-rights problem, or does it just relocate it into treaty conflict, implementing legislation and the next initiative? I'm hazier on the Swiss detail than the Brexit detail, so work from the shape of it rather than anything you're unsure about, and say where you're extrapolating.

Finally: is any of this predictive, or just a vocabulary? Specifically (a) on this view what even is a second referendum — breach, renegotiation, or the contract finally being completed? and (b) give the strongest version of the objection from the vox populi side, the view that a referendum isn't a contract at all but an instruction, or an exercise of constituent power that doesn't have to be interpreted, only obeyed, and say whether my frame can answer that or just assumes it away from the start.
