so i somehow ended up sitting in on a meeting of my university's new-ish Research Integrity Committee last week (my officemate is on it and dragged me along for the free sandwiches), and they were arguing about a draft policy that would make every grant application include a "falsification plan": a signed statement, submitted in advance, naming the observation that would lead the PI to abandon the project's central claim. Half the room thought this was obviously a great idea, half the room kept repeating that you never test a central claim on its own — you test it together with a pile of auxiliary assumptions about the instruments, the calibration, the background conditions, the statistics, where the samples came from — so any PI whose prediction fails can always save the central claim by blaming one of those, and nothing in the data can force them to stop. The meeting broke up after somebody said, only half as a joke, that the falsification-plan requirement didn't look falsifiable itself, and everyone laughed and went to get coffee. I've been chewing on that ever since and want to make something out of it.

Could you write me an Onion-style news article, 600-700 words, reporting that a national funding agency (invent it, give it a convincingly drab name) has actually rolled this rule out, with an automated testability score attached to every application? Here's the shape I have in mind, though feel free to move things around: the early applications that game the score — a PI whose central claim is so hedged and so contentless that nothing could contradict it, another whose claim is maximally falsifiable and dies within a week, a group in some invented field (membrane ecodynamics? narrative macrohistory?) that learns to predict an effect "somewhere in the region of parameter space not yet probed" and files that as a bold conjecture; the agency's subsequent crackdown, where reviewers have to distinguish revisions that add new testable content from revisions that merely protect the original claim, and where whole research programmes get put on probation for surviving only by repeatedly re-describing their failures as instrument error; and then the closing turn, where the compliance office runs its own methodology through its own scorer and the methodological ban on protective stratagems turns out to be a rule the office has decided to adopt rather than a claim that could be tested, and an official says this is fine, in a way that is obviously not fine.

Please keep it deadpan the whole way through — no winking, no "critics would say" paragraphs, no editorializing outside the invented world; the reader should feel the real problems (that you can't test a claim in isolation, that falsifiability is trivially easy to satisfy if you're allowed to bolt on auxiliaries, that what matters is whether a revision opens up new tests or just absorbs the blow, that the most testable theory is the one most likely to be dead) without anyone ever stating them as objections. Include a line or two of quoted comment from an invented spokesperson and one from an invented philosopher.
