I'm the lead nurse on a long-stay psychiatric rehab unit, and every eight weeks we have to score each resident's quality of life, because the score decides whether the care plan stays as it is -- meds, leave, how much we let people try things they might fail at. Our new clinical director is really into this thing called tranquilism (as I get it: the only thing that matters for how well someone's life is going is the absence of craving and the absence of suffering; getting what you want, or enjoying getting it, adds nothing on its own, and pleasure is only good when it's really just a craving switching off). He wants us scoring that way and I have to turn it into something my team can actually run in half an hour per resident, with a care assistant doing most of it and me signing off.

Write me the procedure: the questions in order, what counts as an answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the decision back because it's a values call rather than a fact one. It has to handle: what time window we score over, because someone asleep or sedated for nine hours is doing great on this metric and I don't think we want that; the difference between not having a craving and having one satisfied, suppressed, or just not acted on; residents who say they're fine and look wretched, and the other way round; and the craving-damping protocol we've started on, where we can more or less switch a craving off without stopping the person doing the thing they craved.

Then run it on three people: Dev, 34, flat and slow on a heavy antipsychotic, no cravings, no visible distress, watches daytime TV all day. Amara, 27, consumed with making her podcast work -- wants it constantly, gets it constantly, and is wrecked for an hour whenever an episode flops. Tomas, 58, who has asked us to stop dampening his cravings because he'd rather want things badly than not want them. I'm expecting at least one of these to come out with an answer I won't like, and I want you to say plainly which bits of the procedure I should refuse to follow and what the score just cannot tell us.
