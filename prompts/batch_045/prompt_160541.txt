ok so idk if this is a real thing philosophers have a word for, but here goes. Last spring I was stressing about college stuff and I made a rule for myself and typed it into my notes app: apply to at least two in-state public schools no matter what my senior-year self wants. The thing is, I didn't make that rule because I think future me will be lazy or tempted or weak — I'm pretty sure senior-year me is going to *genuinely* want to go somewhere far away and expensive and will actually believe that's the better choice, like fully endorse it, not just get seduced by a pretty campus or whatever. I made the rule because I expect my judgment to change, not because I expect my willpower to fail. My friend says that's just a Ulysses contract, but when I read about that it seemed like the whole point is that you know you'll be tempted and you want to be stopped — the guy strapped to the mast still thinks sailing past the singers is best, he just can't trust himself to do it once he hears them. Mine feels different, like I'm not restraining myself from something I'll still think is bad, I'm overriding a future person who will disagree with me about what's good in the first place.

And it keeps showing up in other places, which is why I think it might be one pattern. I'm on student council and we have this constitution that a bunch of seniors wrote two years ago — literally none of them go to this school anymore — and there's a clause in it saying we can't spend more than 30% of the budget on a single event, and to change it you need a two-thirds vote plus the advisor signing off. Everyone on council right now thinks that clause is dumb and I'm fairly confident it would lose if it came up as a fresh proposal. But whenever someone moves to suspend it, people act like that's cheating, and the argument is always about whether "we" decided it, even though the "we" who decided it is gone and the "we" now is like eleven completely different people. One guy on council keeps saying something about the dead hand of the past, which I think he half-remembers from somewhere, and someone else said we should just ask whether the rule is good *now*, and neither answer felt right to me.

So: is there an established name for this specific setup — binding a later self or a later group that you expect to *disagree with you about what's good*, rather than one you expect to fail to do what it already thinks is good? Or is it just lumped in with precommitment generally? My hypothesis is that it's genuinely a different animal and that the version with a person and the version with a group/constitution come apart somehow, because in the group case the actual people literally get replaced while in my case it's still me, but idk. I don't just want a label thrown at me though — I want to know whether it's really one phenomenon or two, what it is *not* (like how is it different from ordinary precommitment, and from just being stubborn or from a promise), where the edges are, and if there's no established term then give me one, name it and define it properly. Two more things I'm curious about while you're at it: does it matter if the later self ends up grateful later on, and does it matter whether the earlier self was actually *right* about what's good? Please start with your bottom line in the first sentence — like "yes, it's called X but that doesn't cover your case" or "no, there isn't one" — then give the reasoning, no preamble and don't restate my question back at me.
